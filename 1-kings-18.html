In the third year Elijah shows himself to Ahab, after Obadiah the steward has been sent ahead. On Mount Carmel the prophets of Baal call until the grain offering and no one answers; Elijah heals the altar of Jehovah, the fire falls, the people cry ‘Jehovah, he is God’, and the prophets of Baal are slaughtered at the Kishon. The rain comes, and Elijah runs before Ahab’s chariot to Jezreel.
‘In the third year’, of what the verse does not say. Luke 4:25 and James 5:17 give the drought three years and six months.

Y cuando Acab vio a Elías, le dijo Acab: «¿Eres tú, el que turba a Israel?».

22

And Elijah said to the people: “I have been left a prophet of Jehovah, I alone; and the prophets of Baal are four hundred and fifty men.note
Y dijo Elías al pueblo: «Yo he quedado profeta de Jehová, yo solo; y los profetas de Baal son cuatrocientos cincuenta hombres.
23
וְיִתְּנוּ-לָנוּ שְׁנַיִם פָּרִים, וְיִבְחֲרוּ לָהֶם הַפָּר הָאֶחָד וִינַתְּחֻהוּ וְיָשִׂימוּ עַל-הָעֵצִים, וְאֵשׁ, לֹא יָשִׂימוּ; וַאֲנִי אֶעֱשֶׂה אֶת-הַפָּר הָאֶחָד, וְנָתַתִּי עַל-הָעֵצִים, וְאֵשׁ, לֹא אָשִׂים.
And let them give us two bulls, and let them choose one bull for themselves and cut it into its pieces and put it on the wood, and they shall put no fire to it. And I will prepare the other bull and put it on the wood, and I will put no fire to it.note
Y dennos dos toros, y escojan ellos para sí un toro y córtenlo en sus pedazos y pónganlo sobre la leña, y no le pongan fuego. Y yo prepararé el otro toro y lo pondré sobre la leña, y no le pondré fuego.
24

And you call on the name of your god, and I will call on the name of Jehovah; and the God who answers by fire, he is God.” And all the people answered and said: “The word is good.”note
Y ustedes invoquen el nombre de su dios, y yo invocaré el nombre de Jehová; y el Dios que responda con fuego, él es Dios». Y todo el pueblo respondió y dijo: «Buena es la palabra».
25
וַיֹּאמֶר אֵלִיָּהוּ לִנְבִיאֵי הַבַּעַל, בַּחֲרוּ לָכֶם הַפָּר הָאֶחָד וַעֲשׂוּ רִאשֹׁנָה, כִּי אַתֶּם, הָרַבִּים; וְקִרְאוּ בְּשֵׁם אֱלֹהֵיכֶם, וְאֵשׁ לֹא תָשִׂימוּ.
And Elijah said to the prophets of Baal: “Choose for yourselves one bull and prepare it first, for you are the many; and call on the name of your god, and put no fire to it.”note
Y dijo Elías a los profetas de Baal: «Escojan para ustedes un toro y prepárenlo primero, porque ustedes son los muchos; e invoquen el nombre de su dios, y no le pongan fuego».
26

And they took the bull that he gave them, and prepared it, and called on the name of Baal from morning until noon, saying: “Baal, answer us!” And there was no voice, and no one answering. And they limped around the altar that had been made.note
Y tomaron el toro que les dio, y lo prepararon, e invocaron el nombre de Baal desde la mañana hasta el mediodía, diciendo: «¡Baal, respóndenos!». Y no hubo voz, ni quien respondiera. Y cojeaban alrededor del altar que se había hecho.
27
וַיְהִי בַצָּהֳרַיִם וַיְהַתֵּל בָּהֶם אֵלִיָּהוּ, וַיֹּאמֶר קִרְאוּ בְקוֹל-גָּדוֹל כִּי-אֱלֹהִים הוּא--כִּי שִׂיחַ וְכִי-שִׂיג לוֹ, וְכִי-דֶרֶךְ לוֹ; אוּלַי יָשֵׁן הוּא, וְיִקָץ.
And at noon Elijah mocked them and said: “Call with a great voice, for he is a god! For he is deep in thought, or he has stepped aside, or he is on a journey; perhaps he is asleep, and he will wake.”note

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–2 · 1 Kings 18:1–2 The third year of the drought — “after many days”, “show yourself”, and the famine in Samaria

“After many days … in the third year”. The widow’s household ate “for days” (1 Kings 17:15, now on these pages), and now the days have become many. Third year of what, the verse does not say: the drought, or the stay at Zarephath. Jesus and James give the drought as “three years and six months” (Luke 4:25; James 5:17, now on these pages), and the two counts are not reconciled here, because the text does not reconcile them. The TLB has “three years later”, which settles what the Hebrew leaves open.

“Go, show yourself”. The man told to hide (1 Kings 17:3, now on these pages) is told to be seen. The verb is the one for an appearing, and it comes three times in this chapter, here, in verse 2, and in Elijah’s oath at verse 15. The ASV has “show thyself”, the KJV “shew thyself”, and the NIV “present yourself”, which is court language and loses the hiding. The TLB has “Go and tell King Ahab”, which is a different errand.

“I will give rain on the face of the ground”. The words are the ones Elijah gave the widow: “until the day Jehovah gives rain on the face of the ground” (1 Kings 17:14, now on these pages). The ASV and the KJV have “send rain upon the earth”. The NWT 1984 has “give rain upon the surface of the ground”, tighter on both words. The famine is “severe in Samaria”, the city where Ahab built the house of Baal (1 Kings 16:32, now on these pages).

Verses 3–6 · 1 Kings 18:3–6 Obadiah, Ahab’s steward — a hundred prophets in caves, “cut off”, and a king looking for grass

Obadiah. Ovadyahu means “servant of Jehovah”, and the narrator adds that he “feared Jehovah greatly”, so the name and the sentence say one thing. He is not the prophet of the book of Obadiah, or at least nothing connects them (encyclopedia). “Over the household” is the title of Ahishar under Solomon (1 Kings 4:6) and of Arza, in whose house Elah was drinking when he was killed (1 Kings 16:9, both now on these pages). The ASV has “who was over the household”, the KJV “the governor of his house”, and the NIV “his palace administrator”. A man who feared Jehovah greatly is running the palace of the man who built Baal’s house.

“When Jezebel cut off the prophets of Jehovah”. This is the first the reader hears of it, in a subordinate clause. The verb is hikhrit. Ahab uses the same verb in the next verse: “and not cut off any of the beasts”. She cuts off prophets and he hopes not to cut off mules. The NWT 1984 has “cut off” in both places and is the witness that keeps the echo. The NIV has “killing off” and then “kill”, and the ASV and the KJV have “cut off” and then “lose not all the beasts”.

⚠ “Fifty men in a cave”. The Hebrew has one noun in the singular, “in the cave”, and verse 13 doubles the number, “fifty, fifty men”, which is how Hebrew says “fifty each”. How many caves that makes, it leaves to the reader. The ASV and the KJV have “by fifty in a cave”. The NIV and the TLB have “two caves”, which is the arithmetic and is not in the text. The Douay has “fifty and fifty in caves”.

“And sustained them”. The verb is kilkel again, for the third time in two chapters. Jehovah commanded the ravens to sustain Elijah, and then a widow (1 Kings 17:4, 9, now on these pages), and now the king’s own steward sustains a hundred prophets, in a famine. The ASV and the KJV have “fed”, and the NIV “supplied”.

A king looking for grass. In the third year Ahab goes out in person, and the reason he gives is “horse and mule”. “They divided the land between them” uses the words of Joshua’s allotment, “and they divided the land” (Joshua 14:5, now on these pages), and all that is left to divide is the search for pasture. The brooks are the same nechalim as the one that dried up on Elijah. The NWT 1984 has “torrent valleys”, and the NIV “valleys”. “By himself” is levaddo, twice, and Elijah will say the same word of himself at verse 22.

Verses 7–16 · 1 Kings 18:7–16 Obadiah meets Elijah — “Look — Elijah”, two lords, and the spirit that carries people off

“Is this you, my lord Elijah?” Two words of Hebrew, ha’attah zeh, and Ahab will open with the same two at verse 17, “Is this you, troubler of Israel?” The two men ask one question, with different nouns at the end. The ASV has “Is it thou”, the NIV “Is it really you”, and the NWT 1984 “Is this you”, which this page follows, because the phrase has to return.

Two lords. Obadiah falls on his face and says “my lord Elijah”. Elijah answers “say to your lord”, and he means Ahab. Obadiah then uses “my lord” for Ahab in verse 10 and for Elijah again in verse 13. The man has two lords in one speech, and the danger of his position is in the pronouns. The Hebrew has one word, adon, all the way through. The NWT 1984 and the ASV keep “lord”, and the NIV has “your master” at verse 8, “my master” at verse 10 and “my lord” at verse 13, which sorts out what the Hebrew leaves tangled.

“Look — Elijah”. The message is two words, hinneh Eliyahu, and the steward says them three times, more afraid each time (verses 8, 11, 14). The ASV and the KJV have “Behold, Elijah is here”, with the last two words supplied, and the NWT 1984 has “Here is Elijah”. He calls himself avdekha, “your servant”, and his name means servant of Jehovah.

“As Jehovah your God lives”. The phrase stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and the widow’s (1 Kings 17:12, now on these pages). A Sidonian widow and the steward of Israel’s palace say it in the same words to the same man. Then comes the news that Ahab has had him hunted through every nation and kingdom, and has made each one swear. The reader knows that for much of that time he was in the country of the queen’s father. The TLB has “forced the king of that nation to swear”, and the Hebrew has the kingdom and the nation. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition has Ahab set fire to the kingdom instead of putting it on oath.

“The spirit of Jehovah will carry you”. Obadiah speaks of it as a thing that simply happens to Elijah. The fear comes back in the mouths of the sons of the prophets at 2 Kings 2:16, and the New Testament has the same event with Philip (Acts 8:39, now on these pages). The KJV and the ASV have “the Spirit”, and the NWT 1984 and the Douay “the spirit”.

⚠ “And your servant has feared Jehovah from my youth”. The Hebrew changes person in the middle: “your servant”, and then “my youth”. He is being polite and frightened at once. The KJV and the ASV have “I thy servant … from my youth”, the NWT 1984 “from his youth”, and the Douay “from his infancy”. The NWT 1984 is usually the tighter witness, and here the Hebrew has “my”.

“As Jehovah of armies lives, before whom I stand”. Elijah repeats his oath of 1 Kings 17:1 (now on these pages), with a new title. The NIV has “Almighty” and “whom I serve”, and the ASV “Jehovah of hosts … before whom I stand”. “Today I will show myself to him” is the verb of verse 1, and the steward’s six-verse fear is answered in one word, hayyom, “today”. Verse 16 is a chain of meetings: Obadiah goes to meet Ahab, and Ahab goes to meet Elijah.

Verses 17–20 · 1 Kings 18:17–20 “Troubler of Israel” — Achan’s verb, the Baals, and Jezebel’s table on Mount Carmel

⭐ “Troubler of Israel”. The word is okher, from the verb Joshua threw at Achan, “Why have you troubled us? Jehovah will trouble you this day” (Joshua 7:25, now on these pages). Ahab’s greeting calls Elijah the man whose sin brought ruin on everybody, and Achan ended under a heap of stones. Elijah takes the verb and hands it back: “I have not troubled Israel, but you”. Jacob used it of Simeon and Levi (Genesis 34:30) and Jephthah of his daughter (Judges 11:35, both now on these pages). The ASV has “thou troubler of Israel”, and the KJV “he that troubleth Israel”. The NWT 1984 has “the bringer of ostracism upon Israel”, an unusual choice, and the TLB “the man who brought this disaster upon Israel”.

“By your forsaking … and you went”. “Your forsaking” is plural, Ahab and his father’s house, and “you went” is singular, Ahab himself. The ASV shows it: “ye have forsaken … thou hast followed”. The NIV has “You have abandoned … and have followed”, and English “you” hides the change. The Baals are plural, the word of Judges 2:11 (now on these pages).

Mount Carmel. Carmel stands on the coast, the wet end of the country, between Israel and the Phoenician towns. The verse does not say why Elijah chose it.

“The prophets of the Asherah”. Ahab made “the Asherah” at 1 Kings 16:33 (now on these pages), and now she has four hundred prophets. The ASV has “the prophets of the Asherah”, the NIV “the four hundred prophets of Asherah”, and the KJV, the Geneva and the Douay “the prophets of the groves”, which turns a goddess into trees. The NWT 1984 has “the sacred pole”. Four hundred and fifty and four hundred make eight hundred and fifty. Only the four hundred and fifty appear again in the Hebrew, at verses 22, 25 and 40. The four hundred are summoned and never mentioned, and verse 20 does not say whether they came.

“Who eat at the table of Jezebel”. To eat at a table is to be kept by it: David asked it for the sons of Barzillai (1 Kings 2:7, now on these pages), and Solomon’s table was what the twelve officers supplied. In the third year of a famine the queen’s table is feeding eight hundred and fifty prophets, and Obadiah’s caves are feeding a hundred on bread and water. Verse 20 has Ahab do as he is told by the man he has been hunting, and the text gives no reason.

Verses 21–24 · 1 Kings 18:21–24 “How long will you go limping?” — Elijah’s challenge on Carmel, two opinions, and the God who answers by fire

⭐ “How long will you go limping on the two opinions?” The verb is pasach, to limp, the verb that has been read in the name of the Passover (Exodus 12, now on these pages). It returns at verse 26, of the prophets of Baal at their altar, so Israel and Baal’s prophets are doing the same thing. The noun is uncertain. The word se’ippim stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and “those who are of two minds” (Psalm 119:113, now on these pages). Its relatives are boughs (Ezekiel 31:6) and clefts in rock (Isaiah 2:21), so the picture may be a fork. A bird on two branches and a walker at two roads have both been proposed, and neither can be proved.

The KJV and the Geneva have “halt ye between two opinions”, the ASV “go ye limping between the two sides”, the Douay “halt between two sides”, and the NIV “waver between two opinions”, which drops the limp. The NWT 1984 has “limping upon two different opinions”, and it is right about the preposition: the Hebrew says on. This page follows it, keeps the article, and takes “opinions” as the best English guess for the noun. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition has them limping “on both hams”.

“If Jehovah is God”. The Hebrew has the article, “the God”, and so does the end of the chapter. The offer is made in identical words for each side, “go after him”, and “go after” is what verse 18 has just said Ahab did with the Baals. The NIV has “follow him”. The NWT 1984 brackets “[true] God”.

⭐ “And the people did not answer him a word”. From here the chapter runs on one verb, anah, to answer. The people do not answer. The God who answers by fire will be God. The people answer, “The word is good”. “Baal, answer us” (verse 26), and no answer comes (verses 26 and 29). “Answer me, Jehovah, answer me” (verse 37). The TLB stops the verse at “follow him!” and loses the silence.

“I have been left a prophet of Jehovah, I alone”. The Hebrew is levaddi, the word for Ahab and Obadiah going their ways “by himself”. The reader has just heard of a hundred prophets in the caves, and Obadiah has just told Elijah about them to his face. Elijah says it again at 1 Kings 19:10 (now on these pages), and Paul quotes that later form (Romans 11:3, now on these pages). This chapter does not comment. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition adds the four hundred prophets of the groves to his count.

“Cut it into its pieces”. The verb is the one for a burnt offering in Leviticus 1:6 (now on these pages), and “the wood” has its article as it does there. Elijah is following the rules on a mountain with no priest. The KJV has “lay it on wood”, and the ASV “on the wood”.

“Your god”. The Hebrew eloheikhem is a plural form that serves for one god or many. The KJV has “your gods” at verses 24 and 25, and the Douay “the names of your gods”. The ASV and the NIV have “your god”. “Call on the name” is the phrase of Genesis 4:26 and of Romans 10:13 (now on these pages).

“The God who answers by fire, he is God”. Baal was a storm god, a god of rain and thunder, so the test is on his own ground, though the verse does not say so. The ASV and the KJV have “let him be God”, and the NIV “he is God”, which is what the Hebrew says. “The word is good” is tov ha-davar, Shimei’s answer to Solomon (1 Kings 2:38, now on these pages). The ASV has “It is well spoken”, and the NWT 1984 “The thing is good”. Verse 21’s silent crowd has found something it can agree to.

Verses 25–29 · 1 Kings 18:25–29 Elijah mocks the prophets of Baal — “stepped aside”, the god who sleeps, and “no one listening”

“For you are the many”. The Hebrew has the article, ha-rabbim. The ASV and the KJV have “ye are many”, the NIV “there are so many of you”, and the NWT 1984 “YOU are the majority”, which has the sting: one man is giving the majority first turn. “The bull that he gave them” is active in the Hebrew, and it does not say who gave it. The Douay and the NWT 1984 have “he gave”, and the ASV and the KJV “was given”.

“And there was no voice”. “Baal, answer us” is two words of Hebrew, and the KJV and the ASV have “hear us”, which breaks the chain of verse 21. The NIV keeps “answer us”. The word qol, “voice”, is doing work here. Elijah’s voice was listened to in the last chapter (1 Kings 17:22, now on these pages). Here there is none, twice, and Elijah will tell them to use a great one.

⚠ “They limped around the altar”. This is the verb of verse 21 again, and al again. Mephibosheth “became lame” with the same root (2 Samuel 4:4, now on these pages). A limping dance as part of the rite has been proposed, and this page cannot show it. The KJV has “leaped upon the altar”, the ASV “leaped about”, the NIV “danced”, and the NWT 1984 “kept limping around”, which this page follows, because “danced” hides the joke. “That had been made” is a singular verb in the Hebrew, “he made”, with no subject. The ASV has “which was made”. The NIV and the NWT 1984 have “they had made”, which is what the sense wants and the Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition has.

⭐ “Elijah mocked them”. This form of the verb stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the root is Delilah’s, “you have mocked me” (Judges 16:10, now on these pages). “For he is a god” is ki-elohim hu, with the same words the people will cry at verse 39 in another order, and here it is a joke. The KJV and the ASV have “for he is a god”, and the NIV “Surely he is a god!”, which brings out the tone and adds a word.

⚠ “He is deep in thought, or he has stepped aside”. The two nouns are siach and sig, and they are the hardest thing in the chapter. Siach is usually musing or complaint. The noun sig stands nowhere else with this sense, looks like the root for moving away, and may be an old polite way of leaving the room. Gary Rendsburg argues that the pair together means something plainer, that the god is busy excreting, and he reports that the Targum and Rashi take “stepped aside” that way (Catholic Biblical Quarterly 50, 1988). The shelf divides on it. The KJV has “talking, or he is pursuing”, the Geneva “talketh or pursueth his enemies”, the ASV “musing, or he is gone aside”, the NIV “deep in thought, or busy”, and the Douay “talking, or is in an inn”. The NWT 1984 has “he has excrement and has to go to the privy”, and the TLB “out sitting on the toilet”. That reading is interpretation, and it is defensible. “Stepped aside” is meant to leave the door ajar as the Hebrew seems to.

“Perhaps he is asleep, and he will wake”. The Hebrew has a plain future, “and he will wake”. The KJV and the ASV have “must be awaked”, and the NIV “must be awakened”. The joke may have had a target. Josephus, quoting Menander on Hiram of Tyre, says Hiram was the first to hold the egersis of Heracles in the month Peritius (Antiquities 8.146). The Greek word can mean a raising or an awakening. Whiston takes it as setting up a temple, and others are reported to take it as a rite of waking the god. That Tyre’s Heracles is Melqart, and that Melqart is the Baal of Jezebel’s house, are scholars’ steps and are not in the text.

“They gashed themselves according to their ordinance”. The verb is the one forbidden at Deuteronomy 14:1 (now on these pages), “You shall not gash yourselves”. Kemishpatam is literally “according to their ordinance”, the phrase Leviticus uses for its own procedure (Leviticus 9:16, now on these pages), and the narrator lets it stand. The ASV and the KJV have “after their manner”, and the NIV “as was their custom”. The Hebrew has cherev and romach, sword and spear, the weapons of war. The NIV has “swords and spears”, the KJV “knives and lancets”, and the ASV “knives and lances”.

“They prophesied”. The verb is the one used of Saul with the spear in his hand (1 Samuel 18:10, now on these pages). The NIV has “frantic prophesying”, and the TLB “raved”, and those say what it looked like. The KJV and the ASV keep “prophesied”, and this page does too, so that “prophet” and “prophesied” stay one word in both camps.

“Until the going up of the grain offering”. The Hebrew has minchah and no “evening”. The KJV has “the evening sacrifice”, and the ASV “the evening oblation”. The NWT 1984 has “the going up of the grain offering”. The word marks the hour by a rite in a temple Israel has walked away from, though the law does pair the grain offering with the lamb “between the two evenings” (Exodus 29:41, now on these pages).

The silence grows. The list has gained a member. The last of it, ve’ein qashev, “and no listening”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at 2 Kings 4:31, where Gehazi lays Elisha’s staff on a dead child and gets the same silence. The KJV and the ASV have “nor any that regarded”, and the NIV “paid attention”. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition adds a scene here, in which Elijah tells them to stand aside, and they go.

Verses 30–35 · 1 Kings 18:30–35 Elijah heals the altar of Jehovah — twelve stones, the trench, and four jars of water in a drought

“Come near to me”. The words are Joseph’s, “Come near to me, please” (Genesis 45:4, now on these pages), and Joshua’s before the Jordan (Joshua 3:9, now on these pages). He wants them close enough to see that there is no trick. Josephus says as much: “lest he should privately hide fire among the pieces of wood” (Antiquities 8.13.5, Whiston’s numbering).

⭐ “And he healed the altar”. The verb is rafa, the verb of “I am Jehovah, your healer” (Exodus 15:26, now on these pages). It is not the ordinary word for repairing stonework. The ASV, the KJV and the NIV have “repaired”, and the NWT 1984 “mend”. This page keeps the strange word, because a chapter of limping is being answered.

An altar of Jehovah on Carmel. The verse says there was one, and that it had been torn down, and it does not say by whom. Elijah will accuse Israel of it at 1 Kings 19:10 (now on these pages). The book that condemns the high places (1 Kings 15:14, now on these pages) and the law of the one place (Deuteronomy 12:13, now on these pages) stand behind this story, and the narrator has no complaint against this altar. Later rabbinic discussion is reported to treat it as a one-time exception. Tacitus, writing of Vespasian, says of Carmel, in Fyfe’s translation: “There is no image or temple: only an altar where they reverently worship” (Histories 2.78).

Twelve stones. The kingdom has been torn in two since 1 Kings 12 (now on these pages), and the man is standing in the north. He takes “twelve stones, according to the number of the tribes of the sons of Jacob”, as Moses set up twelve pillars (Exodus 24:4) and as Joshua took twelve stones from the river (Joshua 4:3, both now on these pages). The phrase “twelve stones” in this spelling stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible. “Israel shall be your name” is Genesis 35:10 (now on these pages), and the sentence reminds the northern kingdom whose name it is using. The TLB cuts the clause.

⚠ The plot for the trench. The Hebrew is ke-veit sa’tayim zera, “as a house of two seahs of seed” (the measure of Genesis 18:6, now on these pages). The expression stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. A “house” of seed is most naturally the ground that much seed will sow, and Leviticus values a field “according to its seed” (Leviticus 27:16, now on these pages). The NWT 1984 has “the area sowed with two seah measures of seed”. The ASV and the KJV have “as great as would contain two measures of seed”, and the NIV “large enough to hold two seahs of seed”, which make it a capacity. The Douay has “the breadth of two furrows”, and the TLB “about three feet wide”, which the Hebrew does not give.

⚠ “Fill four jars with water”. The English numbering splits here. The KJV, the ASV and the NIV print this order at the end of their verse 33, and so does the NWT 1984. The Hebrew has it in verse 34, with the Douay and the Geneva. The word is kad, the widow’s jar of flour. Four of them are filled, three times, and poured out on the wood in the third year of a drought, and nothing says where the water came from. Josephus supplies “the water of the fountain” (Antiquities 8.13.5), which the Hebrew does not have. The ASV has “jars”, the KJV and the Geneva “barrels”, and the Douay “buckets”. The orders are one word each in the Hebrew, roughly “second it” and “third it”.

Verses 36–39 · 1 Kings 18:36–39 Elijah’s prayer and the fire of Jehovah — “by your word”, “their heart backward”, and “Jehovah, he is God”

“Jehovah, God of Abraham, Isaac and Israel”. The usual formula ends in Jacob. This form stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and twice in Chronicles. It follows the name he has just recalled at verse 31. The prayer is short. The prophets of Baal took a day, and this takes two verses.

⭐ “By your word I have done all these things”. The written Hebrew has “your words” and the reading tradition “your word” (see ketiv / qere), and the translation follows the reading. The thread started with “except by my word” (1 Kings 17:1, now on these pages) and went through the widow’s “the word of Jehovah in your mouth is truth” (1 Kings 17:24). Now he says whose word it was. The ASV and the KJV have “at thy word”, the NIV “at your command”, and the NWT 1984 “by your word”. “In Israel” is exact: the Douay and the Geneva have “the God of Israel”, and the ASV “God in Israel”, a claim about where he is God. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition has him ask for fire by name.

⚠ “And you have turned their heart backward”. This is the hardest clause of the prayer, and the readings pull in opposite directions. The word acharannit, “backward”, stands in six verses of the Hebrew Bible, and on these pages it is physical: Shem and Japheth walking in backward (Genesis 9:23) and Eli falling backward off his seat (1 Samuel 4:18, both now on these pages). One reading has God turning their heart back to himself, and the ASV and the KJV have “turned their heart back again”, the NIV “turning their hearts back again”, and the TLB “brought them back to yourself”. The other has God himself behind their turning away, so that when they see the fire they will know that too. The NWT 1984 has “have turned their heart back”, close to the Hebrew and as open. The Hebrew verb is a perfect, past in form, and the present tense of the NIV chooses a reading. This page keeps “backward” and does not choose.

⭐ “And the fire of Jehovah fell”. The sentence is built on Leviticus 9:24: fire comes out and consumes the burnt offering, all the people see, and they fall on their faces (now on these pages). That was the first offering of the priesthood at the tent of meeting, and this is one man on a hill in the north. “The fire of Jehovah” is also what burned at the edge of the camp (Numbers 11:1, now on these pages). The list goes downward: offering, wood, stones, dust, water. The stones are the twelve he has just healed into an altar, and they are consumed with the rest.

“It licked up”. The form stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the verb is Moab’s picture of an ox at pasture (Numbers 22:4, now on these pages). Fire is doing what a cow does to grass. The ASV, the KJV and the NIV have “licked up”. The TLB has “evaporated”, and Josephus has “till the very water was set on fire”.

⭐ “Jehovah, he is God! Jehovah, he is God!” The people have been silent since verse 24, and these are their last words. The sentence stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, among them Moses’ “You were shown this” (Deuteronomy 4:35, now on these pages) and Solomon’s blessing (1 Kings 8:60, now on these pages). Psalm 100 has it without the article (Psalm 100:3, now on these pages). Elijah’s own name, Eliyahu, is “my God is Jehovah”, so the crowd has ended by saying his name in other words. The ASV has “Jehovah, he is God”, and the TLB “Jehovah is God!”, which prints the name. The NWT 1984 has “Jehovah is the [true] God!”. Jewish liturgy is reported to close the Day of Atonement with this cry.

Verse 40 · 1 Kings 18:40 Elijah kills the prophets of Baal at the brook Kishon — “slaughtered”, Deuteronomy, and the use made of this verse

“Let not a man of them escape”. The Geneva has “let not a man of them escape”, and the ASV and the KJV “let not one of them escape”. The brook Kishon runs at the foot of Carmel, and it is the brook that “swept them away” for Deborah (Judges 5:21, now on these pages).

⚠ “And slaughtered them there”. The verb is shachat, the word for killing an animal at the altar (Leviticus 1:5, now on these pages). The subject is singular: Elijah. The NIV has “had them brought down … and slaughtered”, which makes him the one giving the order. The Hebrew has him do it. The ASV and the KJV have “slew”, and the TLB “killed”.

The law behind it is Deuteronomy 13:6 (now on these pages), “that prophet … shall be put to death”, with 18:20 on the prophet who speaks in the name of other gods. The story has also told the reader that Jezebel killed the prophets of Jehovah (verses 4 and 13). The chapter gives neither as a reason. Elijah’s words at verse 36, “by your word I have done all these things”, come before this verse. There is no word of Jehovah commanding it, and the narrator does not praise or blame it.

⚠ How the verse has been used. It has been cited as a warrant for killing religious opponents. In his Sermon to the Princes of 13 July 1524, Thomas Müntzer told the Saxon rulers that those who oppose God’s revelation should “be strangled without any mercy”, as Hezekiah, Josiah, Cyrus, Daniel and “Elijah, 1 Kings 18” destroyed the priests of Baal (Baylor’s translation). That is a misuse of the text. The verse is a narrative of what one prophet did, and a narrative does not command its reader. The law it stands near is addressed to Israel about its own covenant, and it gives no Christian prince a sword against other Christians. When two disciples offered to call down fire on a Samaritan village, Jesus “turned and rebuked them” (Luke 9:54–55, now on these pages).

Verses 41–46 · 1 Kings 18:41–46 The rain returns — “the voice of the roar of the rain”, a cloud like a man’s palm, and Elijah running before Ahab to Jezreel

⭐ “The voice of the roar of the rain”. The Hebrew is qol hamon ha-geshem. All afternoon there was “no voice” (verses 26 and 29), and the next qol in the chapter is the rain’s. Hamon is the noise of a crowd, the “multitude” of Sisera’s army (Judges 4:7, now on these pages). The ASV and the KJV have “sound of abundance of rain”, the NIV “the sound of a heavy rain”, and the NWT 1984 “the sound of the turmoil of a downpour”, which is the nearest. This page keeps “voice”, because the word has to carry from verse 26. The servant is about to report nothing at all in the sky, so Elijah is hearing it ahead of anyone.

“He crouched to the ground”. The verb stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and twice of Elisha over the Shunammite’s son (2 Kings 4:34–35). When Elijah stretched himself on the child he used another verb, and the one that reaches Elisha’s story is this. The Geneva has “crouched”, the NWT 1984 “crouching”, the ASV “bowed himself down”, and the KJV “cast himself down”. The verse has no word for prayer. James reads it as prayer: “he prayed again, and the heaven gave rain” (James 5:18, now on these pages).

“Go back, seven times”. The Hebrew does not say how the sevens were counted. The NIV has “Seven times Elijah said”, and the TLB “Go again, and again, and again”. Na’ar is a young attendant, and he goes up to look while the prophet stays down. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition writes the seven journeys out.

“A small cloud like a man’s palm”. The word is kaf, the hollow of a hand or the sole of a foot. The NWT 1984 has “a small cloud like a man’s palm”, the ASV “as small as a man’s hand”, and the Douay “like a man’s foot”, with the Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition, which has a man’s footprint. It rises “from the sea”, the Mediterranean, the side the rain comes from. Carmel is the first land it meets.

⭐ “So that the rain does not shut you in”. The verb is atsar, the verb of the drought itself: “he will shut up the heavens and there will be no rain” (Deuteronomy 11:17), “When the heavens are shut up” (1 Kings 8:35, both now on these pages). For three years it has been the heavens that were shut, and now the danger is that the rain will shut up the king. The ASV and the KJV have “stop thee not”, and the NIV “before the rain stops you”. “Harness” is Joseph’s verb at Genesis 46:29 (now on these pages), and the NIV has “Hitch up your chariot”. The word “chariot” is supplied by the sense.

“While this and that went on”. The phrase ad-koh ve-ad-koh, “until thus and until thus”, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and its sense, guessed from context, is something like “in no time”. The KJV has “in the mean while”, the ASV “in a little while”, and the Douay “while he turned himself this way and that way”. “Grew black” stands in this form in one verse. This is the rain the whole of chapter 17 was waiting for, and the verse gives it three words of Hebrew and moves on to Ahab. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition has Ahab weep where the Hebrew has him ride, so the Greek seems to have read a different verb.

“The hand of Jehovah was on Elijah”. The ASV and the KJV keep “the hand”. The NIV has “power”, and the TLB “special strength”, which say what the phrase means and take the hand away. “He bound up his loins” is shinnes, a verb that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. It is not the usual verb for girding, the one of Exodus 12:11 (now on these pages). The ASV and the KJV have “girded up his loins”. The NIV has “tucking his cloak into his belt”, which is probably the action.

⭐ “And he ran before Ahab”. To run before someone is what a king’s men do: Absalom had “fifty men to run before him” (2 Samuel 15:1) and so did Adonijah (1 Kings 1:5, both now on these pages). The man Ahab hunted through every kingdom ends the day running in front of the royal chariot like a palace runner, in the rain, on foot, and ahead of it. From the traditional site of the contest to Jezreel is reported as some twenty-five kilometres. The chapter stops there, with the king and the prophet going to the same town, and 1 Kings 19 (now on these pages) opens with Ahab telling Jezebel.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “show yourself”, the hidden man told to be seen; “cut off” for Jezebel and for Ahab’s mules alike; “sustained” for kilkel, the third time; “fifty men in a cave”, with no “two”; “Is this you” for Obadiah and for Ahab; “Look — Elijah”, three times; “lord” for adon, all the way through a man with two; “troubler” and “troubled”, Achan’s verb; “limping on the two opinions”, and “limped” again at the altar; “answer” every time; “stepped aside”, with the door left ajar; “gashed”, as in Deuteronomy 14; “according to their ordinance”; “prophesied” in both camps; “the grain offering”, with no “evening”; “healed” the altar; “jars” for kad; “backward”, unresolved; “consumed” and “licked up”; “slaughtered”; “the voice of the roar of the rain”; “a man’s palm”; “shut you in”, the verb of the shut heavens; and “bound up his loins”, a verb found nowhere else. The written and read Hebrew are recorded at verse 36.

Echoes paid. “Except by my word” (1 Kings 17:1) and the word thread through verses 24 and 36; “for days” become “many days” (1 Kings 17:15); hide and show (1 Kings 17:3); rain on the face of the ground (1 Kings 17:14); to sustain (1 Kings 4:7; 17:4, 9); “over the household” (1 Kings 4:6; 16:9); “As Jehovah your God lives” (1 Kings 17:12); “before whom I stand” (1 Kings 17:1); the troubler (Joshua 7:25); the Asherah (1 Kings 16:33); to limp (Exodus 12; 2 Samuel 4:4); two minds (Psalm 119:113); gashing (Deuteronomy 14:1); “Come near to me” (Genesis 45:4; Joshua 3:9); twelve stones (Exodus 24:4; Joshua 4:3); “Israel shall be your name” (Genesis 35:10); the widow’s jar (1 Kings 17:12); the fire of Leviticus 9:24; “Jehovah, he is God” (Deuteronomy 4:35; 1 Kings 8:60; Psalm 100:3); the brook Kishon (Judges 5:21); the shut heavens (Deuteronomy 11:17; 1 Kings 8:35); and the pages that had already cited this chapter (Luke 4:25; James 5:17–18; Acts 8:39; Romans 10:13; 11:3).

Echoes planted. “I alone” (1 Kings 19:10, now on these pages); the altars torn down (1 Kings 19:10, now on these pages); Ahab telling Jezebel (1 Kings 19:1, now on these pages); the spirit that carries off (2 Kings 2:16); “no voice … no one listening” (2 Kings 4:31); and the crouching verb (2 Kings 4:34–35). The 2 Kings passages are yet to come in this translation.
