Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–4 · encouragement and partnership, the same mind, selfish ambition and empty glory, and humility

“If, then, there is any encouragement in Christ … any partnership in the Spirit.” Four “if” clauses, each assuming the thing is so: there is encouragement, there is consolation. Paraklēsis is encouragement or appeal (dictionary). Koinōnia is the “partnership” of Philippians 1:5 (dictionary); whether pneumatos is the Spirit they share or a shared human spirit, the Greek does not mark. Splanchna is the “inward affection” of Philippians 1:8 (dictionary). The KJV reads “if any bowels and mercies”; the NWT 1984 reads “if any sharing of spirit”; the TLB turns the four clauses into questions: “Is there any such thing as Christians cheering each other up?”

“Make my joy complete: be of the same mind.” Phroneō is to set the mind on something, to think a certain way; it is the “think” of Philippians 1:7, it comes twice in this verse and again in v5, and at Philippians 4:2 Euodia and Syntyche are urged “to be of the same mind in the Lord.” “Have the same mind toward one another” is the instruction of Romans 12:16. Sympsychoi, “joined in soul,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The KJV reads “that ye be likeminded, having the same love, being of one accord, of one mind”; the NWT 1984 reads “being joined together in soul, holding the one thought in mind.”

“Selfish ambition … empty glory … counting one another as surpassing yourselves.” Eritheia, “selfish ambition,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament; at Philippians 1:17 it was the motive of the rival preachers, and here it is named as a danger inside the congregation. Kenodoxia, “empty glory,” and its adjective stand in two verses: this one and Galatians 5:26, “Let us not become hungry for empty glory.” Tapeinophrosynē is the “humility” of Ephesians 4:2 (dictionary). Hyperechō, “surpass,” is the verb of Philippians 4:7, the peace that “surpasses all understanding.” The KJV reads “let each esteem other better than themselves”; the NWT 1984 reads “considering that the others are superior to YOU”; the NIV reads “value others above yourselves.”

Verses 5–8 · the hymn, the form of God, “a thing to be grasped,” emptied himself, and death on a cross

⚠ “Have this mind among yourselves which is also in Christ Jesus.” The second half of the sentence has no verb in Greek, “this, which also in Christ Jesus,” and readers fill it two ways. Most supply “was”: think as Christ thought, and the lines that follow are his example. Others supply “you have”: think among yourselves in the way that belongs to those who are in Christ Jesus. This page keeps the Greek’s openness and does not choose. The Byzantine text reads “For let this mind be in you,” and so the KJV: “Let this mind be in you, which was also in Christ Jesus”; the NIV reads “have the same mindset as Christ Jesus”; the NWT 1984 reads “Keep this mental attitude in YOU that was also in Christ Jesus.”

⚠ Verses 6–11 — a hymn? These lines are set out here as verse because many readers take them as a hymn, perhaps older than the letter, which Paul quotes; others think he wrote them for this place. Either way they move in two halves: down, from the form of God to a slave’s death (vv6–8), and up, from “Therefore God” to every knee (vv9–11). Colossians 1:15 begins another passage often read as a hymn. The verse division differs between editions: the critical text ends v7 with “and being found in outward form as a human being,” while the KJV begins v8 with it: “And being found in fashion as a man, he humbled himself.”

⚠ “Existing in the form of God” — morphē. Morphē, “form,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: Mark 16:12 and the two here, “the form of God” and “the form of a slave” (dictionary). What it means is argued: the outward shape by which a thing is known, or, as others read it here, the very nature. The two phrases are set against each other on purpose. The ASV reads “who, existing in the form of God”; the NIV reads “Who, being in very nature God”; the TLB reads “who, though he was God.”

⚠ “Did not count being equal with God a thing to be grasped” — harpagmos. Harpagmos, from harpazō, “to snatch,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and the sentence turns on it. Three readings have long histories. (1) Robbery: equality with God was his by right, so to claim it was no theft; so the Vulgate’s non rapinam arbitratus est, and the KJV: “thought it not robbery to be equal with God.” (2) A thing to be seized: he did not reach for an equality he did not have; the NWT 1984 reads “gave no consideration to a seizure, namely, that he should be equal to God,” a rendering that fits the Witnesses’ teaching that the Son is not equal to God. (3) A thing to be held on to or used: he had it and did not cling to it or use it for himself; the NIV reads “did not consider equality with God something to be used to his own advantage.” English “grasp” covers both reaching and holding, as the Greek noun can, and this page sets out the readings without choosing. “Equal with God” is the charge of John 5:18, “making himself equal with God” (dictionary).

⚠ “But emptied himself” — kenōsis. Kenoō stands in five verses of the New Testament (dictionary); everywhere else a thing is emptied or made void, as faith “has been emptied” at Romans 4:14, and only here does someone empty himself. Of what, the verse does not say; the phrases after it say how: taking a slave’s form, coming in human likeness. From this verse “kenotic” theology takes its name, and how far the emptying goes, a laying aside of divine powers or of status and glory only, is argued. Some hear Isaiah 53:12 behind it, where the servant “poured out his life to death”; the Greek Old Testament there uses other words. The KJV reads “But made himself of no reputation”; the ASV reads “but emptied himself”; the NIV reads “he made himself nothing”; the TLB reads “laid aside his mighty power and glory,” one reading printed as the text.

“The form of a slave … the likeness of human beings … outward form.” Doulos is a slave (dictionary), the word Paul and Timothy use of themselves at Philippians 1:1. Homoiōma, “likeness,” is the word of Romans 8:3, “in the likeness of sinful flesh” (dictionary). Schēma is the shape a thing shows from outside, the “form of this world” at 1 Corinthians 7:31 (dictionary); this translation writes “form” for morphē and “outward form” for schēma so that the two stay apart in English. The KJV reads “and took upon him the form of a servant”; the NWT 1984 reads “took a slave’s form.”

⚠ “Obedient to the point of death — and death on a cross.” Hypēkoos, “obedient,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: Acts 7:39, 2 Corinthians 2:9 and this one. The last phrase adds the lowest step: not death only, but the cross, the death of slaves and rebels (dictionary). The KJV reads “even the death of the cross”; the NIV reads “even death on a cross!”; the NWT 1984 reads “death on a torture stake,” a doctrinal rendering that rests on the older sense of stauros as an upright stake.

Verses 9–11 · highly exalted, the name above every name, every knee and every tongue, and “Jesus Christ is Lord”

“Therefore God also exalted him above all.” Hyperypsoō, to raise above, stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. “Graciously gave” is charizomai, the verb of charis, grace. The critical text reads “the name,” the Byzantine “a name.” Which name is meant, “Lord” of v11, the Greek Old Testament’s word for the divine name, or “Jesus” of v10, or the name as rank, readers answer differently. The KJV reads “Wherefore God also hath highly exalted him, and given him a name which is above every name”; the ASV reads “and gave unto him the name”; the NWT 1984 reads “God exalted him to a superior position.”

⚠ “Every knee should bow … every tongue should confess” — Isaiah 45:23. In Isaiah 45 Jehovah speaks, and swears by himself: in the Hebrew, “to me every knee will bow, every tongue will swear” (45:23); the Greek Old Testament has “every tongue will swear by God.” Paul quotes the same oath at Romans 14:11, of God’s judgment: “every knee will bow to me, and every tongue will confess to God.” Here the knees bow “at the name of Jesus.” Readers divide: many take the hymn to give Jesus the honor that Isaiah reserves for Jehovah; others stress its last line, “to the glory of God the Father,” and read Jesus as exalted by God and subordinate to him. This page sets out both and does not choose. Exomologeō, “confess,” is to say openly (dictionary). The KJV reads “That at the name of Jesus every knee should bow”; the NWT 1984 reads “every tongue should openly acknowledge.”

“In heaven, on earth, under the earth.” Three adjectives, beings or things: epouranios, the “heavenly” of Ephesians; epigeios, earthly; and katachthonios, under the earth, which stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Whether they name angels, the living and the dead, or the whole of creation, the Greek forms do not settle. The KJV reads “of things in heaven, and things in earth, and things under the earth”; the NWT 1984 reads “of those in heaven and those on earth and those under the ground.”

“That Jesus Christ is Lord.” Here kyrios is said of Jesus, and this translation prints “Lord” (dictionary). It is the confession of Romans 10:9, “if you confess in your mouth Jesus as Lord,” and of 1 Corinthians 12:3, “Jesus is Lord.” The KJV reads “that Jesus Christ is Lord, to the glory of God the Father”; the Douay, from the Vulgate’s in gloria est Dei Patris, reads “that the Lord Jesus Christ is in the glory of God the Father.”

Verses 12–18 · work out your own salvation, God at work, grumblings, lights in a crooked generation, and a drink offering

⚠ “Work out your own salvation with fear and trembling; for it is God who is at work in you.” Katergazomai is to work something through to its end (dictionary). The verse has been set against “by grace you have been saved, through faith … not of works” (Ephesians 2:8), and readers hold the two together in different ways: the working out of a salvation already given; the welfare of the congregation, since “your” is plural; or God’s working as the ground of theirs, which the next verse states. This page sets out the readings and does not choose. The same verb, energeō, is used for God’s working and for theirs: God works both the willing and the working. “For the sake of good will” has no “his” in Greek; eudokia is the “good will” of Philippians 1:15 (dictionary), and whether it is God’s or the readers’ is read both ways. The KJV reads “both to will and to do of his good pleasure”; the NWT 1984 reads “for the sake of [his] good pleasure,” marking the word it supplies.

“Without grumblings … children of God without blemish in the midst of a crooked and twisted generation.” Gongysmos is the “grumblings” of Israel in the wilderness, “your grumblings are not against us but against Jehovah” (Exodus 16:8; dictionary). The next line takes up Moses’ song: in Deuteronomy 32:5 Israel is “no longer his children … a generation crooked and warped,” and in the Greek Old Testament “blemished children, a crooked and twisted generation.” Paul turns it round: children of God without blemish, in the midst of that generation. Diastrephō, “twist,” is the word of Luke 9:41, “O faithless and twisted generation” (dictionary). Skolios, “crooked,” stands in four verses: Luke 3:5, “the crooked shall become straight,” Acts 2:40, “this crooked generation,” this one, and 1 Peter 2:18. Akeraios, “innocent,” stands in three verses: Matthew 10:16, Romans 16:19 and this one. The KJV, from the Byzantine text, reads “the sons of God, without rebuke, in the midst of a crooked and perverse nation”; the ASV reads “children of God without blemish in the midst of a crooked and perverse generation.”

“Lights in the world … holding fast the word of life.” Phōstēr, “light,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Revelation 21:11, the radiance of the holy city. In the Greek Old Testament it is the word for the lights of creation, “Let there be lights in the vault of the sky” (Genesis 1:14). Epechontes can mean “holding fast” or “holding out,” and both are read. “In vain” is eis kenon, which stands in four verses; at Galatians 2:2 Paul feared he “might somehow be running, or had run, in vain,” and at Galatians 4:11 that he had “labored over you in vain.” The KJV reads “Holding forth the word of life”; the NWT 1984 reads “keeping a tight grip on the word of life”; the NIV reads “as you hold firmly to the word of life.”

⚠ “Poured out as a drink offering.” Spendō stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and 2 Timothy 4:6, “I am already being poured out.” The drink offering was poured over the sacrifice, as at Numbers 28:7 (dictionary). Whether Paul means his death, the last wine poured on their offering, or his labor while he lives, is read both ways. Leitourgia, “service,” is public and priestly service; Paul calls himself a leitourgos at Romans 15:16. Then joy twice over: “I rejoice, and I rejoice with all of you.” The KJV reads “Yea, and if I be offered upon the sacrifice and service of your faith”; the NIV reads “poured out like a drink offering”; the Douay reads “if I be made a victim upon the sacrifice.”

Verses 19–24 · Timothy, a man of like soul, and a son serving as a slave

“I hope in the Lord Jesus to send Timothy.” Timothy is named beside Paul at Philippians 1:1; he joined Paul at Acts 16:1 (encyclopedia), and at 1 Corinthians 4:17 Paul sends him as “my beloved and faithful child in the Lord.” Eupsycheō, “to be in good heart,” and isopsychos, “of like soul,” each stand in one verse of the New Testament. Like whose soul, Paul’s or Timothy’s, the Greek leaves open. The KJV reads “who will naturally care for your state”; the NWT 1984 reads “of a disposition like his.”

“Genuinely anxious for your affairs … they all seek their own things.” Merimnaō is the verb of Philippians 4:6, “Be anxious about nothing”; here it is the right kind of care. Gnēsiōs, “genuinely,” is the adverb of the “genuine yokefellow” of Philippians 4:3. Who “they all” are, the letter does not say. The critical text reads “Jesus Christ”; Westcott–Hort and the Byzantine text read “Christ Jesus,” and so the NWT 1984: “not those of Christ Jesus”; the KJV reads “not the things which are Jesus Christ’s.”

“Proven character … as a child with a father he served as a slave with me.” Dokimē is the “proven character” of Romans 5:4 (dictionary). Douleuō is the verb of doulos: Timothy too has taken the slave’s part of v7. The sentence turns as it goes: “as a child with a father” leads us to expect “he served me,” and Paul writes “he served with me.” The KJV reads “as a son with the father, he hath served with me in the gospel”; the ASV reads “as a child serveth a father, so he served with me”; the NWT 1984 reads “he slaved with me.”

Verses 25–30 · Epaphroditus, your messenger, anguished, close to death, and gambling with his life

“Epaphroditus, my brother and fellow worker and fellow soldier, and your messenger.” Epaphroditos stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Philippians 4:18, where he has brought their gift (encyclopedia). “Fellow soldier” is Archippus’s title at Philemon 1:2. “Messenger” is apostolos, the word for an apostle, here as at 2 Corinthians 8:23, “messengers of the congregations” (dictionary); “servant” is leitourgos, as at Romans 15:16 (dictionary). The KJV reads “but your messenger, and he that ministered to my wants”; the Douay reads “but your apostle”; the NWT 1984 reads “YOUR envoy and private servant for my need.”

“Anguished … sick, close to death … sorrow upon sorrow.” Adēmoneō, “to be anguished,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: two in Gethsemane, Matthew 26:37 and Mark 14:33, and this one. Epaphroditus is anguished not at his illness but at their hearing of it. “I am sending” is a past tense in Greek, written from the time the letter is read; he probably carried it. The KJV reads “and was full of heaviness”; the NWT 1984 reads “is depressed”; the NIV reads “is distressed because you heard he was ill.”

⚠ “Gambling with his life.” Paraboleuomai stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; it is reported to be a gamester’s word, to stake everything on a throw, and a later Christian brotherhood that nursed the sick in Alexandria, the parabolani, is reported to have taken its name from it. The Byzantine text reads paraboulesamenos, “having disregarded,” and so the KJV: “not regarding his life”; the ASV reads “hazarding his life.” The critical text reads “the work of Christ,” Westcott–Hort “the work of the Lord,” and so the NWT 1984: “on account of the Lord’s work.” “What was lacking in your service” is not a reproach: they were far away, and he came in their place.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “partnership,” as at 1:5; “inward affection,” as at 1:8; “selfish ambition,” as at 1:17; “empty glory,” as at Galatians 5:26; “surpassing,” as at 4:7; “form” for morphē and “outward form” for schēma; “a thing to be grasped”; “emptied,” as at Romans 4:14; “slave,” as at 1:1; “likeness,” as at Romans 8:3; “Lord” for Jesus at 2:11; “confess,” as at Romans 14:11; “good will,” as at 1:15; “grumblings,” as at Exodus 16:8; “crooked and twisted,” as the Greek of Deuteronomy 32:5 has it, and “twisted” as at Luke 9:41; “lights,” as at Genesis 1:14; “drink offering,” as at Numbers 28:7; “anxious,” as at 4:6; “proven character,” as at Romans 5:4; “messenger,” as at 2 Corinthians 8:23; and “anguished,” as at Mark 14:33.

Echoes paid. Selfish ambition, planted at Philippians 1:17 and turned on the congregation; slaves from Philippians 1:1, now the form Christ takes; partnership and inward affection from Philippians 1:5 and Philippians 1:8; joy, again and again; Isaiah’s oath as Paul quoted it at Romans 14:11; the wilderness grumblings of Exodus 16:8; the crooked generation of Deuteronomy 32:5; the lights of Genesis 1:14; running in vain from Galatians 2:2; the drink offering of Numbers 28:7; Timothy from Acts 16:1 and 1 Corinthians 4:17; and the anguish of Gethsemane, Mark 14:33.

Echoes planted. “Rejoice” at 3:1 and again at Philippians 4:4; “the same mind” for Euodia and Syntyche at Philippians 4:2; Epaphroditus and the gift at Philippians 4:18; counting, the verb of 2:3 and 2:6, when Paul counts his gains as loss at 3:7–8; and form and outward form together again at 3:21, where the body of humiliation is changed into the form of his glory.

Next in this book: Philippians 3, already on these pages. “Rejoice in the Lord,” a warning about “the dogs,” and the gains Paul counts as loss.
