The end of the same letter, written from Corinth. Chapter 15 ended with Paul on his way to Jerusalem with the collection and then to Spain. Here the letter closes with greetings from the city where it was written: Gaius hosts Paul and the whole congregation, Erastus is the city’s treasurer, and Tertius, the scribe, adds his own name. Acts 20:3 places Paul in Greece for three months before he leaves for Jerusalem, and the people named here are the ones who travel with him.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 15. The chapter names no date and no event. Its clock is the journey that chapter 15 set out: Jerusalem, then Rome, then Spain.

Verses 1–2 · Συνίστημι … Φοίβην … διάκονον … προστάτις Synistēmi … Phoibēn … diakonon … prostatis — Phoebe, a servant of the congregation at Cenchreae, and the patron of many — Romans 16:1–2

“I commend to you Phoebe, our sister” (Synistēmi de hymin Phoibēn tēn adelphēn hēmōn, Romans 16:1). The verb synistēmi is the verb of a letter of recommendation, and the letter is most often read as one that Phoebe herself carried to Rome, which fits the way Paul introduces her before he names what she has done. Her home is the congregation at Cenchreae, the eastern harbor of Corinth, the port from which Paul sailed for Syria after his haircut at Acts 18:18. The shelf: NWT 1984 “a minister of the congregation that is in Cenʹchre·ae,” ASV “a servant of the church that is at Cenchreae,” and NIV “a deacon of the church in Cenchreae.”

“A servant of the congregation” (kai diakonon tēs ekklēsias, Romans 16:1). The noun diakonos (dictionary) is the word Paul uses of his own work when he says that Christ became a servant of the circumcision (Romans 15:8). The Greek word does not by itself name an office. The English “deacon” in the NIV puts a later title into the text, and the other versions keep the plainer word. Ekklēsia is rendered congregation throughout this translation, as the fixed rendering for the New Testament requires.

“A patron of many” (kai gar autē prostatis pollōn egenēthē, Romans 16:2). The noun prostatis is the feminine of prostatēs, a patron or protector: someone who stands in front of others and speaks for them in a city’s affairs. It appears in the New Testament in this verse and nowhere else. The shelf: KJV “a succourer of many,” NWT 1984 “a defender of many, yes, of me myself,” and NIV “the benefactor of many people, including me.” The NIV turns the office into a kindness; the NWT keeps the protecting role. The verse also says that the readers should stand by her (parastēte) in whatever matter she needs them for, which is the language of a patron asking for help on behalf of a client.

Verses 3–5 · Πρίσκαν καὶ Ἀκύλαν … τὸν ἑαυτῶν τράχηλον ὑπέθηκαν … τὴν κατʼ οἶκον αὐτῶν ἐκκλησίαν Priskan kai Akylan … ton heautōn trachēlon hypethēkan … tēn kat’ oikon autōn ekklēsian — Prisca and Aquila, who risked their necks, and the congregation in their house — Romans 16:3–5

“Prisca and Aquila, my fellow workers” (Aspasasthe Priskan kai Akylan tous synergous mou en Christō Iēsou, Romans 16:3). The couple appear in Acts first at Acts 18:2, where Aquila is a Jew from Pontus who has come from Italy, and then at Acts 18:26, where the two of them take Apollos aside and explain the way of God to him more accurately. Paul calls them his fellow workers, synergoi (dictionary). Paul writes the name as Priska, and Luke writes Priskilla in Acts; the two forms name the same woman. Her encyclopedia entry is under Priscilla.

“Who risked their own necks for my life” (hoitines hyper tēs psychēs mou ton heautōn trachēlon hypethēkan, Romans 16:4). The verb hypotithēmi means to set under, to put one’s neck under the blade. It appears in the New Testament in this verse only. The shelf: NWT 1984 “who have risked their own necks for my soul,” KJV “Who have for my life laid down their own necks,” and ASV “who for my life laid down their own necks.” The occasion is not stated. Paul does not say where or when they put themselves at risk, and the note does not supply it.

“The congregation in their house” (tēn kat’ oikon autōn ekklēsian, Romans 16:5). The Greek places a congregation in the home of the couple. The English term “house-church” is a modern term from the study of archaeology, not a Greek word, and the translation keeps to the Greek, a congregation in their house. The shelf: NWT 1984 “the congregation that is in their house,” KJV “the church that is in their house.”

“Epaenetus, the firstfruits of Asia” (Epainetou tou agapētou mou, hos estin aparchē tēs Asias eis Christon, Romans 16:5). Aparchē, firstfruits, is the word for the first portion of a harvest, the same word Paul uses of the first portion of the dough in Romans 11:16. In Paul’s words Epaenetus is the first convert in Asia, the Roman province on the western coast of Asia Minor. The shelf: NWT 1984 “a firstfruits of Asia for Christ,” and KJV “the firstfruits of Achaia unto Christ.” The KJV names a different province, Achaia, and the English versions do not agree on which one is meant.

Verses 6–7 · Μαριάμ … Ἀνδρόνικον καὶ Ἰουνίαν … ἐπίσημοι ἐν τοῖς ἀποστόλοις Mariam … Andronikon kai Iounian … episēmoi en tois apostolois — Mary, who worked hard, and Andronicus and Junia, notable among the apostles — Romans 16:6–7

“Mary, who labored much for you” (Aspasasthe Mariam hētis polla ekopiasen eis hymas, Romans 16:6). The verb kopiaō means to labor to exhaustion; the same verb is used of the women in verse 12. The critical apparatus records a variant at the word hymas, you, and some witnesses read hēmas, us. The shelf: NWT 1984 “who has performed many labors for YOU,” KJV “who bestowed much labour on us.” The KJV follows the reading that says us. The name is the same as that of the mother of Jesus (see Mary the mother of Jesus), and nothing in the verse connects this Mary to any other.

“Andronicus and Junia, my relatives and fellow prisoners” (Andronikon kai Iounian tous syngeneis mou kai synaichmalōtous mou, Romans 16:7). Paul calls them syngeneis, relatives, a word he also uses of his own people in Romans 9:3. The word synaichmalōtos, fellow prisoner, shows that they were imprisoned with him at some point, and the verse does not say where. The name Iounian is the accusative of either a man’s name, Junias, or a woman’s name, Junia. The Greek form does not decide between them. The shelf: NWT 1984 “An·dronʹi·cus and Juʹni·as my relatives and my fellow captives,” KJV “Andronicus and Junia, my kinsmen and my fellowprisoners,” and NIV “Andronicus and Junia, my fellow Jews who have been in prison with me.” The translations divide on the name, and this note reports the split rather than voting on it.

“Outstanding among the apostles” (hoitines eisin episēmoi en tois apostolois, Romans 16:7). The adjective episēmos means marked, notable, with a clear sign of distinction. It stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and Matthew 27:16, where Pilate offers the crowd a notorious prisoner, Barabbas. The Greek phrase en tois apostolois can mean that they were notable in the eyes of the apostles or that they were counted among them; the phrase does not say which. The clause that follows says they were in Christ before Paul. The shelf: ASV “who are of note among the apostles, who also have been in Christ before me,” and TLB “They are respected by the apostles and became Christians before I did.”

Verses 8–16 · Ἀμπλιᾶτον … Οὐρβανὸν … Ἀπελλῆν … Ἡρῳδίωνα … Τρύφαιναν … Ῥοῦφον … Ἑρμῆν Ampliaton … Ourbanon … Apellēn … Hērōdiōna … Tryphainan … Rhouphon … Hermēn — The long list of names, and the holy kiss — Romans 16:8–16

“Greet” (aspasasthe, Romans 16:8–16). The verb aspazomai is repeated through this section at nearly every name, and the list is built on it. Two households are named without a head: those of Aristobulus and of Narcissus. The Greek phrase tōn ek, those from, does not say whether the head of the house is himself a believer. The shelf for verse 10: KJV “Salute them which are of Aristobulus’ household,” and NWT 1984 “Greet those from the household of A·ris·tobʹu·lus.”

“Rufus, chosen in the Lord, and his mother and mine” (Rhouphon ton eklekton en kyriō kai tēn mētera autou kai emou, Romans 16:13). The phrase tēn mētera autou kai emou is one of the most personal lines in the letter: Paul says that Rufus’s mother has also been a mother to him. Mark 15:21 names a Rufus, son of Simon of Cyrene, and the two may or may not be the same man; this chapter does not say so, and the note does not claim it. The shelf: NWT 1984 “Ruʹfus the chosen one in [the] Lord, and his mother and mine,” KJV “Salute Rufus chosen in the Lord, and his mother and mine.”

“Hermes” (Hermēn, Romans 16:14). The name is the god’s name, which the crowd at Lystra gave to Paul in Acts 14:12. In this verse it is a man in a list of believers. Nothing here ties him to the god, and the encyclopedia keeps the two as separate entries. The shelf: NWT 1984 “Herʹmes,” and KJV “Hermas, Patrobas, Hermes.”

“With a holy kiss” (en philēmati hagiō, Romans 16:16). The kiss is a sign of greeting among the believers, and Paul asks for it in other letters as well. The noun philēma (dictionary) is the word for a kiss. The verse ends with the greeting of all the congregations of Christ, a wider circle than the one named in the list.

Verses 17–20 · σκοπεῖν τοὺς τὰς διχοστασίας καὶ τὰ σκάνδαλα … χρηστολογίας … ἀκεραίους … ὁ θεὸς τῆς εἰρήνης συντρίψει τὸν Σατανᾶν skopein tous tas dichostasias kai ta skandala … chrēstologias … akeraious … ho theos tēs eirēnēs syntripsei ton Satanan — Divisions and stumbling blocks, smooth talk, and the God who crushes Satan — Romans 16:17–20

“Watch out for those who cause divisions” (Parakalō de hymas, adelphoi, skopein tous tas dichostasias kai ta skandala, Romans 16:17). The noun dichostasia, division, stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and Galatians 5:20 (not yet on these pages), where it is listed among the works of the flesh. The word skandalon, a stumbling block, is the one used in Romans 14:13. The shelf: NWT 1984 “those who cause divisions and occasions for stumbling,” NIV “those who cause divisions and put obstacles in your way,” and TLB “those who cause divisions and are upsetting people’s faith.”

“Smooth and flattering speech” (dia tēs chrēstologias kai eulogias, Romans 16:18). The noun chrēstologia, smooth or kindly speech, appears only here in the New Testament. The word koilia is the belly; the NIV’s “appetites” reads it as the desire that the belly stands for. The shelf: KJV “their own belly; and by good words and fair speeches deceive the hearts of the simple,” NWT 1984 “their own bellies; and by smooth talk and complimentary speech they seduce the hearts of guileless ones,” and NIV “their own appetites. By smooth talk and flattery.”

“Wise as to what is good, innocent as to what is evil” (sophous einai eis to agathon, akeraious de eis to kakon, Romans 16:19). The adjective akeraios, unmixed, innocent, stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Matthew 10:16, where Jesus sends his disciples out as sheep among wolves, and Philippians 2:15 (not yet on these pages). The shelf: KJV “simple concerning evil,” NWT 1984 “innocent as to what is evil,” and ASV “simple unto that which is evil.”

“The God of peace will crush Satan under your feet” (ho de theos tēs eirēnēs syntripsei ton Satanan hypo tous podas hymōn en tachei, Romans 16:20). The phrase God of peace ends the letter as it ended the argument in Romans 15:33. The image of the crushing foot is the one of Genesis 3:15, where the offspring of the woman is to crush the head of the serpent. The shelf: NIV “The God of peace will soon crush Satan under your feet,” KJV “shall bruise Satan under your feet shortly,” and Geneva “shall tread Satan under your feet shortly.” The English verbs differ; the Greek verb is syntribō, to crush.

Verses 21–24 · Τιμόθεος … Λούκιος καὶ Ἰάσων καὶ Σωσίπατρος … Τέρτιος … Γάϊος … Ἔραστος … Κούαρτος Timotheos … Loukios kai Iasōn kai Sōsipatros … Tertios … Gaios … Erastos … Kouartos — The names at the end of the letter, and the scribe who wrote it — Romans 16:21–24

“Timothy, my fellow worker, greets you” (Aspazetai hymas Timotheos ho synergos mou, Romans 16:21). Timothy is first introduced in Acts 16:1, where he is the son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, and Paul has him circumcised (Acts 16:3). Lucius may be the man named at Acts 13:1 as Lucius of Cyrene; the verse gives no city, so the identification is not stated here. Jason is the host at Thessalonica in Acts 17:5. The Sosipater of this verse may be the Sopater of Berea named in Acts 20:4; the note reports the possibility and does not claim it.

“I, Tertius, who wrote this letter” (Aspazomai hymas egō Tertios ho grapsas tēn epistolēn, Romans 16:22). Here the scribe speaks in his own voice. It means that Paul dictated the letter and Tertius wrote it down, and the greeting is Tertius’s own. The shelf: NWT 1984 “I, Terʹtius, who have done the writing of this letter, greet YOU in [the] Lord,” and KJV “I Tertius, who wrote this epistle, salute you in the Lord.”

“Gaius, my host and the host of the whole congregation” (Aspazetai hymas Gaios ho xenos mou kai holēs tēs ekklēsias, Romans 16:23). The word xenos means host or guest, and the phrase says that Gaius hosted Paul and the whole congregation in his home. The name is common. Acts 19:29 names another Gaius, a Macedonian in Ephesus, and this verse does not connect the two. The shelf: NWT 1984 “Gaʹius, my host and that of all the congregation,” and KJV “Gaius mine host, and of the whole church, saluteth you.”

“Erastus, the city’s treasurer” (Erastos ho oikonomos tēs poleōs, Romans 16:23). The Greek is oikonomos, a steward or manager of a household or a public fund, and here it is the title of a city official. Acts 19:22 sends a companion named Erastus into Macedonia, and the text does not say whether he is this man. An inscription from Corinth is reported to name an Erastus who paid for a paving project; this page has not read that inscription and does not rely on it. The shelf: TLB “Erastus, the city treasurer,” NIV “Erastus, who is the city’s director of public works,” and KJV “Erastus the chamberlain of the city.”

The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ (Romans 16:24, bracketed in the critical text). The benediction in verse 24 is printed in brackets in the critical text, which means its editors doubted that it belongs in this place. The KJV and ASV keep it, and the NWT 1984 leaves verse 24 empty, numbering the doxology 25–27.

Verses 25–27 — The doxology, which this text does not print — Romans 16:25–27

No Greek for 25–27 on these pages. The critical text this translation is set from ends the letter at verse 24. The doxology, “Now to him who is able to establish you,” appears in the English shelf at verses 25–27 (KJV, ASV, NWT 1984). Some traditions place it after Romans 14:23 or after Romans 15:33, and some omit it altogether; those placements are reported here and are not checked against the manuscripts. The verses are not translated on these pages, and their absence is shown, not sanded over.

Romans ends here. The next book in the New Testament is 1 Corinthians, and its first chapter is already on these pages: 1 Corinthians 1.
