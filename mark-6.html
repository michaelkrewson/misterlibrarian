🕰
Jesus is rejected in his own hometown, then sends the twelve out two by two. Herod Antipas, hearing the reports, is haunted by the memory of beheading John the Baptist at his stepdaughter's request during a birthday banquet — told here as a flashback. The apostles return and Jesus feeds five thousand men from five loaves and two fish, then walks to their boat across the water that night: 'Take courage. It is I. Do not be afraid.'
c. AD 29, the same span as Matthew 14 — John's death likely close to Passover of that year (John 6:4 places the feeding of the five thousand near a Passover) — roughly the midpoint of Jesus' public ministry.

Mark 6 — The Mister Translation

1
Καὶ ἐξῆλθεν ἐκεῖθεν, καὶ ἔρχεται εἰς τὴν πατρίδα αὐτοῦ, καὶ ἀκολουθοῦσιν αὐτῷ οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ.
And he went out from there, and he comes into his hometown, and his disciples follow him.note
Y salió de allí, y viene a su tierra natal, y sus discípulos lo siguen.
2
καὶ γενομένου σαββάτου ἤρξατο διδάσκειν ἐν τῇ συναγωγῇ· καὶ οἱ πολλοὶ ἀκούοντες ἐξεπλήσσοντο λέγοντες· Πόθεν τούτῳ ταῦτα, καὶ τίς ἡ σοφία ἡ δοθεῖσα τούτῳ, καὶ αἱ δυνάμεις τοιαῦται διὰ τῶν χειρῶν αὐτοῦ γινόμεναι;
And when the Sabbath came, he began to teach in the synagogue, and the many who heard him were astounded, saying, “Where did this man get these things? And what is this wisdom that has been given to him? And such miracles as these, happening through his hands?note
Y cuando llegó el sábado, comenzó a enseñar en la sinagoga, y muchos de los que lo oían quedaban atónitos, diciendo: “¿De dónde le vienen a este estas cosas? ¿Y qué es esta sabiduría que le ha sido dada? ¿Y semejantes milagros, hechos por sus manos?
3
οὐχ οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ τέκτων, ὁ υἱὸς τῆς Μαρίας καὶ ἀδελφὸς Ἰακώβου καὶ Ἰωσῆτος καὶ Ἰούδα καὶ Σίμωνος; καὶ οὐκ εἰσὶν αἱ ἀδελφαὶ αὐτοῦ ὧδε πρὸς ἡμᾶς; καὶ ἐσκανδαλίζοντο ἐν αὐτῷ.
Is not this the carpenter, the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon? And are not his sisters here with us?” And they took offense at him.note
¿No es este el carpintero, el hijo de María y hermano de Santiago y de José y de Judas y de Simón? ¿Y no están sus hermanas aquí con nosotros?”. Y se escandalizaban de él.
4
καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς ὁ Ἰησοῦς ὅτι Οὐκ ἔστιν προφήτης ἄτιμος εἰ μὴ ἐν τῇ πατρίδι αὐτοῦ καὶ ἐν τοῖς συγγενεῦσιν αὐτοῦ καὶ ἐν τῇ οἰκίᾳ αὐτοῦ.
And Jesus said to them, “A prophet is not without honor, except in his hometown, and among his relatives, and in his own house.”note
Y Jesús les decía: “Un profeta no carece de honra, sino en su tierra natal, y entre sus parientes, y en su propia casa”.
5
καὶ οὐκ ἐδύνατο ἐκεῖ ποιῆσαι οὐδεμίαν δύναμιν, εἰ μὴ ὀλίγοις ἀρρώστοις ἐπιθεὶς τὰς χεῖρας ἐθεράπευσεν·
And he was not able to do any miracle there, except that he laid his hands on a few sick people and healed them.note
Y no pudo hacer allí ningún milagro, salvo que puso las manos sobre unos pocos enfermos y los sanó.
6
καὶ ἐθαύμαζεν διὰ τὴν ἀπιστίαν αὐτῶν. Καὶ περιῆγεν τὰς κώμας κύκλῳ διδάσκων.
And he was amazed at their unbelief. And he went around the surrounding villages, teaching.note
Y se maravillaba de la incredulidad de ellos. Y recorría las aldeas de alrededor, enseñando.
7
καὶ προσκαλεῖται τοὺς δώδεκα, καὶ ἤρξατο αὐτοὺς ἀποστέλλειν δύο δύο, καὶ ἐδίδου αὐτοῖς ἐξουσίαν τῶν πνευμάτων τῶν ἀκαθάρτων,
And he calls the twelve to him, and began to send them out two by two, and gave them authority over the unclean spirits,note
Y llama a los doce, y comenzó a enviarlos de dos en dos, y les dio autoridad sobre los espíritus inmundos,
8
καὶ παρήγγειλεν αὐτοῖς ἵνα μηδὲν αἴρωσιν εἰς ὁδὸν εἰ μὴ ῥάβδον μόνον, μὴ ἄρτον, μὴ πήραν, μὴ εἰς τὴν ζώνην χαλκόν,
and instructed them to take nothing for the journey except a staff only — no bread, no bag, no copper coins in their belts —note
y les instruyó que no llevaran nada para el camino, salvo solamente un bastón: ni pan, ni alforja, ni monedas de cobre en el cinturón,
9
ἀλλὰ ὑποδεδεμένους σανδάλια, καὶ μὴ ἐνδύσησθε δύο χιτῶνας.
but to wear sandals, and not to put on two tunics.note
sino que calzaran sandalias, y no se pusieran dos túnicas.
10
καὶ ἔλεγεν αὐτοῖς· Ὅπου ἐὰν εἰσέλθητε εἰς οἰκίαν, ἐκεῖ μένετε ἕως ἂν ἐξέλθητε ἐκεῖθεν.
And he said to them, “Wherever you enter a house, stay there until you leave that place.note
Y les decía: “Dondequiera que entren en una casa, quédense allí hasta que salgan de ese lugar.
11
καὶ ὃς ἂν τόπος μὴ δέξηται ὑμᾶς μηδὲ ἀκούσωσιν ὑμῶν, ἐκπορευόμενοι ἐκεῖθεν ἐκτινάξατε τὸν χοῦν τὸν ὑποκάτω τῶν ποδῶν ὑμῶν εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς.
And whatever place will not receive you, and they will not listen to you, as you go out from there, shake off the dust that is under your feet, as a testimony against them.”note
Y cualquier lugar que no los reciba, ni los escuche, al salir de allí, sacudan el polvo que está bajo sus pies, como testimonio contra ellos”.
12
Καὶ ἐξελθόντες ἐκήρυξαν ἵνα μετανοῶσιν.
And going out, they preached that people should repent.note
Y saliendo, predicaban que la gente se arrepintiera.
13
καὶ δαιμόνια πολλὰ ἐξέβαλλον, καὶ ἤλειφον ἐλαίῳ πολλοὺς ἀρρώστους καὶ ἐθεράπευον.
And they cast out many demons, and anointed with oil many who were sick, and healed them.note
Y expulsaban muchos demonios, y ungían con aceite a muchos enfermos, y los sanaban.
14
Καὶ ἤκουσεν ὁ βασιλεὺς Ἡρῴδης, φανερὸν γὰρ ἐγένετο τὸ ὄνομα αὐτοῦ, καὶ ἔλεγον ὅτι Ἰωάννης ὁ βαπτίζων ἐγήγερται ἐκ νεκρῶν, καὶ διὰ τοῦτο ἐνεργοῦσιν αἱ δυνάμεις ἐν αὐτῷ·
And King Herod heard of it, for his name had become well known, and people were saying, “John the Baptizer has been raised from the dead, and because of this these miraculous powers are at work in him.”note
Y el rey Herodes oyó de esto, porque su nombre se había hecho notorio, y la gente decía: “Juan el Bautista ha sido levantado de entre los muertos, y por eso operan en él estos poderes milagrosos”.
15
ἄλλοι δὲ ἔλεγον ὅτι Ἠλίας ἐστίν· ἄλλοι δὲ ἔλεγον ὅτι προφήτης ὡς εἷς τῶν προφητῶν.
But others were saying, “He is Elijah.” And others were saying, “He is a prophet, like one of the prophets of old.”note
Pero otros decían: “Es Elías”. Y otros decían: “Es un profeta, como uno de los profetas de antaño”.
16
ἀκούσας δὲ ὁ Ἡρῴδης ἔλεγεν· Ὃν ἐγὼ ἀπεκεφάλισα Ἰωάννην, οὗτος ἠγέρθη.
But when Herod heard this, he said, “John, whom I beheaded — he has been raised.”note
Pero al oír esto, Herodes decía: “Juan, a quien yo decapité, este ha sido levantado”.
17
Αὐτὸς γὰρ ὁ Ἡρῴδης ἀποστείλας ἐκράτησεν τὸν Ἰωάννην καὶ ἔδησεν αὐτὸν ἐν φυλακῇ διὰ Ἡρῳδιάδα τὴν γυναῖκα Φιλίππου τοῦ ἀδελφοῦ αὐτοῦ, ὅτι αὐτὴν ἐγάμησεν·
For Herod himself had sent and seized John, and bound him in prison, because of Herodias, the wife of his brother Philip, because he had married her.note
Porque el mismo Herodes había mandado prender a Juan, y lo había atado en la cárcel, a causa de Herodías, la esposa de su hermano Filipo, porque se había casado con ella.
18
ἔλεγεν γὰρ ὁ Ἰωάννης τῷ Ἡρῴδῃ ὅτι Οὐκ ἔξεστίν σοι ἔχειν τὴν γυναῖκα τοῦ ἀδελφοῦ σου.
For John had been telling Herod, “It is not lawful for you to have your brother’s wife.”note
Pues Juan le había estado diciendo a Herodes: “No te es lícito tener a la esposa de tu hermano”.
19
ἡ δὲ Ἡρῳδιὰς ἐνεῖχεν αὐτῷ καὶ ἤθελεν αὐτὸν ἀποκτεῖναι, καὶ οὐκ ἠδύνατο·
So Herodias held a grudge against him and wanted to kill him, but she could not,note
Así que Herodías le guardaba rencor y quería matarlo, pero no podía,
20
ὁ γὰρ Ἡρῴδης ἐφοβεῖτο τὸν Ἰωάννην, εἰδὼς αὐτὸν ἄνδρα δίκαιον καὶ ἅγιον, καὶ συνετήρει αὐτόν, καὶ ἀκούσας αὐτοῦ πολλὰ ἠπόρει καὶ ἡδέως αὐτοῦ ἤκουεν.
for Herod feared John, knowing him to be a righteous and holy man, and he kept him safe. And when he heard him, he was greatly perplexed, and yet he heard him gladly.note
porque Herodes temía a Juan, sabiendo que era un hombre justo y santo, y lo protegía. Y al oírlo, quedaba muy perplejo, y sin embargo lo oía de buena gana.
21
Καὶ γενομένης ἡμέρας εὐκαίρου ὅτε Ἡρῴδης τοῖς γενεσίοις αὐτοῦ δεῖπνον ἐποίησεν τοῖς μεγιστᾶσιν αὐτοῦ καὶ τοῖς χιλιάρχοις καὶ τοῖς πρώτοις τῆς Γαλιλαίας,
Then an opportune day came, when Herod, on his birthday, gave a banquet for his nobles and military commanders and the leading men of Galilee.note
Entonces llegó un día oportuno, cuando Herodes, en su cumpleaños, ofreció un banquete a sus nobles, a los comandantes militares y a los principales de Galilea.
22
καὶ εἰσελθούσης τῆς θυγατρὸς αὐτῆς τῆς Ἡρῳδιάδος καὶ ὀρχησαμένης καὶ ἀρεσάσης τῷ Ἡρῴδῃ καὶ τοῖς συνανακειμένοις, εἶπεν ὁ βασιλεὺς τῷ κορασίῳ· Αἴτησόν με ὃ ἐὰν θέλῃς, καὶ δώσω σοι·
And when the daughter of Herodias herself came in and danced, she pleased Herod and those reclining with him. And the king said to the girl, “Ask me for whatever you want, and I will give it to you.”note
Y cuando entró la hija de la misma Herodías y bailó, agradó a Herodes y a los que estaban reclinados con él. Y el rey dijo a la muchacha: “Pídeme lo que quieras, y te lo daré”.
23
καὶ ὤμοσεν αὐτῇ· Ὅ τι ἐάν με αἰτήσῃς δώσω σοι ἕως ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου.
And he swore to her, “Whatever you ask me, I will give it to you, up to half of my kingdom.”note
Y le juró: “Todo lo que me pidas te lo daré, hasta la mitad de mi reino”.
24
καὶ ἐξελθοῦσα εἶπεν τῇ μητρὶ αὐτῆς· Τί αἰτήσωμαι; ἡ δὲ εἶπεν· Τὴν κεφαλὴν Ἰωάννου τοῦ βαπτίζοντος.
And she went out and said to her mother, “What should I ask for?” And she said, “The head of John the Baptizer.”note
Y saliendo, dijo a su madre: “¿Qué debo pedir?”. Y ella dijo: “La cabeza de Juan el Bautista”.
25
καὶ εἰσελθοῦσα εὐθὺς μετὰ σπουδῆς πρὸς τὸν βασιλέα ᾐτήσατο λέγουσα· Θέλω ἵνα ἐξαυτῆς δῷς μοι ἐπὶ πίνακι τὴν κεφαλὴν Ἰωάννου τοῦ βαπτιστοῦ.
And immediately she went in with haste to the king and asked, saying, “I want you to give me at once the head of John the Baptist on a platter.”note
Y entrando enseguida, con premura, ante el rey, pidió, diciendo: “Quiero que me des ahora mismo, en un plato, la cabeza de Juan el Bautista”.
26
καὶ περίλυπος γενόμενος ὁ βασιλεὺς διὰ τοὺς ὅρκους καὶ τοὺς ἀνακειμένους οὐκ ἠθέλησεν ἀθετῆσαι αὐτήν·
And the king became deeply grieved, but because of his oaths and his dinner guests, he did not want to refuse her.note
Y el rey se entristeció profundamente, pero a causa de sus juramentos y de los invitados, no quiso negárselo.
27
καὶ εὐθὺς ἀποστείλας ὁ βασιλεὺς σπεκουλάτορα ἐπέταξεν ἐνέγκαι τὴν κεφαλὴν αὐτοῦ. καὶ ἀπελθὼν ἀπεκεφάλισεν αὐτὸν ἐν τῇ φυλακῇ
And immediately the king sent an executioner and commanded him to bring his head. And he went and beheaded him in the prison,note
Y enseguida el rey envió a un verdugo y ordenó que le trajeran su cabeza. Y este fue y lo decapitó en la cárcel,
28
καὶ ἤνεγκεν τὴν κεφαλὴν αὐτοῦ ἐπὶ πίνακι καὶ ἔδωκεν αὐτὴν τῷ κορασίῳ, καὶ τὸ κοράσιον ἔδωκεν αὐτὴν τῇ μητρὶ αὐτῆς.
and brought his head on a platter and gave it to the girl, and the girl gave it to her mother.note
y trajo su cabeza en un plato, y se la dio a la muchacha, y la muchacha se la dio a su madre.
29
καὶ ἀκούσαντες οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ ἦλθον καὶ ἦραν τὸ πτῶμα αὐτοῦ καὶ ἔθηκαν αὐτὸ ἐν μνημείῳ.
And when his disciples heard about it, they came and took his corpse and laid it in a tomb.note
Y al oírlo, sus discípulos vinieron y tomaron su cadáver, y lo pusieron en un sepulcro.
30
Καὶ συνάγονται οἱ ἀπόστολοι πρὸς τὸν Ἰησοῦν, καὶ ἀπήγγειλαν αὐτῷ πάντα ὅσα ἐποίησαν καὶ ὅσα ἐδίδαξαν.
And the apostles gather to Jesus, and reported to him all that they had done and all that they had taught.note
Y los apóstoles se reúnen con Jesús, y le contaron todo lo que habían hecho y todo lo que habían enseñado.
31
καὶ λέγει αὐτοῖς· Δεῦτε ὑμεῖς αὐτοὶ κατʼ ἰδίαν εἰς ἔρημον τόπον καὶ ἀναπαύσασθε ὀλίγον. ἦσαν γὰρ οἱ ἐρχόμενοι καὶ οἱ ὑπάγοντες πολλοί, καὶ οὐδὲ φαγεῖν εὐκαίρουν.
And he says to them, “Come away by yourselves to a desolate place, and rest a while.” For many were coming and going, and they did not even have time to eat.note
Y les dice: “Vengan ustedes solos, aparte, a un lugar solitario, y descansen un poco”. Porque eran muchos los que iban y venían, y ni siquiera tenían tiempo de comer.
32
καὶ ἀπῆλθον ἐν τῷ πλοίῳ εἰς ἔρημον τόπον κατʼ ἰδίαν.
And they went away in the boat to a desolate place by themselves.note
Y se fueron en la barca a un lugar solitario, aparte.
33
καὶ εἶδον αὐτοὺς ὑπάγοντας καὶ ἐπέγνωσαν πολλοί, καὶ πεζῇ ἀπὸ πασῶν τῶν πόλεων συνέδραμον ἐκεῖ καὶ προῆλθον αὐτούς.
But many saw them going and recognized them, and ran there on foot from all the towns, and arrived ahead of them.note
Pero muchos los vieron ir y los reconocieron, y corrieron allá a pie desde todas las ciudades, y llegaron antes que ellos.
34
καὶ ἐξελθὼν εἶδεν πολὺν ὄχλον, καὶ ἐσπλαγχνίσθη ἐπʼ αὐτοὺς ὅτι ἦσαν ὡς πρόβατα μὴ ἔχοντα ποιμένα, καὶ ἤρξατο διδάσκειν αὐτοὺς πολλά.
And when he went ashore, he saw a great crowd, and he had compassion on them, because they were like sheep without a shepherd. And he began to teach them many things.note
Y cuando desembarcó, vio una gran multitud, y sintió compasión de ellos, porque eran como ovejas que no tienen pastor. Y comenzó a enseñarles muchas cosas.
35
Καὶ ἤδη ὥρας πολλῆς γενομένης προσελθόντες αὐτῷ οἱ μαθηταὶ αὐτοῦ ἔλεγον ὅτι Ἔρημός ἐστιν ὁ τόπος, καὶ ἤδη ὥρα πολλή·
And when it was already late, his disciples came to him and said, “This is a desolate place, and it is already late.note
Y siendo ya muy tarde, sus discípulos se acercaron a él y le dijeron: “Este es un lugar solitario, y ya es muy tarde.
36
ἀπόλυσον αὐτούς, ἵνα ἀπελθόντες εἰς τοὺς κύκλῳ ἀγροὺς καὶ κώμας ἀγοράσωσιν ἑαυτοῖς τί φάγωσιν.
Send them away, so that they may go into the surrounding countryside and villages and buy themselves something to eat.”note
Despídelos, para que vayan a los campos y aldeas de alrededor y se compren algo de comer”.
37
ὁ δὲ ἀποκριθεὶς εἶπεν αὐτοῖς· Δότε αὐτοῖς ὑμεῖς φαγεῖν. καὶ λέγουσιν αὐτῷ· Ἀπελθόντες ἀγοράσωμεν δηναρίων διακοσίων ἄρτους καὶ δώσομεν αὐτοῖς φαγεῖν;
But he answered them, “You give them something to eat.” And they say to him, “Shall we go and buy two hundred denarii worth of bread, and give it to them to eat?”note
Pero él respondió y les dijo: “Denles ustedes de comer”. Y le dicen: “¿Vamos a comprar pan por doscientos denarios, y les damos de comer?”.
38
ὁ δὲ λέγει αὐτοῖς· Πόσους ἔχετε ἄρτους; ὑπάγετε ἴδετε. καὶ γνόντες λέγουσιν· Πέντε, καὶ δύο ἰχθύας.
And he says to them, “How many loaves do you have? Go and see.” And when they found out, they say, “Five, and two fish.”note
Y él les dice: “¿Cuántos panes tienen? Vayan y vean”. Y al averiguarlo, dicen: “Cinco, y dos peces”.
39
καὶ ἐπέταξεν αὐτοῖς ἀνακλῖναι πάντας συμπόσια συμπόσια ἐπὶ τῷ χλωρῷ χόρτῳ.
And he commanded them all to sit down in groups, group by group, on the green grass.note
Y les ordenó que todos se recostaran en grupos, grupo por grupo, sobre la hierba verde.
40
καὶ ἀνέπεσαν πρασιαὶ πρασιαὶ κατὰ ἑκατὸν καὶ κατὰ πεντήκοντα.
And they sat down in rows, rows like garden plots, by hundreds and by fifties.note
Y se recostaron en hileras, hileras como cuadros de huerto, de cien en cien y de cincuenta en cincuenta.
41
καὶ λαβὼν τοὺς πέντε ἄρτους καὶ τοὺς δύο ἰχθύας ἀναβλέψας εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν εὐλόγησεν καὶ κατέκλασεν τοὺς ἄρτους καὶ ἐδίδου τοῖς μαθηταῖς αὐτοῦ ἵνα παρατιθῶσιν αὐτοῖς, καὶ τοὺς δύο ἰχθύας ἐμέρισεν πᾶσιν.
And taking the five loaves and the two fish, and looking up to heaven, he blessed and broke the loaves and gave them to his disciples to set before the people; and he divided the two fish among them all.note
Y tomando los cinco panes y los dos peces, y alzando la vista al cielo, bendijo y partió los panes, y los dio a sus discípulos para que se los sirvieran; y repartió los dos peces entre todos.
42
καὶ ἔφαγον πάντες καὶ ἐχορτάσθησαν·
And they all ate and were satisfied.note
Y todos comieron y quedaron saciados.
43
καὶ ἦραν κλάσματα δώδεκα κοφίνων πληρώματα καὶ ἀπὸ τῶν ἰχθύων.
And they took up twelve baskets full of broken pieces, and also of the fish.note
Y recogieron doce canastas llenas de pedazos, y también de los peces.
44
καὶ ἦσαν οἱ φαγόντες τοὺς ἄρτους πεντακισχίλιοι ἄνδρες.
And those who ate the loaves were five thousand men.note
Y los que comieron los panes eran cinco mil hombres.
45
Καὶ εὐθὺς ἠνάγκασεν τοὺς μαθητὰς αὐτοῦ ἐμβῆναι εἰς τὸ πλοῖον καὶ προάγειν εἰς τὸ πέραν πρὸς Βηθσαϊδάν, ἕως αὐτὸς ἀπολύει τὸν ὄχλον.
And immediately he made his disciples get into the boat and go ahead of him to the other side, toward Bethsaida, while he himself dismissed the crowd.note
Y enseguida hizo que sus discípulos subieran a la barca y fueran delante de él a la otra orilla, hacia Betsaida, mientras él despedía a la multitud.
46
καὶ ἀποταξάμενος αὐτοῖς ἀπῆλθεν εἰς τὸ ὄρος προσεύξασθαι.
And after saying farewell to them, he went up on the mountain to pray.note
Y despidiéndose de ellos, se fue al monte a orar.
47
Καὶ ὀψίας γενομένης ἦν τὸ πλοῖον ἐν μέσῳ τῆς θαλάσσης, καὶ αὐτὸς μόνος ἐπὶ τῆς γῆς.
And when evening came, the boat was in the middle of the sea, and he was alone on the land.note
Y al caer la noche, la barca estaba en medio del mar, y él estaba solo en tierra.
48
καὶ ἰδὼν αὐτοὺς βασανιζομένους ἐν τῷ ἐλαύνειν, ἦν γὰρ ὁ ἄνεμος ἐναντίος αὐτοῖς, περὶ τετάρτην φυλακὴν τῆς νυκτὸς ἔρχεται πρὸς αὐτοὺς περιπατῶν ἐπὶ τῆς θαλάσσης· καὶ ἤθελεν παρελθεῖν αὐτούς.
And seeing them tormented as they rowed, for the wind was against them, about the fourth watch of the night he comes to them, walking on the sea. And he meant to pass them by.note
Y viéndolos atormentados al remar, porque el viento les era contrario, cerca de la cuarta vigilia de la noche viene hacia ellos, caminando sobre el mar. Y quería pasar de largo junto a ellos.
49
οἱ δὲ ἰδόντες αὐτὸν ἐπὶ τῆς θαλάσσης περιπατοῦντα ἔδοξαν ὅτι φάντασμά ἐστιν καὶ ἀνέκραξαν,
But when they saw him walking on the sea, they thought it was a ghost, and cried out,note
Pero al verlo caminando sobre el mar, pensaron que era un fantasma, y gritaron,

Verses 1–6 · οὐκ ἔστιν προφήτης ἄτιμος ouk estin prophētēs atimos — a prophet without honor, and, for once, without power

Mark never names the town. “His patris” (v1) — hometown, fatherland — is left for the reader to supply from Mark 1:9's opening line, ‘Nazareth of Galilee’; Luke will say the word outright (4:16, not yet on these pages) where Mark simply assumes it.

⚠ “Is not this the carpenter?” (v3) names Jesus's own trade directly — the parallel scene in Matthew asks instead ‘is not this the carpenter's son?’ (Matthew 13:55, already on these pages), pointing at Joseph's trade rather than Jesus's. Mark's harder reading — Jesus himself at the bench — is also the one an early scribe would have the most obvious reason to soften, which is the usual argument for keeping it. “Son of Mary” is unusual too: sons in this world are named for their fathers, and Joseph himself never appears by name anywhere in this Gospel — though this is not quite the clean case it can look like. A Capernaum crowd, in a scene from this same general period, will say of Jesus, ‘is not this… the son of Joseph, whose father and mother we know?’ (John 6:42, already on these pages) — present tense, and naming Joseph outright. Whether that means Joseph was still living, or only still remembered, that verse does not say either, and this translation does not resolve here a question the New Testament itself leaves open.

The town's whole complaint is familiarity — they can list the trade, the mother, all four brothers by name, the sisters ‘here with us.’ ⚠ What stops them is not doubt about the miracles but certainty about the man.

“He was not able” (v5) is blunt in a way Matthew's retelling is not: Matthew's parallel reads ‘he did not do many mighty works there, because of their unbelief’ (13:58, already on these pages) — a choice not made, where Mark states a plain incapacity. Both Matthew's own shelf versions keep the same split at 13:58: NIV and NWT there render the verb as a simple negative, ‘did not do,’ while nothing on Mark's own English shelf below softens this verse's ‘could not.’ Whatever is happening here, this Gospel does not present it as Jesus choosing not to act.

Jesus is amazed at their unbelief (v6) — the same verb, thaumazō, this Gospel elsewhere reserves for something worth remarking on. Mark does not soften this either: it sits beside the miracle-shortage without comment, one household's astonishment answered by another's.

Verses 7–13 · δύο δύο dyo dyo — two by two, and nothing else

“Two by two” (v7) doubles the number the way Hebrew narrative doubles a word for distribution rather than emphasis (‘two and two’, not ‘very two’) — the identical construction this chapter uses twice more for the crowd at the feeding, ‘group by group’ and ‘row by row’ (vv39–40, below). Mark reaches for the same distributive doubling three times in one chapter, for a very different kind of dividing each time.

⚠ Mark allows a staff and sandals; Matthew's version of this same sending forbids both. ‘No bag for the road, or two tunics, or sandals, or a staff’ (Matthew 10:10, already on these pages) is stricter than ‘nothing… except a staff only… wear sandals’ (vv8–9) — two Gospels reporting the same commission with opposite answers on two concrete items. Neither this translation nor the shelf resolves it; both readings are printed exactly as their own Greek states them.

The shaken-off dust (v11) is a testimony against a town, not merely a farewell — the same gesture turned outward, on Israelite towns instead of the more familiar direction. ⚠ The KJV adds a full sentence here that the earliest manuscripts do not have — ‘It shall be more tolerable for Sodom and Gomorrha in the day of judgment, than for that city’ — a line imported wholesale from the parallel sending in Matthew 10:15, present in the later Byzantine text this translation's own apparatus records but absent from the earlier witness DRB follows, and from this translation.

Anointing with oil (v13) is the one detail in this commission with no instruction behind it in vv8–11 — Jesus tells them what to carry and where to stay, and never mentions oil, yet they use it anyway. The same practice reappears later in the New Testament, at the elders' bedside in James 5:14 (not yet on these pages) — a sick believer anointed with oil in Jesus's name.

Verses 14–29 · ἕως ἡμίσους τῆς βασιλείας μου heōs hēmisous tēs basileias mou — up to half a kingdom, sworn over a dance

⚠ Mark calls him “King” Herod (v14); Rome's own paperwork did not. Antipas was a tetrarch, not a king — a rank down, and Matthew's parallel gets the title right: ‘Herod the tetrarch’ (Matthew 14:1, already on these pages). Whether this is Mark writing loosely, or reporting how a court that flattered him actually spoke, the text does not say; either way this translation keeps Mark's own word rather than quietly correcting it to the accurate one.

⚠ “His name” (v14) is genuinely ambiguous in the Greek — grammatically it could point back to Herod, the sentence's own subject, as easily as to Jesus. NWT and NIV both resolve it by inserting ‘[Jesus]’ / ‘Jesus’ outright; KJV, ASV, and DRB all leave the bare pronoun standing, letting the reader do the same work the Greek requires. Context makes Jesus the far likelier referent — the whole verse turns on what people are saying about HIM — so this translation reads it that way without inserting the name the Greek itself withholds.

The chapter's whole account of John's death is a flashback: Herod hears the rumors first (v14), and only then does Mark step back — ‘for Herod himself had sent…’ (v17) — to explain an arrest that happened earlier. ⚠ His brother's name is the New Testament's own unresolved family knot. Mark and Matthew both call him “Philip” (v17); Josephus's account of the identical marriage names Herodias's first husband simply “Herod,” a private citizen, and never uses the name Philip for him at all. This translation reports the Gospel's own reading rather than harmonizing it — the full tangle, including the OTHER Philip this family also produced, is at the Herodias entry.

Herod, hearing John gladly, is described as “greatly perplexed” (v20, ēporei) in the earliest manuscripts — a real textual fork, not a translation choice: the later Byzantine text reads instead ‘he did many things’ (epoiei), a different verb close enough in shape — unspaced and unaccented, as every early manuscript was actually written — that a copyist's eye could plausibly have slipped from one to the other without meaning to change anything at all. ⚠ A wavering Herod, unable to decide what to do with a man he fears and admires, is also the harder reading — the one a copyist has an obvious reason to replace with something tidier.

⚠ The Greek itself forks at the girl's own identity, exactly where the story turns strangest. This translation follows the reading ‘the daughter of Herodias herself’ (v22) — a granddaughter, not Herod's own child — but the earliest and most demanding manuscripts read instead ‘his own daughter, Herodias,’ making the dancing girl Herod's daughter by name. Neither this translation nor the shelf resolves which is original; both readings are attested in the same family of texts this project follows for the New Testament, and the harder one is left named rather than quietly smoothed away. Mark never gives her a name at all — tradition, not this Gospel, calls her Salome.

“Up to half of my kingdom” (v23) is a stock royal extravagance, not a legal offer — Antipas held no kingdom to divide, only a tetrarchy Rome could revoke on a whim, which it eventually did. The line between the daughter's request (v24, ‘the head of John the Baptizer’) and her own demand a verse later (v25, ‘at once… on a platter’) is one this Gospel draws without comment: the mother supplies the target, the girl supplies the theater.

Verses 30–44 · ὡς πρόβατα μὴ ἔχοντα ποιμένα hōs probata mē echonta poimena — sheep without a shepherd, fed anyway

The returning Twelve are called apostles (v30) — the same title Mark gave them once before, in the act of appointing them (3:14, already on these pages: ‘whom he also named apostles’). This is the first time the narration itself uses the word for them doing anything, rather than simply reporting that the name was given.

“Like sheep without a shepherd” (v34) is Moses's own image at his life's end, asking Jehovah for a successor so the congregation would not become exactly this (Numbers 27:17, already on these pages). Mark puts the identical picture in Jesus's own compassion for a crowd that has just run ahead of him on foot — the request from one leader for a shepherd answered, chapters and testaments later, by the shepherd himself arriving anyway.

The doubling this chapter has already used for the sent Twelve returns twice more here: the crowd sits “group by group” (symposia symposia, v39) and then in “rows like garden plots” (prasiai prasiai, v40) — two different Greek words, both doubled the same Semitic way, describing the same seated crowd from two different angles: by table, then by the shape it makes on a hillside. ⚠ Prasiai is literally garden beds, the word choosing to describe rows of reclining people as if seen from above, growing.

Two hundred denarii (v37) is roughly two hundred days' ordinary wages — a denarius is what a laborer earns in a single day elsewhere in this translation (Matthew 20:2, already on these pages) — which is the disciples' own arithmetic for why buying bread for this crowd is not a serious plan.

“He took… he blessed… he broke… and gave” (v41) is the same fourfold sequence this Gospel's own Last Supper will use of bread in an upper room (14:22, not yet on these pages) — planted here, first, over five loaves on open grass, long before either the disciples or the reader has any reason to notice the shape repeating.

Twelve baskets left over (v43) from twelve men's crowd, five thousand men fed by a boy's-day of food — Matthew's parallel spells out what Mark leaves as bare arithmetic: ‘besides women and children’ (Matthew 14:21, already on these pages). Mark's own count, “five thousand men” (v44, andres, not the generic word for people), is exact about exactly one part of the crowd.

Verses 45–52 · Θαρσεῖτε, ἐγώ εἰμι Tharseite, egō eimi — I am, kept bare

⚠ Bethsaida (v45) and Gennesaret (v53) sit on opposite shores of the same lake — a genuine, long-noted geography crux, not a translation choice. Jesus sends the boat toward Bethsaida on the eastern/northern shore; it lands instead at Gennesaret to the west. This translation reports the discrepancy rather than resolving it — proposed answers range from a wind-blown detour to a second, lesser-known Bethsaida on the western shore, and the Gospel itself supplies no explanation.

The disciples are “tormented” at the oars (v48, basanizomenous) — the identical verb the unclean spirit used of itself two chapters ago, begging Jesus, ‘do not torment me’ (5:7, already on these pages). Mark does not remark on the echo: the word that once named a demon's fear of Jesus now names the disciples' own struggle against a contrary wind, moments before the same Jesus arrives calmly on top of the water causing it.

“He meant to pass them by” (v48) is a strange detail for a Gospel to leave standing — Jesus apparently intends to walk past his own struggling disciples rather than join them, until their cry stops him. The phrase echoes the language used of God's own passing appearances to Moses and Elijah, though this translation does not press an Old Testament chapter not currently on these pages into service to prove it.

⚠ “It is I” (v50) translates egō eimi, and this scene has already been told once on these pages. This Gospel's own John 6:20 has Jesus say the nearly identical words — Egō eimi, mē phobeisthe — to a boat crew in the same storm, on the same night, by most readings the same event told a second time. That page's own note already makes the point worth repeating here: egō eimi is ordinary self-identification in context — frightened men who think they see a ghost are being told, plainly, that it is only their teacher — and all seven English shelf versions read it exactly that way, verbatim: NWT, NIV, KJV, ASV, TLB, GNV, and DRB all print ‘it is I.’ The Spanish shelf splits down the middle at this exact verse — RV 1909 and RV60 keep the bare word order, “yo soy,” while TNM and NVI both reorder it to “soy yo” — and this translation follows the reordering, matching its own choice at John 6:20. What it does NOT do is print the bare construction here the way it does at the one place this Gospel's own John lets the identical two words stand with no predicate and nothing ordinary happening around them — ‘before Abraham was, I am’ (John 8:58, already on these pages). That is a genuinely different use: no fear to calm, no boat, no ghost. Keeping the ordinary self-identification ordinary here, and the absolute claim absolute there, is the harder discipline than treating every egō eimi alike.

⚠ Their hearts were “hardened” (v52) — the identical word, and the identical verb root, Mark has already used of the Pharisees watching Jesus heal a withered hand in a synagogue (3:5, already on these pages: ‘grieved… at the hardening of their heart’). There the word marked the opposition plotting against him; here, two chapters and one miraculous feeding later, Mark turns the same diagnosis on the Twelve who have just watched him do it. The Gospel does not soften the comparison by choosing a gentler word for its own inner circle.

Verses 53–56 · κἂν τοῦ κρασπέδου kan tou kraspedou — even the fringe, again

This closing summary compresses an entire region's response into four verses — recognition (v54), a countryside run on foot (v55), and a single repeated request wherever he goes (v56) — the fastest-moving passage in a Gospel already known for its speed.

“Even the fringe of his garment” (v56) is not a new detail invented here — it is the whole countryside now asking, openly and in numbers, for exactly what one woman asked for privately two chapters ago: ‘if I touch even his garments, I will be made well’ (5:28, already on these pages). What was one woman's quiet, ashamed hope, told and retold by everyone who saw it happen, has become an entire region's open practice.

“Made well” (v56, sōzō) is this Gospel's own word for both healing and salvation, used of the bleeding woman in the identical chapter this passage echoes (‘your faith has made you well,’ 5:34, already on these pages). Mark closes an already fast chapter on the word doing both jobs at once, exactly as it has throughout.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “tormented” (v48) kept as the same word the unclean spirit used of itself at 5:7, not varied for the calmer scene; “hardened” (v52) kept as the same diagnosis already given the Pharisees at 3:5; and “it is I” (v50) kept as the ordinary self-identification this Gospel's own John already chose at John 6:20 — the same scene, told a second time — reserving the bare, unpredicated “I am” for the one place John lets it stand with nothing ordinary around it, John 8:58.

Echoes paid. The apostles (v30), named by Jesus at 3:14, are called by the same title in narration for the first time here, doing what the name describes. The fringe of his garment (v56), one woman's private hope at 5:27–28, has become an entire region's open request. And “tormented” and “hardened”, both above, turn words this Gospel first used of its opponents onto Jesus's own disciples instead.

Echoes planted. The fourfold took…blessed…broke…gave (v41) over five loaves, waiting to recur over bread in an upper room at 14:22. And the word for hardened (v52) itself is not finished — this Gospel's own dictionary entry for it already notes it returns a third time, on the disciples again, at 8:17.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether Herod's brother was named Philip, as Mark and Matthew both say, or something else, as Josephus's account of the same marriage suggests (vv17–18). Whether the dancing girl was “the daughter of Herodias herself” or, in the harder and equally ancient reading, “his own daughter” (v22) — a genuine fork in the earliest manuscripts, not a translation choice. And how a boat sent toward Bethsaida lands at Gennesaret instead (vv45, 53), a geography this chapter raises and does not resolve.
