At Iconium the pair go into the synagogue and a great number of Jews and Greeks believe; the Jews who disobeyed afflict the souls of the nations, the city is split, and when an impulse comes to treat them outrageously and to stone them they flee to Lystra and Derbe. At Lystra a man lame from his mother's womb is told to stand up on his feet and leaps up; the crowds call Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes and the priest of the Zeus before the city brings bulls and garlands, and the apostles tear their garments and speak of a living God. Jews from Antioch and Iconium persuade the crowds, Paul is stoned and dragged out of the city, and he rises up and goes back in. After Derbe the two return through Lystra, Iconium and Antioch strengthening the disciples and appointing elders, then sail from Attalia to Antioch and report that God had opened to the nations a door of faith.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its only time-markers are ‘a considerable time’ at Iconium (v3), ‘the next day’ (v20) and ‘not a little time’ at Antioch (v28). It follows the arrival at Iconium of 13:51 and ends where 15:1 begins; this library dates the journey by neither.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–7 · ἐν Ἰκονίῳ … κατὰ τὸ αὐτὸ … οὕτως ὥστε … ἀπειθήσαντες … ἐπήγειραν καὶ ἐκάκωσαν τὰς ψυχάς … παρρησιαζόμενοι ἐπὶ τῷ κυρίῳ … ἐσχίσθη … τοῖς ἀποστόλοις … ὁρμή … ὑβρίσαι … Λυκαονίας en Ikoniō … kata to auto … houtōs hōste … apeithēsantes … epēgeiran kai ekakōsan tas psychas … parrhēsiazomenoi epi tō kyriō … eschisthē … tois apostolois … hormē … hybrisai … Lykaonias — Iconium, Acts 14:1–7: a synagogue entered “together,” the souls of the nations afflicted, boldness “relying on the Lord,” a city split, and two “apostles” who are not the Twelve

“Together,” “in such a way that,” and who the Greeks are (Acts 14:1). The chapter opens where chapter 13 closed, with the pair arriving at Iconium after shaking off the dust (13:51), and it repeats what they did at Pisidian Antioch: they go into the synagogue (13:14). Kata to auto is “together” in six of the seven versions: ASV, Douay and NWT 1984 “entered together,” KJV and Geneva “went both together,” TLB “went together.” The NIV has “as usual,” which takes the phrase to mean the same way as before. This translation keeps “together”; the phrase stands in this verse alone in Acts. Luke does not say what the “way” of speaking was: houtōs hōste is “in such a way that” and no more. The NIV “spoke so effectively” and the TLB “preached with such power” supply a quality the Greek does not name (KJV, Geneva and ASV “so spake,” NWT 1984 “in such a manner”). Those who believe are “Jews and Greeks” (Hellēnes), and the Greek does not say whether these Greeks are the “worshiping” Gentiles of 13:43 and 13:50 or others; the TLB prints “Jews and Gentiles,” Geneva “Grecians,” and the other five “Greeks.”

“Disobeyed,” and the souls the Jews “afflicted” (Acts 14:2). The Greek does not say that these Jews “did not believe.” Apeithēsantes is the aorist participle of apeitheō, “to disobey, to refuse to be persuaded,” and the verb stands in Acts here and at ēpeithoun in 19:9. The KJV, Geneva and Douay print “the unbelieving Jews,” the NWT 1984 “the Jews that did not believe,” the NIV “who refused to believe,” the TLB “who spurned God’s message,” and the ASV alone “were disobedient,” as this translation does. The SBLGNT apparatus gives apeithēsantes to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition, and the present participle apeithountes, “who were disobeying,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The two verbs that follow carry chapter 13 forward. Epēgeiran, “stirred up,” is the verb of 13:50, where the Jews “stirred up a persecution” against the pair, and ekakōsan, “afflicted,” is the verb of 12:1, where Herod laid his hands “to afflict” some of the congregation; it stands in five verses of Acts, 7:6, 7:19, 12:1, here and 18:10. The object is “the souls of the nations”: ASV and NWT 1984 “souls,” KJV, Geneva, Douay and NIV “minds.” The verb with them is KJV “made their minds evil affected,” ASV “made them evil affected,” NIV “poisoned,” NWT 1984 “wrongly influenced,” Douay “incensed,” and Geneva “corrupted.” The TLB has the Jews “saying all sorts of evil things” about Paul and Barnabas, which turns the afflicting of souls into slander.

Speaking boldly “relying on the Lord” — the prayer of Acts 4, answered in its own words (Acts 14:3). Hikanon men oun chronon diētripsan, “so then they spent a considerable time”: Luke gives no number. The participle parrhēsiazomenoi, “speaking boldly” (dictionary), is the verb of 9:27 and 9:28, where Saul speaks boldly at Damascus and Jerusalem, and of 13:46, where the pair speak boldly before turning to the nations; it comes again at 18:26, 19:8 and 26:26 (the last not yet on these pages). And the verse answers a prayer. The congregation asked, at 4:29–30, “grant to your slaves to speak your word with all boldness, as you stretch out your hand for healing, and signs and wonders happen through the name of your holy servant Jesus,” and the answer was given in the same terms at 5:12, “through the hands of the apostles many signs and wonders were happening.” Here the three pieces stand in a single sentence: the Lord is didonti, “granting,” sēmeia kai terata ginesthai, “signs and wonders to happen,” dia tōn cheirōn autōn, “through their hands.” The preposition before “the Lord” is epi with the dative, which names the ground a thing stands on, and this translation prints “relying on the Lord.” On the shelf it is KJV, Geneva, ASV and Douay “in the Lord,” NIV “for the Lord,” and NWT 1984 “by the authority of Jehovah”; the TLB leaves the phrase out. This translation keeps “Lord” because kyrios does not say here whether the one who bears witness is Jehovah or Jesus (the same open question as at 13:2); where the NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah” here and at v23, that is a judgment about the referent, not a different Greek word. The apparatus records one more difference before “the word of his grace”: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont read the article tō alone, and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition reads epi tō logō, “upon the word.” The phrase tō logō tēs charitos autou, “the word of his grace,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Paul’s farewell at Acts 20:32.

“Apostles” — Barnabas and Paul called apostles at Acts 14:4 and 14:14. The city is eschisthē, “was split” — the verb of the curtain of the sanctuary (Mark 15:38, Luke 23:45, Matthew 27:51), of the net that “was not torn” (John 21:11) and, in Acts, of the council at 23:7 — and the two sides are “the Jews” and “the apostles.” Those apostles are Paul and Barnabas, and verse 14 says so by name: hoi apostoloi Barnabas kai Paulos. The stem apostol- stands in twenty-nine verses of Acts. In twenty-seven of them it means the apostles of Jerusalem or, at 1:25, their office, and Luke himself keeps the groups apart: Joseph is “surnamed Barnabas by the apostles” (4:36), Barnabas brings Saul “to the apostles” (9:27), and Paul and Barnabas are sent up “to the apostles and elders” (15:2). So these two verses, 14:4 and 14:14, are the only ones in Acts that give the word to people outside that company. The dictionary entry gives the plain sense, “one who is sent.” The Greek does not say whether Luke means a title or a description of two men whom the Holy Spirit had “sent out” (13:4, where the verb is ekpempō and not apostellō), and this page reports both readings and does not choose. At v4 every version on the shelf prints “apostles”; for v14 see the note on vv14–18.

An “impulse,” not an “assault,” and the flight to Lycaonia (Acts 14:5–7). Hormē is an impulse, a rush (dictionary); in the New Testament it stands here and at James 3:4, the steersman’s “impulse” (not yet on these pages). The versions turn it into an event or a scheme: KJV, Geneva and Douay “an assault made,” ASV “an onset,” NWT 1984 “a violent attempt,” NIV “a plot afoot,” TLB “a plot to incite a mob.” The Greek says only that an impulse “came about” on the part of “the nations and the Jews with their rulers,” and the pair then “became aware” (synidontes, the participle of 12:12, where it is Peter who “realizes”; the word stands in these two verses of the New Testament and no others), so that the impulse could be known of before it was carried out. “Their rulers” is autōn, and the Greek does not say whether the rulers belong to the Jews, to both parties, or to the city. The two things intended are hybrisai, “to treat outrageously,” the verb of Luke 18:32, where the Son of Man is “shamefully treated” (KJV “use them despitefully,” ASV “treat them shamefully,” NWT 1984 “treat them insolently,” NIV “mistreat”), and lithobolēsai, “to stone” — the verb of Stephen’s death (7:58). They flee to “the cities of Lycaonia,” and Luke names two, Lystra and Derbe (Lystra, Derbe, Lycaonia); Iconium is not among them in his wording. The stem Lykaon- stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and, in the adverb Lykaonisti, v11. The verb of v7, euangelizomenoi ēsan, “they were bringing the good news,” is the one the pair carry into every place they reach in this chapter; it returns at vv15 and 21.

Verses 8–10 · ἀνὴρ ἀδύνατος … χωλὸς ἐκ κοιλίας μητρὸς … οὐδέποτε περιεπάτησεν … ἀτενίσας … πίστιν τοῦ σωθῆναι … Ἀνάστηθι ἐπὶ τοὺς πόδας σου ὀρθός … ἥλατο καὶ περιεπάτει anēr adynatos … chōlos ek koilias mētros … oudepote periepatēsen … atenisas … pistin tou sōthēnai … Anastēthi epi tous podas sou orthos … hēlato kai periepatei — Lystra, Acts 14:8–10: the second man lame from birth in Acts, “faith to be saved,” “stand up on your feet,” and the leap

Lame from his mother’s womb — the second such man in Acts (Acts 14:8). The phrase is word for word that of Acts 3:2: chōlos ek koilias mētros autou. The two scenes share more than the phrase. In both, someone looks hard at the man (atenisas: Peter at 3:4, “gazing” there, and Paul here, “staring,” as at 13:9); in both the man is told to rise; in both the verb of leaping is followed by periepatei, “was walking” (3:8). The differences are as plain. The man at the Beautiful Gate is carried and set down every day to beg, and Peter says “in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene, rise up and walk” and seizes him by the right hand (3:6–7); the man at Lystra is sitting and listening, and Paul’s command carries no name and no touch. The adjective adynatos is literally “without power”; it is the “impossible” of Matthew 19:26 and Luke 18:27. The KJV, Geneva, ASV and Douay print “impotent in his feet,” which in 1611 meant powerless; the NWT 1984 “disabled in his feet,” the NIV “a man who was lame,” the TLB “a man with crippled feet.” Two details are marked in the apparatus. Westcott–Hort and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition put the adjective before “in Lystra,” Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont after it; and Robinson–Pierpont add hyparchōn, “being,” after “his mother’s womb,” the participle with which Acts 3:2 describes the first man, and read the pluperfect peripepatēkei, “had never walked,” where the other three have the aorist periepatēsen.

“Faith to be saved” — and what Paul saw (Acts 14:9). The Greek is pistin tou sōthēnai, “faith of being saved.” Sōzō (dictionary) is the verb this translation prints “has been saved” of the lame man of Acts 3 at 4:9, and it is the verb of the sentence in 4:12, “by which we must be saved.” All seven versions on the shelf choose a word for bodily healing here: KJV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and TLB “healed,” ASV “made whole,” NWT 1984 “made well.” The verb can mean either, and Luke gives no further clue; the same infinitive, sōthēnai, is what the men from Judea say a Gentile cannot be unless he is circumcised, at 15:1, and what Peter says of Jew and Gentile alike at 15:11. This page keeps “saved” and states the range. How Paul “saw” the faith, the Greek does not say: idōn hoti echei pistin, “seeing that he has faith,” is the construction of Luke 5:20, where Jesus, “seeing their faith,” forgives the paralyzed man. The apparatus marks the verb of v9 as variable: Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont read ēkousen, “heard,” and Westcott–Hort read ēkouen, “was listening”; and Robinson–Pierpont have pistin echei where the other three have echei pistin. The KJV and Geneva “heard Paul speak,” the Douay “heard Paul speaking,” the NIV “listened to Paul as he was speaking,” the NWT 1984 “was listening to Paul speak.”

“Stand up on your feet, upright” — and the leap (Acts 14:10). The command is Anastēthi epi tous podas sou orthos. Anastēthi is the imperative of anistēmi, the verb that Luke uses of rising from the dead and of simply standing up; it is the word with which Peter lifts Cornelius, “Stand up; I myself also am a man” (10:26), a sentence whose second half Paul and Barnabas say again to the crowd at v15. The phrase epi tous podas, “on (or at) the feet,” stands in three verses of Acts: here, with “your”; 10:25, where Cornelius falls “at his feet”; and 26:16, where the risen Jesus tells Saul anastēthi kai stēthi epi tous podas sou, “rise and stand on your feet” (not yet on these pages). Orthos, “upright,” is an adjective in the Nestle–Aland, Westcott–Hort and Tregelles texts and the adverb orthōs, “rightly,” in Robinson–Pierpont’s. The KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “upright,” the NWT 1984 “erect”; the NIV and TLB drop the word. The verb of the leap is hēlato, which this translation renders with the words leaped up; the verb hallomai stands in three verses of the New Testament, John 4:14 (the spring of water “leaping up”), Acts 3:8 (the first man “leaping up” and then “leaping” in the temple) and this one; Robinson–Pierpont have the imperfect hēlleto, “was leaping.” KJV “leaped and walked,” ASV, Geneva and Douay “leaped up, and walked,” NIV “jumped up and began to walk,” NWT 1984 “leaped up and began walking,” TLB “leaped to his feet and started walking.” The man does not speak, and Luke does not report what he did or said afterward.

Verses 11–13 · Λυκαονιστί … Οἱ θεοὶ ὁμοιωθέντες ἀνθρώποις κατέβησαν … Δία … Ἑρμῆν … ὁ ἡγούμενος τοῦ λόγου … ὁ ἱερεὺς τοῦ Διὸς τοῦ ὄντος πρὸ τῆς πόλεως … ταύρους καὶ στέμματα ἐπὶ τοὺς πυλῶνας Lykaonisti … Hoi theoi homoiōthentes anthrōpois katebēsan … Dia … Hermēn … ho hēgoumenos tou logou … ho hiereus tou Dios tou ontos pro tēs poleōs … taurous kai stemmata epi tous pylōnas — Lystra, Acts 14:11–13: Zeus and Hermes, a priest of “the Zeus that was before the city,” and the sacrifice that does not happen

“In Lycaonian” — and the gods “made like humans” (Acts 14:11). Lykaonisti is an adverb, “in the Lycaonian tongue,” and it stands in this verse alone in the New Testament (dictionary). Luke names the language and gives no word of it, and he does not say whether Paul and Barnabas understood it. What he does say is that the apostles act only at v14, when they “heard” (see the note on vv14–18). The cry is Hoi theoi homoiōthentes anthrōpois katebēsan pros hēmas. Homoiōthentes is the aorist passive participle of homoioō, “having been made like,” and the gods are the subject, so the cry is not about two men who are gods but about gods who have come down looking like men: NWT 1984 “have become like humans,” KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “are come down to us in the likeness of men,” NIV “have come down to us in human form.” The TLB “These men are gods in human bodies!” reverses the subject. The apparatus gives te after “crowds” to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28, and de to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont, and Robinson–Pierpont add the article ho before “Paul.”

Zeus and Hermes — not “Jupiter” and “Mercury” (Acts 14:12). The Greek has Dia, the accusative of Zeus, and Hermēn. The KJV prints “Jupiter” and “Mercurius,” Geneva the same, the ASV and Douay “Jupiter” and “Mercury,” the TLB “the Greek god Jupiter” and “Mercury,” which names a Greek god by his Roman name; the NIV and NWT 1984 keep “Zeus” and “Hermes,” as this translation does, because those are the names Luke wrote. The reason Luke gives for Paul’s name is his own, not the crowd’s: epeidē autos ēn ho hēgoumenos tou logou, “since he was the one leading the speaking.” Luke gives no reason for Barnabas’s name, the name of the chief of the gods. The imperfect ekaloun is “they began calling” or “kept calling,” and the apparatus gives Robinson–Pierpont men after the article before Barnabas.

Ovid’s Jupiter and Mercury at the door. The Roman poet Ovid tells, in the eighth book of his Metamorphoses (the tale of Philemon and Baucis, told by Lelex at 8.618–724), of two gods who walk the earth as mortals, and the Latin lines are these (read here from the Perseus edition): Iuppiter huc specie mortali cumque parente venit Atlantiades positis caducifer alis. Mille domos adiere locum requiemque petentes, mille domos clausere serae. Tamen una recepit (8.626–629), “Jupiter came here in mortal form, and with his father came Atlas’s descendant, the staff-bearer, his wings put aside. They approached a thousand houses seeking a place and a rest; a thousand houses were bolted against them. Yet one received them.” Atlantiades, “descendant of Atlas,” is the name Ovid also gives at 1.682 to the winged son of Jupiter and a Pleiad (1.669–670) whom Jupiter sends to kill Argus — that is, to Mercury, the Roman Hermes. The old couple in the one small house are Baucis and Philemon (8.631), and the story is set in the hills of Phrygia (collibus … Phrygiis, 8.621), not in Lycaonia. Commentators often set this tale beside Lystra; Luke does not mention it, and the connection is reported here and not shown. The two stories do run in opposite directions: in Ovid’s the visitors are gods whom the whole neighborhood takes for travelers, and at Lystra the visitors are men whom a whole city takes for gods.

“The Zeus that was before the city” — and no “temple” in the Greek (Acts 14:13). The Greek is ho hiereus tou Dios tou ontos pro tēs poleōs, “the priest of the Zeus that was before the city.” The participle tou ontos, “that was,” agrees with Dios, Zeus, since no other noun stands in the sentence for it to agree with: Luke names the god by the place where he is, and does not write “temple.” The ASV “whose temple was before the city,” the NIV “whose temple was just outside the city,” the NWT 1984 “whose [temple] was before the city” (with brackets), and the TLB “the local priest of the Temple of Jupiter, located on the outskirts of the city” all add it; the KJV “which was before their city” and Geneva “which was before their city” leave a bare “which,” and the Douay “that was before the city” is the nearest to the Greek. The “their” in the KJV and Geneva is the reading of Robinson–Pierpont, autōn, which Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 do not have. The priest brings taurous kai stemmata, “bulls and garlands”; stemma, “garland,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and Luke does not say for whom the garlands are meant, the bulls or the apostles. He brings them epi tous pylōnas, “to the gateways,” and the Greek does not say whose: elsewhere in Acts pylōn is the gateway of a house (Simon’s, 10:17; Mary’s, 12:13–14). NIV “the city gates” and TLB “the city gates” add “city”; the others keep “the gates.” The verb is ēthelen thyein, “he was wanting to sacrifice,” an imperfect; v18 will report that they scarcely stopped it.

Verses 14–18 · διαρρήξαντες τὰ ἱμάτια … ἐξεπήδησαν … ὁμοιοπαθεῖς ἐσμεν ὑμῖν ἄνθρωποι … τῶν ματαίων … ἐπὶ θεὸν ζῶντα … ὃς ἐποίησεν τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ τὴν γῆν καὶ τὴν θάλασσαν … εἴασεν … οὐκ ἀμάρτυρον … ἀγαθουργῶν … εὐφροσύνης diarrēxantes ta himatia … exepēdēsan … homoiopatheis esmen hymin anthrōpoi … tōn mataiōn … epi theon zōnta … hos epoiēsen ton ouranon kai tēn gēn kai tēn thalassan … eiasen … ouk amartyron … agathourgōn … euphrosynēs — Lystra, Acts 14:14–18: torn garments, “we also are humans,” “a living God,” and a witness that is rain and food

Torn garments and a leap (Acts 14:14). The Greek gives the verb akousantes, “when they heard,” no object: Luke says neither that they heard the cry nor that they heard of the sacrifice. The ASV and NWT 1984 supply “of it,” the KJV “of,” Geneva “it,” and the NIV “of this”; the TLB changes the verb to “saw what was happening.” Then diarrēxantes is the participle of the verb of Mark 14:63, where the high priest, “tearing his tunics,” asks what need there is of witnesses, and Matthew 26:65 has the same act in another form of the verb, followed by “He has blasphemed!”; the Greek here gives no reason for the tearing, but the sentence that follows is a refusal of what the crowd has said. The verb of the leap, exepēdēsan, “leaped out,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the apparatus marks it: Robinson–Pierpont read eisepēdēsan, “leaped in,” the verb that stands at Acts 16:29 for the jailer. The KJV and Geneva “ran in among the people” follow that reading, and ASV “sprang forth,” Douay “leaped out,” NIV “rushed out into the crowd,” NWT 1984 “leaped out into the crowd” and TLB “ran out among the people” follow the other. The pronoun after “garments” is also marked: Westcott–Hort have heautōn, “their own,” and the other three editions autōn, “their.” This is the verse in which Luke names the pair “the apostles Barnabas and Paul” (every version on the shelf prints “apostles” here except the TLB, which has only “when Barnabas and Paul saw”), and the order is Barnabas first, as at 15:12 and 15:25; at 13:43 and 13:46 it was Paul first.

“We also are humans who share your nature” — homoiopatheis (Acts 14:15). Andres, “Men” (KJV and ASV “Sirs,” NIV “Friends,” Geneva “O men,” Douay “Ye men”), is followed by kai hēmeis homoiopatheis esmen hymin anthrōpoi. Homoiopathēs is built on homoios, “like,” and pathos, “what one undergoes”; it stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and James 5:17, where Elijah is “a man of like nature with us” (dictionary; James 5 is not yet on these pages, but its next verse, 5:18, has the sky giving rain, as v17 here has God giving rains). KJV and ASV “of like passions with you,” Geneva “subject to the like passions that ye be,” Douay “mortals, men like unto you,” NWT 1984 “having the same infirmities as you,” NIV “only human, like you,” TLB “merely human beings like yourselves.” “Passions” meant, in 1611, things suffered, and the range of the Greek is wider than any of these: feelings, sufferings, the whole common lot. This translation prints “who share your nature” and states the range here. The sentence is the second half of Acts 10:26, Peter’s “I myself also am a man,” said to a man at his feet; here it is said to a crowd. Then the message: euangelizomenoi hymas apo toutōn tōn mataiōn epistrephein, “bringing you the good news that you are to turn from these futile things.” Mataios is “empty, to no purpose” (dictionary); the KJV has “vanities,” Geneva, ASV and NWT 1984 “vain things,” NIV “worthless things,” TLB “foolish things.” The Douay prints “to be converted from these vain things, to the living God.” The God to whom they turn is theon zōnta, with no article in the Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 texts — “a living God,” as the ASV alone prints it — and with ton theon ton zōnta, “the living God,” in Robinson–Pierpont, whose reading the other six versions follow.

“Who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them” — and no verse of scripture quoted (Acts 14:15). The sentence is the congregation’s own: Acts 4:24 has, in the prayer, ho poiēsas ton ouranon kai tēn gēn kai tēn thalassan kai panta ta en autois, and the speech here has hos epoiēsen where the prayer has the participle and the rest word for word. The same list in Hebrew, “the sea and all that is in them,” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: the sabbath commandment (Exodus 20:11 in English numbering, 20:10 in the Hebrew’s and on these pages) and Psalm 146:6 (not yet on these pages). Luke gives no quotation formula in vv15–17, and none of the three verses names a text; the argument is made from what the hearers can see.

“He let all the nations go in their ways” (Acts 14:16). Ho… eiasen panta ta ethnē poreuesthai tais hodois autōn, “who… let all the nations go in their own ways,” with the participle parōichēmenais, “gone by,” in “the generations gone by”: the participle stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. KJV and Douay “in times past suffered all nations,” Geneva “in times past suffered all the Gentiles,” ASV “in the generations gone by suffered all the nations,” NWT 1984 “in the past generations he permitted all the nations,” NIV “In the past, he let all nations go their own way,” TLB “In bygone days he permitted the nations to go their own ways.” Paul says something close at Athens, that God, “overlooking the times of ignorance,” now commands all people everywhere to repent (hyperidōn, Acts 17:30); the verb here, “let,” says only that he did not stop them, and Luke does not say what he thought of the ways.

“Not without witness” — amartyron — “doing good,” and the gladness of Acts 2 (Acts 14:17). Kaitoi ouk amartyron heauton aphēken agathourgōn: “and yet he did not leave himself without witness, doing good.” Two words in it stand nowhere else in the New Testament: amartyron, “without witness” (dictionary), and agathourgōn, “doing good” (dictionary); Robinson–Pierpont read the common agathopoiōn for the second, and Kaitoige for the first word of the verse. The witness is the stem martyr- that Luke used of the Lord at v3, “bearing witness to the word of his grace”: at Iconium the Lord witnesses to the word through signs, at Lystra God has witnessed to everyone through the seasons. The gifts are hyetous, “rains,” from heaven (ouranothen, which stands here and at 26:13), kairous karpophorous, “fruitful seasons,” trophēs, “food,” and euphrosynēs, “gladness”; the last noun stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Acts 2:28, where the quoted psalm has God filling David “with gladness with your face.” The pronouns are marked in the apparatus: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 read hymōn, “your,” at “your hearts,” and Robinson–Pierpont hēmōn, “our”; the KJV and Geneva print “us” and “our” (“gave us rain from heaven… filling our hearts”) and the Douay “filling our hearts,” while the ASV, NWT 1984, NIV and TLB keep “you” and “your.” NIV “has not left himself without testimony: He has shown kindness,” TLB “never left himself without a witness; there were always his reminders.” Verse 18 closes the scene with the verb katapauō: the two “scarcely made the crowds stop” (KJV and ASV “scarce restrained,” NWT 1984 “scarcely restrained,” NIV “had difficulty keeping”) — and the crowds are the ochloi that Luke has now named at vv11, 13, 14 and 18, and names again at v19.

Verses 19–20 · Ἐπῆλθαν δὲ ἀπὸ Ἀντιοχείας καὶ Ἰκονίου Ἰουδαῖοι … πείσαντες τοὺς ὄχλους καὶ λιθάσαντες τὸν Παῦλον ἔσυρον ἔξω τῆς πόλεως … νομίζοντες αὐτὸν τεθνηκέναι … κυκλωσάντων … ἀναστὰς εἰσῆλθεν εἰς τὴν πόλιν … εἰς Δέρβην Epēlthan de apo Antiocheias kai Ikoniou Ioudaioi … peisantes tous ochlous kai lithasantes ton Paulon esyron exō tēs poleōs … nomizontes auton tethnēkenai … kyklōsantōn … anastas eisēlthen eis tēn polin … eis Derbēn — Lystra, Acts 14:19–20: Paul stoned and dragged out, “supposing him to be dead,” and the walk back into the city

The same crowds, persuaded (Acts 14:19). The Jews come apo Antiocheias kai Ikoniou, “from Antioch and Iconium,” the two cities from which the pair were thrown out or driven (13:50, 14:5), and what they do is peisantes tous ochlous, “having persuaded the crowds.” The noun is the one Luke has been repeating since v11: the crowds that wanted to sacrifice to Barnabas and Paul are the crowds that now stone Paul, and Luke does not say how they were turned. The KJV “who persuaded the people,” Douay “persuading the multitude,” NIV “won the crowd over,” and the TLB “turned the crowds into a murderous mob,” which adds a word and adds “only a few days later,” a time the Greek does not give. Two Greek verbs are used of stoning in the chapter. At v5 it was lithobolēsai, the verb of Stephen’s death (7:58–59); here it is lithasantes, and the verb stands in seven verses of the New Testament (in a compound form at Luke 20:6): Luke 20:6, John 10:31 and 11:8, Acts 5:26, this verse, 2 Corinthians 11:25 and Hebrews 11:37. And the scene has an earlier shape in Acts: Stephen was thrown exō tēs poleōs, “outside the city,” and stoned, with the witnesses laying their garments at the feet of a young man called Saul (7:58); here it is that man who is dragged exō tēs poleōs. The verb esyron, “they were dragging,” is an imperfect.

“Supposing him to be dead” — and what Luke does not say (Acts 14:19–20). Nomizontes auton tethnēkenai reports what the stoners supposed: KJV and Geneva “supposing he had been dead,” ASV “supposing that he was dead,” Douay “thinking him to be dead,” NIV “thinking he was dead,” NWT 1984 “imagining he was dead,” TLB “apparently dead,” which turns their supposition into the narrator’s appearance. The apparatus has Robinson–Pierpont reading nomisantes, “having supposed,” for nomizontes. What follows is told in three plain verbs: the disciples kyklōsantōn, “surrounded” him; he anastas, “rose up,” and eisēlthen eis tēn polin, “went into the city”; and on the next day he went out with Barnabas to Derbe. Luke does not say that Paul had died or that he was raised, and readers have taken the passage both ways; this page reports the two readings and does not choose. The verb anastas is the same verb as the command of v10, Anastēthi, “Stand up” (see the note on vv8–10): the man who told a lame man to stand is the man who now stands. The apparatus marks the order of the last words of v20: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 read tōn mathētōn auton and Robinson–Pierpont auton tōn mathētōn, with no difference in sense.

What Paul says of it, and what is not yet on these pages. Paul counts one stoning among his sufferings: hapax elithasthēn, “once I was stoned” (2 Corinthians 11:25, not yet on these pages). Acts reports one stoning of Paul, and the two are commonly taken to be the same; this page reports that and does not verify it. In 2 Timothy 3:11 (not yet on these pages) he lists the persecutions that “came to me in Antioch, in Iconium, in Lystra,” the three cities of 13:50, 14:5 and 14:19, in that order. Derbe, where he goes the next day, is named in four verses of the New Testament: 14:6, here, Acts 20:4, where a Gaius is called Derbaios, “of Derbe,” and Acts 16:1.

Verses 21–23 · μαθητεύσαντες ἱκανούς … ὑπέστρεψαν … ἐπιστηρίζοντες τὰς ψυχὰς … ἐμμένειν τῇ πίστει … διὰ πολλῶν θλίψεων δεῖ ἡμᾶς εἰσελθεῖν εἰς τὴν βασιλείαν … χειροτονήσαντες … κατʼ ἐκκλησίαν πρεσβυτέρους … μετὰ νηστειῶν … παρέθεντο αὐτοὺς τῷ κυρίῳ mathēteusantes hikanous … hypestrepsan … epistērizontes tas psychas … emmenein tē pistei … dia pollōn thlipseōn dei hēmas eiselthein eis tēn basileian … cheirotonēsantes … kat’ ekklēsian presbyterous … meta nēsteiōn … parethento autous tō kyriō — back through Lystra, Iconium and Antioch, Acts 14:21–23: “through many tribulations,” elders appointed in each congregation, and prayer with fastings

Back through the cities that threw them out (Acts 14:21–22). They return eis tēn Lystran kai eis Ikonion kai eis Antiocheian, “to Lystra and to Iconium and to Antioch” — the place of the stoning, the place of the impulse and the place of the expulsion, and Luke gives no sign of how they came back into them. (The apparatus has Robinson–Pierpont omit the preposition eis before “Iconium” and before “Antioch”; the other three editions have it both times.) The verb for what they did at Derbe is mathēteusantes hikanous, “having made disciples of a considerable number”; mathēteuō is the verb of the last command in Matthew, “make disciples of all the nations” (28:19), and it stands in four verses of the New Testament, Matthew 13:52, 27:57, 28:19 and here, which is its only use outside Matthew. Then epistērizontes tas psychas, “strengthening the souls”: the verb stands in three verses of the New Testament, this one and 15:32 and 15:41 (dictionary). “Souls”: KJV and ASV “confirming the souls,” NWT 1984 “strengthening the souls,” Douay “confirming the souls”; Geneva “confirming the disciples hearts,” NIV “strengthening the disciples,” and the TLB “helped the believers to grow in love for God and each other,” which adds what the Greek does not say. They urge the disciples emmenein tē pistei, “to remain in the faith.” The faith is the faith as a thing one can remain in or be obedient to, as at 6:7 (“a great crowd of the priests were obeying the faith”) and 13:8 (“away from the faith”).

“Through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God” (Acts 14:22). Dia pollōn thlipseōn dei hēmas eiselthein eis tēn basileian tou theou, and the first-person plural includes the man who has just walked back into Lystra after being stoned. Dei, “it is necessary,” is the word of 9:16, where the Lord tells Ananias how much Saul “must suffer for the sake of my name”: what was said of one man is now said of all. Thlipsis is printed “distress” in this translation where it is trouble in a narrative (7:10, 11:19), “affliction” at John 16:33, where Jesus says “in the world you have affliction; but take courage,” and “tribulation” where it is a standing expression (the “great tribulation” of Matthew 24:21 and Revelation 7:14); it is the last of these, a sentence the disciples are meant to carry, and so it is “tribulations” here. In Acts the noun stands in five verses: 7:10, 7:11, 11:19, this one and 20:23, where the Holy Spirit testifies in every city that “bonds and afflictions” await Paul. KJV “through much tribulation,” in the singular where the Greek has the plural pollōn thlipseōn; Douay, ASV, NWT 1984 and TLB “through many tribulations”; Geneva “through many afflictions”; NIV “many hardships.”

“Appointed elders” — cheirotonēsantes (Acts 14:23). Cheirotoneō is built on cheir, “hand,” and reference works report that the older Greek sense is to choose by a show of hands, a report this page does not verify (dictionary). In the New Testament the bare verb stands in two verses, here and 2 Corinthians 8:19 (not yet on these pages), where a brother is cheirotonētheis hypo tōn ekklēsiōn, “appointed by the congregations”; a compound stands in Acts 10:41, “chosen beforehand.” In 2 Corinthians the congregations do the choosing. Here the subject is the pair, and the dative autois, “for them,” says the elders are appointed on behalf of the disciples; the Greek does not say whether the disciples took part, and it does not mention the laying on of hands that 13:3 did. The apparatus gives the order kat’ ekklēsian presbyterous to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and presbyterous kat’ ekklēsian to Robinson–Pierpont. Kat’ ekklēsian, “in each congregation,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the Greek does not say whether “each congregation” means one in each city; compare Titus 1:5, where Paul has Titus appoint (katastēsēs, a different verb) elders kata polin, “in every town” (Titus 1:5). This is the first verse in Acts that speaks of appointing elders; elders were first named at 11:30, as those to whom the relief from Antioch is sent. The dictionary entry gives the word’s sense of an older man and an office. KJV “ordained them elders in every church,” Geneva “ordained them Elders by election in every Church,” which adds a vote, ASV “appointed for them elders in every church,” NIV “appointed elders for them in each church,” NWT 1984 “appointed older men for them in each congregation,” TLB “appointed elders in every church,” Douay “ordained to them priests in every church,” where the Latin’s presbyteros has become “priest.”

Prayer with fastings, and “entrusted them to the Lord” (Acts 14:23). Proseuxamenoi meta nēsteiōn, “having prayed with fastings,” is the pair that opened the sending at 13:3; the plural, “fastings,” is the Greek’s own. Parethento is from paratithēmi, “to place beside, to entrust,” the verb of Luke 23:46, where Jesus says “into your hands I entrust my spirit,” and of Paul’s farewell, “I entrust you to God” (Acts 20:32); it is a different verb from the paradidōmi of v26 (dictionary). The one they are entrusted to is tō kyriō eis hon pepisteukeisan, “the Lord, in whom they had believed”; the preposition eis after the verb for believing is “into” or “in,” and the pluperfect says the believing was already done. The Greek does not say whether the Lord is Jehovah or Jesus, and this translation keeps “Lord” as at v3; the NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah in whom they had become believers.” KJV, ASV, NIV and Douay keep “the Lord,” KJV “commended them to the Lord, on whom they believed” and NIV “committed them to the Lord, in whom they had put their trust”; the TLB has “turning them over to the care of the Lord in whom they trusted.”

Verses 24–28 · διελθόντες τὴν Πισιδίαν … λαλήσαντες ἐν Πέργῃ τὸν λόγον … Ἀττάλειαν … ἀπέπλευσαν εἰς Ἀντιόχειαν … παραδεδομένοι τῇ χάριτι τοῦ θεοῦ εἰς τὸ ἔργον ὃ ἐπλήρωσαν … ὅσα ἐποίησεν ὁ θεὸς μετʼ αὐτῶν … ἤνοιξεν τοῖς ἔθνεσιν θύραν πίστεως dielthontes tēn Pisidian … lalēsantes en Pergē ton logon … Attaleian … apepleusan eis Antiocheian … paradedomenoi tē chariti tou theou eis to ergon ho eplērōsan … hosa epoiēsen ho theos met’ autōn … ēnoixen tois ethnesin thyran pisteōs — Perga, Attalia and home, Acts 14:24–28: “the work they had fulfilled,” what God did “with them,” and the “door of faith” opened to the nations

Perga, and Attalia (Acts 14:24–25). The way back runs Pisidia, Pamphylia, Perga, Attalia. At Perga they now “speak the word,” lalēsantes en Pergē ton logon; on the way out Luke reports only that they came to Perga and that John left them (13:13), and he does not say why they spoke there now and not then. The TLB “preached again in Perga” says what Acts 13 does not, that they had preached there the first time; the Douay “the word of the Lord” adds the genitive. KJV “preached the word in Perga,” ASV “spoken the word in Perga,” NIV “preached the word in Perga,” NWT 1984 “after speaking the word in Perga.” Attalia is named here and nowhere else in the New Testament; Luke says only that they “went down” to it (katebēsan), and that from there they sailed. The apparatus marks the article before “Pamphylia” at v24: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have it and Robinson–Pierpont omit it.

“Commended to the grace of God” — and the work they had fulfilled (Acts 14:26). The ship goes apepleusan eis Antiocheian, “sailed away to Antioch,” in the verb with which the pair had “sailed away to Cyprus” in 13:4, so that Luke closes the voyage with the verb that opened it, and names the city they set out from. The Antioch is the Syrian one, hothen ēsan paradedomenoi tē chariti tou theou, “from where they had been commended to the grace of God” — the congregation of 13:1–3. Paradedomenoi is the participle of paradidōmi, and the construction “handed over to the grace of” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Acts 15:40, where Paul chooses Silas and leaves, “commended by the brothers to the grace of the Lord”; here the grace is “of God,” there “of the Lord” in the three editions that agree, while Robinson–Pierpont read “of God” there as here. KJV “recommended to the grace of God,” ASV “committed to the grace of God,” NIV “committed to the grace of God,” NWT 1984 “entrusted to the undeserved kindness of God,” Douay “delivered to the grace of God,” Geneva “commended unto the grace of God,” TLB “committed to God.” “The work” is the ergon of 13:2, “for the work to which I have called them,” which was left unnamed there, and the verb eplērōsan, “fulfilled,” is the verb of Barnabas and Saul “having fulfilled the service” at Jerusalem (12:25) and of John “fulfilling his course” (13:25). Paul will say of John Mark that he “had not gone with them to the work” (15:38). KJV “which they fulfilled,” ASV “which they had fulfilled,” Douay “which they accomplished,” NWT 1984 “they had fully performed,” NIV and TLB “now completed.”

“With them,” and a “door of faith” (Acts 14:27). They gather the congregation and anēngellon, “began to report,” hosa epoiēsen ho theos met’ autōn, “all that God had done with them” (Robinson–Pierpont read the aorist anēngeilan, “reported”). The preposition is meta, “with,” and it is the preposition of the report at 15:4, where the pair tell the Jerusalem congregation the same thing, hosa ho theos epoiēsen met’ autōn; at 15:12 the same account is given with di’ autōn, “through them,” and en tois ethnesin, “among the nations.” KJV and ASV “with them,” Douay “with them,” NIV “through them,” NWT 1984 “by means of them,” Geneva “by them,” and the TLB turns the whole clause into “reported on their trip.” What God did is that ēnoixen tois ethnesin thyran pisteōs, “he opened to the nations a door of faith.” The phrase thyran pisteōs, “door of faith,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the Greek has no article: the ASV and NIV keep “a door of faith,” the KJV, Geneva, Douay and TLB print “the door of faith,” and the NWT 1984 “the door to faith.” Doors opened elsewhere are Paul’s: “a great and effective door has opened to me” (1 Corinthians 16:9), a door “opened” in the Lord at Troas (2 Corinthians 2:12), a door of the word that Paul asks the Colossians to pray for (Colossians 4:3; none of the three yet on these pages), and the “open door” that the risen Lord sets before the congregation at Philadelphia (Revelation 3:8). Here God is the one who opens it, and the door is opened “to the nations,” the matter that chapter 15 begins on at once.

“Not a little time” (Acts 14:28). Diētribon de chronon ouk oligon sun tois mathētais, “and they spent not a little time with the disciples” — the verb is the diatribō of v3. Ouk oligos, “not a little,” is Luke’s understatement for a good deal, and it stands in eight verses of Acts: 12:18, here, 15:2, 17:4, 17:12, 19:23, 19:24 and 27:20 (the last not yet on these pages). KJV “abode long time,” ASV “tarried no little time,” Douay “abode no small time,” NWT 1984 “spent not a little time,” Geneva “abode a long time,” NIV “stayed there a long time,” TLB “stayed there with the believers at Antioch for a long while.” Luke gives no number of days or years. The apparatus has Robinson–Pierpont add ekei, “there,” after de.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “congregation” for ekklēsia at vv23 and 27, never “church”; “the nations” for ta ethnē at vv2, 5, 16 and 27, as at 13:46; “apostles” for apostoloi at vv4 and 14, left as the Greek has it; “afflicted” for kakoō at v2, as at 7:19 and 12:1, against “tribulations” for thlipsis at v22; “stirred up” for epegeirō at v2, as at 13:50; “boldly” for parrhēsiazomai at v3, as at 9:27, 9:28 and 13:46; “staring” for atenizō at v9, as at 13:9; “saved” for sōzō at v9, as at 4:9 and 15:1; “began to walk” for periepatei at v10, as at 3:8; “Zeus” and “Hermes” at v12, in the names Luke wrote; “the good news” for euangelizomai at vv7, 15 and 21, as at 8:4; “made disciples” for mathēteuō at v21, as at Matthew 28:19; “entrusted” for paratithēmi at v23, as at Luke 23:46, against “commended” for paradidōmi at v26, as at 15:40; “Lord” for kyrios at vv3 and 23, where the referent is open, as at 13:2.
