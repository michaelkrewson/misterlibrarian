📖 Romans

Πρὸς Ῥωμαίους (Pros Rhōmaious) — 'To the Romans.' Not a title the author gave it — the manuscripts simply label the letters by their recipients — but an unusually informative one, because the address is the whole problem the letter is solving: Paul is writing to a congregation in the capital of the empire that he did not found, has never visited, and needs.

The most consequential letter ever written, by almost any measure of consequence — the document behind Augustine's conversion, Luther's Reformation, Wesley's warmed heart and Barth's commentary; and, read plainly, a travelling missionary's careful letter of introduction to strangers whose backing he wanted for a journey to Spain.

Chapter by chapter

Romans 1The opening of the most consequential letter ever written. Paul is in Corinth, about fifty-six years old, with the eastern Mediterranean behind him and Spain in front of him — and he needs the backing of congregations in the capital that he has never met and did not found. So he writes them a letter of introduction, and it turns into the most sustained argument in the New Testament. The first seven verses are one enormous Greek sentence in which he calls himself a SLAVE before he calls himself an apostle; then a careful, rather charming approach to strangers, in which he offers to give them a spiritual gift and then catches himself and says he wants one back. And then, in two sentences, the thesis that reorganised Europe: “I am not ashamed of the gospel; for it is God’s power for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek. For in it God’s righteousness is revealed, from faith to faith” — quoting Habakkuk, the answer the prophet climbed his watchtower to wait for, and the line Augustine, Luther and Wesley each dated their turning to. The rest of the chapter is the indictment: humanity knew God, would not honour him, and traded his glory down through the creature-list of Genesis 1 in reverse — man, birds, beasts, things that crawl — after which God, three times, simply “gave them over.” ⚠ It contains the most argued-over paragraph in the Bible, printed here with the vocabulary laid out and the readings given their pedigrees, and no vote cast. ⚠ And it is a trap: the vice list is bait, and the next sentence after the chapter break springs it — “therefore you are without a defense, whoever you are who judges.”Romans 2The trap chapter 1 set springs on the first word: “therefore you are without a defense, whoever you are who judges — for in that in which you judge another, you condemn yourself.” God shows NO PARTIALITY, rendering to each according to works, “to the Jew first and also to the Greek” — the letter's own thesis phrase, now cutting toward judgment instead of toward the gospel's advance. ⚠ Then the sentence that would outlive the argument around it: Gentiles who never received the law can still do by nature what the law requires, “a law to themselves,” its work “written on their hearts” — the New Testament's closest approach to a doctrine of conscience and natural law, quoted for centuries by readers who never opened the rest of the letter. Four rhetorical blows land on the confident teacher who cannot teach himself — steal, adultery, idols, temple robbery — and “the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you, just as it is written.” It closes on the hardest sentence Paul's own closing note to chapter 1 named in advance: the real Jew is one inwardly, and circumcision is of the heart, “by spirit, not by letter.”Romans 3“What, then, is the advantage of the Jew?” Much, Paul answers — first, they were entrusted with the oracles of God — and the chapter turns at once on one root heard four times in two verses: entrusted, unfaithful, unfaithfulness, faithfulness. ⚠ Then comes a chain of quotations from the Psalms and Isaiah that closes the case against everyone (“there is no righteous one, not even one”), strung from the throat to the feet to the eyes, until every mouth is stopped. And the pivot, “But now”: God's righteousness apart from law, through faith in Jesus Christ — or the faith of Jesus Christ, since the Greek genitive allows both — for all who believe, for there is no distinction. ⚠ Then the word nobody can settle: God put him forward as a hilastērion, a “place of atonement,” a propitiation or a mercy seat, “to demonstrate his righteousness” because of the passing over of former sins. Boasting is shut out by a “law of faith,” “God is one” is the premise, and the chapter ends where it began, on the verb abolish: “Do we then abolish law through the faith? May it never be! No, we establish law.”Romans 4“What, then, shall we say that Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh, has found?” Chapter 3 ended on “we establish law,” and chapter 4 calls the witness the Law itself names first. ⚠ Genesis 15:6 — “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” — is the sentence the chapter hangs on, and its ledger verb, “reckon,” stands in eleven of the chapter’s twenty-five verses; the Hebrew verb behind it stands in only three verses of the Bible, and in the other two a man forms an opinion of a woman from what he sees. Paul adds a second witness, David in Psalm 32, and then asks the question that fixes the order: was it reckoned before circumcision or after? Genesis answers by its own ages: at least fourteen years before. ⚠ The sign becomes a “seal,” the promise becomes “heir of the world,” and the law “works out wrath” where there was no line yet to cross. At verse 19 the manuscripts hand us two opposite Abrahams, one who “considered” his body, as good as dead, and one who “considered not.” The chapter ends where it began, with the sentence reckoned “for our sake also,” and Jesus “handed over because of our trespasses and raised because of our justification.”Romans 5“Having been justified, then, out of faith, we have peace with God” — or “let us have,” since one vowel in the manuscripts turns the statement into an exhortation. ⚠ The first half is a chain: we boast in tribulation, because tribulation works out endurance, endurance proven character, proven character hope, and “hope does not put to shame, because the love of God has been poured out in our hearts.” Then the argument from the harder thing to the easier, enemies reconciled by his death and so “much more” saved in his life, and a verse (verse 6) that says “still” twice. ⚠ Verse 12 starts a comparison, “just as through one man sin entered into the world,” and breaks off at a dash; the “so also” comes six verses later. Its last clause, eph’ hō, was read “in whom” in the Latin and can be read “because” in the Greek, and the verses after it say that sin is not entered to an account where there is no law, and yet death reigned from Adam to Moses. Adam is “a type of the one who was to come,” and the chapter turns on the contrast: four Greek words for grace and gift in three verses, a gift “not like the trespass,” and where sin increased, grace abounded far more, “so that, just as sin reigned in death, so also grace might reign through righteousness to eternal life.”Romans 6“Shall we remain in sin, so that grace may increase?” The objection is built from the last verse of chapter 5, in the very verb of “where sin increased,” and Paul answers it with a fact before an order: “we who died to sin,” baptized “into his death,” “buried with him,” “grown together” with the likeness of his death. ⚠ Verse 5 turns on a word that stands in the New Testament only here (symphytoi, “grown together,” not “planted”), verse 6 on the “old man” crucified with him and the “body of sin” abolished, and verse 7 says that a dead person “has been justified from sin.” Then a command to the readers, “reckon yourselves dead to sin and alive to God,” the ledger verb that credited Abraham turned into an order, and sin told not to reign in “your mortal body.” ⚠ The second half answers a second objection, “Shall we sin because we are not under law but under grace?”, with slavery: you are slaves of the one you obey; you were “handed over” to a pattern of teaching; “free in regard to righteousness” was the only freedom the old service gave; and the chapter ends on a soldier’s pay, “the wages of sin is death, but the gracious gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord.”Romans 7“Or do you not know, brothers … that the law is lord over a man for as long as he lives?” Chapter 6 ended with the law standing at its edge (“you are not under law but under grace”), and chapter 7 takes it up with a picture from marriage law: a woman is bound to her husband while he lives and released from the law of the husband when he dies, and so, Paul says, “you also were put to death to the law through the body of Christ.” ⚠ Then the objection the whole letter has been inviting, “Is the law sin?”, answered with “May it never be!” and a speaker who says “I did not know sin except through law,” who knows coveting only because the commandment said “You shall not covet.” Sin takes “an opportunity through the commandment,” comes to life again, deceives and kills: six verbs with sin for their subject, in an order that sounds like Genesis 2–3. ⚠ From verse 14 the speaker turns to the present tense and to the most argued-over “I” in the New Testament: “I am made of flesh, sold under sin,” “what I do not wish, this I do,” “no longer I, but the sin that dwells in me,” and a different law in his members warring against the law of his mind. The chapter ends on a cry and a thanksgiving, “Wretched man that I am! Who will rescue me from the body of this death? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!”, and on one last sentence that rejoins the divided “I myself.”Romans 8The chapter is built on the preposition WITH. Nine syn- compounds run from v16 to v29 — the Spirit witnesses WITH our spirit, we are heirs TOGETHER, we suffer WITH and are glorified TOGETHER, the creation groans TOGETHER and travails TOGETHER, the Spirit takes hold TOGETHER WITH us, all things work TOGETHER, and we are formed WITH the image of the Son. ⚠ Verse 28 is the EIGHTH OF THE NINE, so the most-quoted promise in the chapter is one beat in a pattern rather than a standalone guarantee: the claim is not that events are secretly convenient but that nothing here happens alone. Two cruxes, neither voted on — the clause the KJV and RV carry at v1 and the earliest witnesses lack (it appears to have migrated up from v4, and it turns freedom from condemnation into a condition), and whether GOD is the subject at v28, where NWT and TNM take the longer reading and KJV, ASV and RV take the shorter. Plus proorizo, the most disputed word in the chapter: it names a marked-out DESTINATION and leaves the Calvinist-Arminian question to inference in both directions. And Abba — Aramaic left untranslated, then translated, exactly as at Galatians 4:6 and Mark 14:36.

About the book

Where it sits
The FIRST of Paul's letters in every Bible — placed first not because it was written first (it was not) but because the collection is ordered by LENGTH, longest to shortest, which is how ancient letter collections were arranged. It stands at the head of the New Testament's second half, immediately after Acts, which is the book that tells you who Paul is.
Genre
A LETTER, and the most sustained piece of argument in the New Testament — but a real letter, dictated aloud to a secretary who signs his own name at the end ('I, Tertius, who wrote this letter, greet you' — 16:22), carried by a named woman, and ending with twenty-six personal greetings. It uses the DIATRIBE, the classroom style of Greco-Roman moral teaching in which the speaker argues with an imaginary objector: 'What shall we say then?' 'By no means!' Reading Romans as a treatise loses the voices.
Author
PAUL, uncontested — one of the seven letters accepted as his by essentially all scholarship. He dictated it: Tertius, the scribe, breaks in at 16:22 to greet the readers in his own voice, one of the most human moments in the New Testament and a reminder that these documents were spoken before they were read.
Date written
Around AD 56–57, and datable with unusual confidence for an ancient document. Paul is about to carry the collection to Jerusalem (15:25), has finished his work in the eastern Mediterranean, and is planning Spain by way of Rome (15:24). The place fits Corinth in the winter of the third missionary journey — Gaius his host (16:23) and Erastus the city treasurer (16:23) both belong there, and a first-century Corinthian pavement naming an aedile Erastus has been found.
Place
Almost certainly CORINTH, or its port Cenchreae — Phoebe, who carries the letter, is a deacon of the congregation at Cenchreae (16:1).
Audience
The congregations in ROME — several house-groups rather than one assembly, and mixed. The mix is the letter's live problem: the emperor Claudius had expelled Jews from Rome around AD 49 (Suetonius says it was over disturbances 'at the instigation of Chrestus'), leaving the Roman congregations Gentile; after Claudius died in 54 the exiles came back, to churches that had learned to run without them. Chapters 14–15, on the strong and the weak, are addressed to exactly that room.

How it's laid out

1:1–17Salutation, thanksgiving, and the thesis: the gospel is God's power for salvation, and in it God's righteousness is revealed 'from faith to faith.'
1:18–3:20The indictment — the nations, then the religious, then everyone: 'there is no one righteous, not even one.'
3:21–5:21The answer — righteousness through faith, Abraham as the proof, peace with God, and the two Adams.
6:1–8:39The new life — dead to sin, freed from law, led by the Spirit; the groaning creation, and 'nothing can separate us.'
9:1–11:36Israel — the letter's most anguished section, and the reason 'to the Jew first' had to be said in chapter 1.
12:1–15:13The life that follows — bodies as a living sacrifice, the body and its gifts, governing authorities, and the strong bearing with the weak.
15:14–16:27Travel plans (Jerusalem, Rome, Spain), and a closing chapter of greetings that is a census of the early church.

What it's about

  • The RIGHTEOUSNESS OF GOD — the letter's key phrase, and the one whose grammar has been argued over for five centuries: God's own covenant faithfulness, or a righteousness he gives, or both.
  • FAITH, and what it is that faith does — announced from Habakkuk 2:4 in chapter 1 and never left alone thereafter.
  • JEW AND GENTILE in one people — not an appendix to the argument but its engine; 'to the Jew first and also to the Greek' is stated in chapter 1 and defended for three chapters in the middle.
  • SIN as a power, not merely acts — something people are 'under', which is why the letter's answer is a rescue rather than an improvement.
  • The SPIRIT and the new humanity — chapter 8, the summit of the letter and arguably of Paul.
  • GOD'S FAITHFULNESS to his promises, which is what makes Israel's unbelief an emergency rather than a footnote.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. Romans is textually well attested — P46, from about AD 200, carries most of it — but it has one famous oddity: the ENDING. The closing doxology (16:25-27) floats, appearing after chapter 14 in some manuscripts, after 15 in others, after 16 in others, in two places in one, and not at all in a few; and a handful of witnesses lack 'in Rome' at 1:7 and 1:15. The usual explanation is that a shortened, de-localised edition circulated early for general use. Chapter 1 itself is textually calm apart from two Byzantine expansions the KJV prints: 'the gospel OF CHRIST' at 1:16 and 'fornication' in the vice list at 1:29.

Looking forward

Romans says less about Jesus' life than any comparable New Testament book and more about what his death and resurrection accomplished. He is introduced in the first sentence by a formula that may be older than the letter — descended from David according to the flesh, appointed Son of God in power by resurrection — and thereafter he is the place where God's righteousness is demonstrated (3:21-26), the second Adam undoing the first (5:12-21), the one whose death and rising the believer is baptised into (6:1-11), and the one at God's right hand from whose love nothing can separate (8:34-39). Chapter 1 supplies only the frame: a gospel God had already promised through his prophets, about a son who is both David's descendant and the Lord.

Where the debates are

The MEANING of dikaiosynē theou — God's own righteousness, or a status he confers — which is the hinge of the Reformation and of the 'New Perspective' arguments that reopened it. Whether pistis Christou at 3:22 is faith IN Christ or the faithfulness OF Christ. What chapters 9-11 conclude about Israel, and whether 'all Israel will be saved' means what it appears to. Whether chapter 7's 'I' is Paul, Adam, Israel, or a rhetorical figure. Whether chapter 16 was originally part of this letter or a note to Ephesus. And how much of the letter is timeless theology and how much is aimed at one specific quarrel between Jewish and Gentile believers in one city in one decade. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
