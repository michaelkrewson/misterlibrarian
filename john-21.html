The risen Jesus appears a third time to the disciples, now in Galilee. Seven of them — Peter, Thomas, Nathanael, the sons of Zebedee and two others — fish all night on the Sea of Tiberias and catch nothing; at dawn a man on the beach tells them to cast the net on the right side, and it fills with a hundred and fifty-three large fish without tearing. The disciple whom Jesus loved says 'It is the Lord!' and Peter jumps into the sea. On the beach Jesus has a charcoal fire laid with fish and bread, and after breakfast asks Peter three times 'do you love me?', charging him to feed his lambs, shepherd his sheep and feed his sheep; he foretells the death by which Peter will glorify God and says 'Follow me.' Peter asks about the disciple whom Jesus loved and is told 'what is that to you?'; the Gospel ends by correcting a rumor that that disciple would not die, naming him as its witness, and saying that the world could not hold the books if everything Jesus did were written.
c. AD 30, some time after the two Jerusalem appearances of chapter 20 — a dawn on the Sea of Tiberias in Galilee; the Gospel gives no date.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–3 · ⚠ the Sea of Tiberias, seven disciples of whom five are named, and a night that catches nothing — with a verb this Gospel has used for arrests

‘Tiberias’ stands in only three verses of the New Testament, and all three are in this Gospel: 6:1, 6:23 and here. At 6:1 (already on these pages) the lake is glossed ‘the Sea of Galilee, that is, of Tiberias’; here the gloss is gone and the lake is simply the Sea of Tiberias, named for the city on its western shore (see the entries for the lake and the city). The women at the tomb had been told that Jesus was going ahead of the disciples into Galilee (Matthew 28:7; Mark 16:7, already on these pages); this Gospel does not say how the disciples came to be there, and does not connect the scene to that message.

⚠ ‘Revealed himself’ (ephanerōsen, v1, said twice, and again at v14) is the verb of the Gospel’s earliest announcements: John the Baptist came baptizing ‘so that he might be revealed to Israel’ (1:31), and at Cana Jesus ‘revealed his glory’ (2:11, both already on these pages). It stands nine times in eight verses of this Gospel, three of them in this chapter. The shelf prints it three ways: ASV and NWT 1984 ‘manifested himself,’ KJV DRA and GNV ‘shewed’ or ‘showed himself,’ and NIV and TLB ‘appeared.’ This translation keeps ‘revealed,’ as at 2:11.

Seven are together, five of them named — and the sons of Zebedee are named nowhere else in this Gospel. Peter, Thomas, Nathanael, ‘the sons of Zebedee’ and two unnamed disciples make seven. The sons of Zebedee are the fishermen of Mark’s and Luke’s call stories (Mark 1:19–20; Luke 5:10, already on these pages), but this is the only verse in John that mentions them (see the encyclopedia entry). Thomas is called Didymus for the third and last time (11:16; 20:24); Nathanael, who appears in this Gospel only in chapter 1 (1:45–49), is here given a home town, Cana of Galilee. TLB, which tells the chapter in the first person, prints ‘my brother James and I’ where the Greek has ‘the sons of Zebedee.’ The disciple whom Jesus loved is not on the list but is on the lake by v7, so he is one of the sons of Zebedee or one of the two unnamed; the text does not say which (see the encyclopedia entry).

⚠ The verb behind ‘caught’ (piazō, vv3 and 10) occurs in eight verses of this Gospel, and in the other six it is the verb for seizing Jesus. The authorities ‘sought to seize him’ (7:30), sent officers ‘to seize him’ (7:32), some ‘wished to seize him’ (7:44), ‘no one seized him’ (8:20), they ‘sought again to seize him’ (10:39) and ordered that anyone who knew where he was should report it, ‘so that they might seize him’ (11:57) — all already on these pages. The Gospel does not remark on it; the same verb simply goes back to fishing.

Luke tells a story with the same ingredients at the start of Jesus’s ministry. There too Simon and the sons of Zebedee are on the lake, they have worked the whole night and caught nothing (Luke 5:5), and at Jesus’s word they let down the nets and haul in so many fish that the nets ‘began to tear’ (5:6); the net of v11 below is not torn, and Luke’s ‘from now on you will be catching people’ (5:10) has no counterpart here. Readers have taken the two accounts as one event placed at different points in the story, or as two events; this translation prints each and does not choose. And this Gospel gives no reason why Peter goes fishing.

Verses 4–6 · ⚠ a recognition that fails again, ‘children,’ a word for something to eat with bread, and a catch too heavy to haul

‘The disciples did not know that it was Jesus’ (v4) is the clause the Gospel used of Mary Magdalene at the tomb — ouk ēdei hoti Iēsous estin in 20:14 (already on these pages) — now in the plural: ou mentoi ēdeisan hoi mathētai hoti Iēsous estin. In both scenes he is standing there and in both the recognition comes afterward, there at the sound of her name (20:16), here at the catch (v7).

⚠ ‘Children’ (paidia, v5) is a form of address Jesus uses nowhere else in the four Gospels. At the supper he had called the disciples teknia, ‘little children,’ the only time that word stands in this Gospel (13:33, already on these pages); the other Gospels use paidia only of actual children (Mark 10:14; Luke 18:16, already on these pages). The shelf: ASV KJV and DRA print ‘Children,’ NWT 1984 ‘Young children’ (its 2013 revision, NWT 2013, ‘Children’), NIV ‘Friends,’ TLB ‘boys,’ and GNV ‘Sirs.’

Prosphagion, which this translation prints ‘anything to eat,’ occurs only here in the New Testament. It is built on the verb ‘to eat’ with a prefix meaning ‘in addition,’ and is usually taken to mean what is eaten with bread — in practice, fish — but the Greek does not say ‘fish.’ NIV (‘haven’t you any fish?’) and TLB (‘Any fish, boys?’) supply the word; KJV and DRA print ‘any meat,’ where ‘meat’ means food. The question is the kind that expects the answer no (mē ti), and the answer is a bare ‘No.’

‘And you will find’ has no object in the Greek; ‘some’ is supplied. ASV and KJV print the bare ‘ye shall find’; NWT 1984 brackets the supplied word (‘YOU will find [some]’), and NWT 2013 and NIV print it plainly (‘you will find some’). The Greek says the disciples ‘were no longer strong enough’ (ouketi ischyon) to haul the net in; ASV and KJV print ‘not able to draw it.’

Verses 7–8 · ⚠ the disciple who recognizes, the man who plunges in, and an outer garment put on by someone ‘naked’

The two men keep the roles they had at the tomb: the disciple whom Jesus loved is the first to understand, Peter the first to act. At 20:4–8 (already on these pages) the other disciple outruns Peter and is the first to ‘see and believe,’ while Peter is the first to go in; here the beloved disciple says ‘It is the Lord!’ and Peter is the first into the water (v7) and the first to haul the net (v11).

⚠ ‘Wrapped around himself’ (diezōsato) is the verb of the foot-washing, where Jesus ‘wrapped’ a towel around himself (13:4, with 13:5, already on these pages) — and the same root returns in the prophecy of vv18–19, below. The ‘outer garment’ (ependytēs) is a word found only here in the New Testament. Gymnos, ‘naked,’ can mean wearing only the inner garment, and the shelf is split on how to say it: ASV KJV GNV DRA and NWT 1984 print ‘naked,’ NIV ‘(for he had taken it off),’ and TLB ‘(for he was stripped to the waist).’ This translation prints ‘naked’ and lets the reader take it; the verse says only that he wrapped the garment on because he was.

‘Two hundred cubits’ (v8) is about three hundred feet, ninety metres. A cubit is about eighteen inches. ASV KJV GNV and DRA keep ‘two hundred cubits’; NWT 1984 converts to ‘three hundred feet,’ TLB to ‘300 feet,’ and NIV to ‘a hundred yards.’ The ‘little boat’ (ploiarion) is a diminutive that this Gospel uses at 6:22–24 (already on these pages) and here, and nowhere else in the Gospels except Mark 3:9.

Verses 9–14 · ⚠ the second charcoal fire, a fish on it, 153 fish in the net, a net that does not tear, and a breakfast nobody asks about

⚠ The word for ‘charcoal fire’ (anthrakia, v9) stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and at 18:18, where Peter stood warming himself at the fire the slaves and officers had made — the fire beside which he denied Jesus the second time (18:25). This one is already laid when the disciples reach the land, with fish on it and bread, before anyone brings anything (v10 asks for their fish in addition); the text does not say who laid it. ASV and KJV print ‘a fire of coals,’ GNV and DRA ‘hot coals,’ NIV ‘a fire of burning coals,’ and NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 ‘a charcoal fire.’ The ‘fish’ on it is opsarion, in the singular, which in the New Testament is a word only John uses (6:9, 6:11 and vv9, 10 and 13 here); the ordinary word for fish, ichthys, stands in this Gospel only in the catch itself (vv6, 8, 11).

⚠ The count is given and not explained: ‘a hundred and fifty-three’ (v11). Readers have proposed meanings since antiquity — that 153 is a triangular number (the sum of the whole numbers from 1 to 17), or, in a reading reported from Jerome and not verified here, the number of kinds of fish — but nothing in the text says so, and this translation prints the number and does not choose. And the net ‘was not torn’ (ouk eschisthē): the only other verse in this Gospel with this verb is the soldiers’ ‘Let’s not tear it’ of the seamless tunic (19:24, already on these pages). The Gospel’s word for the divisions among the crowd is the noun built on it, schisma (7:43; 9:16; 10:19). The Gospel does not draw the connection. NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 print ‘the net did not burst’; ASV ‘the net was not rent’; KJV ‘yet was not the net broken’; NIV ‘the net was not torn.’ TLB adds what the Greek does not have: ‘By his count there were 153 large fish.’

⚠ ‘Who are you?’ is the question put to John the Baptist by the priests and Levites (1:19) and to Jesus by those he was speaking with (8:25, both already on these pages) — and here none of the disciples dares to ask it, because they know. The verb for ‘question’ (exetazō) stands in the New Testament only here, at Matthew 2:8 and at 10:11, where it means to search out. ‘Have breakfast’ (aristaō, vv12 and 15) is a verb found elsewhere only at Luke 11:37; ASV prints ‘break your fast,’ NIV and NWT 1984 ‘breakfast,’ and KJV GNV and DRA ‘dine.’

In v13 Jesus ‘takes the bread and gives it to them, and the fish likewise’ — the ‘took,’ the giving and the ‘likewise’ of the feeding at the Sea of Galilee, where he ‘took the loaves’ and distributed them (the compound of the verb for ‘give’), ‘so also of the fish’ (6:11, already on these pages). No thanksgiving is narrated here, and the text does not say that Jesus himself eats (compare Luke 24:42–43, already on these pages, where he does).

‘The third time’ (v14) counts appearances ‘to the disciples’ as a body — the evening of the first day (20:19), the eighth day (20:26), and this one; Mary Magdalene’s meeting at the tomb (20:14–18) is not counted in it.

Verses 15–17 · ⚠ three questions after three denials, two Greek verbs for ‘love,’ ‘more than these,’ and a charge to feed, shepherd and feed

⚠ Three questions answer three denials, beside the only other charcoal fire in the New Testament. Peter denied three times (18:17, 25, 27), exactly as Jesus had said he would (13:38, already on these pages); here he is asked three times. The Gospel does not mention the denials in this scene, and does not say the asking is meant to answer them; the reader’s link is the number and the fire. Jesus uses the old address ‘Simon, son of John’ (1:42, already on these pages) and not the name ‘Peter.’ The printed Greek marks a manuscript variant at all three; KJV prints ‘son of Jonas’ and GNV ‘son of Jonah’ (compare ‘Bar-Jonah’ at Matthew 16:17), while ASV DRA NIV and NWT 1984 print ‘John.’ This translation prints ‘John,’ as at 1:42.

⚠ Two Greek verbs are in play. Jesus asks agapas me (vv15, 16), Peter answers philō se (vv15, 16, 17), and in v17 Jesus asks with Peter’s own verb, phileis me. Peter never answers with agapaō. Many readers hear a falling scale — a higher love asked for, a lower one offered — and the shelf is split: NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 print ‘love’ for Jesus’s first two questions and ‘affection’ for Peter’s answers and Jesus’s third; TLB prints ‘love’ and then ‘you know I am your friend,’ with ‘are you even my friend?’ at v17; while ASV KJV GNV DRA and NIV print ‘love’ all the way through. This Gospel’s own usage argues against a fixed difference: it uses both verbs of the same relationships — the Father and the Son (agapa at 3:35, philei at 5:20), Jesus and Lazarus (phileis at 11:3, ēgapa at 11:5, ephilei at 11:36), and the disciple whom Jesus loved (ēgapa at 13:23, 19:26, v7 and v20, ephilei at 20:2) — so this translation prints ‘love’ for both, as at those verses, and the Greek line above each verse shows the verbs (see the entry on agapē and the entry on phileō). And the narrator gives the cause of Peter’s grief in v17 as the asking — ‘that he said to him the third time’ — not the change of verb.

‘More than these’ (v15) can mean three things, and the Greek (pleon toutōn) does not choose. ‘These’ can be the other disciples (do you love me more than they do?), the same men (do you love me more than you love them?), or the boats and nets and the catch (more than this life?). ASV KJV GNV DRA NIV and NWT 1984 all print ‘more than these’ and leave it open; TLB alone narrows it to people (‘more than these others’). This translation prints the Greek.

‘You know all things’ (v17) is the disciples’ own sentence from the supper — oidas panta in 16:30, which Jesus answered by telling them they would be scattered and leave him alone (16:32, both already on these pages). Peter, now grieved, says it again, and adds a second verb for knowing, ginōskeis (‘you know all things; you know that I love you’), which this translation prints as ‘know’ both times.

⚠ Three charges, in three different shapes: feed my lambs (boske ta arnia), shepherd my sheep (poimaine ta probata), feed my sheep. The verbs are boskō and poimainō, and the diminutive ‘little lamb’ (arnion) stands in the New Testament outside Revelation only here. The shelf varies at every step: KJV and GNV print ‘Feed’ three times (‘lambs,’ ‘sheep,’ ‘sheep’); DRA prints ‘lambs’ at both v15 and v16 and ‘sheep’ at v17; ASV ‘Tend my sheep’ at v16; NIV ‘Take care of my sheep’; and NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 ‘Shepherd my little sheep’ and ‘Feed my little sheep,’ as TLB does at v17. The Greek printed here has the plain probata in v16 and v17, and marks a manuscript variant at the word in v17. The charge to shepherd stands between Peter’s vow to lay down his life (13:37) and the prophecy of his death (vv18–19, below); Jesus is the shepherd who ‘lays down his life for the sheep’ (10:11, already on these pages), and the Gospel does not say that the three are connected.

Verses 18–19 · ⚠ belts, outstretched hands, a death announced in the words used of Jesus’s own, and ‘Follow me’

‘Fasten your own belt’ (ezōnnyes, v18) is the same root as the ‘wrapped’ of v7 and of the foot-washing (13:4–5): it stands in four verses of this Gospel, and in this one the wrapping is done to Peter, not by him. The shelf: ASV KJV GNV and DRA print ‘gird’; NWT 1984 keeps ‘gird yourself’ where its own 2013 revision, NWT 2013, prints ‘clothe yourself’; NIV prints ‘dressed yourself’; and TLB drops the image (‘others will direct you’). This translation prints the belt, which is what the root names.

⚠ ‘To signify by what kind of death’ (v19) is the identical phrase the narrator used twice of Jesus’s own death — sēmainōn poiō thanatō in 12:33 and 18:32 (already on these pages) — here with a different end: not ‘he was going to die’ but ‘he would glorify God.’ ‘Stretch out your hands’ does not say how; early Christian writers read it of crucifixion (reported from Tertullian and from Origen as quoted by Eusebius, neither verified here, and in any case outside the New Testament), and this Gospel says only that it is a death by which God is glorified.

‘Follow me’ is what Jesus said to Philip at 1:43 (already on these pages), and the ‘later’ he promised Peter at the supper seems to have arrived. There Peter was told ‘you cannot follow me now, but you will follow later,’ and answered ‘I will lay down my life for you’ (13:36–37, already on these pages). The Greek is a present imperative: NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 print ‘Continue following me’ (here and at v22); the others print ‘Follow me.’

Verses 20–23 · ⚠ ‘What about this man?’, a saying that went out among the brothers, and a Gospel that corrects a rumor about its own witness

The disciple whom Jesus loved is already following, though no one has told him to; and he is identified by the supper — with the question put in slightly fuller words than the supper had it. At 13:25 (already on these pages) he leaned back and asked, ‘Lord, who is it?’; here the narrator quotes him as asking ‘Lord, who is the one betraying you?’ This translation prints what each Greek line says, as a summary and not a quotation to be matched.

‘And what about this man?’ (v21) is, in the Greek, four words: houtos de ti, ‘and this one, what?’ TLB alone adds a clause the Greek does not have: ‘What about him, Lord? What sort of death will he die?’ Jesus’s answer turns on menein, ‘to remain’: DRA NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 print ‘remain,’ ASV KJV and GNV ‘tarry,’ and NIV narrows it to ‘remain alive’ and TLB to ‘live,’ which the Greek does not say. What ‘until I come’ refers to is as open as the Greek leaves it, and this translation does not choose. And the last word to Peter is emphatic, sy moi akolouthei: ASV KJV DRA and GNV ‘follow thou me,’ NIV ‘You must follow me.’

⚠ The saying that ‘went out among the brothers’ (v23) is quoted, and then corrected, inside the Gospel itself. ‘Brothers’ is the word Jesus used at 20:17 (already on these pages) and here means the believers; the narrator reports the rumor that the disciple ‘would not die,’ says plainly that Jesus did not say so, and repeats Jesus’s own words, ‘If I want him to remain until I come, what is that to you?’, without adding to them. The text does not say whether the disciple had died when this was written; it only refuses the rumor.

Verses 24–25 · ⚠ ‘we know that his testimony is true,’ the Gospel’s last claim about itself, and an ‘I suppose’ found only here

‘This is the disciple’ points back to the one of vv20–23, the one Jesus loved — and he is called both the one who testifies (a present participle, ho martyrōn) and the one who wrote (an aorist, ho grapsas). The Gospel has already vouched for a witness once: at the pierced side, ‘the one who saw it has testified, and his testimony is true, and that man knows that he is telling the truth’ (19:35, already on these pages, where the guarantee is the witness’s own knowledge); here the guarantee is a plural voice, ‘we know.’ The word for ‘true’ changes as well, from alēthinē at 19:35 to alēthēs here, and this translation prints ‘true’ for both. The Greek does not name the disciple and does not say who ‘we’ are. TLB makes it first-person: ‘I am that disciple! I saw these events and have recorded them here’ — which the Greek does not say; the same version has the narrator speak as ‘I’ from v7 (‘Then I said to Peter’). See the encyclopedia entry for who he has been taken to be.

⚠ The last verse is a second ending, in the first person singular: ‘I suppose’ (oimai) occurs only here in the New Testament. It echoes the first ending — ‘Jesus did many other signs too… which are not written in this book’ (20:30, already on these pages) — now widened from ‘signs’ to ‘things,’ and heightened: ‘if they were written one by one’ (kath’ hen), not even the world itself would have room (chōrēsein, from the verb of the six stone jars that ‘held’ two or three measures each at 2:6) for the books. The plural ‘books’ (biblia) is the plural of the ‘book’ of 20:30, and is the last word of the Gospel in the printed Greek. The shelf: ASV KJV GNV DRA NIV and TLB print ‘books’; NWT 1984 prints ‘scrolls’; ASV KJV GNV NIV TLB and NWT 1984 print ‘I suppose’ where DRA prints ‘I think.’ KJV and GNV end with ‘Amen’; the printed Greek, ASV DRA NIV and NWT 1984 do not.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Phaneroō is ‘revealed’ (vv1, 14), as at 2:11. Agapaō and phileō are both ‘love’ (vv15–17), with the Greek verbs shown in the note rather than imposed in English, as at 3:35 and 5:20. Paidia is ‘children’ (v5), prosphagion is ‘anything to eat’ and aristaō is ‘have breakfast.’ Diezōsato is ‘wrapped’ (v7), as diezōsen was at 13:4, and ezōnnyes is ‘fasten your own belt’ (v18). Ependytēs is ‘outer garment,’ gymnos is ‘naked,’ and anthrakia is ‘charcoal fire,’ as at 18:18. Kyrios is ‘the Lord’ or ‘Lord’ wherever the speaker means Jesus (vv7, 12, 15–17, 20, 21). Biblia is ‘books,’ as biblion was ‘book’ at 20:30.

Echoes paid. The charcoal fire of 18:18 returns at v9, as the notes on the last three chapters said it would; Peter’s three denials (18:17, 25, 27) meet three questions at vv15–17; ‘you will follow later’ (13:36) and Peter’s vow to lay down his life (13:37) meet the prophecy and the command of vv18–19; the sentence ‘by what kind of death’ (12:33; 18:32) is said a third time; the beloved disciple of 13:23–25 is named again at v20; the eyewitness of 19:35 is vouched for again at v24; and the ‘many other signs’ of 20:30 become ‘many other things’ at v25. The ‘revealed’ of 1:31 and 2:11 (v1), the bread-and-fish of 6:11 (vv9, 13), the disciples’ ‘you know all things’ of 16:30 (v17), the first ‘Follow me’ of 1:43 (v19) and Thomas’s last appearance (v2; 11:16; 20:24) all come around once more (all already on these pages).

Echoes planted. This is the last chapter of the Gospel, so nothing here is left to be paid inside it. Two threads run out of it into books already on these pages: Peter, one of the two figures of vv20–23, goes up to the temple with John at Acts 3:1 — tradition ties that John to the other figure, though the Gospel does not (see the encyclopedia entry); and the ‘brothers’ among whom the rumor of v23 went out are the believers whom Acts goes on to describe.

This chapter completes the Gospel of John: the book’s own sequence runs unbroken from chapter 1 through this chapter, and the book ends here — its sequel, Acts, already stands on these pages through chapter 4, where Peter and John appear before the Sanhedrin and the gathered community prays for the first time.
