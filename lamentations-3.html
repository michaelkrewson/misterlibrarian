The longest and most personal chapter of the book, a triple acrostic — every Hebrew letter gets three verses instead of one — opening on a single man's own affliction ('I am the man who has seen affliction') before the book's one turn to hope at its structural center: 'his mercies are new every morning; great is your faithfulness' — then back to lament.
Same setting as chapters 1 and 2 — Jerusalem in the immediate aftermath of the 586 BC fall — but the voice shifts from the city's own lament to a single man's, traditionally read as Jeremiah's. Undated within the chapter itself, like the rest of the book; its triple-acrostic form, doubling the book's usual one-verse-per-letter pattern, marks it as the book's structural and emotional center regardless of exactly when it was composed.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–6 · the man, and a chapter unlike any other in this book

⚠ Twenty-two letters, three verses each — the most disciplined poem in a book already built entirely on discipline. Chapters 1, 2, and 4 are each single acrostics, twenty-two verses, one Hebrew letter opening each. Chapter 3 TRIPLES the constraint: every one of the twenty-two letters opens three consecutive verses in a row, sixty-six lines in all, and the letter marker printed on every verse of this translation shows the pattern directly — watch it repeat three times before moving to the next letter. Chapter 5 keeps the twenty-two-verse COUNT without the acrostic itself, as if the form is finally too exhausted to hold. Structurally, this chapter sits at the book's dead center and asks the most of its own form — whatever else it is doing, the poet has chosen to work hardest, formally, at exactly the point of deepest personal anguish.

“I am the man.” Every other chapter of Lamentations speaks corporately — the city, personified as a grieving woman; the poet describing what has happened to “her.” Chapter 3 drops that frame entirely and opens on a single, unnamed first-person voice: gever, not the ordinary word for “man” (ish) but one that carries a note of strength or standing — a MAN, specifically, reduced to affliction. Jewish and Christian tradition alike have long read this “I” as Jeremiah himself, the traditional author of the whole book, though the text never names him; others read a more universal, representative sufferer, an Everyman standing in for the whole besieged city. Both readings coexist in this project without a vote — the anonymity may be the point, letting every later reader step into the same first-person voice.

Verses 7–18 · walled in, hunted, and a strength that runs out

The catalogue of affliction that opens this chapter builds without a single verse of relief for eighteen straight lines — walled in with no way out (v7), prayer itself shut out (v8), a bear and a lion lying in wait (v10), an archer's bow bent and the speaker set up as the target (v12), arrows that reach the kidneys (v13, the seat of the deepest inward feeling in Hebrew anatomy, not merely an organ named for anatomical precision), teeth ground down with gravel (v16), and a soul that has “forgotten what happiness is” (v17). It ends on the flattest possible line: “my strength has perished, and my hope from Jehovah” (v18) — the word translated “hope” here, tochelet, is the exact word the chapter will use again at v21, on the other side of the turn that is about to happen. The poet does not skip past despair to get to comfort; he states the despair as flatly and completely as it can be stated, using the very word hope will need to reclaim three verses later.

Verses 19–24 · “great is your faithfulness” — the most famous six words in the book

The turn. Verse 18 ended on hope's perishing; verse 20 remembers the affliction “well” and is “bowed down” by it — still inside the darkness. Then verse 21, with no external event to explain the shift, simply says: “But this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope.” “This” is not a new fact about the world; it is a decision to remember something ALREADY TRUE that grief had crowded out. The whole turn of the chapter, and arguably of the book, happens inside one small Hebrew clause with no subject change and no new information — only a chosen recollection.

⚠ Chesed and emunah, in one breath. Verse 22's chasdei Jehovah — his STEADFAST, COVENANT-BOUND LOVE, plural in Hebrew as though counting each individual act of it — “never ceases,” and his compassions (rachamim, built on the word for a womb) “never fail.” KJV renders the whole verse in its own famous cadence: “It is of the LORD's mercies that we are not consumed, because his compassions fail not”; NIV modernizes the syntax without changing the claim (“for his compassions never fail”); NWT keeps the covenant sense of chesed most explicitly — “It is because of Jehovah's loyal love that we have not come to our finish, for his mercies never end” — the same “loyal love” this project's own dictionary uses for the word elsewhere. Verse 23 answers with the twin claim: they are “new every morning,” and rabbah emunatecha — GREAT IS YOUR FAITHFULNESS, a phrase KJV, ASV, and NIV all render almost word for word, since the shelf has nothing to argue about here. This is the single most quoted line in Lamentations, the source of Thomas Chisholm's 1923 hymn of the same name (“morning by morning new mercies I see” borrows both the imagery and the timing directly from this verse) — and the Hebrew is worth reading past the hymn's own paraphrase: emunah is not vague trustworthiness but steadiness, the same root behind “amen,” something that can be LEANED on. A chapter that just spent eighteen verses cataloguing what God has done TO the speaker turns, at its exact structural center, to praise what God IS — not a change in circumstance, a change in which fact the speaker chooses to hold onto.

Verses 25–33 · waiting quietly, the yoke, and a God who does not enjoy this

Three sayings on patient endurance (vv25–27), each opening on the word “good” (tov): Jehovah is good to those who wait for him; it is good to wait QUIETLY (dumam, silence, the same root behind the noun demamah in the famous “still, small voice” of 1 Kings 19, not yet on these pages); and it is good for a man to bear the yoke IN HIS YOUTH — discipline accepted early, while there is still time to be shaped by it. What follows is not stoic resignation but something closer to nonviolent submission: sit alone and keep silent (v28), put the mouth in the dust (v29, a posture of total humiliation), offer the cheek to the one who strikes (v30) — language later readers have heard echoed in the Sermon on the Mount's own turn-the-other-cheek teaching, though this project reports the resonance without claiming a direct literary dependence six centuries earlier.

“He does not afflict from his heart.” Verses 31–33 make the chapter's clearest theological claim: God will not reject forever (v31); though he causes grief, he shows compassion “according to the abundance of his loving devotion” (v32, the same chesed from v22); and — the phrase this whole section has been building toward — “he does not afflict from his heart, nor grieve the children of men willingly” (v33). ⚠ This translation keeps the more literal Hebrew idiom, mi-libbo, “from his heart,” for the first clause — KJV instead moves that same idea to the SECOND clause and renders the whole verse “he doth not afflict willingly, nor grieve the children of men,” while ESV keeps the idiom in the same place this translation does (“he does not afflict from his heart”). Both are honest readings of one short Hebrew phrase, and the difference is really about which half of the verse carries the emphasis: that God does not enjoy this, or that he does not choose it lightly. The suffering catalogued in verses 1–18 is not denied or softened; it is set inside a claim that the God inflicting it does not do so gladly. ⚠ Verses 31 through 39 also switch, in the Hebrew, from the divine name Jehovah to Adonai, “the Lord” — nine straight verses on a different title, right at the chapter's theological turn, before the name Jehovah returns at v40's call to return.

Verses 34–39 · what the Lord does NOT approve, and a hard question about complaint

A short, dense list of injustices the text states God does not sanction — crushing prisoners underfoot (v34), denying a man justice “in the presence of the Most High” (v35, as if injustice done anywhere is done in front of him), subverting a man in his own lawsuit (v36). Then the argument tightens into pure theodicy: nothing happens unless the Lord has commanded it (v37); both calamity AND good come from the mouth of the Most High (v38, a claim this book will not soften into “only the good comes from God”); so why should a living man complain about the punishment of his own sins (v39)? The logic is uncomfortable by design — it holds God sovereign over the disaster and, in the very next breath, holds the sufferer responsible for what brought it — and the chapter does not resolve the tension before moving straight into a call to self-examination.

Verses 40–42 · “let us search” — the voice turns from I to we

Three verses, and the whole poem's grammatical person shifts for the first time: after forty verses of a solitary “I,” verse 40 opens on a plural cohortative — “LET US search and examine our ways, and return to Jehovah” — and stays plural through verse 47. The individual sufferer's turn to hope (vv19–24) becomes, here, an invitation extended to the whole community: not just I remember and hope, but let us together examine ourselves and come back. Verse 42 states the confession as plainly as anything in the book: “We have transgressed and rebelled; you have not forgiven.” The admission of guilt and the report that forgiveness has not yet come sit in the very same sentence, with no attempt to soften the second half.

Verses 43–51 · the anger returns, and a letter-pair swaps places again

The plural “we” that opened in confession (v40–42) does not stay in hope for long: verses 43–45 return to raw lament, now addressed directly to God in the second person — “you have covered yourself with anger and pursued us… you have covered yourself with a cloud, so that no prayer can pass through” (v43–44), the exact opposite of a God who hears. The community has been made “scum and refuse among the peoples” (v45). By verse 48 the voice narrows back to a single “my eye” running with streams of tears — the poem's own restless movement between the personal and the corporate, never settling permanently into either.

⚠ Pe before ayin, exactly as promised. This chapter's letter-pair at position 16–17 runs PE (vv46–48) then AYIN (vv49–51) — the reverse of the standard Hebrew order. Lamentations 2's own note on the identical swap there (vv16–17) already named this as one of three chapters that do it — “here, and in chapters 3 and 4, the sixteenth and seventeenth letters are swapped” — and chapter 3 now pays that forward-plant off exactly as stated. The acrostic dictionary entry traces the reversal to a real archaeological find: an abecedary (a practice copy of the whole alphabet in order) discovered at Kuntillet Ajrud writes PE before AYIN too, centuries before Lamentations was composed — which this project can now add to directly. A second, independent abecedary excavated at Izbet Sartah in 1976, dated to roughly the 12th–10th century BCE, does the same, as does a related inscription at Tel Zayit. Three separate finds, not one, point away from scribal error toward two genuinely competing, equally standard orderings of the Hebrew alphabet circulating in ancient Israel — and chapters 2, 3, and 4 of this book simply followed the less common one, a real fossil of a lost alphabetical tradition preserved by accident in a poem about the fall of Jerusalem.

Verses 52–57 · hunted like a bird, thrown in a pit, and heard

The singular voice returns in full, and with it the chapter's most vivid concrete images: hunted like a bird by enemies “without cause” (v52 — chinnam, the same root this project's dictionary already traces from Job 1:9's “is it for nothing”; John 15:25 quotes the identical Hebrew phrase from a psalm, not from this verse, but the word itself is one small thread running through several of the Bible's own accounts of suffering that names itself unearned; ⚠ KJV and NIV both keep the flat legal sense, “without cause,” while NLT unpacks it into a claim instead of a category — “my enemies, whom I have never harmed, hunted me down like a bird” — which makes explicit what “without cause” only implies: not just that no reason was given, but that none existed); “they have silenced my life in the pit and cast a stone on me” (v53) — a near-death image close enough to Jeremiah's own recorded ordeal, thrown into a mud-filled cistern and left to die (an episode this project has not yet translated, though the prophet's other run-ins with hostile officials are already on these pages at Jeremiah 20) that many readers hear the two as describing the same kind of event, whether or not the same literal occasion. Water rises over the speaker's head (v54); and then the pivot — “I called on your name, O Jehovah, from the depths of the pit… you heard my voice… you drew near… you said, DO NOT FEAR” (v55–57). The chapter's second hope-turn is smaller and more concrete than the first: not a general claim about God's character, but a specific memory of a specific rescue, at the exact bottom of the pit.

Verses 58–66 · a case pleaded, and a chapter that ends unresolved

The rescue-memory of verses 55–57 becomes a legal claim: “you have pleaded the cause of my soul; you have redeemed my life” (v58), and on that basis the speaker asks God to act again — “you have SEEN my injustice… judge my cause” (v59), “you have seen all their vengeance, all their schemes against me” (v60). The chapter that opened on undeserved suffering closes by asking for that suffering to be answered in kind, against the enemies who inflicted it — “you will repay them… according to the work of their hands… you will pursue them in anger and destroy them” (vv64–66). ⚠ This project reports rather than resolves the discomfort many readers feel at a chapter that moved through “great is your faithfulness” only to close on an unsoftened call for the enemy's destruction — the same pattern Psalm 139's own note describes, where the closing plea to be searched follows, rather than replaces, an earlier request that God kill the psalmist's enemies. The hope of verses 19–24 and the imprecation of verses 64–66 are not presented as contradicting each other in the text; they sit side by side, both addressed to the same God, in the same sixty-six-verse poem.

Patterns worth carrying forward

What makes this chapter unlike its neighbors. It is the only acrostic in the book that triples its own discipline (three verses per letter instead of one), the only chapter with a first-person individual voice instead of a corporate one, and the only chapter that contains a sustained turn to explicit hope — twice, once general (vv19–24) and once concrete (vv55–57) — before closing on an unresolved plea for vengeance. Structurally and theologically, it sits at the book's center and carries most of its argumentative weight.

Where this translation sides with the literal shelf: keeping gever as “the man” rather than a generic “I” (v1), preserving the Adonai/Jehovah name-shift across vv31–39 rather than flattening both to “the LORD,” and printing every verse's acrostic letter as this project already does for chapters 1 and 2 — here tripled, so the pattern is visible directly rather than only described.

Where it goes its own way: the identity of “the man” is reported as an open question (Jeremiah himself, by tradition, or a representative sufferer) rather than settled; and the chapter's own unresolved tension between hope and imprecation is left standing, not harmonized.
