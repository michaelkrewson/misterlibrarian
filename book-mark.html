📖 Mark

Κατὰ Μᾶρκον (Kata Markon) — 'According to Mark' — the traditional title. The book itself names no author; the ascription to Mark is early and external. Its own opening word, EUANGELION ('good news'), is very likely where the whole genre got the name 'Gospel.'

The shortest, fastest, and probably earliest of the four Gospels — a breathless, urgent account of Jesus in action, with almost no teaching and one relentless adverb: 'immediately.'

Chapter by chapter

Mark 1The most breathless of the four Gospels starts already at a run — no birth, no genealogy, just a grown man at a river and the word 'immediately' eleven times over. The heavens are TORN open at the baptism (the same violent verb as the temple curtain at the end), a demon is the first to name Jesus correctly, and a leper is healed by a man 'moved with anger' who reaches across the line and touches him.Mark 2Five collisions in one chapter, and the first hint that this is going to end badly. ⚠ Four men take a roof apart — Mark uses two verbs for it, un-roofing and DIGGING, which is exactly how a Galilean mud-and-brushwood roof comes off, where Luke retelling it has them go down through TILES. The man is lowered on a krabattos, a straw pallet, a low word both Matthew and Luke replace with a proper bed; this translation keeps the mat. Then a sentence that breaks in half mid-clause and is left broken. ⚠ A tax collector is called from his booth and Mark names him LEVI son of Alphaeus, where the Gospel of Matthew names him Matthew — with the second half of the puzzle stated: Mark’s own list of the Twelve has no Levi in it. A bridegroom who will be TAKEN AWAY, three words long, the first shadow in the book. And then ⚠ “in the time of Abiathar the high priest” — where 1 Samuel says the priest was AHIMELECH and Abiathar is his son, laid out rather than patched, with the evidence against it (Matthew and Luke both drop the phrase) and the real defence for it (Mark uses the same construction at 12:26 to mean “in the passage about…”). The chapter ends on the sentence no other Gospel kept: the sabbath came into being for the human being, and not the other way round.Mark 3The plot to kill him is formed six verses in, by a coalition that should not exist. ⚠ Jesus looks round at them WITH ANGER — and unlike the disputed anger at 1:41 this one is textually secure, which is the best corroboration chapter 1’s harder reading could get. Matthew deletes the look and the emotion; Luke keeps the looking-round, drops the feeling, and four words later hands the fury to the OPPONENTS instead. Then the Twelve are MADE — the plain verb, unsoftened — and among them two men get a nickname nobody ever uses again. ⚠ And then the two verses neither Matthew nor Luke has in any form: his own family come to SEIZE him, saying he is out of his mind. Mark wraps the Beelzebul charge inside that scene — the first clear instance of the sandwich he builds this whole Gospel out of — so that the family’s verdict and the scribes’ verdict are read through each other. ⚠ The unforgivable saying turns on one disputed noun: an eternal SIN, a condition that does not stop, against the later text’s eternal JUDGEMENT, which is where “damnation” comes from. And the chapter ends with his mother outside a circle and the word given to whoever is sitting inside it.Mark 4The boat kept ready at 3:9 finally gets used: Jesus teaches an entire shoreline crowd from the water, starting with a sower whose seed lands on four kinds of ground it cannot be told apart from until the harvest is in. Then the disciples ask why he speaks in parables, and get the hardest sentence in the chapter — parables are given so THAT outsiders may see and not perceive, hear and not understand, ‘lest they should turn and be forgiven.’ ⚠ Matthew's version of the same moment quotes Isaiah as fulfilled prophecy, framed with BECAUSE; Mark's Jesus states it as his own purpose, with no citation at all — a real difference between the two Gospels, not a translator's choice. A lamp and a measure follow, both sayings that surface in entirely different settings in Matthew's Sermon on the Mount. Then a parable found only in Mark: a farmer scatters seed and the earth bears fruit AUTOMATICALLY, he does not know how — and a mustard seed, proverbially the smallest a Galilean farmer would plant, becomes the largest garden plant there is. The chapter ends on a lake: a squall fills the boat, Jesus sleeps through it, and when woken he REBUKES the wind and orders the sea, ‘Be muzzled’ — the identical command, the identical verb, he already gave an unclean spirit at 1:25. The disciples' own fear and unbelief close the chapter, still asking the question it never answers: who is this?Mark 5A man possessed by ‘Legion’ — a Roman military word borrowed to describe a crowd inside one man — is freed among the tombs, and a herd of about two thousand pigs runs downhill into the lake carrying the spirits Jesus lets them enter without recorded comment. ⚠ The region's own name is disputed in the manuscripts, ‘Gadarenes’ against ‘Gerasenes,’ a real textual crux this translation reports rather than resolves. The town, more afraid of the healed man than the possessed one, asks Jesus to leave — the only request in the chapter he does not argue with — and for the first time in this Gospel he tells someone to go home and PROCLAIM rather than stay silent, sending the first missionary of the Decapolis into the story with no fisherman's background at all. Then two miracles interleave: a synagogue ruler named Jairus falls at the feet of the very class of official already plotting against Jesus, for a ‘little daughter’ at the point of death; on the way, a woman twelve years sick — the same number as the age of the girl he is racing toward — touches his garment from behind and is healed by a POWER the text describes almost physically, leaving him and felt leaving. “Daughter,” he calls her, the only time in this Gospel he uses the word for a grown woman. Then Peter, James and John appear together for the first time as a set, taken in alone to a room already laughing at the claim that the child only sleeps — and “Talitha koum,” spoken in the Aramaic Jesus actually used, kept untranslated and then translated immediately, the same pattern already used for ‘Boanerges.’ The silence command returns at once, the opposite instruction to the one just given a chapter before, with no stated rule for why.Mark 6Jesus is rejected in his own hometown — never named, though the reader already knows it — where the crowd can list his trade, his mother, and all four brothers by name, and where he “was not able” to do a miracle, a blunter verb than Matthew's own softened retelling of the same scene. The Twelve are sent out two by two with nothing but a staff and sandals — Matthew's version of the identical sending forbids both. Then Herod, called “king” though Rome's own paperwork made him a tetrarch, and the death of John the Baptist told in full as a flashback: a birthday oath up to half a kingdom, an unnamed dancing girl tradition alone calls Salome, and ⚠ a real fork in the earliest manuscripts over whether she is Herodias's daughter or, in the harder reading, Herod's own. Five thousand men are fed from five loaves and two fish — the crowd seated “group by group” and “row by row,” the same distributive doubling this chapter already used for the Twelve — and Jesus takes, blesses, breaks, and gives the bread in the identical fourfold sequence this Gospel's own Last Supper will use. That night he walks to the disciples across the water, “tormented” at their oars by the same word a demon once used of its own fear of him, and says “it is I” — the same ordinary self-identification, and the same Greek words, this Gospel's own John already uses for this same scene told a second time (John 6:20), reserving the bare, unpredicated “I am” for the one place nothing ordinary is happening around it at all. ⚠ Their hearts are “hardened,” the same diagnosis Mark has already given the Pharisees plotting against him, now turned without comment on the Twelve themselves. It closes on a whole region reaching for the fringe of his garment, the same gesture one woman risked in private two chapters ago, now an entire crowd's open practice.Mark 7Pharisees and scribes from Jerusalem confront Jesus over unwashed hands and hold up hand-washing “with the fist” — one of the most disputed phrases in the whole Gospel — against “the tradition of the elders.” Jesus answers with Isaiah, then a worked example: a son can declare his parents' support “Corban” — a gift devoted to God — and by that single word never have to give it, nullifying God's own commandment by a legal technicality. The same ironic word, “well,” frames the whole chapter — sincere of Isaiah's prophecy, sarcastic of the Pharisees' skill at evasion, sincere again at the very end. A public riddle follows — nothing outside a person defiles him, only what comes out — its full weight withheld until the disciples ask in private; ⚠ one verse (16) is genuinely missing, its familiar words borrowed from a refrain already used twice in chapter 4. Then Jesus's one recorded personal visit to Gentile soil: a Greek, a “Syrophoenician” woman — Matthew's parallel calls her, more loaded, a “Canaanite” — begs for her demon-possessed daughter, is refused with the image of children's bread and little dogs, and out-argues him — and readers divide on whether the sequence shows an intention genuinely changed or a test of faith he meant her to pass. Finally, in the Decapolis, a deaf man who “spoke with difficulty” — mogilalos, a word that occurs nowhere else in the whole New Testament except in the Septuagint's own Isaiah 35:6, part of a messianic prophecy Mark never cites by name. “Ephphatha” — Aramaic, preserved and translated, exactly as Talitha koum was — and the crowd's verdict closes the loop the chapter opened: “He has done all things well.”Mark 8A second miraculous feeding, and the chapter turns on the fact that Mark tells it with different words: the twelve baskets of the five thousand are kophinoi, a Jewish traveller's small hand-basket, and the seven here are spyrides, a hamper big enough to lower a grown man over a city wall in. ⚠ Searched across the whole New Testament the two words never trade places, in any Gospel — which is exactly what makes sense of the strangest interrogation in the book, where Jesus quizzes his disciples on both meals and they get every number right and the point wrong. The Pharisees demand a sign from heaven and get a broken sentence: an unfinished Hebrew oath — ‘truly I say to you, IF a sign is given…’ — that every Bible smooths into a flat no, and that this translation prints unfinished, as the Greek leaves it. ⚠ Matthew's version of the same refusal keeps an exception, the sign of Jonah; Mark's has none. A warning against “the leaven of the Pharisees and the leaven of Herod” — the only Gospel that pairs Herod with them, and the only one that never says what the leaven means. Then the one healing in Mark that does not work the first time: a blind man at Bethsaida who sees people “like trees, walking” and needs a second touch, a scene neither Matthew nor Luke has at all, placed immediately before Peter gets the right title half-right at Caesarea Philippi. ⚠ Peter's confession here is four Greek words long — no blessing, no rock, no keys; everything built on this scene for two thousand years is in Matthew's telling, not Mark's. And then the first prediction of the death, “after three days” where Matthew and Luke both write “on the third day”; a disciple rebuking his teacher with the teacher's own exorcism word; and “Get behind me, Satan” — which is the identical Greek phrase as “come behind me” one verse later, so the rebuke is not a dismissal but a relocation: Peter is being sent back to where a follower stands.Mark 9Six days later, three disciples on a high mountain, and Jesus is TRANSFIGURED — the same verb Matthew keeps and Luke throws away for a plainer 'his face became different.' Elijah appears “with” Moses — Mark's own word order, reversed from Matthew's — and a voice from a cloud echoes Deuteronomy's 'to him you shall listen,' dropping the baptism voice's 'well pleased' and adding an imperative in its place. Coming down, a boy his disciples could not heal, the exact verb Mark already used for a withered hand, and a father who says 'I believe' and 'help my unbelief' in one breath without resolving the contradiction. A second prediction of the death met with silence rather than argument. Then a house in Capernaum, an argument about who is greatest answered with a child set “in the middle of them” — the same three words Matthew's own version reuses three chapters later for 'where two or three are gathered.' John tries to stop a stranger casting out demons in Jesus's name and is told “whoever is not against us is for us” — read beside Matthew's 'whoever is not with me is against me,' the flat reverse of it, answering a different situation. ⚠ A donkey-millstone, and hand, foot and eye each surrendered to Gehenna with the identical refrain from Isaiah's last verse — said once in the earliest manuscripts and copied twice more by a later hand, leaving two verse-number gaps this translation prints as gaps. It closes on salt: 'have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another,' answering, without saying so, the rivalry the chapter opened on.Mark 10Pharisees test Jesus on divorce, and the answer reaches past Moses' permission — NOT a command, a distinction Matthew already made of this same question — to Genesis itself: 'from the beginning of creation, God made them male and female.' Little children are brought to be touched and the disciples rebuke the ones bringing them; Jesus is INDIGNANT, the same word this chapter will use again when the other ten disciples turn on James and John. A man runs up, kneels, calls him “Good Teacher” — a question Matthew's own telling quietly rewrites to avoid — and is told to sell everything; only Mark says Jesus LOVED him before he refuses. A camel through a needle's eye, three Gospels and three different Greek words for the needle. Peter's 'we have left everything' answered with a hundredfold promise Mark alone burdens with persecutions. The third and final passion prediction, promised by chapter and verse two chapters ago. James and John ask for the seats at his right and left — in Matthew, it's their mother who asks — and are told the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life “a ransom IN EXCHANGE FOR many.” ⚠ And at the very gate of Jericho, a blind beggar named Bartimaeus — one of the only miracle recipients this Gospel actually names — throws off his cloak, is healed, and becomes the one person in the whole chapter who actually follows “the way” it has been quietly tracking since verse 17.

About the book

Where it sits
The second Gospel in the traditional order (after Matthew), but on the majority scholarly view the FIRST to be written — the earliest of the four, and a principal source drawn on by both Matthew and Luke. One of the four canonical Gospels that open the New Testament.
Genre
A GOSPEL — a new literary form Mark may effectively have invented: not a biography in the ancient sense, not a chronicle, but a theological narrative of the life, death and resurrection of Jesus, written to proclaim rather than merely to record. Mark's is the most action-driven of the four: heavy on miracle and movement, light on discourse.
Author
By strong and early tradition, JOHN MARK — the companion of Peter and, earlier, of Paul and Barnabas (Acts 12:12, 25; 13:13; 1 Peter 5:13). Papias, writing around AD 130 and quoting an even older source ('the Elder'), says Mark was Peter's INTERPRETER and 'wrote down accurately all that he remembered' of Peter's preaching, 'though not in order.' This makes the Gospel, on the traditional view, essentially the memoirs of PETER as set down by his assistant — which fits its vivid, eyewitness-flavoured detail and its unflattering portrait of Peter himself.
Date written
Most place it around AD 65–70 — near or just after the deaths of Peter and Paul in Rome and around the Jewish war that ended in the destruction of the Temple in AD 70. A few argue for a date in the 50s. Either way it is early: within a generation of the events, and the earliest written Gospel.
Place
By tradition ROME, written for a Gentile, largely Roman audience. The internal signs fit: Mark explains Jewish customs his readers would not know (7:3-4), translates Aramaic phrases into Greek, and uses a striking number of Latin loanwords (legion, denarius, centurion). It reads like a Gospel made for people far from Galilee.
Audience
GENTILE believers, probably under pressure — a church that needed the story of a Messiah who suffered, told to people who might soon suffer themselves. Mark's Jesus is constantly misunderstood, and the disciples constantly fail, which speaks to a community learning that following the crucified one is hard.

How it's laid out

1:1–13The beginning — no birth, straight to John the baptiser, the baptism, and the temptation, all in thirteen verses.
1:14–8:26The ministry in Galilee — a whirlwind of healings, exorcisms, parables and growing crowds, and a mounting question: who is this? (with the disciples steadily failing to answer it).
8:27–10:52The turn — Peter confesses 'you are the Christ,' and from that moment Jesus sets his face toward Jerusalem, predicting his death three times and redefining greatness as service. The hinge of the book.
11:1–16:8Jerusalem — the entry, the temple, the last supper, Gethsemane, the trial, the cross, and the empty tomb. The Gospel that has run at a sprint slows to tell the passion in detail.

What it's about

  • URGENCY — the relentless 'immediately' (euthys), a kingdom breaking in with no pause, a Jesus always on the move. The pace is itself a message.
  • The SUFFERING Messiah — Mark refuses a triumphant Christ. Nearly half the book is the road to the cross, and its climax is not a resurrection appearance but a centurion at a corpse saying 'truly this was the Son of God.'
  • The MESSIANIC SECRET — Jesus repeatedly silences those who would announce him (demons, the healed, even the disciples). His identity is real but hidden, and only fully sayable at the cross.
  • The FAILING disciples — nowhere are the twelve less flattering. They misunderstand, they sleep, they flee, Peter denies him — a hard mirror for a struggling church, and a strange choice for a book supposedly based on Peter's own preaching.
  • AUTHORITY (exousia) — over disease, demons, the Sabbath, sin, the sea. Mark shows rather than argues: what Jesus DOES makes the claim his words leave unspoken.
  • SERVICE (diakoneō) — from the angels in the wilderness and Simon's mother-in-law to the book's great definition, 'the Son of Man came not to be served but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many' (10:45).

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), the critical text edited by Michael W. Holmes — a modern eclectic text weighing the earliest manuscripts. ⚠️ Mark carries two of the most consequential textual questions in the New Testament. Its OPENING (1:1) divides over whether 'Son of God' stood in the first sentence, and 1:41 over whether Jesus was 'moved with COMPASSION' or 'moved with ANGER' at the leper — a one-word difference this translation resolves toward the harder reading, explained in the note. And its ENDING is the single largest textual problem in the Gospels: the oldest manuscripts stop abruptly at 16:8, with the women fleeing the empty tomb 'for they were afraid,' and the familiar 'longer ending' (16:9-20) is a later addition — flagged there when the translation reaches it. This library prints the critical text and lays the variants out with their pedigrees. The seven-version shelf under every chapter compares the NIV, KJV, Douay-Rheims, The Living Bible, the 1599 Geneva, ASV, and NWT.

Looking forward

Mark IS a book about who Jesus is, and it withholds the answer on purpose. The demons know it from the first page; the reader is told it in verse 1; but the human characters cannot see it until the end — and the confession the whole Gospel drives toward comes not from a disciple but from a Roman executioner looking at a dead man: 'truly this man was the Son of God' (15:39). Mark's Christ is the crucified one, and his central claim is that you do not understand the glory until you have seen the cross.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP is relatively secure by ancient standards — the Papias tradition linking Mark to Peter is early and specific — though critical scholars treat 'Mark' as shorthand for the community that produced it. The live questions are the ENDING (almost universally, 16:9-20 is judged a later addition, since the earliest and best manuscripts end at 16:8; whether Mark MEANT to end so abruptly, or whether the original ending was lost, is genuinely debated), the two great VARIANTS at 1:1 and 1:41, and MARKAN PRIORITY — the majority view that Mark was written first and used by Matthew and Luke, against the older tradition that Matthew came first.
