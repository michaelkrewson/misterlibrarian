At Antioch the prophets and teachers are ministering and fasting when the Holy Spirit says to set apart Barnabas and Saul; they are let go and, sent out by the Spirit, sail from Seleucia to Cyprus with John as attendant. At Paphos the magician Bar-Jesus, called Elymas, opposes them and tries to twist the proconsul Sergius Paulus away from the faith; Saul, who is also Paul, calls him son of the devil, the hand of Jehovah is upon him and he is blind for a time, and the proconsul believes. At Perga John withdraws and returns to Jerusalem; at Pisidian Antioch, asked for a word of encouragement, Paul tells Israel's story to David and to Jesus, raised and not seeing decay, and proclaims forgiveness of sins. The next sabbath almost the whole city gathers, the Jews are filled with jealousy, Paul and Barnabas say they are turning to the nations, those appointed to eternal life believe, and the two are thrown out, shake off the dust and go to Iconium, while the disciples are filled with joy and Holy Spirit.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its only time-markers are the sabbath of v14, ‘the next sabbath’ of v42 and ‘the coming sabbath’ of v44, and it follows the return of 12:25 without a date. It names a proconsul of Cyprus, Sergius Paulus, and gives no year for him; this library supplies none.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · προφῆται καὶ διδάσκαλοι … Νίγερ … Μαναὴν … σύντροφος … λειτουργούντων … Ἀφορίσατε δή μοι … προσκέκλημαι … ἐπιθέντες τὰς χεῖρας … ἀπέλυσαν prophētai kai didaskaloi … Niger … Manaēn … syntrophos … leitourgountōn … Aphorisate dē moi … proskeklēmai … epithentes tas cheiras … apelysan — Antioch, Acts 13:1–3: five names, a man brought up with Herod, and the Spirit’s order to “set apart” Barnabas and Saul

Five names, and who comes first. Luke says “prophets and teachers” and then lists five men, and he does not say which are which. The congregation is the one Acts 11 has already described: Cypriot and Cyrenian men first spoke there (11:20), Barnabas was sent and went to Tarsus for Saul (11:25), and prophets came down from Jerusalem (11:27). Barnabas is named first and Saul last, and the order matters for what follows: at vv2 and 7 it is still “Barnabas and Saul,” at v13 Luke writes “Paul and those with him,” and from there Paul’s name stands first at vv43, 46 and 50. Simeon who was called Niger carries a Latin second name, niger, “black” or “dark”; Luke does not say why he bore it, and the Greek links him to no other Simeon. Lucius the Cyrenian is named once more in the New Testament, among those who send greetings at Romans 16:21 (not yet on these pages); nothing in either verse says they are one man. Manaēn is usually taken to be a Greek form of the Hebrew name Menahem, a report this page does not check. The word that sets him apart is syntrophos, which stands in this verse alone (dictionary): it is built on syn-, “with,” and trophos, “one who nourishes,” so “brought up with.” The Greek does not say whether that means a foster-brother, a boy raised at court beside the prince, or a childhood friend. KJV, Geneva and NIV “brought up with Herod the tetrarch,” ASV “the foster-brother of Herod the tetrarch,” Douay “foster brother of Herod the tetrarch,” NWT 1984 “educated with Herod the district ruler,” which adds schooling, TLB “the foster-brother of King Herod.” The Greek says tetraarchēs, “tetrarch,” and so tells this Herod from the “Herod the king” of Acts 12:1, who has died by 12:23; it is Herod Antipas, tetrarch of Galilee (Luke 3:1), the Herod who wanted to see Jesus (Luke 23:8). A person from Herod’s household has already appeared among Jesus’s followers, Joanna, “wife of Chuza, Herod’s steward” (Luke 8:3); Luke links neither her nor Chuza to Manaen. The apparatus gives de to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and de tines, “now there were certain,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which is where the “certain prophets and teachers” of the KJV and Geneva come from; the ASV, Douay, NIV and NWT 1984 print no “certain.” The TLB prints “Paul” for the Saul of the Greek.

“Ministering to the Lord,” and which Lord. Leitourgeō is a verb of public service (dictionary; the noun leitourgos has its own entry, leitourgos). The verb stands in three verses of the New Testament: this one, Romans 15:27 (not yet on these pages), where the Gentiles who share the Jerusalem believers’ spiritual things “owe it to them to minister” to them in material things, and Hebrews 10:11 (not yet on these pages), of a priest standing daily at his service. The one served is tō kyriō, “the Lord,” and the Greek does not say whether that is Jehovah or Jesus; in the next clause the Holy Spirit speaks, and the speaker says “me” and “I have called.” As at Acts 8:25, this translation keeps “Lord” where the referent is open. NWT 1984 prints “publicly ministering to Jehovah”; KJV, ASV and Geneva “ministered to the Lord,” Douay “were ministering to the Lord,” NIV “worshiping the Lord,” and TLB “worshiping” with no object at all. The Spirit speaking aloud to a person or a group is Luke’s way elsewhere too: to Philip at 8:29, to Peter at 10:19, and in Peter’s report at 11:12. The fasting is joined to prayer here and again where elders are appointed at 14:23.

“Set apart,” and the work not yet named. Aphorizō (dictionary) is “to mark off by a boundary.” The verb stands in nine verses of the New Testament: Matthew 13:49 and 25:32, where angels and the Son of Man separate people as a shepherd separates sheep from goats; Luke 6:22, where people “exclude” the disciples; this verse; Acts 19:9, where Paul withdraws the disciples from the synagogue; 2 Corinthians 6:17; Galatians 2:12, where Peter “separated himself” from the Gentile believers; and two verses where Paul uses it of himself: Romans 1:1, “set apart for the gospel of God,” and Galatians 1:15, “who set me apart from my mother’s womb.” The command is Aphorisate dē moi; dē is a small particle of urging, “then,” “now.” Proskeklēmai is a perfect tense, “I have called to myself,” and the “work” is not named here; 14:26 will call the journey “the work” that they “fulfilled.” KJV, ASV and Geneva “Separate me Barnabas and Saul,” Douay “Separate me Saul and Barnabas,” which turns the two names around, NIV “Set apart for me Barnabas and Saul,” NWT 1984 “Of all persons set” Barnabas and Saul “apart for me,” which spells out the dē, and TLB “Dedicate Barnabas and Paul for a special job.” The apparatus gives kai before Saul to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and kai ton, with the article, to Robinson–Pierpont.

Hands laid, and a double sending. The Greek does not name who fasted, prayed and laid on hands; it is the same autōn as in v2. Hands laid on a person stand at 6:6 (the Seven), 8:17 (Samaria), 9:12 and 9:17, where Ananias lays his hands on the Saul now being sent. Apelysan is the verb of release, “let go,” as at 4:21 and 4:23; v4 gives the other half, that the same men are ekpemphthentes hypo tou hagiou pneumatos, “sent out by the Holy Spirit.” KJV and ASV “sent them away,” NIV “sent them off,” Geneva and NWT 1984 “let them go,” TLB “after more fasting and prayer,” which adds “more.”

Verses 4–12 · ἐκπεμφθέντες … Σελεύκειαν … Κύπρον … Σαλαμῖνι … ὑπηρέτην … μάγον ψευδοπροφήτην … Βαριησοῦ … ἀνθυπάτῳ … Ἐλύμας … διαστρέψαι … ὁ καὶ Παῦλος … ἀτενίσας … υἱὲ διαβόλου … χεὶρ κυρίου … ἀχλὺς … χειραγωγούς … ἐκπλησσόμενος ἐπὶ τῇ διδαχῇ ekpemphthentes … Seleukeian … Kypron … Salamini … hypēretēn … magon pseudoprophētēn … Bariēsou … anthypatō … Elymas … diastrepsai … ho kai Paulos … atenisas … huie diabolou … cheir kyriou … achlys … cheiragōgous … ekplēssomenos epi tē didachē — Cyprus, Acts 13:4–12: the magician Bar-Jesus, Sergius Paulus, “Saul, who is also Paul,” and the hand of Jehovah that blinds the man who once was blinded

From Seleucia to Salamis, with John as attendant. The Spirit’s sending (ekpemphthentes) is a verb that stands in this verse and at 17:10 and nowhere else in the New Testament. They go katēlthon, “down,” to Seleucia and from there apepleusan, “sailed away,” to Cyprus, Barnabas’s own island (4:36). At Salamis they were katēggellon, “proclaiming,” the word of God in the synagogues of the Jews; the verb is in the imperfect, a continuing action, and the Greek says “synagogues,” in the plural. Paul’s speech at v38 will use the same verb in the passive: forgiveness “is being proclaimed.” “They also had John as an attendant”: hypēretēs (dictionary) is the word for the synagogue attendant to whom Jesus hands the scroll at Luke 4:20, for the “officers” at Acts 5:22 and 5:26, and for those who “became servants of the word” in Luke 1:2; here the helper of men proclaiming the word is called by the word of Luke’s own preface. This John is John Mark (John Mark; 12:25), though this verse does not use the second name. The apparatus gives autoi, “these,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 at the start of v4, and houtoi to Robinson–Pierpont; it gives “Holy Spirit” in the order hagiou pneumatos to those three and pneumatos tou hagiou to Robinson–Pierpont; it gives the bare eis Seleukeian and eis Kypron to those three and the article, eis tēn, to Robinson–Pierpont; and te to those three and de to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “departed unto Seleucia,” ASV and NIV “went down to Seleucia.” KJV and Geneva “they had also John to their minister,” ASV “they had also John as their attendant,” NIV “John was with them as their helper,” NWT 1984 “They had John also as an attendant,” TLB “John Mark went with them as their assistant,” which supplies the second name.

A magician, a false prophet, a Jew. They go through the whole island as far as Paphos, and there Luke gives the first of two names the narrative uses for the magician. Magos (dictionary) is the word that this translation prints “magi” at Matthew 2:1, 2:7 and 2:16, where the visitors from the east follow a star, and prints “magician” here; it stands in five verses of the New Testament, the three in Matthew and vv6 and 8 of this chapter. Simon in Samaria is said to be mageuōn, “practicing magic” (8:9), and to have amazed the people with his mageiai, “magic arts” (8:11): the same family of words, in two further verses. The reader’s question is what “magician” will be taken to mean: a practitioner of magic, a man with the title of court seer, a fraud. The Greek word covers all three, and Luke calls this man a pseudoprophētēs, “false prophet,” a word that stands in eleven verses of the New Testament and, in Acts, only here. He is Ioudaion, a Jew, and his name is Bariēsou, a Greek form of Aramaic bar-Yeshua, “son of Jesus,” or “son of Joshua”; the apparatus gives the form Bariēsou to Nestle–Aland 28 and Bariēsous to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont. KJV and ASV print “sorcerer,” NIV “Jewish sorcerer and false prophet,” NWT 1984 “a sorcerer, a false prophet,” Douay “a magician, a false prophet,” TLB “a fake prophet.” The apparatus gives holēn, “whole,” in “the whole island” and andra, “a man,” in “a certain man” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and leaves both words out for Robinson–Pierpont. KJV and Geneva print “the isle,” with no “whole,” and NIV “a Jewish sorcerer,” with no “man.”

The proconsul, and “Elymas.” Anthypatos, “proconsul,” stands in five verses of the New Testament: vv7, 8 and 12 here, 18:12 of Gallio in Achaia, and 19:38 (19:38 not yet on these pages). Luke gives the man’s name as Sergiō Paulō, Sergius Paulus, and a description: anēr synetos, “a man of understanding,” a word built on the verb “to put together.” KJV, Geneva and Douay “a prudent man,” ASV “a man of understanding,” NIV and NWT 1984 “an intelligent man.” The name Paulos, “Paul,” therefore stands first in Acts as the proconsul’s, two verses before it is Saul’s. The man “was with” the proconsul (ēn syn); the Greek does not say whether he was a house seer, a friend or a hanger-on. NIV “an attendant of the proconsul,” TLB “attached himself to the governor.” The proconsul epezētēsen akousai, “earnestly sought to hear,” the word of God, NWT 1984 “earnestly sought to hear,” KJV “desired to hear,” ASV “sought to hear.” Then Luke says: Elymas ho magos, houtōs gar methermēneuetai to onoma autou, “Elymas the magician — for so his name is translated.” The clause says what Elymas means, and it stands next to magos: it does not say that Elymas is a translation of Bar-Jesus, and the two are not identical in meaning, so this page does not tie them together; where Elymas’s name comes from is a question the verse leaves open. KJV, ASV and Geneva “for so is his name by interpretation,” NIV “for that is what his name means,” NWT 1984 “that, in fact, is the way his name is translated.” He anthistato, “was opposing” them, and he sought diastrepsai, “to twist,” the proconsul apo tēs pisteōs, “away from the faith.”

“Twist” — and the straight ways. Diastrephō (dictionary) stands in seven verses of the New Testament. It is the “twisted generation” of Matthew 17:17 and Luke 9:41, the “subverting our nation” of the charge at Luke 23:2, the two uses of this chapter (v8, and v10 below), “speaking twisted things” at 20:30, and “crooked” at Philippians 2:15 (the last two not yet on these pages). This translation gives both uses in Acts 13 the same English, since Paul’s question in v10 answers Elymas’s aim in v8: he twists a person away from the faith, and he twists the straight ways. KJV “turn away the deputy,” ASV “turn aside the proconsul,” NWT 1984 “turn the proconsul away,” TLB “keep him from trusting the Lord.” At v10, KJV “pervert the right ways,” NWT 1984 “distorting the right ways,” Geneva “pervert the straight ways,” NIV “perverting the right ways,” TLB “end your opposition,” with no ways at all.

“Saul, who is also Paul.” The doubled name is the hinge of the book: Saulos de, ho kai Paulos, “but Saul, the one also Paul.” Luke gives no reason for the second name and does not say that Saul chose it here. After this verse the narrative calls him Paul, and the name Saul for him stands again only in Paul’s own retellings of the road to Damascus (Saoul, 22:7, 22:13 and 26:14; none yet on these pages); at v21 below it is King Saul. The Latin paulus means “small,” and an old suggestion that he took the name from the proconsul is one Luke does not make. Atenisas is a verb of fixed gazing, printed “gazing” at 3:4, 10:4 and 11:6 and “staring” at 6:15 and 7:55, where the eyes are on a face or on heaven; here, as there, the look goes before the words. The phrase plēstheis pneumatos hagiou, “filled with Holy Spirit,” is the one used of Peter (4:8) and of Saul himself at 9:17. The apparatus gives the plain hagiou to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and hagiou kai, “Holy Spirit and,” to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “set his eyes on him,” ASV “fastened his eyes on him,” NWT 1984 “looked at him intently,” NIV “looked straight at Elymas,” TLB “glared angrily at the sorcerer,” which adds an anger the verb does not carry.

“Son of the devil,” and a name that means “son of Jesus.” Dolos is “deceit,” the word Jesus uses of Nathanael, in whom there is none (John 1:47). Rhadiourgia stands in this verse alone (dictionary); it is built on rhadios, “easy,” and ergon, “work,” and means wrongdoing done without effort. A related noun stands at 18:14. Huie diabolou, “son of the devil,” is said to a man whose own name was Bar-Jesus: bar is Aramaic for “son,” as in “Simon Bar-Jonah” (Matthew 16:17), and the Greek spells Iēsous, “Jesus,” the name this translation prints “Joshua” where the man of the Old Testament is meant (Acts 7:45; Joshua). “Son of Jesus” beside “son of the devil” is a contrast Luke does not point at, and neither does this page. The straight ways recall Hosea 14:10 (not yet on these pages), where “the ways of Jehovah are right” (Hebrew yesharim darkei YHWH); the translation prints “Jehovah” for kyriou here because the phrase is the Old Testament’s. The apparatus gives hodous kyriou to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont and hodous tou kyriou, with the article, to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28.

“The hand of Jehovah is upon you.” Cheir kyriou, “hand of the Lord,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: Luke 1:66 of the child John, Acts 11:21, where it is “with them” and a great number believe, and this verse, where it is “upon you.” The hand is “with” the child, “with” the Antioch believers, and here “upon” Elymas. Achlys, “mist,” stands in this verse alone. The man goes about zētei cheiragōgous, “seeking hand-leaders” (dictionary); the verb cheiragōgeō and the noun cheiragōgos stand in three verses: Acts 9:8, where the blinded Saul is led by the hand into Damascus, this verse, and Paul’s retelling at 22:11 (not yet on these pages). The man struck blind seeks leaders by the hand, as Saul himself was led. The apparatus gives de after parachrēma to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont and te to Nestle–Aland 28, and epesen to those three and epepesen to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV and ASV “the hand of the Lord is upon thee,” NWT 1984 “Jehovah’s hand is upon you,” NIV “the hand of the Lord is against you,” which turns “upon” into “against,” TLB “God has laid his hand of punishment upon you.” Achri kairou is “until a time”: KJV and ASV “for a season,” NWT 1984 “for a period of time,” NIV “for a time.” Luke does not say how long, and does not say that the man’s sight returns.

“Astonished at the teaching of the Lord.” The proconsul episteusen, “believed,” ekplēssomenos epi tē didachē tou kyriou, “being astonished at the teaching of the Lord.” The construction — astonished at the teaching — stands in six verses of the New Testament, and five are the crowds at Jesus: Matthew 7:28, 22:33, Mark 1:22, 11:18 and Luke 4:32. This verse is the only one that uses it outside the Gospels. Tou kyriou can be the teaching that the Lord gives or the teaching about the Lord, and the Greek leaves it open (see v2); this translation keeps “Lord.” The thing that astonishes him is to gegonos, “what had happened” — and Luke puts the astonishment on the teaching, not on the blinding. KJV “astonished at the doctrine of the Lord,” ASV “astonished at the teaching of the Lord,” NIV “amazed at the teaching about the Lord,” which chooses the second sense, NWT 1984 “astounded at the teaching of Jehovah,” TLB “astonished at the power of God’s message,” which changes the object to a power. With the proconsul’s belief the first stop of the journey ends, and Luke does not report what became of Bar-Jesus.

Verses 13–15 · οἱ περὶ Παῦλον … Πέργην … Ἰωάννης … ἀποχωρήσας … Ἀντιόχειαν τὴν Πισιδίαν … ἀρχισυνάγωγοι … Ἄνδρες ἀδελφοί … λόγος παρακλήσεως hoi peri Paulon … Pergēn … Iōannēs … apochōrēsas … Antiocheian tēn Pisidian … archisynagōgoi … Andres adelphoi … logos paraklēseōs — Perga and Pisidian Antioch, Acts 13:13–15: John leaves, the rulers of the synagogue, and a “word of encouragement”

“Paul and those with him,” and John who withdraws. Hoi peri Paulon, literally “those around Paul,” is the first time Luke names Paul as the head of the company: until v9 the order was “Barnabas and Saul” (see the note on vv1–3). The ship is anachthentes, “put out to sea,” the verb of Luke 8:22, where the disciples “put out” on the lake. The Greek names Perga as a city “of Pamphylia” (Pamphylia) and says no more of it. Then the shortest report in the chapter: Iōannēs de apochōrēsas ap’ autōn hypestrepsen eis Hierosolyma, “but John, withdrawing from them, returned to Jerusalem.” Luke gives no reason and no judgment. The John who was their attendant at v5 is John Mark (John Mark), whose mother’s house stood in Jerusalem (12:12); Paul will name this departure later in other words, saying that Mark “deserted them in Pamphylia and had not gone with them to the work” (15:38), where the verb is apostanta and not the one used here, and Barnabas will then want to take him along again (15:37). KJV “loosed from Paphos,” ASV “set sail from Paphos,” NWT 1984 “put out to sea from Paphos,” NIV “sailed to Perga,” TLB “left Paphos by ship for Turkey,” a modern country in place of a first-century province. For John: KJV “John departing from them returned to Jerusalem,” ASV “John departed from them,” NWT 1984 “John withdrew from them,” NIV “where John left them,” TLB “John Mark deserted them,” which uses Paul’s later word and the second name together. The apparatus gives hoi peri to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and hoi peri ton, with the article, to Robinson–Pierpont.

“Pisidian Antioch,” and the synagogue on the sabbath. The Greek is Antiocheian tēn Pisidian, literally “Antioch the Pisidian,” an adjective that tells this city from the Antioch of v1 and of 11:19–26 (Antioch of Pisidia). The apparatus gives that adjective to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and tēs Pisidias, “of Pisidia,” to Robinson–Pierpont. NIV “Pisidian Antioch” keeps the adjective; KJV and Douay “Antioch in Pisidia,” ASV “Antioch of Pisidia,” Geneva “Antioch a city of Pisidia,” TLB “a city in the province of Pisidia,” which adds a province. They went in on tē hēmera tōn sabbatōn, literally “the day of the sabbaths,” and sat down, as Jesus sat down in the synagogue at Luke 4:20. The apparatus gives eiselthontes, “going in,” to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and elthontes, “coming,” to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles.

“After the reading of the Law and the Prophets.” This is Luke’s one description of the order of a synagogue gathering in Acts: a reading from nomos and from the prophētai, and then those in charge. He gives the same two readings in 15:21, where Moses is “read aloud in the synagogues every sabbath,” and in v27 below, where the voices of the prophets are “read every sabbath”; in the Gospel Jesus is handed the scroll of Isaiah at Nazareth (Luke 4:16–17). The archisynagōgoi, “rulers of the synagogue” (dictionary), stand in nine verses of the New Testament: four of Jairus’s house in Mark 5 (vv22, 35, 36, 38), Luke 8:49, Luke 13:14, this verse, and 18:8 and 18:17. They apesteilan, “sent,” to the visitors and did not address them in person. Their words begin Andres adelphoi, “Men, brothers,” the address used at 1:16, 2:29 and, with “fathers” added, at 7:2. They ask for a logos paraklēseōs, “word of encouragement.” Paraklēsis (dictionary) is the word in the name Barnabas, “son of encouragement” (4:36), and the phrase logos paraklēseōs stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Hebrews 13:22 (not yet on these pages). The man who stands up in v16 is Paul, and Luke does not remark on the coincidence of the word. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “any word of exhortation,” NIV “a word of exhortation,” NWT 1984 “word of encouragement,” TLB “any word of instruction,” a different kind of word. For the rulers: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “the rulers of the synagogue,” NIV “the leaders of the synagogue,” NWT 1984 “the presiding officers of the synagogue,” TLB “those in charge of the service.” The apparatus gives ei tis, “if any,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and ei without tis to Robinson–Pierpont; and the order en hymin logos, “among you a word,” to those three, with logos en hymin to Robinson–Pierpont.

Verses 16–25 · κατασείσας τῇ χειρί … οἱ φοβούμενοι τὸν θεόν … ὕψωσεν … παροικίᾳ … βραχίονος ὑψηλοῦ … ἐτροποφόρησεν … κατεκληρονόμησεν … ἔτεσι τετρακοσίοις καὶ πεντήκοντα … Εὗρον Δαυίδ … σωτῆρα Ἰησοῦν … προκηρύξαντος … τὸν δρόμον kataseisas tē cheiri … hoi phoboumenoi ton theon … hypsōsen … paroikia … brachionos hypsēlou … etropophorēsen … kateklēronomēsen … etesi tetrakosiois kai pentēkonta … Heuron Dauid … sōtēra Iēsoun … prokēryxantos … ton dromon — Paul’s speech at Antioch, Acts 13:16–25: Israel’s history in one breath, “a man after my heart,” the 450 years, and John fulfilling his course

The hand, and “you who fear God.” Kataseisas tē cheiri, “motioning with his hand,” is the gesture Peter used at 12:17, and the verb stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 12:17, this verse, 19:33 and 21:40 (the last two not yet on these pages). Paul addresses two groups: Andres Israēlitai, “Men, Israelites,” and hoi phoboumenoi ton theon, “those who fear God.” The second expression stands in four verses, all in Acts: 10:2 and 10:22, of Cornelius, and v16 and v26 here. Peter treats Cornelius as one “of another tribe” (10:28), so the phrase can name a person outside Israel who worships Israel’s God; the Greek here does not itself say “Gentile.” NIV prints “you Gentiles who worship God” at v16 and “you God-fearing Gentiles” at v26, and so adds the word; KJV and ASV “ye that fear God” leave it out; NWT 1984 “YOU [others] that fear God,” marking its own addition with brackets; TLB “all others here who reverence God.” Whether “God-fearers” were a recognized category in synagogues, apart from the proselytes of v43, is a question historians have argued; the verse does not settle it. For the rest of the verse, ASV “beckoning with the hand,” Douay “with his hand bespeaking silence,” NWT 1984 “motioning with his hand,” TLB “waved a greeting,” which turns a call for quiet into a greeting.

“Chose,” “exalted,” “an uplifted arm.” The sentence gives God three verbs: exelexato, “chose,” the fathers; hypsōsen, “exalted” (dictionary), the people; and exēgagen, “led out,” the verb of 5:19, 7:36 and 12:17. The “stay” in Egypt is paroikia, which stands in this verse and 1 Peter 1:17 and nowhere else in the New Testament; its relatives are Stephen’s paroikon, “resident alien,” for the seed of Abraham in a foreign land (7:6), and Moses’s own condition in Midian (7:29); this translation prints “resident aliens” in all three. The arm is brachionos hypsēlou, “high” or “uplifted.” The Hebrew phrase for the same deed is “an outstretched arm,” bizroa netuyah, as in Exodus 6:6; the Greek “high arm” is a phrase that belongs to the Greek Old Testament, a report this page does not check, since the site holds no Greek Old Testament. KJV, Geneva and Douay “an high arm,” ASV “a high arm,” NWT 1984 “an uplifted arm,” NIV “with mighty power,” and TLB “gloriously leading them out of their slavery,” which drops the arm and adds “slavery.” The apparatus leaves out the word “Israel” in “this people Israel” for Robinson–Pierpont, and gives Aigyptou, the genitive, to the other three editions and Aigyptō, the dative, to Robinson–Pierpont.

“Bore with their ways” — one letter apart. Etropophorēsen stands in this verse alone. Built on tropos, “way,” and pherō, “to bear,” it means to put up with someone’s ways. A verb that differs by one letter, trophophoreō, “to carry as a nurse carries,” is the verb of the Greek Old Testament’s Deuteronomy 1:31 (reported; the site holds no Greek Old Testament), and the Hebrew of that verse says “the LORD your God bore you (nesa’akha), as a man bears his son” (Deuteronomy 1:31). The apparatus printed with this text records no variant at this word, and the shelf is divided between the two senses: ASV “as a nursing-father bare he them” and TLB “he nursed them” take the carrying sense; KJV and Geneva “suffered he their manners,” Douay “endured their manners,” NIV “endured their conduct” and NWT 1984 “put up with their manner of action” take the enduring sense. This translation prints “bore with their ways” for the verb the apparatus prints, and the range goes in this note. The forty years are Stephen’s forty years (7:36, 7:42), and the adjective tesserakontaetēs, “of forty years,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, both in Acts: here and 7:23, where Moses reaches forty years of age.

Seven nations, an inheritance, and 450 years. The seven nations are the list of Deuteronomy 7:1: the Hittite, Girgashite, Amorite, Canaanite, Perizzite, Hivite and Jebusite; Joshua 3:10 names the same seven in another order. Luke does not list them, and neither does Stephen, who speaks only of “the nations whom God drove out” (7:45). Kateklēronomēsen stands in this verse alone. It is built on klēronomos, “heir,” and means to make someone an heir of something: “gave as an inheritance.” “By lot” would be built on klēros, a different word: KJV and Geneva “divided their land to them by lot,” Douay “divided their land among them, by lot,” and NWT 1984 “distributed the land of them by lot” print it, while ASV “gave them their land for an inheritance,” NIV “giving their land to his people as their inheritance” and TLB “gave Israel their land as an inheritance” print the sense of the verb that stands here. The apparatus gives kai before kathelōn to Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont and leaves it out for Westcott–Hort, and it adds autois, “to them,” after the verb for Robinson–Pierpont.

The number is where the editions part. Hōs etesi tetrakosiois kai pentēkonta, “for about four hundred and fifty years,” stands before kai meta tauta, “and after these things,” in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, where it measures the time from the choosing of the fathers to the inheritance of the land; and after it in Robinson–Pierpont, where it measures the judges. That is why the shelf divides. ASV “for about four hundred and fifty years,” NWT 1984 “all that during about four hundred and fifty years” and NIV “all this took about 450 years” attach the number to what comes before; KJV “he gave unto them judges about the space of four hundred and fifty years,” Geneva “he gave unto them Judges about four hundred and fifty years” and TLB “Judges ruled for about four hundred and fifty years” attach it to the judges; and Douay “as it were, after four hundred and fifty years” gives the number a place of its own. The Hebrew text of 1 Kings 6:1 puts the building of the temple in “the four hundred and eightieth year after the children of Israel were come out of the land of Egypt” (not yet on these pages), and how Paul’s “about” relates to that total is a question this page reports and does not decide. The apparatus gives the article before “prophet” (tou prophētou) to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and leaves it out for Westcott–Hort and Tregelles.

Saul, forty years, and a man after my heart. The king is Saoul huion Kis, “Saul the son of Kish, a man of the tribe of Benjamin” (the Hebrew of 1 Samuel 9:1 has “a man of Benjamin, whose name was Kish”). Paul, who is “Saul, who is also Paul,” is telling the story of the first king of his own tribe, and the Greek spells King Saul as it spells the apostle’s Hebrew name in 9:4 and 9:17: Saoul. (That Paul himself was of Benjamin is said at Romans 11:1 and Philippians 3:5, neither yet on these pages.) The “forty years” is Paul’s figure. The Hebrew of 1 Samuel 13:1, where the length of Saul’s reign is given, has ben-shanah, literally “a son of a year,” for his age at his accession and “two years” for his reign, and the number forty does not stand there. The next clause puts three Old Testament sentences into one saying of God. Heuron Dauid, “I have found David,” is Psalm 89:21 in the Hebrew numbering, “I have found David My servant”; andra kata tēn kardian mou, “a man after my heart,” is 1 Samuel 13:14, where Samuel tells Saul that Jehovah “has sought out for himself a man after his own heart,” in the third person; and hos poiēsei panta ta thelēmata mou, “who will do all my wishes,” is Isaiah 44:28, where the one who “shall perform all My pleasure” is Cyrus. “A savior, Jesus,” and John’s course. Sōtēr, “savior” (dictionary), stands in Acts at 5:31, where God exalts Jesus “as Author and Savior,” and in this verse only; the Gospel has it at Luke 2:11. The verb printed here is ēgagen, “brought”; KJV “raised unto Israel,” Geneva and Douay “raised up to Israel,” and so print which is the English of the verb Paul uses of David in v22, ēgeiren, and not of the one printed here. ASV “brought unto Israel,” NIV “brought to Israel,” NWT 1984 “has brought to Israel a savior, Jesus.” The apparatus gives sōtēra Iēsoun, “a savior, Jesus,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and sōtērian, “salvation,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The harbinger is John: prokēryxantos, “having proclaimed beforehand,” stands in this verse alone, and the phrase pro prosōpou, “before the face of,” stands in six verses of the New Testament, five of them a messenger sent ahead — Matthew 11:10, Mark 1:2, Luke 7:27, 9:52 and 10:1 — and this one. The “baptism of repentance” is the one of Luke 3:3; Paul says it was proclaimed “to all the people of Israel,” and the apparatus gives panti tō laō to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and tō laō, without “all,” to Robinson–Pierpont.

Hōs de eplērou Iōannēs ton dromon, “as John was fulfilling his course.” Dromos stands in three verses of the New Testament, and the other two are Paul’s own words about himself: Acts 20:24, “to finish my course,” and 2 Timothy 4:7, “I have finished the course” (neither yet on these pages). The saying Paul quotes is Ti eme hyponoeite einai?, “What do you suppose me to be?” The apparatus gives ti eme to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and tina me, “whom,” to Robinson–Pierpont: the KJV “Whom think ye that I am?” follows the second; ASV “What suppose ye that I am?” and NWT 1984 “What do YOU suppose I am?” follow the first, while NIV “Who do you suppose I am?” puts a person where the printed word says “what,” and TLB “Do you think I am the Messiah?” names the question. The Gospels give John’s answer differently: Luke 3:15–16 has the people wondering whether he might be the Christ and John promising “the strap of whose sandals I am not worthy to untie”; Matthew 3:11 has “whose sandals I am not worthy to carry”; and here it is to hypodēma tōn podōn lysai, “the sandal of whose feet.” John’s “I am not he” answers the same question as John 1:20, where he confesses, “I am not the Messiah.” The apparatus gives Iōannēs to those three editions and ho Iōannēs to Robinson–Pierpont.

Verses 26–37 · ἡμῖν ὁ λόγος τῆς σωτηρίας ταύτης ἐξαπεστάλη … ἀγνοήσαντες … ἐπλήρωσαν … ἀπὸ τοῦ ξύλου … ἐκπεπλήρωκεν … ἀναστήσας … ψαλμῷ … τῷ δευτέρῳ … τὰ ὅσια Δαυὶδ τὰ πιστά … οὐ δώσεις τὸν ὅσιόν σου ἰδεῖν διαφθοράν … ἐκοιμήθη hēmin ho logos tēs sōtērias tautēs exapestalē … agnoēsantes … eplērōsan … apo tou xylou … ekpeplērōken … anastēsas … psalmō … tō deuterō … ta hosia Dauid ta pista … ou dōseis ton hosion sou idein diaphthoran … ekoimēthē — Acts 13:26–37: the word of salvation, the tree, “you are my Son,” the “holy things of David,” and a David who saw decay

“To us the word of this salvation has been sent out.” The apparatus gives hēmin, “to us,” to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28, and hymin, “to you,” to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont, which is where the shelf splits: KJV “to you is the word of this salvation sent,” and Geneva and Douay “to you,” with the same sense; ASV “to us is the word of this salvation sent forth,” NIV “it is to us that this message of salvation has been sent,” NWT 1984 “has been sent forth to us,” TLB “this salvation is for all of us.” The verb is exapestalē in the first three editions and apestalē in Robinson–Pierpont. Exapostellō, “send out,” stands in seven verses of Acts: 7:12, 9:30, 11:22, where Barnabas is sent out to Antioch, 12:11, where Jehovah sent out his angel, this verse, and 17:14 and 22:21 (the second not yet on these pages). The word of salvation is sent out as men and an angel were. The noun sōtēria, “salvation,” of this verse is the sōtēr, “savior,” of v23 in another form, and it comes again in the quotation of v47.

Ignorance, voices, and “passing judgment.” Agnoēsantes, “being ignorant of,” has two objects, “this one” and “the voices of the prophets that are read every sabbath”; the second repeats v15 and 15:21. Peter said the same of the same people: “you acted in ignorance, as did your rulers also” (3:17), and the verb that follows here, eplērōsan, “fulfilled,” is the verb of 3:18, where God “fulfilled” what the prophets announced. The Greek says krinantes, “having judged,” and gives it no object; “by passing judgment” keeps that. Most of the shelf supplies one: KJV “they have fulfilled them in condemning him,” ASV “fulfilled them by condemning him,” Geneva “they have fulfilled them in condemning him,” Douay “judging him have fulfilled them,” NIV “in condemning him they fulfilled the words of the prophets,” and TLB “fulfilled prophecy by killing Jesus,” which changes the verb. NWT 1984 “when acting as judges, they fulfilled the things voiced by the Prophets” supplies no object. The phrase mēdemian aitian thanatou, “no ground for death,” stands in this verse and at 28:18, where Paul says the Romans found none in him (not yet on these pages); the Gospel has the related noun at Luke 23:22, “no guilt deserving of death.” The Greek does not say “in him,” and four versions add it: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “no cause of death in him.” NIV “no proper ground for a death sentence” and NWT 1984 “they found no cause for death” do not. Pilate was the man who “had decided to release him” (3:13; Pontius Pilate). Anairethēnai, “be killed,” is the verb of 10:39, “whom they also killed, hanging him on a tree.”

“From the tree,” and who took him down. Eteleisan panta ta peri autou gegrammena, “they completed all the things written about him,” uses the verb of John 19:28 and 19:30, “accomplished” and “finished,” and of Luke 18:31, where everything written by the prophets “will be accomplished.” Xylon (dictionary) is “wood” or “tree,” and it is Peter’s word at 5:30 and 10:39; it echoes the Hebrew of Deuteronomy 21:22–23, where the body of a man put to death is hung al-ets, “on a tree,” and “he that is hanged is a reproach unto God” (qilelat Elohim talui). Galatians 3:13 quotes that verse of Christ (not yet on these pages). KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “tree,” NIV “cross,” NWT 1984 “stake.” The grammatical subject of “took him down” and “laid” is the same “they” as in vv27–28, the people of Jerusalem and their rulers. The Gospel gives the burial to another man: Joseph of Arimathea, who “taking it down” — the same verb, kathelōn — “laid him in a tomb cut into the rock” (Luke 23:53). Paul’s summary is a summary, and this page sets the two accounts side by side and does not join them. TLB “he was taken from the cross and placed in a tomb” leaves the subject out altogether; NWT 1984 “laid him in a memorial tomb.”

“But God raised him,” and the witnesses who went up. The next sentence is a single clause with a contrast: ho de theos ēgeiren auton ek nekrōn, “but God raised him from the dead.” He ōphthē epi hēmeras pleious, “was seen for many days,” the forty of Acts 1:3, by those who synanabasin, “had gone up with him” from Galilee. Synanabainō stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Mark 15:41, where the women who followed him in Galilee include “many other women who had come up with him to Jerusalem.” Here the participle is masculine plural, which in Greek can cover a mixed group. They are now martyres, “witnesses” (1:22), and the apparatus leaves out the word nyn, “now,” for Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “who are his witnesses” has no “now”; ASV “who are now his witnesses,” NWT 1984 “who are now his witnesses” and NIV “they are now his witnesses” have it.

“We bring you the good news,” and “raised up Jesus.” Euangelizometha, “we bring the good news,” is the verb of 5:42, 8:4, 8:35 and 11:20, and the promise is epangelia, as at 2:39, where “the promise is for you and for your children.” Ekpeplērōken, “has completely fulfilled,” carries ek-, “out,” and stands in this verse alone as a verb; a noun from the same root, ekplērōsis, stands at 21:26 (not yet on these pages). The apparatus gives tois teknois hēmōn, “to our children,” to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles, and tois teknois autōn hēmin, “to their children, to us,” to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. ASV “unto our children” and Douay “to our children” print the first; KJV and Geneva “unto us their children,” NWT 1984 “to us their children” and NIV “for us, their children” print the second. TLB “has come true in our own time” paraphrases both.

Anastēsas Iēsoun, “raised up Jesus,” can be read three ways, and this page does not choose between them. It can mean the resurrection; but the same verb is used of God bringing someone onto the stage of history, as Moses promised God would “raise up for you a prophet” (3:22, 7:37) and as Peter said that God, “having raised up his servant, sent him to you first” (3:26); and v34 begins again with hoti de anestēsen auton ek nekrōn, “and that he raised him from the dead,” as a fresh point, which some read as meaning that v33 has the first sense and v34 the second, and others as meaning that both speak of the resurrection. The shelf has chosen: NWT 1984 “in that he resurrected Jesus” and TLB “in that God brought Jesus back to life again” take the resurrection; KJV “in that he hath raised up Jesus again” adds “again”; ASV “in that he raised up Jesus” and NIV “by raising up Jesus” leave it open, as this translation does. The text quoted is Huios mou ei su, egō sēmeron gegennēka se, “You are my Son; today I have begotten you,” Psalm 2:7 (the Hebrew yelidtikha, “I have begotten you”; the psalm itself is not yet on these pages, though its first two verses are quoted at Acts 4:25–26), which Hebrews also quotes at Hebrews 1:5, where this translation prints “begotten” too. NWT 1984 “I have become your Father” and NIV “today I have become your father” turn “begotten” into a role; KJV and ASV print “begotten.” The psalm is the “second” in the apparatus of Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 (psalmō gegraptai tō deuterō) and of Robinson–Pierpont, which puts the verb after (psalmō tō deuterō gegraptai); Tregelles reads prōtō psalmō gegraptai, “in the first psalm,” a reading usually explained by copies that counted Psalms 1 and 2 as a single psalm, a report this page does not check. All seven English versions on the shelf print “second.”

“The holy things of David, the faithful ones” — and “your holy one.” Mēketi mellonta hypostrephein eis diaphthoran, “no longer about to return to decay,” opens the series of quotations that makes Paul’s argument, and diaphthora (dictionary), “decay,” is the word that carries it: it stands in six verses of the New Testament, all in Acts, two in Peter’s sermon (2:27 and 2:31) and four in vv34–37 here. The first quotation is Dōsō hymin ta hosia Dauid ta pista, Isaiah 55:3, which in the Hebrew reads chasdei David ha-ne’emanim, “the sure mercies of David” (the Hebrew edition on this site prints “the sure mercies of David”); the surrounding verse, “I will make an everlasting covenant with you,” Acts does not quote, and the “you” there is plural, as it is here. Hosia (dictionary) are the “holy” or “devoted” things, and the second quotation, Psalm 16:10, has the same Greek root in ton hosion sou, “your holy one,” for the Hebrew chasidkha; chasdei and chasidkha share the Hebrew root as hosia and hosion share the Greek, and that shared root is a common explanation of why the sermon joins the two with “because,” a reading this page reports and does not check. Peter quoted Psalm 16:10 and made the same point about David’s tomb (2:29). The Hebrew word for what the holy one is not to see is shachat, which the Hebrew edition on this site prints “the pit”; the Greek is diaphthora, “decay.” The shelf for v34: ASV “the holy and sure blessings of David,” KJV “the sure mercies of David,” NIV “the holy and sure blessings promised to David,” NWT 1984 “the loving-kindnesses to David that are faithful,” Douay “the holy things of David faithful,” Geneva “the holy things of David, which are faithful,” TLB “the wonderful thing I promised David.” For v35: KJV “Thou shalt not suffer thine Holy One to see corruption,” ASV “Thou wilt not give Thy Holy One to see corruption,” NIV “You will not let your holy one see decay,” NWT 1984 “You will not allow your loyal one to see corruption,” TLB “God will not let his Holy One decay.” The link is diotī, “because”: the apparatus gives it to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and dio, “wherefore,” to Robinson–Pierpont; ASV “Because he saith also in another psalm” prints the first and KJV “Wherefore he saith also in another psalm” the second. The Greek says only en heterō, “in another”: KJV, ASV, NWT 1984 and TLB supply “psalm,” Geneva and Douay “in another place,” NIV “elsewhere.” The apparatus adds the article (en tō) for Robinson–Pierpont.

David “fell asleep,” and the one who did not see decay. David men gar is “for David, on the one hand,” and v37 supplies the other hand: hon de ho theos ēgeiren, “but he whom God raised.” David ekoimēthē, “fell asleep” (dictionary), as Stephen did (7:60); the verb stands in Acts at 7:60, 12:6, where it means “sleeping,” and here. He prosetethē pros tous pateras autou, literally “was added to his fathers,” and then eiden diaphthoran, “saw decay.” The Greek has two datives that can be read two ways: idia genea hypēretēsas tē tou theou boulē is “having served in his own generation the counsel of God” or “having served his own generation by the counsel of God.” This translation prints the first. ASV “after he had in his own generation served the counsel of God,” NIV “when David had served God’s purpose in his own generation,” and NWT 1984 “served the express will of God in his own generation” take it that way; KJV “after he had served his own generation by the will of God,” Geneva “after he had served his time by the counsel of God,” Douay “when he had served in his generation, according to the will of God” and TLB “after David had served his generation according to the will of God” take the other. For the burial: KJV and ASV “was laid unto his fathers,” Geneva “was laid with his fathers,” NWT 1984 “was laid with his forefathers,” NIV “he was buried with his ancestors,” TLB “he died and was buried.” Luke has Peter say the same of the same man: “he both died and was buried, and his tomb is among us to this day” (2:29).

Verses 38–41 · γνωστὸν οὖν ἔστω ὑμῖν … ἄφεσις ἁμαρτιῶν καταγγέλλεται … δικαιωθῆναι … δικαιοῦται … οἱ καταφρονηταί … ἔργον ἐργάζομαι … ἐκδιηγῆται gnōston oun estō hymin … aphesis hamartiōn katangelletai … dikaiōthēnai … dikaioutai … hoi kataphronētai … ergon ergazomai … ekdiēgētai — Acts 13:38–41: forgiveness proclaimed, “justified,” and Habakkuk’s warning to the despisers

“Let it be known to you,” and forgiveness proclaimed. Gnōston oun estō hymin, “let it therefore be known to you,” is the formula of 2:14 and 4:10, a speaker marking the point of the whole speech; here it falls after the proof and before the offer. The offer is aphesis hamartiōn, “forgiveness of sins,” katangelletai, “is being proclaimed,” the verb of v5 in the passive, and the object of the preaching Jesus names at Luke 24:47: “repentance and forgiveness of sins would be proclaimed in his name to all the nations.” The phrase stands in Acts at 2:38 (with “your”), 5:31, 10:43, this verse, and 26:18 (the last not yet on these pages). NIV “through Jesus the forgiveness of sins is proclaimed to you” names the one the Greek calls toutou, “this one.”

“Justified” — and a sentence that wraps around the verse break. Dikaioō (dictionary) is the verb of the verdict, “to declare righteous,” and in Acts it stands in these two verses and nowhere else; elsewhere it is the verb of Luke’s “you are the ones who justify yourselves” (Luke 16:15) and of the tax collector who went down “justified” (Luke 18:14), and of the letters, as at Romans 8:30 and 8:33. The Greek sentence is built across the verse division: kai apo pantōn hōn ouk ēdynēthēte en nomō Mōuseōs dikaiōthēnai, en toutō pas ho pisteuōn dikaioutai, “and from all the things from which you were not able to be justified by the Law of Moses, in this one everyone who believes is justified.” The words “from all the things” belong to the last verb as well as the first: everyone who believes is justified from all of them. This translation keeps the Greek order, so that the reader meets the clause as the hearers did, and the shelf has to move it. NIV “everyone who believes is set free from every sin,” which turns the verdict-verb into “set free” and names “sin” where the Greek has “things.” NWT 1984 prints “declared guiltless” at both places, TLB “freed from all guilt and declared righteous.” The apparatus adds the article (en tō nomō) for Robinson–Pierpont.

“Watch out,” and Habakkuk’s despisers. Blepete oun, “watch out, then,” turns to a warning, and the warning is a verse from “the Prophets,” the book Luke’s hearers would have known as part of the Twelve: Habakkuk 1:5. The Hebrew there reads re’u vaggoyim vehabbitu, vehittammehu temahu, “look among the nations, and behold, and be astounded, astounded” (the Hebrew edition on this site prints “Look ye among the nations, and behold, and wonder marvellously”), and the next verse names the work: “I raise up the Chaldeans.” Acts follows a Greek form of the verse: idete hoi kataphronētai, “look, you despisers,” for the Hebrew “among the nations,” and thaumasate kai aphanisthēte, “wonder and vanish,” for the doubled “be astounded.” The Greek of the Old Testament is where that form is reported to come from, a point this page does not check, since the site holds no Greek Old Testament. Kataphronētēs, “despiser,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. The saying keeps one Hebrew figure intact: ergon ergazomai egō, “I am doing a work” for po’al po’el, a work worked. And “you will never believe” (ou mē pisteusēte) “if anyone tells it in full” (ekdiēgētai): the verb ekdiēgeomai stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 15:3, where Paul and Barnabas pass through Phoenicia and Samaria “describing in detail the conversion of the nations.” The shelf for the quotation: KJV and ASV “Behold, ye despisers, and wonder, and perish,” Douay “Behold, ye despisers, and wonder, and perish,” Geneva “and wonder, and vanish away,” NIV “Look, you scoffers, wonder and perish,” NWT 1984 “Behold it, YOU scorners, and wonder at it, and vanish away,” TLB “you despisers of the truth,” with “of the truth” added. At the end: KJV “ye shall in no wise believe,” NWT 1984 “will by no means believe,” NIV “that you would never believe.” The apparatus adds eph’ hymas, “upon you,” after epelthē for Robinson–Pierpont, where the other three editions leave the “you” for the English to supply; it gives ergazomai egō to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and egō ergazomai to Robinson–Pierpont; and it leaves the second ergon, “a work,” out of Robinson–Pierpont.

Verses 42–47 · εἰς τὸ μεταξὺ σάββατον … τῶν σεβομένων προσηλύτων … προσμένειν τῇ χάριτι … ζήλου … ἀντέλεγον … βλασφημοῦντες … παρρησιασάμενοι … ἀπωθεῖσθε … στρεφόμεθα εἰς τὰ ἔθνη … τέθεικά σε εἰς φῶς ἐθνῶν eis to metaxy sabbaton … tōn sebomenōn prosēlytōn … prosmenein tē chariti … zēlou … antelegon … blasphēmountes … parrhēsiasamenoi … apōtheisthe … strephometha eis ta ethnē … tetheika se eis phōs ethnōn — Acts 13:42–47: the next sabbath, the jealousy, “we are turning to the nations,” and a light to the end of the earth

“The next sabbath,” and who asked. The printed Greek has no named subject for parekaloun, “were urging”: Exiontōn de autōn parekaloun, “and as they were going out, [they] were urging.” English needs a subject, and this translation supplies “people.” The apparatus shows how much hangs on it. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print autōn alone; the NIV’s Greek reads autōn ek tēs synagōgēs, “as they were going out of the synagogue”; and Robinson–Pierpont reads ek tēs synagōgēs tōn Ioudaiōn, “out of the synagogue of the Jews,” and then parekaloun ta ethnē, “the nations were urging,” and leaves out tauta, “these.” That last reading is the source of the KJV: “when the Jews were gone out of the synagogue, the Gentiles besought that these words might be preached to them the next sabbath,” and of Geneva: “when they were come out of the Synagogue of the Jews, the Gentiles besought.” The others do not have a “Gentiles”: ASV “as they went out, they besought that these words might be spoken to them the next sabbath,” Douay “as they went out, they desired them, that on the next sabbath, they would speak unto them these words,” NWT 1984 “the people began entreating for these matters to be spoken to them on the following sabbath,” NIV “the people invited them to speak further about these things on the next Sabbath,” TLB “they asked Paul to return and speak to them again the next week,” which turns a sabbath into a week and adds “again.” The date is to metaxy sabbaton, literally “the sabbath between.” Metaxy stands in nine verses of the New Testament, in Acts at 12:6, “between two soldiers,” here, and 15:9, and only here is it used as an adjective before a noun. Six of the seven English versions on the shelf say “next” or “following” sabbath, and the TLB says “the next week”; “in the interval,” meaning during the week between, is a possible sense of the same words, and v44 then says tō erchomenō sabbatō, “on the coming sabbath,” in different words.

“Worshiping proselytes,” and the grace to remain in. Lytheisēs de tēs synagōgēs, “when the synagogue was dismissed,” uses synagōgē of the gathering and not of the building, and the three versions that print “congregation” for it — KJV “when the congregation was broken up,” Geneva “when the congregation was dissolved” and NIV “when the congregation was dismissed” — use the word this translation keeps for ekklēsia, a different Greek word. ASV “when the synagogue broke up,” Douay “when the synagogue was broken up,” NWT 1984 “after the synagogue assembly was dissolved.” Those who follow are “many of the Jews and of the sebomenōn prosēlytōn.” Prosēlytos (dictionary) stands in four verses of the New Testament: Matthew 23:15, Acts 2:11, 6:5, where Nicolaus is “a proselyte of Antioch,” and here. The participle sebomenos, “worshiping,” stands in six verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 13:43, 13:50, 16:14, 17:4, 17:17 and 18:7. Luke thus has three expressions that overlap: phoboumenoi ton theon, “those who fear God” (vv16, 26); sebomenoi prosēlytoi, “worshiping proselytes” (v43); and sebomenai gynaikes, “worshiping women” (v50). The Greek does not say how they differ, and this page does not decide. They are urged prosmenein tē chariti tou theou, “to remain in the grace of God”: the same verb and almost the same words Barnabas used at Antioch, where he “saw the grace of God” and was “encouraging them all to remain with the Lord” (11:23). The apparatus gives prosmenein to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and epimenein, “to continue,” to Robinson–Pierpont, and leaves autois, “to them,” out of the Robinson–Pierpont text. Six of the seven English versions on the shelf say “continue”: KJV “persuaded them to continue in the grace of God,” NIV “urged them to continue in the grace of God,” NWT 1984 “urging them to continue in the undeserved kindness of God,” and ASV, Geneva and Douay “continue in the grace of God”; TLB “urged them to accept the mercies God was offering” changes “remain” to “accept.” Epeithon is an imperfect, “were urging” or “were trying to persuade,” and says nothing of whether they succeeded.

“Filled with jealousy.” Idontes de hoi Ioudaioi tous ochlous eplēsthēsan zēlou, “but the Jews, seeing the crowds, were filled with jealousy,” uses the words with which Luke describes the high priest and the Sadducees when the apostles are healing in Jerusalem (5:17); the two verses are the only ones in the New Testament with eplēsthēsan zēlou. Zēlos is “jealousy” or “zeal,” and the word can be either; “jealousy” is what the occasion says, since what stirs them is the sight of the crowds. They antelegon, “were contradicting” (the verb of the sign “spoken against” at Luke 2:34; at Acts 4:14 the council “had nothing to say in reply,” with the aorist of the same verb; it returns at 28:19 and 28:22, not yet on these pages), and the participle is blasphēmountes, “blaspheming,” the word of the “blasphemous words” against Moses and God of 6:11. The apparatus gives blasphēmountes to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and antilegontes kai blasphēmountes, “contradicting and blaspheming,” to Robinson–Pierpont, and gives laloumenois, “being spoken,” to the first three and legomenois, “being said,” to Robinson–Pierpont; it adds the article before Paul’s name (hypo tou Paulou) for Robinson–Pierpont as well. KJV “filled with envy, and spake against those things which were spoken by Paul, contradicting and blaspheming,” ASV “filled with jealousy, and contradicted the things which were spoken by Paul, and blasphemed,” NWT 1984 “filled with jealousy and began blasphemously contradicting the things being spoken by Paul,” NIV “filled with jealousy. They began to contradict what Paul was saying and heaped abuse on him,” Geneva “full of envy, and spake against those things, which were spoken of Paul, contrarying them, and railing on them,” TLB “they were jealous, and cursed and argued against whatever Paul said.”

“It was necessary that the word of God be spoken to you first.” Parrhēsiasamenoi, “speaking boldly,” is the verb of 9:27 and 9:28, where Barnabas and then Saul speak boldly in the name of Jesus (dictionary); it stands in nine verses of the New Testament, seven of them in Acts. The order of address is hymin ēn anagkaion prōton, “to you it was necessary first.” “First” says what comes first in a sequence and is not a judgment of rank; the same order stands in Peter’s “God, having raised up his servant, sent him to you first” (3:26) and in Paul’s “to the Jew first and also to the Greek” (Romans 1:16; 2:10). The verb of rejection is apōtheisthe: “you push it away.” It is the verb of 7:27 and 7:39, where Israel “pushed away” Moses, and of Romans 11:1–2, “Has God pushed away his people?” (not yet on these pages); it stands in six verses of the New Testament, and this translation prints “push away” at each of the two in Acts 7. KJV “ye put it from you,” ASV “ye thrust it from you,” NWT 1984 “YOU are thrusting it away from YOU,” NIV “you reject it,” Douay “you reject it,” TLB “you have rejected it and shown yourselves unworthy of eternal life.” “Eternal life” stands in Acts only here and at v48. The apparatus gives te after parrhēsiasamenoi to the first three editions and de to Robinson–Pierpont, and it adds de after epeidē for Robinson–Pierpont.

“We are turning to the nations.” Idou strephometha eis ta ethnē: the verb is a present, “we are turning,” and it is said of this city, to these hearers, on this day. Whatever it means for the book, it is not said of the whole Jewish people: the speakers are themselves Jews, Barnabas a Levite (4:36); the hearers of v43 include “many of the Jews” who followed them; and the next stop, Iconium, begins with the pair going into the synagogue again (14:1). Paul says something like it again at 18:6 and 28:28, and at Ephesus, after the first of those, he is in the synagogue again (19:8; none of these yet on these pages). This verse has been used in Christian preaching and polemic over many centuries to say that the Jews as a people were cast off and replaced by the nations, and the opposition of one city’s synagogue crowd has been charged against all Jews; neither the Greek of these verses nor the sentences that follow them say it, and Paul’s own question about it is Romans 11:1, “Has God pushed away his people?” (not yet on these pages). NIV “we now turn to the Gentiles,” KJV, ASV, Geneva and TLB print “Gentiles”; NWT 1984 “look! we turn to the nations” is the only one of the seven English versions on the shelf that keeps ethnē as “nations,” as this translation does at 11:1.

“A light of nations,” to the end of the earth. Houtōs gar entetaltai hēmin ho kyrios, “for so Jehovah has commanded us”: the translation prints “Jehovah” for ho kyrios because the sentence introduces a quotation from the Old Testament, and the Greek says kyrios. The quotation is Tetheika se eis phōs ethnōn tou einai se eis sōtērian heōs eschatou tēs gēs, Isaiah 49:6. In the Hebrew that verse reads unetatikha le’or goyim, liheyot yeshu’ati ‘ad-qetseh ha’arets, “and I will give you for a light of the nations, that my salvation may be unto the end of the earth” (Isaiah 49 is not yet on these pages): the Hebrew has “I will give you” where Acts has “I have set you,” and “my salvation” where Acts has “salvation.” The words heōs eschatou tēs gēs, “to the end of the earth,” stand in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 1:8, where the risen Jesus tells the apostles they will be his witnesses “to the end of the earth.” The sentence has a singular “you” (the Servant of Isaiah) and a plural “us” (Paul and Barnabas), and Luke applies the same verse elsewhere to Jesus: Simeon calls the child “a light for revelation to the nations” (Luke 2:32), and Paul will say at 26:23 (not yet on these pages) that the Christ would proclaim light to the people and the nations. This page sets the uses side by side and does not decide between them. KJV “I have set thee to be a light of the Gentiles, that thou shouldest be for salvation unto the ends of the earth,” ASV “I have set thee for a light of the Gentiles, that thou shouldest be for salvation unto the uttermost part of the earth,” NIV “I have made you a light for the Gentiles, that you may bring salvation to the ends of the earth,” NWT 1984 “I have appointed you as a light of nations, for you to be a salvation to the extremity of the earth,” Geneva “to the end of the world,” Douay “unto the utmost part of the earth,” TLB “to lead them from the farthest corners of the earth to my salvation,” which turns the light into a guide and the salvation into a destination.

Verses 48–52 · ἐδόξαζον τὸν λόγον τοῦ κυρίου … ὅσοι ἦσαν τεταγμένοι εἰς ζωὴν αἰώνιον … διεφέρετο … παρώτρυναν … ἐπήγειραν διωγμόν … ἐκτιναξάμενοι τὸν κονιορτόν … Ἰκόνιον … ἐπληροῦντο χαρᾶς καὶ πνεύματος ἁγίου edoxazon ton logon tou kyriou … hosoi ēsan tetagmenoi eis zōēn aiōnion … diephereto … parōtrynan … epēgeiran diōgmon … ektinaxamenoi ton koniorton … Ikonion … eplērounto charas kai pneumatos hagiou — Acts 13:48–52: “appointed to eternal life,” a persecution, shaken-off dust, and disciples filled with joy

“As many as were appointed to eternal life.” The nations ēchairon, “began to rejoice,” and edoxazon ton logon tou kyriou, “to glorify the word of the Lord”; the object of the glorifying is the word, where at 4:21 and 11:18 it is God. “The word of the Lord” stands here and at vv44 and 49, and “the word of God” at vv5, 7 and 46: Luke moves between the two as at 8:14 and 8:25, and this translation keeps “Lord” where the referent is open, as at 8:25. The apparatus gives kyriou at v44 to Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and theou, “of God,” to Westcott–Hort and Robinson–Pierpont; at v48 it gives kyriou to Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont and theou to Westcott–Hort; and it gives echairon, the plural verb, to the first three editions and echairen, the singular that Greek allows with a neuter plural subject, to Robinson–Pierpont. NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah” at vv44, 48 and 49; the KJV prints “the word of God” at v44 and KJV “the word of the Lord” at v48; NIV “the word of the Lord” at both.

The clause on which the most has been built is episteusan hosoi ēsan tetagmenoi eis zōēn aiōnion, “as many as were appointed to eternal life believed.” Tetagmenoi (dictionary) is a perfect participle of tassō, “to put in order, assign, appoint,” and in the perfect the middle and the passive have the same form; so the Greek can mean “had been appointed” (by someone), “had been set” in a state of readiness, or “had put themselves.” The same verb is the one this translation prints “arranged” at Matthew 28:16, where Jesus “had arranged to meet them,” and at Acts 15:2, where it “was arranged” that Paul and Barnabas go up, and “placed under authority” at Luke 7:8; it stands also at 22:10 and 28:23 of Acts and at Romans 13:1 (none yet on these pages). The Greek does not say who did the appointing or on what ground, and the old division between readers who take God as the one who appoints, and readers who take the hearers’ own readiness as what is described, is a division this page presents and does not settle. “Eternal life” (zōē aiōnios) stands in Acts here and at v46, where Paul and Barnabas say that those who push the word away “judge themselves not worthy” of it. The shelf: KJV “as many as were ordained to eternal life believed,” ASV “as many as were ordained to eternal life believed,” Geneva “as many as were ordained unto eternal life, believed,” Douay “as many as were ordained to life everlasting, believed,” NIV “all who were appointed for eternal life believed,” TLB “as many as wanted eternal life, believed,” which turns the appointing into a wanting; and NWT 1984 “all those who were rightly disposed for everlasting life became believers,” which adds “rightly” and turns the appointing into a disposition. That is a doctrinal rendering, and is flagged as one: it settles in English the question the Greek leaves open.

“Carried through the whole region.” Diephereto, “was being carried through,” is built on pherō, “to carry,” and at Mark 11:16 it is what Jesus would not allow: to “carry a vessel through the temple.” Its only other use in Acts is 27:27 (not yet on these pages), where “we” are “driven” across the Adriatic. The word moves through the region as a thing carried. KJV “was published throughout all the region,” ASV “was spread abroad throughout all the region,” NWT 1984 “went on being carried throughout the whole country,” NIV “spread through the whole region.”

Incited women, leading men, and a persecution. Parōtrynan, “incited,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the verb of epēgeiran diōgmon, “stirred up a persecution,” stands in two verses, both in Acts: this one and 14:2, where the same verb is followed by the verb of 12:1, ekakōsan, “afflicted.” Diōgmos, “persecution,” is the word of 8:1 and 11:19. The women are tas sebomenas gynaikas tas euschēmonas, “the worshiping women of good standing,” and the men tous prōtous tēs poleōs, “the leading men of the city”; euschēmōn is the word of Joseph of Arimathea, “a prominent council member,” at Mark 15:43, and it stands again at 17:12. The “Jews” who incite them are the ones of v45, in one city, and Luke does not say how many. They exebalon, “threw out,” the pair apo tōn horiōn autōn, “from their borders,” with the verb of 7:58, where Stephen is “thrown out of the city” while a young man called Saul watches the garments; here the men thrown out include the same Saul. The apparatus gives gynaikas to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and gynaikas kai, “women and the,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which is where “devout and honourable” comes from: KJV “the devout and honourable women,” Geneva “certain devout and honorable women” and Douay “religious and honourable women” print the “and”; ASV “the devout women of honorable estate,” NIV “the God-fearing women of high standing” and NWT 1984 “the reputable women who worshiped” do not. It gives kai Barnaban to the first three and kai ton Barnaban to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “raised persecution against Paul and Barnabas, and expelled them out of their coasts,” NWT 1984 “raised up a persecution against Paul” and “threw them outside their boundaries,” NIV “stirred up persecution against Paul and Barnabas, and expelled them from their region,” TLB “ran them out of town.”

“Shaking off the dust,” and joy. Ektinaxamenoi ton koniorton tōn podōn ep’ autous, “shaking off the dust of their feet against them.” Ektinassō is the verb of the instructions to the Twelve in Matthew 10:14 and Mark 6:11, and it stands in four verses of the New Testament: those two, this verse, and 18:6. Luke’s own Gospel gives the instruction with a different verb, apotinassō, “shake off,” “as a testimony against them” (Luke 9:5), and a third, apomassō, “wipe off,” at Luke 10:11; Acts 13:51 uses the one the other two Gospels use. The act is the one commanded to the Twelve, and Luke records only that they did it. The apparatus adds autōn, “their,” after podōn for Robinson–Pierpont. KJV, ASV and Geneva “shook off the dust of their feet against them,” Douay “shaking off the dust of their feet against them,” NWT 1984 “shook the dust off their feet against them,” NIV “shook the dust off their feet as a warning to them,” TLB “shook off the dust of their feet against the town,” which changes the object. They went to Iconium; Luke names the city and gives it nothing else here.

The chapter ends on a pairing: hoi te mathētai eplērounto charas kai pneumatos hagiou, “and the disciples were being filled with joy and Holy Spirit.” Eplērounto is an imperfect passive, “were being filled,” a continuing state; the Jews of v45 were eplēsthēsan, “filled,” with jealousy at one moment (aorist), and Saul at v9 was plēstheis pneumatos hagiou, “filled with Holy Spirit.” “Joy” is the word of 8:8, “much joy in that city,” and of 12:14, where Rhoda, “for joy,” did not open the gateway. The apparatus gives te to the first three editions and de to Robinson–Pierpont. NWT 1984 “continued to be filled with joy and holy spirit” keeps the continuing tense; KJV “filled with joy, and with the Holy Ghost,” NIV “filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit,” TLB “their converts were filled with joy and with the Holy Spirit,” which replaces “the disciples” with “their converts.”
