The same offering laws restated for the priests who run the altar rather than the worshippers who approach it: the fire that must never go out, the priests' own share of the grain-offering, the anointed priest's daily offering that is entirely burned because it is his own, and the sin-offering's rule of holy contact.
Same setting as ch. 1-5 — spoken at Sinai in the same roughly one-month window before Israel breaks camp (Numbers 10:11). The offering laws of Leviticus 1-7 are given as one continuous block; nothing in the text marks a separate date for this chapter. ⚠ This chapter is Hebrew Leviticus 6 in full (23 verses), corresponding to English 6:8-30 — continuing the versification split first noted at Leviticus 5.

The same chapter, continued — and the audience just changed

This is the second half of Hebrew Leviticus 5–6, picking up exactly where the versification note at Leviticus 5 promised: Hebrew chapter 6 begins at English Leviticus 6:8. English Bibles split Hebrew 5:20–26 off as their own ‘6:1–7’, so this chapter’s 23 verses correspond to English 6:8–30 — a reader cross-checking KJV, ESV, or NIV should expect every citation here to land seven verses higher in their own Bible.

⚠ Just as important as the versification: the ADDRESSEE has changed. Leviticus 1–5 spoke to any Israelite bringing an offering (‘when anyone of you brings an offering’, 1:2). This chapter opens ‘command AARON AND HIS SONS’ (v2) and stays there for the rest of the unit — three times over, a formula repeats: ‘this is the law of’ the burnt-offering (v2), the grain-offering (v7), the sin-offering (v18), each addressed to the priests who run the altar, not the worshippers who approach it. The same offerings, described a second time, from the other side of the smoke.

The fire that is never allowed to go out

Verse 6 closes the burnt-offering law on a flat, absolute command: fire shall burn on the altar continually; it shall not go out. This is a DIFFERENT perpetual institution from the lampstand’s own ner tamid (Exodus 27:20–21, already on these pages) — that lamp burned ‘from evening to morning’, a defined night-shift; this fire is never supposed to go out at all, every day, indefinitely. Two distinct ‘tamid’ commands run through the tabernacle’s daily operation, one for light and one for flame, and this chapter is careful to give the fire its own separate, even more absolute wording.

⚠ The grain-offering law that follows (v7–11) fixes something Leviticus 2 left open: who actually eats the priests’ share, and under what restriction. Unleavened, in a holy place, in the courtyard — every condition here narrows what Leviticus 2 only implied. And the closing line is stark: ‘whatever touches them shall become holy’ (v11) — holiness here is not a quality inside the food a person could be careless with; it is treated as something that can spread outward, by contact, onto whatever it touches next.

The one offering a priest can never eat — because it is his own

Every other grain-offering in this book is split: a memorial handful burned, the rest eaten by the priests as food (Leviticus 2, 6:9–11 above). But when the offering is the ANOINTED PRIEST’S OWN — brought daily, morning and evening, starting the day he is ordained (v13) — the logic breaks down: there is no one else to eat it FOR him, and he cannot eat his own offering as if he were both giver and receiver at once. So it is entirely burned instead (v15–16), the one grain-offering in the whole system with no eaten portion at all — solving by ELIMINATION a conflict none of the laity’s own offerings ever created.

⚠ Verse 14’s cooking instructions contain a real, acknowledged crux: the word this translation renders ‘well-soaked’ (murbekhet) occurs ONLY here in the entire Hebrew Bible, and no other biblical context exists to confirm its precise meaning. Scalded, soaked, well-mixed, and fried have all been proposed by different translators; every version on this shelf is making an educated guess at a word nobody can cross-check.

Why a clay pot gets smashed and a bronze one just gets scrubbed

The sin-offering’s own law closes the chapter with the same holiness-by-contact rule already stated for the grain-offering (v20), but now worked out in uncomfortable physical detail: an earthen pot the meat was boiled in must be BROKEN, while a bronze pot only needs to be scoured and rinsed (v21). The distinction is genuinely practical, not arbitrary — ancient unglazed earthenware is porous and will have absorbed fat and residue no washing can fully remove, where bronze has a sealed, scrubbable surface. Holiness here behaves almost like a physical contaminant: whatever can be cleaned stays in service, whatever cannot is destroyed rather than risked.

⚠ Verse 19 calls the officiating priest ha-kohen ha-mechateh, literally ‘the priest who SIN-OFFERS it’ — the same root as chattat used here as a rare denominative verb, a noun turned into an action. And the chapter’s very last line supplies the rule that Leviticus 4’s own cases were already following without ever stating it outright: any sin-offering whose blood is carried INTO the tent of meeting may never be eaten — it must be burned (v23), which is exactly why the anointed priest’s and the whole congregation’s sin-offerings at Leviticus 4:12 and 4:21 (already on these pages) were burned outside the camp rather than eaten. That chapter showed the exception in practice; this one finally states it as the rule.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. This chapter is Hebrew Leviticus 6 in full (1–23), corresponding to English 6:8–30 — see the note at v1–6 for the versification split inherited from Leviticus 5. ‘Law’ is kept for torah in its narrow technical sense (‘this is the law of’, v2, v7, v18), the same word behind the name of the whole Pentateuch used here for one ritual’s own instruction — see the Dictionary. ‘Entirely’ is kept for kalil (v15, v16) rather than a more archaic ‘wholly’, to keep the emphasis plain in contemporary English. Every {ס} and {פ} in this chapter is reproduced exactly as the Masoretic text marks it — {ס}, setumah, a CLOSED paragraph space (v6); {פ}, petuchah, an OPEN one (v11, v16, v23) — each break falling at the close of one of the chapter’s four addressed units.

⚠ A note on order: Leviticus stands on these pages at chapters 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, and 19. Leviticus 7–18 and 20–27 remain the open sequence.
