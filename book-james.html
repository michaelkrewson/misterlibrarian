📖 James

Ἰακώβου Ἐπιστολή (Iakōbou Epistolē) — 'The Letter of James' — Iakōbos is the Greek for JACOB, which English routes through the Latin Iacomus to 'James' (and Spanish to 'Santiago'); the addressees are 'the twelve tribes in the Dispersion'.

The most Jewish and most practical book in the New Testament — wisdom literature in a Christian key, very possibly by the Lord's own brother. Famous for one sentence Luther could not stomach ('faith without works is dead') and one he might have loved ('pure religion… to look after orphans and widows'), it reads throughout like the Sermon on the Mount turned into proverbs.

Chapter by chapter

James 1The most Jewish and most practical book in the New Testament — wisdom literature in a Christian key, very possibly by JAMES the brother of Jesus, who calls himself only “a slave” and never mentions the family tie. It opens on trials: “count it all joy… when you fall into TRIALS,” because testing makes endurance and endurance makes a whole person. ⚠ The chapter turns on one Greek word, peirasmos — a “trial” that comes from outside (v2) and a “temptation” that works from inside (v13) — and James uses the pivot to block the oldest excuse: God tests no one; each is dragged off by “his own desire,” which conceives and bears sin, and sin brings forth death. Against that he sets a God who does not change — “the Father of the lights, with whom there is no shadow of turning” — who instead gives us birth by the word of truth. It is thick with echoes of the Sermon on the Mount (ask and it will be given; the good Father’s gifts; the hearer who does), coins the word “double-souled,” and ends on the definition that has outlived a thousand sermons: ⚠ “Pure and undefiled RELIGION… is this: to look in on orphans and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unspotted from the world.”

About the book

Where it sits
The TWENTIETH book of the New Testament and the first of the seven 'General' or 'Catholic' Epistles (James, 1-2 Peter, 1-3 John, Jude), addressed to the church at large rather than to one congregation. ⚠ Its road into the canon was rocky: Eusebius listed it among the 'disputed' books, the Syriac church was slow to receive it, and Luther, sixteen centuries on, ranked it below the rest.
Genre
WISDOM literature and moral exhortation — closer to Proverbs, Sirach and the sayings of Jesus than to a Pauline argument. It is a chain of loosely linked practical instructions on trials, wealth, the tongue, prayer and works, with almost no narrative and little sustained theology; some have called it a sermon, or a collection of homiletic fragments, in a letter's clothing.
Author
Traditionally JAMES 'the Just', the brother of Jesus and head of the Jerusalem church (Acts 15; Galatians 2:9) — and the Jewish texture, the closeness to the sayings of Jesus, and the concern for the poor all fit him. ⚠ Two things are urged against it: the Greek is more polished than expected of a Galilean, and a letter so insistent on 'works' has been read as a reply to a misunderstood Paul, which would push it late. Defenders answer that a secretary accounts for the Greek and that James rebuts a garbled slogan, not Paul himself. The main options: the Lord's brother writing early; the Lord's brother with a skilled amanuensis; or a later writer in his name. The library reports them and does not vote.
Date written
Disputed across a very wide range. On the early dating (c. AD 45–49) it is one of the earliest Christian documents, written before the Jerusalem council — which its silence on the Gentile-circumcision question may support; on the late dating it is post-70 and possibly pseudonymous. The early date is taken in the chronology, with the range reported.
Place
JERUSALEM, if James the Just is the author — written from the mother church to Jewish believers 'in the Dispersion'.
Audience
'The TWELVE TRIBES in the Dispersion' (1:1) — most naturally Jewish Christians scattered outside the land, though the phrase also evokes the whole people of God in exile. They are a community with rich and poor sitting uneasily together (2:1-7), quarrels and sharp tongues (chs 3-4), labourers cheated of their wages (5:4), and a faith gone slack — orthodox in belief ('you believe God is one; even the demons believe', 2:19) but thin in practice. The letter's aim is to close the gap between what they profess and how they live.

How it's laid out

1:1–27Trials, wisdom, wealth and the word — 'be doers, not hearers', and 'pure religion'.
2:1–13Against favouritism: the poor man and the gold-ringed man, and the 'royal law' to love your neighbour.
2:14–26Faith and works — 'faith without works is dead', with Abraham and Rahab as proof.
3:1–18The tongue — 'a fire, a restless evil' — and the wisdom from above, 'first pure, then peaceable'.
4:1–17Quarrels, friendship with the world, and 'you do not have because you do not ask'.
5:1–20Woe to the oppressive rich; patience till the Lord's coming; the prayer of faith and the anointing of the sick.

What it's about

  • FAITH and WORKS — the most famous and most contested theme (2:14-26), 'faith without works is dead'; whether it opposes Paul or completes him is an old debate the letter itself may predate.
  • THE TONGUE — bridled at 1:26 and unleashed in chapter 3 as 'a fire… a world of iniquity', the small rudder that steers the whole life.
  • RICH and POOR — the New Testament's bluntest treatment of wealth: the rich a fading flower (1:10), shown shameful favouritism (2:1-7), and warned of rotting riches and unpaid wages (5:1-6).
  • WISDOM — asked for in prayer (1:5) and defined at 3:17 as 'first pure, then peaceable, gentle, open to reason' — practical, from above, and known by its fruits.
  • TRIALS and ENDURANCE — 'count it all joy' (1:2), the testing that makes a complete person, with Job and the prophets held up as patient (5:10-11).
  • PRAYER — running through the letter: ask for wisdom (1:5), 'you do not have because you do not ask' (4:2), and the prayer of faith that heals (5:14-16), 'the prayer of a righteous person has great power'.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. The Greek of James is surprisingly good — fluent, vivid, full of imagery drawn from nature and daily life, and rich in words found nowhere else in the New Testament (some, like dipsychos, 'double-souled', very likely coined by the author). ⚠ Its echoes of the Old Testament and of the sayings of Jesus are usually allusive rather than exact, woven into the argument rather than quoted; the letter never cites a Gospel yet is saturated with the Sermon on the Mount, which suggests it draws on the same stream of Jesus' teaching at an early stage. The text is well preserved; chapter 1's only notable difficulty is the knotted syntax of 1:17.

Looking forward

James names Jesus only twice (1:1; 2:1) and never narrates his death or resurrection — which has troubled readers looking for a fuller Christology. ⚠ But the letter is steeped in Jesus' TEACHING: it echoes the Sermon on the Mount on almost every page (trials and joy, asking and receiving, treasure, oaths, judging, the doer and the hearer), so that Christ is present less as a doctrine than as a voice. What it does assert is high enough: Jesus is 'the Lord Jesus Christ' set beside God as the one whose slave James is (1:1), and 'our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of GLORY' (2:1) — the divine glory attached to his name. And he is the coming Judge: 'the coming of the Lord is at hand… the Judge is standing at the door' (5:7-9). The letter's Christ is the Teacher whose words are to be done and the Lord whose return is near.

Where the debates are

Its relation to PAUL on faith and works — the same words (faith, works, justified, Abraham) reach apparently opposite conclusions (2:24 against Romans 3:28), and whether they contradict, complement, or simply predate one another is the classic question, and the root of Luther's low estimate ('an epistle of straw'). The AUTHORSHIP and DATE, which stand or fall together. Whether the tone and the 'assembly' of 2:2 place the letter very early or later. And the meaning of the healing-and-anointing passage of 5:14-16. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
