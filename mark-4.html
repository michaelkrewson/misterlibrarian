Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–9 · ἔπλει … εἰς πλοῖον ἐμβάντα καθῆσθαι eplei … eis ploion embanta kathēsthai — the boat gets used

⚠ Chapter 3 kept a promise chapter 4 now cashes. At 3:9 Jesus told his disciples to keep a small boat ready for him because of the crowd, so that they would not crush him — a detail that read, at the time, like ordinary crowd management. Nothing happened with the boat for the rest of that chapter. Here it is: he gets in, sits down, and teaches an entire crowd standing on the shore while he speaks from the water. Mark plants props before he uses them.

The yield triples and escalates. Thirty, sixty, a hundred — not one number repeated for emphasis but three, each doubling-or-more the last, for seed that lands on ground indistinguishable from the failed ground until the crop is in. The parable does not say how to tell good soil from bad in advance. It only reports what each one does.

“Let him hear” (v9) ends the parable exactly as it began (v3), “Listen” — framing the whole thing as a hearing-test before it is explained as one.

Verses 10–20 · ἵνα βλέποντες βλέπωσι καὶ μὴ ἴδωσιν hina blepontes blepōsi kai mē idōsin — the hardest sentence about parables anywhere

⚠ This is the hardest sentence Mark has Jesus say, and it is harder than Matthew’s version of the same moment. Matthew 13’s note already traced the Isaiah 6:9-10 background behind this saying — the Hebrew’s blunt imperative to the prophet, ‘make the heart of this people fat,’ softened by the Greek Old Testament into a description, the heart ‘has grown dull.’ Matthew’s Jesus cites that softened Greek text as fulfilled prophecy, framed with hoti — because they already see without perceiving (13:13). Mark’s Jesus never quotes Isaiah at all. He simply states, in his own voice, a purpose clause — hina, so that — using Isaiah’s vocabulary without Isaiah’s citation formula: parables are given so that outsiders will see and not perceive, hear and not understand, lest they should turn and be forgiven. Every version on this shelf keeps the purpose intact — KJV “lest at any time they should be converted, and their sins should be forgiven them,” ASV “lest haply they should turn again, and it should be forgiven them,” NIV “otherwise they might turn and be forgiven” — and RV60/NVI do the same in Spanish. No version on either shelf softens Mark's hina into Matthew's hoti; that difference belongs to the Greek text Mark wrote, not to any translator's later choice.

The library reports two readings of what this means, and adopts neither: that parables are themselves a further, deserved hardening of those already resistant; or that Mark states as purpose what Matthew states as description, and the practical difference between the two may be smaller than the grammar makes it sound. ⚠ What Mark's version does not do is what Matthew's does — give itself the cover of being a quotation rather than Jesus's own stated reason.

“The mystery,” singular. Mark gives the Twelve one mystērion; Matthew's parallel gives them mystēria, plural — a small difference, likely without much weight, but this translation keeps each Gospel's own number rather than harmonizing them.

What follows (vv13-20) is Jesus explaining his own parable point by point — something he does for almost no other parable in any Gospel. The explanation is not obscure. The reason for needing one, just given, is the obscure part.

Verses 21–25 · μήτι ἔρχεται ὁ λύχνος mēti erchetai ho lychnos — two sayings that live elsewhere too

⚠ Neither saying in this unit stays put. The lamp under a basket recurs at Matthew 5:15, inside the Sermon on the Mount, with no connection to parables at all. The measure you measure recurs at Matthew 7:2, attached to judging other people, again with no parables in sight. Mark ties both to this moment, hearing and understanding; Matthew ties them to two entirely different moments. Sayings this portable were plainly used more than once, by more than one arrangement of the same teacher's material — the library reports where else each one surfaces rather than picking a single original setting.

Read where Mark puts them, the logic runs forward from v11: something has been hidden (the mystery, given to the Twelve and not the crowd) precisely so that it can later come to light (v22). The lamp is not a general principle about honesty. It is, here, a statement about the parables themselves — concealment with an expiration date.

“Lamp” or “candle”? KJV alone reads “candle” here, an anachronism for a first-century oil lamp; every other version compared, ASV and NIV included, reads “lamp.” The container it must not be hidden under is a “bushel” in KJV/ASV, a dry-measure basket about the size to smother a small flame; NIV generalizes to “bowl.”

Verses 26–29 · ὡς ἄνθρωπος βάλῃ τὸν σπόρον hōs anthrōpos balē ton sporon — a parable no other Gospel kept

The Growing Seed is found only here. Matthew and Luke both keep the sower and the mustard seed, the parables on either side of this one; neither keeps this one. Whatever its source, only Mark thought it belonged in the finished book.

⚠ The farmer contributes almost nothing. He scatters, he sleeps, he rises, and “he does not know how” the seed sprouts and grows. The earth is what “bears fruit by itself” — automatē, the word behind the English automatic. The only work given to the man is the last verse: when the grain is ready, he reaps. A kingdom-parable with no technique in it, only patience and a harvest he did not cause.

Verses 30–34 · κόκκῳ σινάπεως kokkō sinapeōs — the smallest seed a Galilean farmer would have planted

“Smaller than all the seeds on the ground” is a farmer's claim, not a botanist's — proverbially the smallest seed a first-century Galilean sower would actually have sown, not a claim ranking every seed on earth (orchid seeds, unknown to that audience, are smaller still). The point is the contrast the parable actually makes: the smallest seed anyone present had ever planted becomes, of every plant they grew, the largest — big enough for birds to nest in its shade, the same phrase Ezekiel uses of the mighty cedar that shelters every kind of bird (Ezekiel 17:23; 31:6). A garden herb given the imagery of a cedar of Lebanon.

Verses 33-34 close the whole unit the way it opened: parables to the crowd, in public, pitched to what they were able to hear; everything unpacked afterward, in private, to the disciples alone. The chapter's own method is stated as its final sentence.

Verses 35–41 · Σιώπα, πεφίμωσο siōpa, pephimōso — the same command given to a demon and a sea

⚠ ‘Be muzzled’ has one prior use in this book, and it was not addressed to weather. At 1:25, Jesus rebukes (epetimēsen) an unclean spirit and orders it, phimōthēti, be muzzled. Here he rebukes (epetimēsen, the identical verb) the wind and orders the sea, pephimōso, the same root. Mark does not editorialize about what kind of thing a storm is. He has Jesus speak to it exactly as he spoke to a demon, and lets the repetition make the claim.

“Does it not matter to you” is a real question, not simply panic — the disciples are experienced fishermen on their own working sea, and the boat is already filling (v37) before anyone wakes him. KJV “carest thou not”, NIV “don't you care”, NWT “do you not care” — every version keeps the accusation aimed at him, not just at the storm.

⚠ Not every version keeps the muzzle. RV60 reads “Calla, enmudece” — the second word from the same root as “mudo,” mute, keeping the same forceful silencing image as the Greek; NVI generalizes to “¡Cálmate!”, “calm yourself,” losing the command-to-silence entirely. This translation follows the Greek and RV60 rather than the smoother reading.

The chapter that opened with a crowd straining to hear a story about hearing ends with the disciples themselves failing the same test — “why are you cowardly, do you still have no faith” (v40) — and asking, in the very last line, the question the whole chapter has been posing sideways since v1: who is this?

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: "so that" for hina at v12, the purpose clause kept as a purpose clause rather than softened toward Matthew's descriptive "because"; "the mystery" (v11) kept singular, against Matthew's plural, rather than harmonized; "be muzzled" for pephimōso (v39), matched to the identical verb used of the unclean spirit at 1:25; and "does it not matter to you" (v38), keeping the accusation aimed at Jesus rather than softened into plain alarm at the storm.

Echoes paid. The boat kept ready at 3:9 is the boat used here (v1) — a detail planted three chapters back and only now spent. The rebuke-and-muzzle pairing (v39) repeats 1:25's exact verbs, this time aimed at wind and sea rather than an unclean spirit.

Echoes planted. The disciples' own fear and lack of understanding (v40) — the hearing-test the parables posed to the crowd, failed here by the Twelve themselves, and a thread this Gospel will keep pulling. The question that closes the chapter, “who is this?” (v41), left open rather than answered.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether v12's purpose clause hardens the already-hard or creates the hardness it describes — both readings are old, both are argued, and this chapter alone does not settle which. Where the lamp and measure sayings originally belonged (vv21-24), since both surface in entirely different settings in Matthew. And how exactly the seed "grows, he does not know how" (v27) — a parable that describes a process without explaining it, on purpose.
