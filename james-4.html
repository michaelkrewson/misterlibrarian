Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–3 · Wars and fights — “your pleasures that wage war in your members,” and “you do not have, because you do not ask”

“Where do wars come from, and where do fights among you come from? Is it not from here — from your pleasures that wage war in your members?” The noun polemos, “war,” stands in sixteen verses, nine of them in Revelation (dictionary); here it is turned on a quarrel among the letter’s own readers, a step after the “disorder” of James 3:16. Robinson–Pierpont has “where” only once (the apparatus). Hēdonē, “pleasure,” stands in five verses, among them “the anxieties and riches and pleasures of life” at Luke 8:14 and “slaves to various desires and pleasures” at Titus 3:3 (dictionary). The verb strateuomai, “wage war, serve as a soldier,” stands in seven verses; 1 Peter 2:11 has desires that wage war “against the soul,” and Paul has a law “warring against the law of my mind” at Romans 7:23 (dictionary; dictionary). “Members” are the body’s parts, as the tongue among “our members” at James 3:6. The KJV reads “even of your lusts that war in your members”; the NWT 1984 reads “from YOUR cravings for sensual pleasure that carry on a conflict in YOUR members”; the NIV reads “What causes fights and quarrels among you?”

⚠ “You desire, and you do not have; you murder and you are jealous, and you cannot obtain; you fight and wage war. You do not have, because you do not ask. You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly, so that you may spend it on your pleasures.” “Murder” is phoneuete, the verb of “Do not murder” at James 2:11, and every Greek edition consulted here reads it (checked). Because the word seems too strong for a quarrel, Erasmus is reported to have proposed phthoneite, “you envy,” a guess with no Greek manuscript behind it (reported, not checked here); this translation prints what the Greek says. The editions also divide the clauses differently: as here, or with the murder following from the wanting, as the NIV punctuates them; readings differ. “Jealous” is the verb of the “jealousy” at James 3:14 and 3:16. The verb machomai, “fight,” stands in four verses. “You do not ask” answers “let him ask of God, who gives to all generously” at James 1:5. Dapanaō, “spend,” stands in five verses, among them the woman who “had spent everything she had” at Mark 5:26 and the younger son who “had spent everything” at Luke 15:14. The KJV reads “ye kill, and desire to have”; the Douay reads “you kill, and envy”; the NWT 1984 reads “because YOU are asking for a wrong purpose”; the NIV reads “You desire but do not have, so you kill.”

Verses 4–6 · “Adulteresses!” — friendship with the world, “the spirit that he made to dwell in us,” and Proverbs 3:34

“Adulteresses! Do you not know that friendship with the world is hostility toward God? So whoever wants to be a friend of the world makes himself an enemy of God.” The feminine moichalis, “adulteress,” stands in six verses; Jesus uses it of “an evil and adulterous generation” at Matthew 12:39 and Mark 8:38 (dictionary). It carries the prophets’ picture of the people as Jehovah’s unfaithful wife, as at Hosea 1:2, “the land goes utterly whoring away from following Jehovah,” and in the Hebrew of Jeremiah 3:20, a wife who betrays her husband (checked). Robinson–Pierpont reads “Adulterers and adulteresses” (the apparatus). Philia, “friendship,” stands in one verse (dictionary); its “friend” is the word that called Abraham “a friend of God” at James 2:23 (dictionary). Echthra, “hostility,” stands in six verses, among them “the mind of the flesh is hostility toward God” at Romans 8:7 (dictionary). “Makes himself” is the verb of the tongue that “is set among our members” at James 3:6. The KJV reads “Ye adulterers and adulteresses”; the ASV reads “maketh himself an enemy of God”; the NWT 1984 reads “is constituting himself an enemy of God”; the NIV reads “You adulterous people.”

⚠ “Or do you think that the scripture says in vain, ‘The spirit that he made to dwell in us yearns enviously’?” The hardest sentence in the letter. The words quoted are not found in any book of the Old Testament as it has come down, and readers have proposed a lost writing or a summary of several passages (reported, not checked here). The Greek pneuma, “spirit,” can be the subject or the object of the verb, and there are three main readings: the human spirit God placed in us longs with envy; God yearns jealously for the spirit he made to dwell in us; or the holy spirit God gave yearns jealously over us. English cannot keep the choice open, and the rendering printed here takes “spirit” as the subject, as the ASV does; readings differ (dictionary). Phthonos, “envy,” with its verb stands in ten verses, and everywhere else it is a vice, as the “malice and envy” of Titus 3:3 (dictionary). The verb epipotheō, “yearn,” stands in nine verses, as Paul’s “how I long for you all” at Philippians 1:8 (dictionary). Katōkisen, “he made to dwell,” stands in one verse; Robinson–Pierpont spells it katōkēsen, “which dwelt” (the apparatus). The KJV reads “The spirit that dwelleth in us lusteth to envy”; the NWT 1984 reads “It is with a tendency to envy that the spirit which has taken up residence within us keeps longing”; the NIV reads “he jealously longs for the spirit he has caused to dwell in us”; the TLB reads “the Holy Spirit, whom God has placed within us, watches over us with tender jealousy.”

“But he gives greater grace. That is why it says, ‘God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble.’” The line is Proverbs 3:34 in its Greek form, word for word except that the Greek of Proverbs has “the Lord” where James has “God” (checked); the Hebrew reads “The scoffers he scoffs at, but to the humble he gives grace,” with “humble” the word read aloud and “poor” the word written (checked). 1 Peter 5:5 quotes the same line, also with “God,” and goes on, as James does, to “humble yourselves” and to “resist” the devil, in the other order (checked). Hyperēphanos, “proud,” stands in five verses, among them “he has scattered the proud” at Luke 1:51 (dictionary). Antitassō, “oppose,” stands in five verses, and returns at 5:6. “Grace” is charis (dictionary). The KJV reads “God resisteth the proud”; the NWT 1984 reads “the undeserved kindness which he gives is greater”; the NIV reads “shows favor to the humble.”

Verses 7–10 · “Resist the devil, and he will flee from you” — “draw near to God,” the double-souled, and laughter turned to mourning

“Submit yourselves, then, to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, sinners, and purify your hearts, you double-souled.” “Submit” is hypotassō (dictionary); “resist” is the verb of “able to resist on the evil day” at Ephesians 6:13; and the devil leaves at Matthew 4:11 (dictionary). “Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you” has the shape of Zechariah 1:3, “Return to me … and I will return to you,” though the Greek of Zechariah uses a different verb, “turn” (checked). “Hands” and “hearts” recall “clean hands and a pure heart” at Psalm 24:4 (yet to come in this translation, checked in the Hebrew). Hagnizō, “purify,” stands in four verses; the other three are ritual purification, as Paul is told to “be purified” at Acts 21:24 (dictionary). Dipsychos, “double-souled,” stands in two verses, here and “a double-souled man, unstable in all his ways” at James 1:8 (dictionary). The ASV reads “Be subject therefore unto God”; the KJV reads “purify your hearts, ye double minded”; the NWT 1984 reads “YOU indecisive ones”; the NIV reads “you double-minded.”

“Be wretched and mourn and weep. Let your laughter be turned to mourning, and your joy to gloom. Humble yourselves before Jehovah, and he will exalt you.” The verb talaipōreō, “be wretched,” stands in one verse; its adjective is “Wretched man that I am!” at Romans 7:24 (dictionary). “Mourn and weep” are the two verbs of Luke 6:25, “Woe to you who laugh now — for you will mourn and weep.” Gelōs, “laughter,” stands in one verse. Katēpheia, “gloom,” stands in one verse (dictionary), and is said to picture eyes cast down (reported, not checked here). Robinson–Pierpont has another verb for “be turned” with the same sense (the apparatus). “Before Jehovah”: the Greek reads kyriou, “Lord,” and the one it names is the God of verses 6–8, so this translation prints “Jehovah,” as at James 1:7. “He will exalt you” is Jesus’ “whoever will humble himself will be exalted” at Matthew 23:12 and Luke 14:11, and the lowly brother’s “height” at James 1:9 (dictionary; dictionary). The KJV reads “Be afflicted, and mourn, and weep”; the NWT 1984 reads “Give way to misery” and “Humble yourselves in the eyes of Jehovah”; the NIV reads “your joy to gloom.”

Verses 11–12 · “Do not speak against one another” — judging the law, and “one lawgiver and judge”

“Do not speak against one another, brothers. The one who speaks against a brother or judges his brother speaks against the law and judges the law; and if you judge the law, you are not a doer of the law but a judge.” The verb katalaleō, “speak against,” stands in three verses; the other two are in 1 Peter, where outsiders speak against believers. Its noun is the “slanders” of 2 Corinthians 12:20, and its adjective the “slanderers” of Romans 1:30 (dictionary). “A doer of the law” is the phrase of Romans 2:13, “the doers of the law will be justified,” and of “doers of the word” at James 1:22; “judges” recalls the “judges with evil thoughts” of James 2:4. Robinson–Pierpont reads “and judges” for “or judges” (the apparatus). The ASV reads “Speak not one against another”; the KJV reads “Speak not evil one of another”; the NWT 1984 reads “Quit speaking against one another”; the NIV reads “do not slander one another.”

“There is one lawgiver and judge, the one who is able to save and to destroy. But who are you, who judge your neighbor?” Nomothetēs, “lawgiver,” stands in one verse (dictionary); the “giving of the law” at Romans 9:4 is its cousin (dictionary). “Able to save” is the phrase of “the implanted word, which is able to save your souls” at James 1:21, and “to destroy” recalls “the one who can destroy both soul and body in Gehenna” at Matthew 10:28. “Neighbor” is the one of the royal law at James 2:8 (dictionary). Robinson–Pierpont lacks “and judge” and reads “who judges the other” (the apparatus). The KJV reads “There is one lawgiver, who is able to save and to destroy: who art thou that judgest another?”; the ASV reads “One only is the lawgiver and judge”; the NWT 1984 reads “But you, who are you to be judging [your] neighbor?”

Verses 13–15 · “Come now” — today or tomorrow, a mist that appears and disappears, and “if the Lord wills”

“Come now, you who say, ‘Today or tomorrow we will go into such and such a city, and spend a year there, and trade, and make a profit’ — you who do not know what your life will be tomorrow. For you are a mist that appears for a little while and then disappears.” “Come now” opens 5:1 too. “Such and such a city” is “this city here,” any city the speaker might name. Emporeuomai, “trade,” stands in two verses, the other at 2 Peter 2:3 (dictionary). The plan is the rich man’s of Luke 12:19, “Soul, you have many goods laid up for many years,” answered at 12:20, “This very night your soul is demanded back from you.” ⚠ The text of verse 14 is printed as the critical editions have it, “what your life will be tomorrow”; Robinson–Pierpont adds “for,” making a question, “For what is your life?”, and reads “it will be” for “you are” (the apparatus). Atmis, “mist,” stands in two verses, here and “a vapour of smoke” at Acts 2:19 (dictionary). “Appears … disappears” keeps the Greek pair phainomenē … aphanizomenē; the verb aphanizō stands in five verses, among them the moth and devouring that “make them disappear” at Matthew 6:19 (dictionary). The KJV reads “For what is your life? It is even a vapour”; the ASV reads “we will go into this city”; the NWT 1984 reads “whereas YOU do not know what YOUR life will be tomorrow. For YOU are a mist”; the NIV reads “go to this or that city.”

⚠ “Instead you ought to say, ‘If the Lord wills, we will both live and do this or that.’” The Greek is ho kyrios, and it can name God, as the New World Translation takes it, or Jesus, whom the letter calls “Lord” at James 1:1 and James 2:1; readings differ, so this translation keeps “Lord” here while printing “Jehovah” at verse 10, where God is named in the verses around it. Paul uses the same words at 1 Corinthians 4:19, “if the Lord wills,” and “if the Lord permits” at 1 Corinthians 16:7; the formula is “God willing” at Acts 18:21 and “if God permits” at Hebrews 6:3. “Both live and do” keeps the Greek “both … and”; some editions read the verbs so that the condition takes in the living too, “if the Lord wills and we live, we will do this or that.” The ASV reads “If the Lord will, we shall both live, and do this or that”; the Douay reads “If the Lord will, and if we shall live, we will do this or that”; the NWT 1984 reads “If Jehovah wills, we shall live and also do this or that”; the NIV reads “If it is the Lord’s will.”

Verses 16–17 · Boasting in “arrogance” — and the good known and left undone

“But as it is, you boast in your arrogance; all such boasting is evil. So for the one who knows how to do good and does not do it, it is sin to him.” Alazoneia, “arrogance,” stands in two verses, here in the plural, “your arrogances,” the shows of it; its adjective is the “boasters” of Romans 1:30 (dictionary). Kauchēsis, “boasting,” stands in eleven verses, all the others in Paul; the verb is the lowly brother’s “boast in his height” at James 1:9, so the letter has a good boasting and a bad one (dictionary). Verse 17 reads like a proverb: knowing the good is not yet doing it, as with “that slave who knew his master’s will, and did not prepare or act according to his will” at Luke 12:47, and as with the hearer who is not a doer at James 1:22. “Good” is kalon (dictionary). The KJV reads “But now ye rejoice in your boastings”; the ASV reads “ye glory in your vauntings”; the NWT 1984 reads “YOUR self-assuming brags”; the NIV reads “you boast in your arrogant schemes.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “wars” and “fights”; “pleasures,” as at Luke 8:14; “members,” as at 3:6; “murder,” with no conjecture; “adulteresses,” feminine, as the Greek is; “hostility,” as at Romans 8:7; “the scripture,” as at 2:8; “yearns enviously,” with the three readings in the note; “greater grace”; “double-souled,” as at 1:8; “be wretched,” as at Romans 7:24; “Jehovah” at verse 10 and “the Lord” at verse 15; “speak against”; “a doer of the law,” as at Romans 2:13; “lawgiver”; “neighbor,” as at 2:8; “mist”; “appears … disappears”; and “arrogance.”

Echoes paid. “Where do wars come from?” after the “disorder” of James 3:16; the jealousy of James 3:14; “friendship with the world” against the “friend of God” of James 2:23; “do not speak against one another”; the members of James 3:6; ask from James 1:5; the double-souled of James 1:8; the lowly lifted up from James 1:9; doers from James 1:22; the neighbor of James 2:8; able to save from James 1:21; and murder from James 2:11.

Echoes planted. “Come now” again at 5:1, to the rich; the wretchedness of 5:1, the noun of verse 9’s verb; “oppose” at 5:6; the “rust” at 5:3, the same word ios as the “venom” of 3:8; the “judge standing before the doors” at 5:9; and prayer at 5:13–18, after “you ask wrongly” here.

Next in this book: James 5, already on these pages. “Come now, you rich”; gold and silver rusted; the wages held back that cry out; “be patient until the presence of the Lord”; “let your yes be yes”; the elders, the oil and the prayer of faith; Elijah, and the one who turns a sinner back.
