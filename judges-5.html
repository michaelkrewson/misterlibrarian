Deborah and Barak sing the victory on the day of the battle. The song names the tribes who came down and those who stayed, tells how the stars and the brook Kishon fought, praises Jael, and closes with Sisera's mother waiting for a chariot that does not return.
Undated within the chapter. The song is set on the day of the battle in Judges 4, so it takes that chapter's undated window.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · the song's title, the loose hair, and the kings who are told to listen

The song is attributed to two singers, and the prose of chapter 4 is its frame. Verse 1 says Deborah and Barak sang "on that day", the day the prose has just described. The Hebrew vatashar is the verb for singing a song, and the chapter is the book's first poem; the translation keeps the verb.

"When the locks hang loose." Biphro'a pera'ot refers to men who let their hair grow for war, and the phrase is hard to read. The NWT 1984 reads "letting the hair hang loose in Israel", and the TNM 2019 "por los cabellos sueltos en Israel". The translation keeps the image without adding the war, which the Hebrew does not state.

"Bless Jehovah." The refrain comes twice more, in verse 2 and verse 9 (barkhu YHVH). The ASV reads "Bless ye Jehovah", and this translation keeps "bless" throughout, so the refrain stays one word.

"Kings" and "rulers". Verse 3 calls on kings and princes to listen. The address is to the whole world beyond Israel, and the song has the nations as its audience.

Verses 4–5 · Jehovah marches out from Seir, and the mountains shake

The divine march comes from the south-east, not from Canaan. Seir and the field of Edom lie south-east of the land, and the song places the storm on that route.

"The heavens dripped." Gam-shamayim natafu, "the heavens also dripped," describes the rain. The translation keeps "dripped" rather than "poured," because that is the verb the Hebrew uses.

"Even that Sinai." Verse 5 says the mountains quaked "before Jehovah, even that Sinai", using the demonstrative zeh, "this one", to point at the mountain in the reader's sight. The translation keeps "that Sinai" for the same reason.

Verses 6–8 · the days of Shamgar and Jael, the villagers, and the new gods

The roads were empty "in the days of Jael". Verse 6 dates the emptiness to the time of Shamgar (Judges 3:31, already on these pages) and of Jael. The two names put the story's two heroes beside a time when travel stopped. The translation keeps the name Jael, which the reader has already met in Judges 4:21, now on these pages.

"The villagers ceased." Peraz, the village-dweller, is a word for people who live without walls. The line says the villages failed until Deborah rose as a mother in Israel, and the translation says "villagers" to keep the word as a class of people, not a place.

"They chose new gods; then war was at the gates." Yivchar elohim chadashim, the choice of new gods, comes before the war in verse 8. The shelf reads the first half closely: the NWT 2013 "They chose new gods," and the ASV "They chose new gods; then was war in the gates." The reading is the same in both; the translation keeps the order of cause and war.

"Forty thousand." Verse 8 asks whether a shield or spear was seen among forty thousand in Israel. The number is the size of the whole nation's fighting force in the song's account, and the question is that no weapon was visible, not that the army had none.

Verses 9–11 · the commanders who offered themselves, and the ones who ride

"My heart goes out." Libbi le-chokeqei Yisrael, "my heart is with the lawgivers of Israel," is the poet speaking for herself. The translation uses "commanders," because the word is the one used of military leaders in verse 14.

"Those who ride on white donkeys." Verse 10 addresses the rich: riders on white donkeys and people who sit on rugs. The song turns from the commanders to the wealthy and then to the poor on the road, and asks all of them to speak. The translation keeps the three groups, in the same order.

"The righteous acts of his villagers." Tsidqot pirzono is the same word as peraz in verse 7, and the translation keeps it as "villagers" in both places. The phrase credits the people who had no walls with the righteous acts that saved them.

"Then the people of Jehovah went down to the gates." The gates were where justice was done, so the song ends its first section by bringing the people back to the place of judgment that the war had driven them from.

Verses 12–13 · wake up, Deborah, and take the captives

"Wake up, wake up." Ruri ruri Devorah, the doubled imperative, is the song's loudest sound, and the translation keeps the doubling. The ASV keeps the doubling as "Awake, awake".

"Take your captives." Shevah shevyekha means to take captives, and the verse is an order to Barak to lead away the prisoners of the battle. The translation says "take your captives," which keeps the Hebrew's sense.

"The remnant." Sarid, a survivor, is the word for those left after a defeat, and verse 13 says the survivors came down to the nobles. The sentence is hard, and the translation keeps the grammar: the remnant came down, and Jehovah came down for the poet among the mighty.

Verses 14–18 · the roll of tribes, who came and who stayed

The roll call sorts the tribes into those who came and those who did not. Ephraim, Benjamin, Machir, Zebulun, Issachar, Naphtali come down to the battle. Reuben, Gilead, Dan and Asher stay where they are. The song does not condemn the stayers in so many words; it asks questions, and the translation keeps the questions.

"Whose root is in Amalek." The Hebrew says Ephraim's root is "in Amalek," which is the old enemy of Israel in the wilderness. The phrase is the poet's joke at Ephraim, and the translation keeps the word "root," because the Hebrew shorsham is a root in the botanical sense.

"Why did you sit between the saddlebags?" Verse 16 asks Reuben why he sat among the sheepfolds listening to pipes. The word mishpetayim is the word the shelf reads as saddlebags, sheepfolds, or the space between two pens, and the translation says "saddlebags" because that is the reading the TNM 1987 and the NWT 1984 both choose.

Dan and the ships. Verse 17 asks why Dan stayed among the ships. The sea tribes are Asher and Dan, and the song's Dan does not fight. The translation keeps "ships," the word the witnesses also use.

"Zebulun is a people who risked their lives to death." Verse 18 is the song's most direct praise, and the translation keeps the verb cherefe, to risk or expose, as the Hebrew uses it of soldiers who put their lives at stake.

Verses 19–23 · the kings at Taanach, the stars, the brook, and Meroz

"The waters of Megiddo" and Taanach. Verse 19 places the kings of Canaan at Taanach by the waters of Megiddo, the same two towns that Joshua 12:21 lists among the defeated (already on these pages). The song adds that they took no spoil of silver, which is the point of the battle: the victory was not for plunder.

"The stars fought." Hakokhavim mimsilotam nilchamu, the stars fought from their courses. The shelf keeps the image; the KJV has "the stars in their courses fought against Sisera," and the ASV has "From their courses they fought against Sisera," and the translation keeps the same picture.

"The brook Kishon swept them away." Nachal Qishon geraḥam, the brook swept them away, is the flood of the song, and the verse says the brook is "that ancient brook." The translation keeps the repeated name, because the poem repeats it.

"Meroz." Verse 23 is a curse on a town, Meroz, that did not come to help Jehovah. The name Meroz stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, so no other passage tells us where it was or why it stayed out. The translation keeps the name, the curse, and the angel who speaks it.

Verses 24–27 · Jael, blessed above women, with the milk and the peg

"Blessed above women." Tevorakh mi-nashim, the blessing above women, is the song's praise of Jael, and it is the verse the chapter has been building toward since Judges 4:21. The translation keeps "blessed" because the Hebrew says it.

"Water he asked; milk she gave." Verse 25 repeats the sequence of Judges 4:19, and the song adds a "lordly bowl" for the milk, which the prose did not. The bowl is the poet's richness, and the translation keeps "lordly," because the Hebrew adirim is the word for the great.

"Her hand reached for the tent peg." Verse 26 is the poem's climax, and it names the peg, the hammer and the temple exactly as Judges 4:21 does. The shelf reads the hammer as "workmen's hammer" or "mallet," and the translation keeps "workmen's hammer."

"Between her feet he sank." Verse 27 repeats the fall three times, each with a different verb: bowed, fell, lay. The repetition is the poem's slowing, and the translation keeps the three verbs, which the ASV also keeps.

Verses 28–30 · the mother at the lattice, and the answer she gives herself

"Through the window she looked out and wailed." Verse 28 shifts to Sisera's mother, watching for the chariot that is not coming. The verb vattiyabev, she wailed, is a cry, and the translation keeps that sense rather than a look.

"She answered herself." Verse 29 says the wisest of her ladies answered, and then "she herself answered her." The Hebrew af-hi is the emphatic "she also," and the translation keeps the line as the poet wrote it, so the reader meets the mother's own reassurance.

"A womb, two wombs." Verse 30 is the hardest line in the song. Rekhem rakhamatayim is a word for womb or for women, used as a spoil for each warrior. The Hebrew has the word for womb, and the shelf reads it three ways: the NWT 1984 "a womb—two wombs", the TNM 1987 "una matriz... dos matrices", and the TNM 2019 "una joven, dos jóvenes" in the translation of the same phrase. The translation keeps "womb," because that is what the Hebrew says, and the note reports the reading that treats the word as "girls" without adopting it.

Verse 31 · the enemies perish, and the land rests forty years

The song's last line is a prayer. Kein yoavdu kol-oyevekha, so let all your enemies perish, turns the song from narrative to petition, and it ends with the forty years of rest that the book has been counting since Judges 3:11.

"Those who love him." The sun going out in its might is the image of the faithful, and the translation keeps "the sun going out" as the Hebrew has it.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: "bless" for the refrain barkhu, kept as one word through the three appearances; "villagers" for peraz and pirzon, the same word in both places; "womb" for rekhem, with the reading that treats it as "girls" reported and not adopted; "saddlebags" for mishpetayim, the hapax; and "tent peg" for yated, as in Judges 4.

Echoes paid. The "tent peg" of Judges 4:21 is repeated in verse 26, and "the brook Kishon" of Judges 4:7 is the flood of verse 21. The forty years of rest, counted from Judges 3:11, is the song's last word. Taanach, which the book names in Joshua 12:21 (already on these pages), is the place where the kings of Canaan fought.

Echoes planted. Meroz, cursed for staying home, is the last town the song names. The next chapter is the story of Gideon, the man who comes up from the threshing floor.
