A scroll sealed with seven seals appears in the hand of the one on the throne, and no one in heaven, on earth, or under the earth is found worthy to open it — until John is told to look, and sees not a lion but a Lamb, 'standing, as though it had been slain.' The Lamb takes the scroll, and heaven erupts in worship: the twenty-four elders, then myriads of angels, then, finally, 'every creature in heaven and on earth and under the earth and in the sea.'
c. AD 95, continuing directly from chapter 4's throne-room vision — the scroll's opening, seal by seal, drives the whole judgment sequence that follows through chapter 8.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–4 · a scroll no one is able to open · βιβλίον… κατεσφραγισμένον σφραγῖσιν ἑπτά biblion… katesphragismenon sphragisin hepta

The scroll is sealed with seven seals — not one seal to break and read, but a document that will be opened seal by seal, each break its own event, across the chapters that follow. ⚠ Written ‘on the inside and on the back’ (v1) most likely pictures a papyrus roll so full its writer had to turn it over and keep going, or a double-written legal document of the kind Roman law required to be sealed by multiple witnesses — either way, a document meant to be complete and unappealable once opened.

⚠ John weeps ‘much’ (v4) — a real, extended verb in Greek, not a moment's disappointment — because a search across the whole of creation, heaven and earth and under the earth, has failed. The chapter spends four verses establishing that failure before it answers it, so the answer in v5 lands as an actual reversal rather than a foregone conclusion.

⚠ ‘Scroll’ or ‘book’? KJV and ASV both read ‘book’ throughout this chapter; NIV and ESV read ‘scroll,’ the physical object biblion actually names in the first century — a rolled papyrus or parchment, not a bound codex, which did not yet exist.

Verses 5–7 · you hear a lion, and you see a lamb · ὁ λέων… ἀρνίον ἑστηκὸς ὡς ἐσφαγμένον ho leōn… arnion hestēkos hōs esphagmenon

⚠ The chapter's central move happens between v5 and v6, and it is a deliberate mismatch between what John is told and what he sees. The elder announces a Lion — ‘the Lion of the tribe of Judah, the Root of David,’ two royal, conquering titles (Genesis 49:9-10; Isaiah 11:1,10) that promise exactly the kind of champion the search in vv1-4 seems to require. John turns to look at the Lion, and what stands there is a Lamb, standing as though slain — a slaughtered-and-risen sacrificial animal, not a predator. The conquest announced in v5 turns out to have already happened, and it was the slaying.

⚠ KJV alone drops a word Greek actually has. The text reads the Lamb hestēkos, ‘standing,’ as though slain — KJV renders only ‘a Lamb as it had been slain,’ leaving the standing out entirely; ASV, NIV and ESV all keep it. A small omission, but it is the detail that makes the image work: a slain animal that is nonetheless on its feet.

The word for ‘Lamb’ here is arnion, a diminutive — ‘little lamb’ — and it is the word this book will use for Christ twenty-eight more times, never once switching to amnos, the plain word used of Christ at John 1:29 and 1:36, Acts 8:32, and 1 Peter 1:19. Revelation's Lamb is consistently the small, vulnerable word, standing next to a throne, straight through to the wedding and the final chapters.

Verses 8–10 · a new song, and a textual crux over who is singing about whom · ἠγόρασας… ἐκ πάσης φυλῆς καὶ γλώσσης καὶ λαοῦ καὶ ἔθνους ēgorasas… ek pasēs phylēs kai glōssēs kai laou kai ethnous

⚠ Verses 9–10 carry a real, well-known textual variant, not a translation choice. KJV reads ‘hast redeemed us to God… hast made us… kings and priests… we shall reign,’ first person throughout — following the Greek text behind it. ASV, NIV and ESV all read third person — ‘persons… them… they’ — following the earlier manuscripts, which is the reading kept here. The same split holds in Spanish: RV60 reads ‘nos’ throughout, NVI reads ‘ellos.’ The elders and living creatures are singing about the redeemed, in the earlier text — not necessarily singing as members of that redeemed company themselves.

⚠ ‘Every tribe and tongue and people and nation’ is a four-part formula this book will repeat, with small variations in order, six more times (7:9, 10:11, 11:9, 13:7, 14:6, 17:15) — Revelation's own standing phrase for the whole of humanity, first used here.

Verses 11–14 · the count widens, three times · Ἄξιόν ἐστιν τὸ ἀρνίον τὸ ἐσφαγμένον axion estin to arnion to esphagmenon

The chapter closes by widening its own choir three times over: first the twenty-four elders and four living creatures (v8), then ‘myriads of myriads and thousands of thousands’ of angels (v11) — a Greek phrase for an uncountable number, not a census — and finally ‘every creature… in heaven and on the earth and under the earth and on the sea’ (v13), the same three-tiered cosmos the seven-sealed scroll was declared unopenable in back at v3, only now filled with a single voice instead of a single silence.

⚠ The angels' hymn (v12) names seven things — power, wealth, wisdom, might, honor, glory, blessing — the same number as the scroll's seals, the Lamb's horns, and the Lamb's eyes in this chapter alone. And the hymn is grounded in a different reason than chapter 4's: there the twenty-four elders declared God worthy for creating (4:11); here the Lamb is declared worthy for being slain. Two worthiness-hymns, two grounds, one throne.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation sides with the earlier text over the Textus Receptus: v9-10's third-person “them…they” rather than KJV/RV60's first-person “us…we” — a real manuscript-tradition split, not a stylistic one, and flagged as such rather than silently adopting either shelf's choice.

Echoes paid. The door standing open and the throne room from chapter 4 continue directly into this scene — same setting, same twenty-four elders and four living creatures. The seven spirits of God (v6), now in a fourth setting after 1:4, 3:1, and 4:5. And chapter 4's creation-hymn to God is answered here by a redemption-hymn to the Lamb — the pairing that closing note promised.

Echoes planted. The four-part “tribe and tongue and people and nation” formula (v9), which recurs six more times before this book ends. “Kingdom and priests” (v10), already used of the redeemed at Revelation 1:6 and behind both at Exodus 19:6's ‘kingdom of priests.’ And the Lamb, standing as though slain (v6) — the single image this entire book will keep returning to, worship scene after worship scene, all the way to its final chapters.

⚠ Left open on purpose. Whether the elders and living creatures sing of themselves or of others in vv9-10 depends on which Greek text is read, and the library reports the split rather than resolving it. And exactly what the seven seals will each release — this chapter names the scroll and the one worthy to open it, and stops there on purpose.
