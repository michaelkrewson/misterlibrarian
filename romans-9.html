The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. Chapter 8 ended on nothing able to separate us from the love of God; chapter 9 opens, with no connecting word, on an oath and a grief for Paul’s own people, and then answers the charge that God’s word has “fallen through” in four moves: from Isaac and Rebekah’s twins, from Pharaoh and the potter, from Hosea and Isaiah, and from a stone of stumbling.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 8; this library dates the letter no more closely than those chapters do. The only time-markers the chapter uses are the ones it quotes: “at this season” (verse 9), “not yet born” (verse 11) and “in the place where it was said” (verse 26).

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · Ἀλήθειαν λέγω ἐν Χριστῷ, οὐ ψεύδομαι … ηὐχόμην γὰρ ἀνάθεμα εἶναι αὐτὸς ἐγὼ ἀπὸ τοῦ Χριστοῦ alētheian legō en Christō, ou pseudomai … ēuchomēn gar anathema einai autos egō apo tou Christou — Romans 9:1–3: “I could pray to be, I myself, accursed, away from the Christ.” An oath, a grief, and a wish that nothing in the Greek lets him carry out

“I am telling the truth in Christ, I am not lying” (alētheian legō en Christō, ou pseudomai, Romans 9:1). After the end of chapter 8, which closes on Romans 8:39 (“nor height nor depth, nor any other created thing will be able to separate us from the love of God”), the letter changes key without a connecting word, and Paul begins by swearing. The words “I am not lying” (ou pseudomai) stand in four verses of the New Testament (searched): here; Galatians 1:20 (“look, before God — I am not lying”); 2 Corinthians 11:31 (“the God and Father of the Lord Jesus knows, he who is blessed forever, that I am not lying”); and 1 Timothy 2:7, where “I am telling the truth, I am not lying” is the same string of Greek words as the opening of this verse with “in Christ” taken out (these last two not yet on these pages; Greek checked). Here the oath is sworn “in Christ” and the witness is the conscience, not God by name. What he is about to say follows “there is therefore now no condemnation” (8:1) and a list in which nothing can separate, and it needs an oath for that reason: the same writer has a grief that does not stop.

“My conscience bearing witness with me” (symmartyrousēs moi tēs syneidēseōs mou). The compound symmartyreō, “bear witness with,” stands in three verses (symmartyreō, searched), and the three make a short chain: in Romans 2:15 the conscience of people who have no written law “bears witness with” their thoughts, in the same genitive-absolute construction as here (symmartyrousēs autōn tēs syneidēseōs / symmartyrousēs moi tēs syneidēseōs mou); in 8:16 the Spirit bears witness with “our spirit”; and here the conscience bears witness “in Holy Spirit.” Syneidēsis (dictionary) is the knowing-together that Paul has already named as a witness, and now he calls it as his own, with a qualifier that the earlier use did not need. The Greek has no article before “Holy Spirit” (en pneumati hagiō). NWT 1984 “in holy spirit” prints it lower-case, which is a doctrinal choice (the NWT does not treat the Spirit as a person); KJV “in the Holy Ghost”, ASV “in the Holy Spirit” and NIV “through the Holy Spirit” supply “the,” and the NIV’s “through” turns the Spirit into the means by which the conscience confirms it. This page prints “in the Holy Spirit,” as at Acts 11:16, and the preposition stays “in.”

“Great grief … unceasing pain” (lypē … adialeiptos odynē, Romans 9:2). Lypē (dictionary), “grief,” stands next to odynē, “pain,” and the verse puts “unceasing” on the second. The adialeipt- family stands in six verses (adialeipt-, searched): this one; Romans 1:9; 1 Thessalonians 1:2; 2 Timothy 1:3; and 1 Thessalonians 2:13 and 5:17 (these two not yet on these pages). The letter’s first use is the prayer: “how unceasingly I make mention of you.” So the writer who said in chapter 1 that he prays for Rome without stopping says in chapter 9 that there is a pain in his heart which does not stop, and he does not say that the second cancels the first. The sentence does not say what the pain is for; verse 3 does.

“I could pray to be accursed” (ēuchomēn anathema einai). Three things in the clause can be read more than one way. The verb is euchomai, which stands in seven verses of the New Testament (euchomai, searched; proseuchomai and the “thanks” words are separate) and means “pray,” “wish” or “vow,” the first two so close that the Greek does not ask which. The shipped pages print it “pray” at Acts 26:29 (“I would pray to God”) and 27:29 (“they were praying for day to come”), and so does this page, even though in this clause the shelf says “wish.” The tense is the imperfect with no an, and so the Greek leaves open three things: that he once kept praying it, that he is praying it now, or that he would pray it if praying it could do any good. The shelf takes the third or the first. KJV “I could wish”, ASV “I could wish”, NIV “I could wish” and NWT 1984 “I could wish” are the potential; Geneva “I would wish” and TLB “I would be willing” are close to it; Douay “I wished myself to be an anathema” keeps the past. The Greek does not have a helping “if that would save you” (the TLB’s reading of the sentence); what it has is a wish, stated, that is not carried out.

“Accursed” (anathema). The noun stands in six verses (anathema, searched; the verb anathematizō of Mark 14:71 is a seventh verse of the same stem): Acts 23:14 (the forty men who say “with a curse we have bound ourselves under a curse”), this verse, Galatians 1:8 and 1:9 (“let him be accursed”), and 1 Corinthians 12:3 and 16:22 (these two not yet on these pages). At Galatians it is pronounced on someone else, and at 1 Corinthians 16:22 on anyone who does not love the Lord; in Acts the forty put it on themselves to kill; here the speaker wishes it on himself for the sake of others. This page prints “accursed” as at Galatians 1:8–9. The shelf is split on the whole of it. KJV “accursed from Christ” and ASV “anathema from Christ” are literal; Douay “an anathema from Christ” carries the Greek word over; NIV “cursed and cut off from Christ” adds “cut off”; NWT 1984 “separated as the cursed one from the Christ” adds “separated”; Geneva “separate from Christ” has no word for the curse at all; and the TLB “forever damned” says what it thinks the curse means. The preposition is apo, “away from,” and the Greek does not say what is cut: it does not say “damned,” and it does not say “separated from the love of God,” the phrase of 8:39 whose words it echoes.

Many readers set beside this wish the prayer of Moses at Exodus 32:32, “if not, blot me, I pray, out of your book that you have written,” after the people had made the calf. The two do not share a word in the Greek (the Greek Old Testament there has exaleipson me ek tēs biblou sou), so it is a parallel of situation and not a quotation, and Paul does not name it. The text gives it one more thing in its favor: in verse 15 Paul quotes the next chapter of Exodus, 33:19, the answer to Moses’s request, “Show me, I pray, your glory” (33:18).

The apparatus marks one variant in verse 3, and it is only the order of three words. The SBLGNT has anathema einai autos egō, with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition; the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont has autos egō anathema einai, which puts “I myself” before the curse. The sense is the same and the weight falls a little differently.

“My brothers, my relatives according to the flesh” (tōn adelphōn mou tōn syngenōn mou kata sarka). In this letter “brothers” has been the word for the congregation (7:1, 7:4), and Paul adds two things so that it is not heard that way here: “my relatives” and “according to the flesh,” the phrase of 4:1 (“our forefather according to the flesh”) and of 1:3 (“from the seed of David according to the flesh”). The noun syngenēs stands in Romans only here and in chapter 16 (Romans 16:7, 16:11 and 16:21, not yet on these pages; searched), where it is used of named individuals. Here it is a people.

Verses 4–5 · οἵτινές εἰσιν Ἰσραηλῖται, ὧν ἡ υἱοθεσία … ὁ χριστὸς τὸ κατὰ σάρκα, ὁ ὢν ἐπὶ πάντων, θεὸς εὐλογητὸς εἰς τοὺς αἰῶνας hoitines eisin Israēlitai, hōn hē huiothesia … ho christos to kata sarka, ho ōn epi pantōn, theos eulogētos eis tous aiōnas — Romans 9:4–5: “who are Israelites, whose are the adoption … who is over all, God blessed forever.” Six possessions, two more clauses, and a sentence with three places to put the full stop

“Who are Israelites” (hoitines eisin Israēlitai, Romans 9:4). Israēlitēs stands in nine verses of the New Testament (Israēlit-, searched). Five are the address “Men, Israelites” in Acts (2:22, 3:12, 5:35, 13:16, 21:28); one is John 1:47, “truly an Israelite in whom there is no deceit”; and three are Paul speaking of himself or of his kin: this verse, Romans 11:1 (“for I too am an Israelite”) and 2 Corinthians 11:22 (“Are they Israelites? So am I”), the last not yet on these pages. The pronoun is hoitines, “who, being such as they are” (NWT 1984 “who, as such, are Israelites”), and the clause that follows is the list.

The list: six nouns, each with its own article. “Whose are the adoption, and the glory, and the covenants, and the giving of the law, and the sacred service, and the promises.” Each item is worth a line, and the shelf shows where it was read narrowly.

Adoption (huiothesia, dictionary) stands in five verses (searched): Romans 8:15 and 8:23, here, Galatians 4:5 (not yet on these pages) and Ephesians 1:5. The word that chapter 8 gave to those who cry “Abba” is the first thing here that belongs to Israel, and the Old Testament says it in a sentence: “Israel is my firstborn son” (Exodus 4:22). The glory (hē doxa) is the glory of Jehovah that “filled the tabernacle” (Exodus 40:34), and it is set in the list as something with an address. The covenants is plural in the Greek (hai diathēkai), and no manuscript variant is marked at this word in the SBLGNT apparatus; KJV “the covenants”, ASV “the covenants”, NIV “the covenants”, NWT 1984 “the covenants” and Geneva “the Covenants” keep the plural, and Douay “the testament” has the singular. The giving of the law (hē nomothesia) stands in one verse of the New Testament (nomothesia, searched), and it is the giving, an act of God, that is the possession. NIV “the receiving of the law” turns it round to Israel’s side, and KJV, ASV and NWT 1984 “the giving of the law” do not. The sacred service (hē latreia) stands in five verses (latreia, searched; the verb latreuō is separate): this one, John 16:2 (“offering sacred service to God”), Romans 12:1 and Hebrews 9:1 and 9:6 (these two not yet on these pages). NWT 1984 “the sacred service” is the wording this page prints at John 16:2; KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva “the service of God” add “of God,” which the Greek does not have; and NIV “the temple worship” narrows it to the building. The promises is plural; in chapter 4 the promise was singular (4:13, 4:16). Epangelia (dictionary) returns in verses 8 and 9, where the chapter first argues from a promise.

“Whose are the fathers, and from whom is the Christ according to the flesh” (ho christos to kata sarka). The list changes grammar at the seventh item: the first six are nouns that Israel has, and the last two are clauses about where Israel came from and who came from Israel. The fathers are the patriarchs (“our forefather Abraham,” 4:1; “Isaac our father,” 9:10). The Christ has the article, ho christos, so that it is the title and not yet the name. The phrase to kata sarka, with the article before “according to the flesh,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one (to kata sarka, searched): “the Christ, as far as the flesh goes.” It limits the claim and does not deny anything beyond it; it is the qualification of Romans 1:3 (“from the seed of David according to the flesh”), which is followed there by “appointed Son of God in power.” The shelf does what the Greek does not: NIV “from them is traced the human ancestry of the Messiah” and the TLB “a Jew so far as his human nature is concerned” add “human ancestry” and “human nature,” which are ways of reading “flesh” and not what it says.

“Who is over all, God blessed forever” (ho ōn epi pantōn, theos eulogētos eis tous aiōnas). The Greek has no mark between the words, and the SBLGNT prints a comma after pantōn. Three ways of dividing it have all been printed in Bibles. First, one sentence about the Christ, with “God” as a name given to him: “the Christ, … who is over all, God blessed forever.” That is the reading of KJV “who is over all, God blessed for ever”, ASV “who is over all, God blessed for ever”, Douay “who is over all things, God blessed for ever”, Geneva “who is God over all, blessed forever” and the text of the NIV “who is God over all, forever praised!” Second, a full stop after “flesh,” and then a separate blessing of the Father: “God, who is over all, be blessed forever.” That is NWT 1984 “God, who is over all, [be] blessed forever”, with its verb in brackets because the Greek has none, and its 2013 revision “God, who is over all, be praised forever”. Third, a full stop after “all”: “the Christ, … who is over all. God be blessed forever.” Of the seven versions on the shelf this is the TLB “he who now rules over all things. Praise God forever!” The NIV prints the first reading and puts the other two in its footnote at this verse (“Or Messiah, who is over all. God be forever praised! Or Messiah. God who is over all be forever praised!”), and the Geneva Bible of 1599 has a marginal note on the first reading, “A most manifest testimony of the Godhead and divinity of Christ.” The count on the shelf is five, one and one, and this page does not vote on it.

The Greek gives each side something to point to. Eulogētos stands in eight verses of the New Testament (eulogētos, searched). In four of them a blessing is spoken to God, and the word opens the clause: Luke 1:68, Ephesians 1:3, 1 Peter 1:3 and 2 Corinthians 1:3. In three it follows a name or a title and says something about the one named: Romans 1:25 (“the Creator, who is blessed forever. Amen”), 2 Corinthians 11:31 (ho ōn eulogētos eis tous aiōnas, “he who is blessed forever,” after “the God and Father of the Lord Jesus”; not yet on these pages; Greek checked) and Mark 14:61 (“the Son of the Blessed”). Romans 9:5 is the eighth. Those who take the first reading point to the three: here, too, the word comes after the name and goes with ho ōn, “who is.” Those who take the second or the third point to the four, where a blessing to God is spoken with the word first and this one is not, and to the phrase epi pantōn, “over all,” which stands in two verses (epi pantōn, searched), here and Ephesians 4:6, where it is said of the Father (not yet on these pages). The early Greek manuscripts are reported to carry little or no punctuation (reported, not checked), so the choice is made by the editor and the translator, and this page prints the comma of the Greek text and leaves the rest to the reader.

Verses 6–9 · οὐχ οἷον δὲ ὅτι ἐκπέπτωκεν ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ. οὐ γὰρ πάντες οἱ ἐξ Ἰσραήλ, οὗτοι Ἰσραήλ … Ἐν Ἰσαὰκ κληθήσεταί σοι σπέρμα ouch hoion de hoti ekpeptōken ho logos tou theou. ou gar pantes hoi ex Israēl, houtoi Israēl … En Isaak klēthēsetai soi sperma — Romans 9:6–9: “the word of God has fallen through” and “not all who are out of Israel are Israel.” Two Israels, a seed that is “called,” and a promise stitched from two verses of Genesis

“The word of God has fallen through” (ekpeptōken ho logos tou theou, Romans 9:6). The sentence begins with a half-sentence, ouch hoion de hoti, “it is not such as that” (Paul has no verb for the first clause and the English adds one: “it is not as though”). The verb ekpiptō, “fall out, fall off, fall away,” stands in ten verses of the New Testament (ekpiptō, searched), and what falls in the other nine is a set of chains (Acts 12:7), a ship that runs aground and a boat that is let go (Acts 27:17, 26, 29, 32), grace that a person falls from (Galatians 5:4), a flower that falls off (James 1:11, 1 Peter 1:24) and a person who may fall from steadiness (2 Peter 3:17); Galatians 5:4 and 2 Peter 3:17 are not yet on these pages. Here it is a word. The picture is of a thing that was held and is no longer held, and this page prints “fallen through,” as one says of a plan. The shelf has four ways of saying it. KJV “taken none effect” and Geneva “take none effect” use the old phrase for “become void”; ASV “come to nought”; Douay “miscarried”; and NIV “failed”, NWT 1984 “failed” and the TLB “failed to fulfill his promises” say the modern one, the last with the object put in.

“Not all who are out of Israel — these are Israel” (ou gar pantes hoi ex Israēl, houtoi Israēl). The one word Israēl stands twice in the verse, and it names two different groups: first the people who come “out of” the man Israel (that is, Jacob, renamed in Genesis 32:29 on these pages), then something narrower. The Greek does not say what the narrower group is. The verses after it answer with names (Isaac, then Jacob), and the chapter’s own answer later uses a word (“the remnant,” verse 27), but the verse itself only says that the line drawn is not the line of descent. Readers differ over who the second “Israel” is: the ones within the nation whom God has called, a remnant, which is the word the chapter reaches at verse 27; or a spiritual Israel of those who believe, understood to include people from the nations. The margin of the Geneva Bible of 1599 reads the word the first way round: “Israel in the first place, is taken for Jacob: and in the second, for the Israelites,” the man first and the people second. The TLB “not everyone born into a Jewish family is truly a Jew” has changed the name “Israel” into “Jew,” which is a second step. What the verse does not do is shut anyone out of the promise, and the letter says so itself: in verse 4 they “are Israelites,” and at 11:1 Paul asks, “God has not pushed away his people, has he?” and answers, “May it never be!” (mē apōsato ho theos ton laon autou? mē genoito; Greek checked).

“In Isaac shall seed be called for you” (En Isaak klēthēsetai soi sperma, Romans 9:7). The quotation is Genesis 21:12 as the Greek Old Testament has it (en Isaak klēthēsetai soi sperma, with the opening “for” left off), and its Hebrew is ki b’Yitzchaq yiqqare lekha zara, “for in Isaac seed shall be called for you.” The verse has a setting that matters: God is telling Abraham to listen to Sarah about the slave-woman and the boy, and the next verse says of the other son, “I will make him into a nation, for he is your seed” (21:13). So in Genesis the line that carries the promise is drawn between two sons who are both called Abraham’s seed; and Paul’s clause, “nor, because they are Abraham’s seed, are they all children,” draws it in the same place. The word sperma, “seed,” stands in three verses of this chapter (verses 7, 8 and 29; searched in Romans), and in verse 29 it is what Isaiah says Jehovah of armies left. The verb klēthēsetai is “shall be called” in the sense of “named, designated,” as the Hebrew yiqqare is, and it is the first appearance of a verb (kaleō) that the chapter keeps using: verses 7, 12, 24, 25 and 26.

“Reckoned as seed” (logizetai eis sperma, Romans 9:8). The verb is the ledger verb of Romans 4 (dictionary; “reckoned to him as righteousness,” 4:3), turned from Abraham to his children: Abraham was counted righteous by his faith, and here the children of the promise are counted as seed. KJV “counted for the seed”, Geneva “counted for the seed”, ASV “reckoned for a seed”, Douay “accounted for the seed”, NIV “regarded as Abraham’s offspring” and NWT 1984 “counted as the seed” all keep an accounting word except the NIV, which adds “Abraham’s.” The contrast is ta tekna tēs sarkos, “the children of the flesh,” against ta tekna tēs epangelias, “the children of the promise,” and in the Greek the second is not “children of God by belief”: the TLB’s “only those who believe the promise of salvation which he made to Abraham” has put belief into a verse that says only “of the promise.”

“At this season I will come, and Sarah shall have a son” (kata ton kairon touton eleusomai kai estai tē Sarra huios, Romans 9:9). The quotation is put together from two sentences of Genesis 18. Genesis 18:10 in the Greek Old Testament has kata ton kairon touton, “at this season,” with the verb hēxō; 18:14 has kai estai tē Sarra huios, “and Sarah shall have a son,” with the verb anastrepsō. Paul’s opening phrase is the first of these and his closing clause is the second, and his verb eleusomai, “I will come,” is in neither. The Hebrew of both verses has shov ashuv and ashuv, “I will return.” The shelf follows Paul’s verb or the Hebrew one: ASV “According to this season will I come”, KJV “At this time will I come” and NWT 1984 “At this time I will come” say “come,” and NIV “At the appointed time I will return” says “return,” with a footnote that names both verses of Genesis 18, “10,14.” The verb is a small thing to notice, and it shows how the quotation is made: Paul keeps what the promise says and does not keep every word of the verse it comes from.

Verses 10–13 · Ῥεβέκκα ἐξ ἑνὸς κοίτην ἔχουσα … ἡ κατʼ ἐκλογὴν πρόθεσις … Ὁ μείζων δουλεύσει τῷ ἐλάσσονι … Τὸν Ἰακὼβ ἠγάπησα, τὸν δὲ Ἠσαῦ ἐμίσησα Rhebekka ex henos koitēn echousa … hē kat’ eklogēn prothesis … Ho meizōn douleusei tō elassoni … Ton Iakōb ēgapēsa, ton de Ēsau emisēsa — Romans 9:10–13: “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated.” Twins by one father, a purpose “according to choosing,” and a verb of hatred in a verse about two peoples

“Rebekah also, having conceived by one man” (Rhebekka ex henos koitēn echousa, Romans 9:10). The first example (verses 7–9) could be answered by pointing to the mothers: Sarah was the wife and Hagar the slave-woman. The second takes that away. Rebekah conceives “by one man, Isaac our father,” and the twins are born together (Genesis 25:24: “there were twins in her womb”), so that nothing differs between them in father, mother or time. The margin of the Geneva Bible of 1599 sees the same step: it says the twins were “of one mother, and at one birth, and not at divers as Ishmael and Isaac were.” The Greek idiom is koitēn echein, “to have a bed,” and koitē stands in four verses of the New Testament (koitē, searched; compounds and koitōn, “bedroom,” are not counted): Luke 11:7 (“my children are with me in bed”), this verse, and Romans 13:13 and Hebrews 13:4 (these two not yet on these pages). It means the bed as the place where a child begins, and the sentence says “conceived” in that sense. Paul includes himself in the line: “our father.”

“Not yet born, nor practiced anything good or worthless” (mēpō gennēthentōn mēde praxantōn ti agathon ē phaulon, Romans 9:11). Phaulos stands in six verses (phaulos, searched): John 3:20 (“everyone who does worthless things hates the light”), John 5:29, this verse, 2 Corinthians 5:10, Titus 2:8 and James 3:16 (the last three not yet on these pages). Three of the six set it beside “good” as the two possible kinds of deed: John 5:29, this verse and 2 Corinthians 5:10. In John 5:29 “those who have done good things” and “those who have practiced vile things” (this page prints “vile” there, and “worthless” at 3:20) come out to a resurrection of life or of judgment, and in 2 Corinthians 5:10 each person receives what is due “for what he practiced … whether good or worthless” (Greek checked). In those two the pair is what a person is judged by. Here it is named in order to say that it is not what the choice was made on: it had not yet been practiced. The apparatus has a variant at this word. The SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition read phaulon; the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont reads kakon, “evil.” On the shelf, KJV “good or evil”, Geneva “neither done good nor evil” and Douay “any good or evil” say “evil” (the English word could translate either Greek word); ASV “good or bad”, NIV “good or bad” and NWT 1984 “good or vile” are nearer phaulon. This page prints “worthless,” as at John 3:20.

“The purpose of God according to choosing might remain” (hē kat’ eklogēn prothesis tou theou menē). Prothesis (dictionary) is the noun of Romans 8:28 (“called according to purpose”), and eklogē (dictionary) is the “choosing” the dictionary asks readers to see before the doctrine grew on it. The verb is menē, “might remain,” and the margin of the Geneva Bible reads it closely: “Paul saith not, might be made, but being made, might remain.” The shelf puts it three ways. KJV “might stand”, ASV “might stand”, NIV “might stand” and Douay “might stand” read the verb as “hold up”; Geneva “might remain according to election” keeps the verb the Greek has; and NWT 1984 “might continue dependent, not upon works, but upon the One who calls” adds “dependent” and makes the next clause its complement. The clause ouk ex ergōn all’ ek tou kalountos (verse 12), “not out of works but out of the one who calls,” sets the two sources against each other as 3:20 and 4:4–6 did, and the participle kalountos, “the one who calls,” is the God of Romans 4:17 who “calls the things that are not as though they were.” The same participle returns for the people called in verses 24–26.

“The greater shall be a slave to the lesser” (ho meizōn douleusei tō elassoni, Romans 9:12). The quotation is the last clause of Genesis 25:23, which in the Greek Old Testament has the same Greek words, and the oracle it ends begins “Two nations are in your womb, and two peoples from within you will be divided.” So the sentence Paul takes from Genesis is a sentence about two peoples, said to Rebekah before the birth, and the Hebrew has rav ya’avod tsa’ir, literally “the great shall serve the small.” Greek has its own word for “older,” presbyteros, as in the older son of Luke 15:25; Paul’s word, meizōn, is the comparative of “great,” and this page prints it that way and so differs from the shelf. Six of the seven versions on the shelf say “elder” or “older” (KJV “The elder shall serve the younger”, ASV “The elder shall serve the younger”, Douay “The elder shall serve the younger”, Geneva “The Elder shall serve the younger”, NIV “The older will serve the younger” and NWT 1984 “The older will be the slave of the younger”), and the TLB says “Esau, the child born first, would be a servant to Jacob.” The verb douleuō is the “slave” verb of 6:6 and 7:6.

“Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated” (Ton Iakōb ēgapēsa, ton de Ēsau emisēsa, Romans 9:13). The opening words, kathōs gegraptai, “as it is written,” have one variant in the apparatus: Tregelles, the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont read kathōs, and Westcott–Hort read kathaper, “just as.” The English is the same either way. The quotation is Malachi 1:2–3, and the Greek Old Testament has the same two verbs with the objects after them (ēgapēsa ton Iakōb, ton de Ēsau emisēsa); Paul has put the names first. In Malachi the speaker is Jehovah, the question is “In what have you loved us?”, and the answer begins with a family fact, “Was not Esau Jacob’s brother?”, and goes on to what was done to Esau’s land: “I made his mountains a desolation.” So the verse is about a people and a place as well as a man. The verb is miseō, which the dictionary explains at Luke 14:26, and the Hebrew behind it, sana’ti, is used the same way in the law and the narratives. In Deuteronomy 21:15 a man has two wives, “the one loved and the one hated,” and the next verses forbid him to treat the loved wife’s son as firstborn over the hated one’s son (21:16–17). In Genesis 29:30–31 Jacob “loved Rachel more than Leah,” and the next verse says Leah was shənu’ah, the passive participle of the same verb, which this site prints “unloved” (29:31). The Gospels have the same ranking in two forms, Luke 14:26 (“does not hate his own father and mother”) and Matthew 10:37 (“loves father or mother more than me”). In each of these the word sets one over another. What the word does not tell a reader is whether the ranking is a feeling, a choice of one line over another, or a statement about the two peoples; the verse says only that it was said, and that it is written.

The shelf shows how hard the verb is to leave alone. KJV “Jacob have I loved, but Esau have I hated”, ASV “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated”, NIV “Jacob I loved, but Esau I hated”, NWT 1984 “I loved Jacob, but Esau I hated” and Geneva “I have loved Jacob, and have hated Esau” all keep “hated.” The TLB does not: it makes the pair “I chose to bless Jacob but not Esau,” a paraphrase that moves the sentence from a feeling to a decision and removes the quotation altogether. Readers differ over the whole passage: whether verses 10–13 are about the two sons as persons and their standing before God, or about the two peoples descended from them and their roles in a history that Genesis 25:23 and Malachi 1 both speak of; and, inside either reading, whether verse 11 (“not yet born, nor practiced anything”) is about salvation or about a place in the story. This page prints the words and the sources they come from, and does not vote.

Verses 14–18 · μὴ ἀδικία παρὰ τῷ θεῷ; μὴ γένοιτο … Ἐλεήσω ὃν ἂν ἐλεῶ … ἐξήγειρά σε … ὃν δὲ θέλει σκληρύνει mē adikia para tō theō? mē genoito … Eleēsō hon an eleō … exēgeira se … hon de thelei sklērynei — Romans 9:14–18: “Is there unrighteousness with God? May it never be!” and “whom he wishes he hardens.” The question of 3:5 asked again, two sentences from the Moses story, and the only verse in the New Testament where God is the one who hardens

“Is there unrighteousness with God? May it never be!” (mē adikia para tō theō? mē genoito, Romans 9:14). The question comes in the form the letter has used since chapter 4: “What shall we say, then?” (4:1, 6:1, 7:7). The answer, mē genoito, “May it never be!”, stands in ten verses of Romans (mē genoito, searched), and this is the eighth: 3:4, 3:6, 3:31, 6:2, 6:15, 7:7 and 7:13 come before it, and 11:1 and 11:1 and 11:11 after. The question itself was asked at 3:5 (“Is God unrighteous, he who brings down the wrath?”) and answered at 3:6 with the same two words, and the phrase para tō theō, “with God,” is the one of 2:11 (“there is no partiality with God”). So the reader has met this question before, in two forms, and has the answer in both. The noun adikia is built on the root of dikaiosynē, “righteousness,” so that the word the whole letter has been building is in the question. The shelf splits over the English: KJV “unrighteousness”, ASV “unrighteousness” and Geneva “unrighteousness” keep the pair; Douay “injustice” and NWT 1984 “injustice” say the court word; NIV “Is God unjust?” and the TLB “Was God being unfair?” turn the noun into a verdict on God. Notice also what Paul does not do. He gives no argument from fairness. The next sentence begins “For to Moses he says,” and what follows is a quotation.

“I will have mercy on whomever I have mercy” (Eleēsō hon an eleō, kai oiktirēsō hon an oiktirō, Romans 9:15). The opening, tō Mōusei gar legei, has only a difference of order in the apparatus: the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition put gar, “for,” after “to Moses,” and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont puts it before. The quotation is Exodus 33:19, and the Greek Old Testament has Paul’s words there, with only a different spelling of the second verb (oikteirēsō). Its setting is Moses asking, “Show me, I pray, your glory” (33:18), after the calf, and the answer is that Jehovah will make all his goodness pass before him and proclaim his name, and then this sentence. In the Hebrew the sentence repeats its verb after the relative word (v’chanotti et-asher achon v’richamti et-asher ’rachem), the construction of “I WILL BE WHAT I WILL BE” at Exodus 3:14, so the sentence that explains the name there and the sentence about mercy here are built alike. This site prints the two Hebrew verbs in Exodus 33:19 “show favor” and “show mercy,” in that order, and Paul’s Greek verbs, eleeō and oiktirō, “have mercy” and “have compassion” here; the two pairs of English words do not line up one to one, and a reader should not build on the difference. The shelf renders the Greek as follows. KJV “I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy” and Geneva “I will have mercy on him, to whom I will show mercy” use the future in both halves; ASV “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy” and NIV “I will have mercy on whom I have mercy” use the present in the second; NWT 1984 “upon whomever I do have mercy” adds “do”; Douay “I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy; and I will shew mercy to whom I will shew mercy” has “mercy” in both halves, which loses the second Greek verb; and the TLB “If I want to be kind to someone, I will” turns the relative clause into a wish.

“Not of the one who wishes, nor of the one who runs, but of God who has mercy” (ou tou thelontos oude tou trechontos alla tou eleōntos theou, Romans 9:16). The verse has no verb; the three genitives hang from an unspoken “it is.” Two things in it are worth seeing. The first is the verb thelō, “wish,” which is used four times in this stretch of the chapter, and the subject changes: the person who “wishes” is outside the decision in verse 16, and God is the one who “wishes” in verse 18 (twice) and in verse 22 (thelei and thelōn). The second is that the verse does not say that wishing and running are bad. It says what the outcome is “of”; and Geneva “not in him that willeth, nor in him that runneth, but in God” puts “in” where the others put “of,” and its margin explains the pair: “By will, he meaneth the thought and endeavor of heart, and by running, good works.” KJV “not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth”, ASV “not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth” and Douay “not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth” stay with the genitive; NWT 1984 “it depends, not upon the one wishing nor upon the one running” adds “depends”; and NIV “human desire or effort” drops the two participles for two nouns.

“For this very thing I raised you up” (eis auto touto exēgeira se, Romans 9:17). The quotation is Exodus 9:16, and Paul’s Greek is not the Greek Old Testament’s and not the Hebrew. The Hebrew verb is he’emadtikha, “I have made you stand,” and this site prints it “I have kept you standing”; the verse before it explains why the verb is about survival: “by now I could have stretched out my hand and struck you … and you would have been wiped from the earth” (9:15). The Greek Old Testament has dietērēthēs, “you were kept through,” and the word for the power is ischyn. Paul has exēgeira, “I raised you up,” and dynamin, “power.” The verb exegeirō stands in two verses of the New Testament (exegeirō, searched): this one, and 1 Corinthians 6:14, where it is said of God, “God both raised the Lord and will raise us up” (not yet on these pages; Greek checked). It is a compound of egeirō, the verb the letter has used of the resurrection (4:24, 4:25, 6:4, 6:9, 7:4), and “raised up” leaves open whether Pharaoh was brought to the throne, spared the plagues, or both. The shelf shows the difference between the Hebrew and the Greek: NWT 1984 “I have let you remain” follows the Hebrew verb, whereas KJV “raised thee up”, ASV “did I raise thee up”, NIV “I raised you up”, Douay “have I raised thee” and Geneva “have I stirred thee up” follow Paul’s. The TLB “he had given him the kingdom of Egypt” reads the verb as an appointment. The NWT’s rendering is closer to Exodus; it is not closer to the Greek of Romans, and this page is translating Romans.

“Whom he wishes he hardens” (hon de thelei sklērynei, Romans 9:18). The verse is built like verse 16: ara oun, “so then,” after a Scripture. The verb sklērynō stands in six verses of the New Testament (sklērynō, searched), and only here is God the one who does it. In Acts 19:9 some “were hardening themselves”; in Hebrews 3:8, 3:15 and 4:7 it is the command “do not harden your hearts,” and in 3:13 the warning “that none of you be hardened” (the four Hebrews verses are not yet on these pages; Greek checked). The Exodus story does not keep one subject. Read in the Hebrew, Jehovah is the doer of the hardening in Exodus 4:21 (achazzeq, “I will harden”), 7:3 (aqsheh, “I will harden”), 9:12 (vayechazzeq, which this site prints “made strong”), 10:1 (hikhbadti, “I have made heavy”) and 10:20 (vayechazzeq, “made strong”); Pharaoh is the doer in 9:34 (vaykhbed libbo, “he made his heart heavy”); and in 7:13 and 8:15 the heart simply grows firm (vayechezaq, which this site prints “was hardened” at 7:13 and “was strong” at 8:15), with no one named (these are the verses read here and not a count of the whole story). The Greek Old Testament has sklērynō at 4:21, 7:3, 8:15, 9:12, 10:1 and 10:20, with Jehovah as the subject at five of them, and barynō, “make heavy,” for Pharaoh at 9:34. So the verb Paul takes is the one the Greek Old Testament has, in these verses, where Jehovah is the doer. The shelf: KJV “whom he will he hardeneth”, ASV “hath mercy on whom he will” (with “hardeneth” at the end of the verse), Douay “whom he will, he hardeneth”, Geneva “whom he will he hardeneth” and NIV “hardens whom he wants to harden” keep the verb and its subject. NWT 1984 “whom he wishes he lets become obstinate” changes a verb that does into a verb that allows, and says “obstinate” for “hard”; the 2013 revision keeps “lets … become obstinate.” The TLB “he makes some refuse to listen” is a third wording, and an interpretation. The Greek has the active verb with God as subject; what the verse does not say is whether the hardening is a first act or a confirming one, and the Exodus verses above show that Pharaoh’s story is told both ways. Readers divide over whether Romans 9 is about the destiny of individuals or the roles of peoples in history, and this verse is where the division is felt most. This page leaves the question where Paul leaves it: in the next verse, an objector asks it.

Verses 19–21 · Ἐρεῖς μοι οὖν· Τί οὖν ἔτι μέμφεται; τῷ γὰρ βουλήματι αὐτοῦ τίς ἀνθέστηκεν; ὦ ἄνθρωπε, μενοῦνγε σὺ τίς εἶ … ὁ κεραμεὺς τοῦ πηλοῦ ἐκ τοῦ αὐτοῦ φυράματος Ereis moi oun· Ti oun eti memphetai? tō gar boulēmati autou tis anthestēken? ō anthrōpe, menounge su tis ei … ho kerameus tou pēlou ek tou autou phyramatos — Romans 9:19–21: “who has withstood his plan?” and “O man, who are you, answering back to God?” The objector again, a word for “plan” that is not the word for “wish,” and a potter whom the prophets used before Paul

“You will say to me, then” (Ereis moi oun, Romans 9:19). The voice that asks is an imagined one (“You will say to me”), and the address that answers it in verse 20, ō anthrōpe, “O man,” is the address Paul used to the judge of Romans 2:1 and 2:3. The phrase ō anthrōpe stands in five verses of the New Testament (ō anthrōpe, searched): those two, this verse, 1 Timothy 6:11 and James 2:20 (the last two not yet on these pages). Here the apparatus marks two small variants in verse 19, one of word order (moi oun against the Byzantine oun moi) and one of a particle, the second oun of “Why then (oun) does he still find fault?” which the SBLGNT keeps with the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and which Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont leave out. No meaning turns on either. Douay “Why doth he then find fault?” and NIV “Then why does God still blame us?” keep a “then”; KJV “Why doth he yet find fault?”, ASV “Why doth he still find fault?” and NWT 1984 “Why does he yet find fault?” do not.

“Who has withstood his plan?” (tō boulēmati autou tis anthestēken). The objector’s noun is boulēma, built on boulomai, “intend, plan.” It stands in three verses of the New Testament (boulēma, searched): here; Acts 27:43, where the centurion “prevented them from their plan”; and 1 Peter 4:3, the “intention” of the nations (not yet on these pages; Greek checked). It is not the word Paul has used for God’s wishing in verses 16, 18 and 22, thelō. The objector speaks of God’s plan; Paul has spoken of what God wishes. The English shelf does not mark the difference: KJV “his will”, ASV “his will”, NIV “his will”, Douay “his will” and Geneva “his will” use “will” for both words, and only NWT 1984 “his express will” marks that this one is not the common one. The perfect anthestēken is “has stood against and is standing against”: KJV “hath resisted” and Geneva “hath resisted”, ASV “withstandeth” and NWT 1984 “has withstood”, NIV “is able to resist” (which adds “able”) and Douay “resisteth.” The question sets up the answer that does not come. The objection is the one verse 18 invites: if he hardens whom he wishes, why does he still find fault?

“O man — on the contrary, who are you, answering back to God?” (ō anthrōpe, menounge su tis ei ho antapokrinomenos tō theō, Romans 9:20). Two words repay a look. Menounge is a particle that corrects the question instead of answering it: “no, rather …” (the apparatus has only its place: the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition put it after ō anthrōpe, and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont before it); it stands in Luke 11:28 (menoun), here, Romans 10:18 and Philippians 3:8 (menoun(ge), searched; the last not yet on these pages), and in this verse it is the Greek of KJV and ASV “Nay but”, NIV “But” and the TLB “No, don’t say that.” NWT 1984 “who, then, really are you” makes it “really.” (The Douay leaves the particle out.) Antapokrinomai, “answer back,” stands in two verses (antapokrinomai, searched), this one and Luke 14:6, where the lawyers and Pharisees “were not able to answer him back on these things”; this page prints it the same way in both. KJV, ASV and Douay “repliest against” and Geneva “pleadest against” are older English for the same act, and NIV “talk back” is the modern one.

“Will the thing molded say to the one who molded it” (mē erei to plasma tō plasanti). The first six Greek words are those of the Greek Old Testament at Isaiah 29:16 (mē erei to plasma tō plasanti; the chapter is not yet on these pages), and the question that follows, Ti me epoiēsas houtōs?, “Why did you make me like this?”, is Paul’s own wording of a question the prophets ask in other forms. In Hebrew, Isaiah 29:16 has the clay-worker say, “He did not make me”, and Isaiah 45:9 has the clay say to its potter, “What are you making?” (mah-ta’aseh; both read in the Hebrew); the NIV’s footnote at this verse names both, “Isaiah 29:16; 45:9.” The verb plassō, “mold, form,” stands in two verses of the New Testament (plassō, searched; the other stems that contain these letters are unrelated words): here and 1 Timothy 2:13 (“Adam was formed first”; not yet on these pages). It is the verb of the Greek Old Testament’s Genesis 2:7 (kai eplasen ho theos ton anthrōpon, “and God formed the man”), for the Hebrew vayyitser, from yatsar, the potter’s verb. So the image has a root in the first creation: the human is the thing formed from the ground, and the one who forms is the one of whom the question is asked.

“The potter … out of the same lump” (ho kerameus tou pēlou ek tou autou phyramatos, Romans 9:21). Kerameus stands in three verses (kerameus, searched): here, and Matthew 27:7 and 27:10, the field of the potter. Phyrama, “lump, kneaded mass,” stands in five verses (phyrama, searched), and in the other four it is a mass of dough: three with leaven (1 Corinthians 5:6, 5:7 and Galatians 5:9, none yet on these pages) and one the lump whose first portion is holy (Romans 11:16). This is the one verse where it is clay. The pair eis timēn … eis atimian, “for honor … for dishonor,” has its noun for the second also at Romans 1:26, “passions of dishonor” (the noun atimia stands in Romans only there and here; searched), and a later letter uses the same two phrases for the vessels of a great house and adds a condition: “if anyone cleanses himself from these things, he will be a vessel for honor” (2 Timothy 2:20–21; not yet on these pages; Greek checked). The shelf: KJV “one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour”, ASV “one part a vessel unto honor, and another unto dishonor”, Douay “one vessel unto honour, and another unto dishonour” and NWT 1984 “one vessel for an honorable use, another for a dishonorable use” keep the pair; NIV “some pottery for special purposes and some for common use” softens the second to “common”; and the TLB makes the two jars into “beautiful, to be used for holding flowers” and one “to throw garbage into,” which adds a picture the Greek does not have. The Greek says skeuos, “vessel” (dictionary), the word of Acts 9:15.

The image is older than Paul, and its parts do not all point the same way. At the potter’s house in Jeremiah 18:4 the vessel that was ruined “in the potter’s hand” is made again “as it seemed right in the potter’s eyes”; in 18:6 Jehovah says, “Can I not do to you as this potter does, house of Israel?”; and the verses that follow set a condition inside the same scene: if a nation God has spoken against “turns back from its evil … then I relent” (18:7–8), and if a nation does evil “not listening to my voice, then I relent of the good” (18:9–10). Paul’s verse takes the first of these, the potter’s right (exousia, “authority”), and does not quote the second. A reader should know both are in the prophet’s scene, and that this verse does not say what Jeremiah 18:7–10 says. The encyclopedia entry for the potter has the craft behind the metaphor.

Verses 22–24 · εἰ δὲ θέλων ὁ θεὸς ἐνδείξασθαι τὴν ὀργὴν … σκεύη ὀργῆς κατηρτισμένα εἰς ἀπώλειαν … σκεύη ἐλέους ἃ προητοίμασεν εἰς δόξαν ei de thelōn ho theos endeixasthai tēn orgēn … skeuē orgēs katērtismena eis apōleian … skeuē eleous ha proētoimasen eis doxan — Romans 9:22–24: “vessels of wrath that had been made ready for destruction … vessels of mercy, which he prepared beforehand for glory.” A long “if” with no “then,” two verbs for getting a vessel ready, and a relative word that changes from things to people

“But if God …” (ei de thelōn ho theos, Romans 9:22). The sentence of verses 22–24 begins with an “if” and never reaches its “then”; the SBLGNT ends it at verse 24 with a question mark and a dash. All seven versions on the shelf finish the sentence for Paul, and the way it does so is the first thing to see. KJV “What if God, willing to shew his wrath”, ASV “What if God, willing to show his wrath”, Douay “What if God, willing to shew his wrath” and NIV “What if God, although choosing to show his wrath” supply “What” in front; Geneva “What and if God would, to show his wrath” does the same in an older form; NWT 1984 “If, now, God, although having the will to demonstrate his wrath” keeps the bare “if” and puts the missing question in brackets at the end of verse 24, “[what of it]?”, and the 2013 revision moves it to the front, “What, then, if God had the will”; and the TLB “Does not God have a perfect right to show his fury” changes the “if” into a question about a right. This page prints the bare “But if” and the dash, and the broken sentence stays broken. The participle thelōn, “wishing,” is the verb of verses 16 and 18, and the shelf divides over how it joins the clause: NIV “although choosing” and NWT 1984 “although having the will” make it concessive (God wished to show wrath, and yet he bore with patience), while KJV, ASV and Douay “willing” leave the link open. The Greek participle alone does not say either.

“To show his wrath and to make his power known” (endeixasthai tēn orgēn kai gnōrisai to dynaton autou). The verb endeiknymi is the verb of verse 17 (endeixōmai en soi tēn dynamin mou, “I might show in you my power”), so verses 17 and 22 use the same verb for what God wishes to show, his power. The verb and its noun stand in Romans at 2:15, 3:25, 3:26, 9:17 and 9:22 (searched; the noun endeixis at 3:25–26, “to demonstrate his righteousness”), and the wrath is the wrath of 1:18, 2:5 (“storing up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath”) and 3:5. Gnōrisai, “make known,” is used again in verse 23 for the riches of his glory, so that one verb makes known both his power and his glory.

“Bore with much patience” (ēnenken en pollē makrothymia). The patience is the makrothymia of Romans 2:4 (“the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience”), the noun of the dictionary. The verb is pherō, “carry, bear.” KJV “endured”, ASV “endured” and Douay “endured” make it a thing God put up with; NIV “bore with” and NWT 1984 “tolerated” are nearer; Geneva “suffer” is the older sense. The riches come back in the next verse as the ploutos of God’s glory, as they were the riches of his kindness in 2:4.

“Vessels of wrath that had been made ready for destruction” (skeuē orgēs katērtismena eis apōleian). The noun is skeuos (dictionary), carried over from verse 21, and eis apōleian, “for destruction,” stands in five verses of the New Testament (dictionary): Acts 8:20, here, and Hebrews 10:39 and Revelation 17:8 and 17:11 (these three not yet on these pages). The participle katērtismena is a perfect with an ending that is the same for the middle and the passive in Greek, so it can mean “made ready (by someone)” or “having made themselves ready,” and it does not name the agent. The verb katartizō and its nouns stand in sixteen verses (katartizō, searched; two of the sixteen, 2 Corinthians 13:9 and Ephesians 4:12, have the noun), and its sense is “put in order, mend, fit out”: it is said of mending nets (Matthew 4:21, Mark 1:19), of a student who is “fully trained” (Luke 6:40), of the praise “you have prepared” out of the mouth of infants (Matthew 21:16) and of the worlds that “were set in order by God’s word” (Hebrews 11:3). The word is about fitting a thing for its use. Whether in 9:22 someone fitted the vessels, or they fitted themselves, the Greek does not say. The shelf: KJV “fitted to destruction”, ASV “fitted unto destruction” and Douay “fitted for destruction” keep the sense of fitting; NWT 1984 “made fit for destruction” keeps the sense of fitting and reads it as a passive; Geneva “prepared to destruction” and NIV “prepared for destruction” use the same English verb as their next verse does for the other vessels, so that the two Greek verbs become one; and the TLB “fit only for destruction” adds “only.”

“Vessels of mercy, which he prepared beforehand for glory” (skeuē eleous ha proētoimasen eis doxan, Romans 9:23). The verb is a different one. Proetoimazō, “prepare beforehand,” stands in two verses (proetoimazō, searched): this one, and Ephesians 2:10, “good works, which God prepared beforehand.” It is active and has God as its subject, and it has the prefix pro-; the verb for the vessels of wrath has neither a stated agent nor the “beforehand.” Paul’s other “beforehand” verbs of this letter are the ones of 8:29, “those he knew beforehand, he also marked out beforehand,” and the sequence that follows in 8:30 ends in “glorified,” so that “for glory” here is the end of that chain. The apparatus has a variant in verse 23. The text reads kai hina, “and in order that,” with Tregelles, the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont; Westcott–Hort leave out kai. With it, the clause can be read as a second purpose beside “to show his wrath”; without it, as the purpose of the patience of verse 22. This page prints “and so that.”

“Even us, whom he also called” (hous kai ekalesen hēmas, Romans 9:24). The relative pronoun changes. In verse 23 it is ha, neuter, for the vessels; in verse 24 it is hous, masculine, for persons, and the persons are “us.” The vessels of wrath are not named in the sentence; the vessels of mercy are, and the group they are drawn from is “not only from Jews but also from nations,” the pairing of 3:29 (“Or is God the God of Jews only? Is he not also of the nations?”). The verb is kaleō, the one of 8:30 (“those he called, these he also justified”) and of verses 7 and 12 of this chapter, and it opens the quotation of verse 25. The Greek has no article before “Jews” or “nations”; the page leaves them bare. KJV “not of the Jews only, but also of the Gentiles” and ASV “not from the Jews only, but also from the Gentiles” add the article and use “Gentiles”; NWT 1984 “not only from among Jews but also from among nations” keeps the bare nouns.

Verses 25–26 · ὡς καὶ ἐν τῷ Ὡσηὲ λέγει· Καλέσω τὸν οὐ λαόν μου λαόν μου καὶ τὴν οὐκ ἠγαπημένην ἠγαπημένην … υἱοὶ θεοῦ ζῶντος hōs kai en tō Hōsēe legei· Kalesō ton ou laon mou laon mou kai tēn ouk ēgapēmenēn ēgapēmenēn … huioi theou zōntos — Romans 9:25–26: “I will call the one who is not my people ‘my people.’” Two sentences of Hosea, a verb that is not in either, and the sons of verse 26 who answer the adoption of verse 4

“I will call the one who is not my people ‘my people’” (Kalesō ton ou laon mou laon mou, Romans 9:25). Paul says the words are “in Hosea” (en tō Hōsēe), and they are made from two places in the book. The first sentence is built on Hosea 2:25 in the Hebrew numbering (English Bibles number it 2:23; the NIV’s footnote at this verse reads “Hosea 2:23”; the chapter is not yet on these pages), which in Hebrew begins “And I will sow her for myself in the land” (the verb of zera) and goes on: v’richamti et-lo ruchamah, v’amarti l’lo-ammi ammi-attah, “and I will have compassion on Not-Shown-Compassion, and I will say to Not-My-People, ‘You are my people.’” The Greek Old Testament there has agapēsō tēn ouk ēgapēmenēn, kai erō tō ou laō mou, laos mou ei su. Compare Paul. His phrase for the woman, tēn ouk ēgapēmenēn ēgapēmenēn, is the Greek Old Testament’s participle (“the not-beloved”), and so the Greek of Romans has “beloved” where the Hebrew has “compassion”; but the first word of the quotation, Kalesō, “I will call,” is in neither the Hebrew nor the Greek of Hosea 2:25. It is the verb of this chapter (verses 7, 12, 24), and the God who “calls the things that are not as though they were” (Romans 4:17) now calls the one who is not. The shelf: KJV “I will call them my people, which were not my people; and her beloved, which was not beloved”, ASV “I will call that my people, which was not my people; and her beloved, that was not beloved”, NIV “I will call them ‘my people’ who are not my people; and I will call her ‘my loved one’ who is not my loved one”, NWT 1984 “Those not my people I will call ‘my people,’ and her who was not beloved ‘beloved’” and Geneva “I will call them, My people, which were not my people: and her, Beloved, which was not beloved” follow the Greek. Douay adds a third clause, “and her that had not obtained mercy, one that hath obtained mercy,” which the Greek of Romans 9:25 does not have and the Hebrew of Hosea does. The TLB turns the verse into “he will find other children for himself (who are not from his Jewish family),” and the bracketed clause is the TLB’s, not Paul’s or Hosea’s.

The names behind the quotation (Hosea 1:6, 1:9). The two names are Lo-Ruchamah and Lo-Ammi, which this site prints “Not-Pitied” and “Not-My-People”: the names Jehovah gives to the daughter and the younger son in the household of Hosea, as signs. The first is explained, “for I will no longer have compassion on the house of Israel” (1:6), and the second, “for you are not my people, and I will not be I-WILL-BE to you” (1:9). So the people who are “not my people” in Hosea are the house of Israel, and the sentence of reversal is said to them. Paul applies it to the ones “whom he also called, not only from Jews but also from nations” (verse 24), and it can be read as a use of the passage for a new group or as an application of a rule it states for the old one; the TLB takes the first side and the bare text of Romans takes neither. A later letter, 1 Peter 2:10, writes the same pair of phrases to its readers, “who once were not a people, but now are the people of God” (the Greek says hoi pote ou laos nyn de laos theou; not yet on these pages; Greek checked).

“There they shall be called sons of the living God” (ekei klēthēsontai huioi theou zōntos, Romans 9:26). This is Hosea 2:1 in the Hebrew numbering (English 1:10; the NIV’s footnote: “Hosea 1:10”), which says in the Hebrew, “in the place where it was said to them, ‘You are not my people,’ it shall be said to them, ‘Sons of the living God’” (b’nei El-chai), and in the Greek Old Testament kai estai en tō topō hou errhēthē autois, ou laos mou hymeis, klēthēsontai kai autoi huioi theou zōntos. Paul’s Greek is that, with ekei, “there,” for the “also they” of the Greek Old Testament. The SBLGNT apparatus marks one variant here: the word autois, “to them,” is in the text with Westcott–Hort, the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont, and Tregelles leaves it out. Nothing in the sense turns on it. The verb is again kaleō, “be called,” and the noun is huioi, “sons.” The chapter has said “children of God” (tekna tou theou, verse 8), and the first thing in Israel’s list was huiothesia, “adoption as sons” (verse 4); now “sons” is what the called ones are named. KJV “the children of the living God”, NIV “children of the living God” and Geneva “the children of the living God” say “children”; ASV “sons of the living God”, NWT 1984 “sons of the living God” and Douay “the sons of the living God” say “sons” (the Greek word is huioi), and the TLB “And the heathen, of whom it once was said” adds “the heathen” as its subject.

Verses 27–29 · Ἠσαΐας δὲ κράζει ὑπὲρ τοῦ Ἰσραήλ· Ἐὰν ᾖ ὁ ἀριθμὸς τῶν υἱῶν Ἰσραὴλ ὡς ἡ ἄμμος τῆς θαλάσσης, τὸ ὑπόλειμμα σωθήσεται … Εἰ μὴ κύριος Σαβαὼθ ἐγκατέλιπεν ἡμῖν σπέρμα Ēsaias de krazei hyper tou Israēl· Ean ē ho arithmos tōn huiōn Israēl hōs hē ammos tēs thalassēs, to hypoleimma sōthēsetai … Ei mē kyrios Sabaōth enkatelipen hēmin sperma — Romans 9:27–29: “the remnant shall be saved” and “unless Jehovah of armies had left us a seed.” Two verses of Isaiah and the last words of a verse of Hosea, joined into one sentence

“Isaiah cries out concerning Israel” (Ēsaias de krazei hyper tou Israēl, Romans 9:27). The verb is the present krazei, “cries out,” so that the prophet is still crying, and the preposition is hyper, which at its plainest means “on behalf of.” All seven versions on the shelf have “concerning” (KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva, NIV and NWT 1984 “concerning Israel”, and the TLB “concerning the Jews”), and this page does the same, because a cry on behalf of Israel that says “only a remnant” would be a strange advocate. The first clause is made of two sources. Its opening, ean, “if,” and its last words, to hypoleimma sōthēsetai, “the remnant shall be saved,” belong to Isaiah 10:22, whose Greek Old Testament has kai ean genētai ho laos Israēl hōs hē ammos tēs thalassēs, to kataleimma autōn sōthēsetai (not yet on these pages). The middle, ho arithmos tōn huiōn Israēl hōs hē ammos tēs thalassēs, “the number of the sons of Israel like the sand of the sea,” is the opening of the Greek Old Testament’s Hosea 2:1, the verse whose second half Paul quoted in verse 26. In Hosea the sand of the sea is a promise of a great number: the Hebrew has v’hayah mispar b’nei-Yisra’el k’chol hayyam asher lo yimmad v’lo yissafer, “yet the number of the children of Israel shall be as the sand of the sea, which cannot be measured nor numbered.” In Paul’s sentence it has become a concession: even if they are that many.

“The remnant shall be saved” (to hypoleimma sōthēsetai). The Hebrew of Isaiah 10:22 is sh’ar yashuv bo, “a remnant of it shall return,” the two words of the name Shear-Yashub that Isaiah gave his son (she’ar yashuv, Isaiah 7:3; both read in the Hebrew). The Greek Old Testament replaced “return” with “be saved,” and Paul keeps “be saved”; so the verb of the Greek of Romans is not the verb of Isaiah’s Hebrew. The noun is the second thing to see. The SBLGNT has hypoleimma, with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition, and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont has kataleimma, which is the word of the Greek Old Testament’s Isaiah 10:22 given above. Hypoleimma stands in one verse of the New Testament (hypoleimma, searched), this one, and so the reading the Byzantine text prints is the one that agrees with the prophet. The shelf has “a remnant” (KJV “a remnant shall be saved”, Douay “a remnant shall be saved”, Geneva “but a remnant be saved”, NIV “only the remnant will be saved”) and “the remnant” (ASV “it is the remnant that shall be saved”, NWT 1984 “it is the remnant that will be saved”). The Greek has the article, to hypoleimma, and this page prints “the remnant.” The TLB “only a small number would ever be saved” makes it a count.

“Jehovah will carry out a word upon the earth, finishing it and cutting it short” (logon gar syntelōn kai syntemnōn poiēsei kyrios epi tēs gēs, Romans 9:28). The Greek reads kyrios, and the quotation is from the Old Testament, so the page prints “Jehovah.” The apparatus has a long variant at this verse. The SBLGNT ends the quoted clause at syntemnōn, “cutting short,” with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition; the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont adds after it en dikaiosynē hoti logon syntetmēmenon, “in righteousness, because a cut-short word,” before the rest. Those added words are the Greek Old Testament’s Isaiah 10:23 (syntemnōn en dikaiosynē, hoti logon syntetmēmenon Kyrios poiēsei en tē oikoumenē holē), so the longer reading is the one that agrees with the Greek prophet, as at verse 27; and the shorter is the one that does not fill the gap. The Hebrew of Isaiah 10:22–23 has killayon charuts, shotef tsedaqah, “a decreed destruction, overflowing with righteousness,” and kalah v’nechertsah, “an end, and a decreed one,” the Lord Jehovah of armies making it “in the midst of all the earth.” The shelf is divided exactly where the manuscripts are. The longer text is in KJV “cut it short in righteousness: because a short work will the Lord make upon the earth”, Douay “cut it short in justice; because a short word shall the Lord make upon the earth” and Geneva “gather it into a short sum with righteousness: for the Lord will make a short count in the earth”; the shorter text is in ASV “finishing it and cutting it short” and NWT 1984 “concluding it and cutting it short”; the TLB “justly cutting them short” has the added word; and the NIV “with speed and finality” carries a footnote that reads “Isaiah 10:22,23 (see Septuagint).” The Hebrew of Isaiah 10:23 has no noun for “word”; logos, “word, account, sentence,” is the Greek prophet’s noun, and this page prints “word.”

“Unless Jehovah of armies had left us a seed” (ei mē kyrios Sabaōth enkatelipen hēmin sperma, Romans 9:29). The quotation is Isaiah 1:9, and it is the Greek Old Testament’s sentence, with the opening “and” left off and one verb given its augment. The Hebrew has sarid kim’at, “a few survivors” (as this site prints it; dictionary), where the Greek Old Testament and Paul have sperma, “seed.” So the noun of verses 7 and 8, “Abraham’s seed” and “reckoned as seed,” is also the noun of this verse for what Jehovah left. The title kyrios Sabaōth keeps the Hebrew word tseva’ot in Greek letters, and Sabaōth stands in two verses of the New Testament (Sabaōth, searched): here and James 5:4 (not yet on these pages). This site prints the title “Jehovah of armies” (dictionary). KJV “the Lord of Sabaoth”, ASV “the Lord of Sabaoth” and Douay “the Lord of Sabaoth” keep the Greek word; Geneva “the Lord of hosts”, NIV “the Lord Almighty” and NWT 1984 “Jehovah of armies” translate it. The cities are Sodom and Gomorrah. The shelf has “a seed” in KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva and NWT 1984 (“a seed”), and “descendants” in the NIV “had left us descendants.” The TLB “except for God’s mercy all the Jews would be destroyed” has replaced the seed with mercy.

Verses 30–33 · ἔθνη τὰ μὴ διώκοντα δικαιοσύνην κατέλαβεν δικαιοσύνην … Ἰσραὴλ δὲ διώκων νόμον δικαιοσύνης εἰς νόμον οὐκ ἔφθασεν … λίθον προσκόμματος καὶ πέτραν σκανδάλου ethnē ta mē diōkonta dikaiosynēn katelaben dikaiosynēn … Israēl de diōkōn nomon dikaiosynēs eis nomon ouk ephthasen … lithon proskommatos kai petran skandalou — Romans 9:30–33: “Israel, pursuing a law of righteousness, did not arrive at law.” A race that the runners who were not running won, a law that is bare in the Greek and dressed in the Bibles, and a stone made from two prophets

“What shall we say, then?” (Ti oun eroumen?, Romans 9:30). The question of verse 14 is asked again, with no objection after it this time, and the answer is a report: nations who were not pursuing righteousness “laid hold of” it. The two verbs are the verbs of a race. Diōkō, “pursue,” is run-after; katalambanō, “lay hold of,” is what the runner does at the end of it, as in 1 Corinthians 9:24, “Run in such a way that you may lay hold” (houtōs trechete hina katalabēte; not yet on these pages; Greek checked). The race belongs to the chapter already: in verse 16 the outcome is “not of the one who runs.” The third verb, in verse 31, is phthanō, “arrive, come upon,” and it stands in eight verses of the New Testament (phthanō, searched), among them Matthew 12:28 and Luke 11:20, where the kingdom of God “has come upon you.” Here it is the word for reaching the mark, and it is the one thing the pursuer did not do. The nations’ righteousness is said again in the words of the letter’s first theme: it is “the righteousness out of faith” (dikaiosynēn de tēn ek pisteōs), in the language of 1:17 (“from faith to faith”) and 5:1 (“justified, then, out of faith”).

“A law of righteousness … did not arrive at law” (nomon dikaiosynēs eis nomon ouk ephthasen, Romans 9:31). There is no article before either “law,” and the first has a genitive, so Israel pursues “a law that is about righteousness” or “a law that yields it,” and the second “law” is left to be the same one, or the same kind. The letter has said that no such law is a way to it (3:27: “Through what law? Of works? No, but through a law of faith”). The apparatus has a variant here, and the shelf follows it. The SBLGNT has the second nomon bare, with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition; the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont adds dikaiosynēs after it, “did not arrive at the law of righteousness,” so that the thing sought and the thing missed have the same name. KJV “hath not attained to the law of righteousness”, Douay “is not come unto the law of justice” and Geneva “could not attain unto the Law of righteousness” have the longer reading, and ASV “did not arrive at that law” and NWT 1984 “did not attain to the law” the shorter. The NIV “who pursued the law as the way of righteousness, have not attained their goal” changes both: the first “law” gets an article and a purpose, and the second becomes “their goal.” The TLB “tried so hard to get right with God by keeping his laws” writes the whole of a position into the participle.

“Not out of faith but as out of works” (ouk ek pisteōs all’ hōs ex ergōn, Romans 9:32). The word hōs, “as, as though,” is easily dropped, and the NIV keeps it (“as if it were by works”): the verse says the pursuit went on as though the thing to be reached came out of works, and not that Israel reached for works as such. There is a second variant. After ergōn the Byzantine text adds nomou, so that the phrase is “works of the law,” the phrase of 3:20, 28. The SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition have the shorter “works.” The shelf divides again: KJV “as it were by the works of the law” and Geneva “as it were by the works of the Law” have the longer; ASV “as it were by works”, Douay “as it were of works”, NIV “as if it were by works” and NWT 1984 “as by works” the shorter. A third small variant is the Byzantine gar, “for,” after proskopsan: KJV “For they stumbled”, Douay “For they stumbled” and Geneva “for they have stumbled” have it, and the ASV and NIV “They stumbled” do not. The verb prosekopsan, “they stumbled,” and the noun proskomma (dictionary), “a stumbling,” are one word family, and the noun stands in six verses of the New Testament (proskomma, searched): twice here (verses 32 and 33), Romans 14:13 and 14:20, 1 Corinthians 8:9 and 1 Peter 2:8 (these four not yet on these pages).

“I am placing in Zion a stone of stumbling and a rock of stumbling-block” (Idou tithēmi en Siōn lithon proskommatos kai petran skandalou, Romans 9:33). The quotation is made from two verses of Isaiah, and it does not follow either exactly. Its opening, “Look, I am placing in Zion a stone,” is Isaiah 28:16, where the Hebrew has “Behold, I lay in Zion for a foundation a stone, a tried stone, a costly corner-stone” and the Greek Old Testament Idou egō emballō eis ta themelia Siōn lithon polytelē, eklekton, akrogōniaion, entimon; Paul has none of the adjectives. Its middle, “a stone of stumbling and a rock of stumbling-block,” is Isaiah 8:14, where the Hebrew is l’even negef ul’tsur mikhshol, “for a stone of striking and for a rock of stumbling,” said in the verse after “Jehovah of armies, him you shall sanctify” (Isaiah 8:13), of one who “shall be for a sanctuary” and for the two houses of Israel a stone and a rock, “a gin and a snare.” Paul’s Greek follows the Hebrew there and not the Greek Old Testament, which at Isaiah 8:14 says the opposite (kai ouch hōs lithou proskommati synantēsesthe, “and you will not come upon him as upon a stone of stumbling,” after “if you trust in him, he will be for you a sanctuary”). Its end, “and the one who believes on it will not be put to shame,” goes back to Isaiah 28:16 and takes the Greek Old Testament’s verb, kataischynthē, where the Hebrew has lo yachish, “will not hasten.” (Isaiah 8 and 28 are not yet on these pages; Hebrew and Greek checked.) Two Greek words stand for the two Hebrew ones, proskomma for the striking and skandalon (dictionary), the trigger of a trap, for the stumbling; and the same pair, with the same word for the stone, is in 1 Peter 2:8 (lithos proskommatos kai petra skandalou), which also quotes Isaiah 28:16 two verses before and the Hosea sentence two verses after (1 Peter 2:6 and 2:10; not yet on these pages; Greek checked).

The last words carry two decisions for the translator. The apparatus has the Byzantine pas, “everyone,” before ho pisteuōn: KJV “whosoever believeth on him”, Douay “whosoever believeth in him” and Geneva “everyone that believeth in him” have it, and it is the wording Paul gives himself at Romans 10:11 (pas ho pisteuōn ep’ autō ou kataischynthēsetai; now on these pages); the others do not have it here. And the pronoun in ep’ autō is masculine, which agrees with lithos, “stone,” and says nothing of its own about a person. The English has to choose between “him” and “it.” KJV, ASV “on him”, NIV, Douay and Geneva “in him” say “him”; the NWT 1984 “rests his faith on it” says “it”; and the TLB “(Jesus)” names him in a parenthesis. This page prints “on it,” because that is what the Greek pronoun agrees with, and the question of who the stone is, if anyone, is not answered in this chapter. The verb of the end of the verse, kataischynō, is the verb of 5:5, “hope does not put to shame.” The chapter closes on a promise given to the one who believes, and on a stumbling for the one who pursues.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “May it never be!” for mē genoito (v. 14), as at 3:4, 3:6, 3:31, 6:2, 6:15, 7:7 and 7:13; “accursed” for anathema (v. 3), as at Galatians 1:8–9; “pray” for euchomai (v. 3), as at Acts 26:29 and 27:29; “adoption” for huiothesia (v. 4), as at 8:15 and 8:23; “sacred service” for latreia (v. 4), as at John 16:2; “reckoned” for logizomai (v. 8), as at 4:3; “worthless” for phaulos (v. 11), as at John 3:20; “choosing” for eklogē (v. 11), the noun that Acts 9:15 prints as “chosen” in “chosen vessel”; “slave” for douleuō (v. 12), as at 6:6 and 7:6; “hated” for miseō (v. 13), as at Luke 14:26; “plan” for boulēma (v. 19), as at Acts 27:43; “answering back” for antapokrinomai (v. 20), as at Luke 14:6; “vessel” for skeuos (vv. 21–23), as at Acts 9:15; “patience” for makrothymia (v. 22), as at 2:4; “prepared beforehand” for proetoimazō (v. 23), as at Ephesians 2:10; “Jehovah” for kyrios in the two quotations from Isaiah (vv. 28 and 29), as at 4:8; “nations” for ethnē (vv. 24 and 30), as at 1:13 and 3:29; “stumbling-block” for skandalon (v. 33), as at Matthew 16:23 and 18:7; and “put to shame” for kataischynō (v. 33), as at 5:5. Five pairs are split on purpose: eleeō is “have mercy” and oiktirō “have compassion” (v. 15), because the Greek has two verbs; thelō is “wish” (vv. 16, 18, 22) and boulēma “plan” (v. 19); katartizō is “made ready” (v. 22) and proetoimazō “prepared beforehand” (v. 23); tekna is “children” (vv. 7–8) and huioi “sons” (v. 26); proskomma is “stumbling” and skandalon “stumbling-block” (vv. 32–33). And meizōn and elassōn are “greater” and “lesser” (v. 12) where every version on the shelf but one has “elder” or “older” and “younger.” The page prints “vile” at John 5:29 and “worthless” here and at John 3:20 for the same Greek word.

Echoes paid. “What shall we say, then?” (4:1, 6:1, 7:7) — vv. 14 and 30. “May it never be!”, with the question asked at 3:5 (“Is God unrighteous?”) and “no partiality with God” (2:11) — v. 14. Wrath, and forbearance and patience (1:18, 2:4–5) and the demonstration of righteousness (3:25–26) — vv. 17 and 22–23. “O man” (2:1, 3) — v. 20. Conscience bearing witness with (2:15) and the Spirit bearing witness with our spirit (8:16) — v. 1. “Unceasingly” (1:9) — v. 2. “I am not lying” (Galatians 1:20) — v. 1. “According to the flesh” (1:3, 4:1) — vv. 3 and 5. Nothing able to separate us from the love of God (8:39) — the wish of verse 3. Abraham and the promise (4:1, 4:13–21) and the ledger verb (4:3) — vv. 7–9. The God who calls the things that are not (4:17) — vv. 12 and 24–26. “Works” (3:20, 4:4–6) — vv. 11–12 and 32. “Called according to purpose” (8:28), “marked out beforehand … called … glorified” (8:29–30) — v. 11 and vv. 23–24. Adoption (8:15, 23) — v. 4, and the sons of verse 26. “Law of faith” (3:27) — vv. 31–32. Righteousness out of faith (1:17, 5:1) — v. 30. “Hope does not put to shame” (5:5) — v. 33. The eighth “May it never be!” of the letter is verse 14.

Echoes planted. “May it never be!” has two verses left, Romans 11:1 and 11:11, and the first asks whether God has pushed away his people (Greek checked). Eklogē, “choosing,” returns at Romans 11:5, 11:7 and 11:28, with the remnant of verse 27 in the phrase leimma kat’ eklogēn charitos, “a remnant according to a choosing of grace,” at 11:5, and the lump of verse 21 at 11:16. “Righteousness out of faith” (v. 30) is the phrase of Romans 10:6, and “the one who believes on it will not be put to shame” (v. 33) is quoted again, with “everyone” in front, at 10:11 (now on these pages). The stone of stumbling, the rock of the stumbling-block and the Hosea sentence (vv. 25–26, 33) are put together again in 1 Peter 2:6–10; the sacred service (v. 4) returns in Romans 12:1; the pair “for honor … for dishonor” (v. 21) in 2 Timothy 2:20–21; “Jehovah of armies” (v. 29) in James 5:4; and “I am not lying” (v. 1) in 2 Corinthians 11:31 and 1 Timothy 2:7 (these two not yet on these pages). “Are they Israelites?” (v. 4) is 2 Corinthians 11:22 (not yet on these pages). “Judged by what he practiced, good or worthless” (v. 11) is 2 Corinthians 5:10, and running to lay hold (v. 16, v. 30) is 1 Corinthians 9:24 (these two not yet on these pages).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether ēuchomēn in verse 3 is a wish once made, a wish still made, or a wish that would be made if it could help; where the full stop falls in verse 5 (three divisions, and the Greek has none), and so whether “God blessed forever” is said of the Christ or of the Father; who the second “Israel” of verse 6 is; whether verses 10–13 concern two sons as persons or two peoples, and what “hated” ranks; whether sklērynō in verse 18 is a first act of God or a confirming one; whether the participle thelōn in verse 22 is concessive or causal, whether the “if” of verses 22–24 has an unspoken “then,” and whether katērtismena is passive or middle; whether the kai before hina in verse 23 belongs to the text; whether Hosea’s “not my people” (Israel, in Hosea) is applied or extended in verses 25–26; whether the pronoun of verse 33 is “him” or “it”; and whether Romans 9 is about persons or peoples.
