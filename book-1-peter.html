📖 1 Peter

Πέτρου Α (Petrou A) — 'Of Peter, 1' — the first of two letters bearing the name of the apostle Peter, addressed to 'the chosen exiles of the Dispersion' in Asia Minor.

The New Testament's great letter of HOPE in suffering — written to scattered, harassed Christians, calling them exiles born again to a living hope and an inheritance kept in heaven. Tender and dense with Old Testament and liturgy, it teaches a church how to suffer without bitterness: 'Christ suffered for you, leaving you an example.'

Chapter by chapter

1 Peter 1The New Testament’s great letter of hope in suffering — written to scattered EXILES across five provinces of Asia Minor, by (on the traditional reading) the fisherman Peter. It names its readers by their whole condition, parepidēmoi, resident aliens living in a world that is not home, and answers their hardship with a birth: ⚠ “he gave us NEW BIRTH into a living hope through the resurrection,” into “an inheritance imperishable and undefiled and unfading, kept in the heavens.” Present trials are “a little while” and an assayer’s fire: the tested faith is worth more than gold, which perishes though gold itself is proved by flame. ⚠ The chapter holds some of the NT’s most memorable lines — loving a Christ “whom, not having seen, you love… with joy inexpressible”; the prophets who “searched their own oracles” for “the sufferings of Christ and the glories after”; “gird up the loins of your mind”; “be holy, for I am holy”; and the ransom paid “not with silver or gold but with the precious blood of Christ, a lamb without blemish.” It closes on the word that outlasts the grass: “all flesh is grass… but the word of the Lord remains forever.”

About the book

Where it sits
The TWENTY-FIRST book of the New Testament, the second of the General Epistles — and, unlike its sequel 2 Peter, one of the best-attested and least-disputed books in the early canon, quoted by Polycarp and others by the early second century.
Genre
A LETTER, and a genuinely pastoral one — encouragement and instruction for believers under social hostility, woven through with Old Testament quotation, echoes of Jesus' teaching, and what look like fragments of early hymns and creeds (3:18-22) and baptismal catechesis. Some read it as a baptismal sermon turned into a letter, though the letter-form is real.
Author
Named as PETER, the apostle — Simon Peter, the fisherman and leader of the Twelve. ⚠ The traditional authorship went little questioned in the early church and is defended today; but a substantial body of scholarship judges the letter pseudonymous, chiefly because its Greek is polished and cultured (hard to credit of a Galilean fisherman), it leans on the Greek Old Testament, and the persecution it assumes may fit a later date. Defenders answer that Peter names SILVANUS as the one 'through whom' he wrote (5:12) — an educated secretary who would account for the style — and that the sufferings are local hostility, not state persecution. The main options: Peter through Silvanus, or a later disciple in his name. The library reports them and does not vote.
Date written
c. AD 62–64 on the traditional reading, from Rome shortly before Peter's death under Nero; c. AD 80–95 on the pseudonymous view. The date follows the authorship question.
Place
ROME — written from 'Babylon' (5:13), a code-name for Rome used also in Revelation and understood so throughout the early church. Peter, like Paul, is remembered as martyred there under Nero.
Audience
'The chosen EXILES of the Dispersion' in five provinces of northern Asia Minor — Pontus, Galatia, Cappadocia, Asia and Bithynia (1:1). They are mostly GENTILE converts (1:14, 18; 4:3, 'the futile ways inherited from your fathers'), now cut off from their old pagan social world and suffering for it: slander, ostracism, 'various trials' (1:6), 'a fiery ordeal' (4:12), maligned as evildoers (2:12). The letter's aim is to give them an identity — 'a chosen race, a royal priesthood' (2:9) — and a way to suffer, not by retaliation but by doing good, after the pattern of Christ.

How it's laid out

1:1–2:10The new birth and the new people: living hope, an imperishable inheritance, holiness, and 'living stones' built into a spiritual house.
2:11–3:12Living as exiles among outsiders: submission, servants and masters, husbands and wives, and Christ's example under unjust suffering.
3:13–4:19Suffering for doing good: Christ's victory and the 'spirits in prison', arming yourselves with his mind, and rejoicing to share his sufferings.
5:1–14To elders and the flock: humility, watchfulness against the devil 'like a roaring lion', and greetings from 'Babylon'.

What it's about

  • HOPE in SUFFERING — the letter's whole burden: present trials are 'a little while' and a refining fire, set against a 'living hope' and an inheritance 'kept in heaven' (1:3-7).
  • EXILE and IDENTITY — believers are 'exiles', 'strangers and pilgrims' (1:1; 2:11), and also 'a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation, God's own people' (2:9) — dispossessed in the world, given the titles of Israel.
  • THE EXAMPLE OF CHRIST — suffering without retaliation, 'who committed no sin… when he was reviled, did not revile in return' (2:21-23), the pattern the exiles are to follow.
  • NEW BIRTH and HOLINESS — born again of imperishable seed (1:23), called to 'be holy as I am holy' (1:16) and to a conduct so good it silences slander (2:12, 15).
  • SUFFERING then GLORY — the prophets' pattern (1:11) running the length of the letter: share Christ's sufferings now, his glory when he is revealed (4:13; 5:1, 10).
  • GOOD CONDUCT — anastrophē, a keyword: a manner of life among outsiders that is the church's best apologetic, winning some 'without a word' (3:1-2).

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. The Greek of 1 Peter is among the most polished in the New Testament — cultured, rhythmic, and steeped in the Greek Old Testament, which it quotes constantly (Isaiah, Psalms, Proverbs, Leviticus) and often follows against the Hebrew. ⚠ That very quality is central to the authorship debate: it is the main reason some doubt a Galilean fisherman wrote it unaided, and the main reason others point to Silvanus, the named amanuensis (5:12). The text is well preserved; chapter 1 has no major variants, only minor questions of word order and the bracketed 'through the Spirit' at 1:22.

Looking forward

1 Peter's Christ is above all the SUFFERING and GLORIFIED one — the pattern the letter presses on a suffering church. He was 'foreknown before the foundation of the world but made manifest at the end of the times' (1:20), the 'lamb without blemish' whose precious blood ransoms (1:19), the Passover of a new Exodus. He is the example under injustice — 'who committed no sin… who, when he was reviled, did not revile in return, but entrusted himself to the one who judges justly' (2:21-23) — and the one who 'bore our sins in his body on the tree' (2:24, echoing Isaiah 53). ⚠ Two passages have kept interpreters busy for centuries: 3:18-20, Christ 'made alive in the spirit' preaching 'to the spirits in prison', and 4:6, the gospel 'preached even to the dead' — roots of the creed's 'he descended into hell'. And he is the coming 'chief Shepherd' whose appearing will bring the glory the sufferings lead to (5:4).

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP and DATE, which stand together — Peter through Silvanus, or a later hand. The nature and extent of the suffering (local hostility or official persecution), which bears on the date. The meaning of the 'spirits in prison' (3:19) and the gospel 'preached to the dead' (4:6) — Christ's descent, a proclamation to the fallen angels of Genesis 6, or something else. And whether the letter incorporates a baptismal liturgy. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
