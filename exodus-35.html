Construction finally begins. Moses restates the sabbath, adding that no fire may be kindled on it, then repeats the full materials list from chapters 25-31. The people respond by bringing gold, dyed yarn, wood, and thread as a freewill offering — including jewelry described with the identical word used of what Aaron melted for the calf.
c. 1446 BC on the early-date chronology this project foregrounds — begins after Moses' second descent from the mountain (chapter 34); construction runs through chapter 39, ending at the dated erection of chapter 40.

Exodus 35 — The Mister Translation

1
וַיַּקְהֵל מֹשֶׁה, אֶת-כָּל-עֲדַת בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל--וַיֹּאמֶר אֲלֵהֶם: אֵלֶּה, הַדְּבָרִים, אֲשֶׁר-צִוָּה יְהוָה, לַעֲשֹׂת אֹתָם.
And Moses assembled all the congregation of the children of Israel, and said to them: These are the words that Jehovah has commanded, that you should do them.note
Y Moisés reunió a toda la congregación de los hijos de Israel, y les dijo: Estas son las palabras que Jehová ha mandado, que las hagáis.

Six days work shall be done, but on the seventh day there shall be to you a holy day, a sabbath of solemn rest to Jehovah. Whoever does any work on it shall be put to death.note
Seis días se hará obra, mas el séptimo día os será día santo, un sábado de descanso solemne para Jehová. Cualquiera que haga obra en él morirá.

The sabbath, spoken first this time

Every earlier giving of the tabernacle instructions closed with the sabbath command, almost as an afterthought after the building itself (Exodus 31:12–17, already on these pages). Here, as the actual construction finally begins, Moses reverses the order and puts it first. Nothing gets built until the day nothing gets built is settled.

⚠ ‘You shall kindle no fire throughout your dwellings on the sabbath day’ (v3) is a genuinely new detail — the first time any version of the sabbath law has named a specific forbidden act. Every earlier statement (Exodus 20:8–11; 23:12; 31:12–17, all already on these pages) commands rest in general terms; this is the first time the text gets concrete about what counts as work. The single example became disproportionately influential: later rabbinic law built much of its detailed sabbath restrictions by reasoning outward from this one named act, and centuries later it would become a real fault line between rabbinic and Karaite Judaism over whether a fire lit before sundown could be left burning through the sabbath itself.

A list recited twice, now finally about to be built

The material list and the inventory of tabernacle furnishings that fill the rest of this section (vv5–19) are close to a word-for-word recitation of the opening of the whole instruction, seven chapters back (Exodus 25:1–9, already on these pages). Nothing here is new information; Moses is not adding to what Jehovah commanded on the mountain, he is repeating it back to the people who were not there to hear it the first time — the gap between revelation and execution finally closed.

The itemized inventory that follows — tabernacle, ark, table, lampstand, incense altar, altar of burnt offering, courtyard, priestly garments — matches the exact sequence of chapters 25 through 31 in order. This chapter is, in effect, a table of contents for everything about to happen in the six chapters still ahead.

The same gold, given back

‘They brought nose-rings, and ear-rings, and signet-rings, and bracelets, all articles of gold’ (v22) is the same word, nezem, ‘ring,’ that named exactly what Aaron demanded three chapters back — ‘break off the golden rings that are in the ears of your wives, your sons, and your daughters’ (Exodus 32:2, already on these pages). The same jewelry, stripped once under pressure to build an idol, is now handed over freely to build a sanctuary. Nothing forces this offering; the text goes out of its way to say the opposite — ‘everyone whose heart stirred him’ (v21), repeated as ‘as many as were willing-hearted’ (v22) and again as a ‘freewill offering’ (v29). The golden calf was Israel taking gold from God without asking; this is Israel giving gold to God without being asked twice. ⚠ The exact jewelry list is a real fork in the shelf: KJV reads ‘bracelets, and earrings, and rings, and tablets’; ASV and NASB both read ‘brooches, and earrings, and signet-rings, and armlets/bracelets’; NIV simplifies to the general ‘brooches, earrings, rings, and ornaments.’ Four items whose precise ancient shape no version can fully recover; what every reading agrees on is that all of it was gold, and all of it was given.

⚠ Women are named explicitly and separately as skilled craftswomen: ‘all the women who were wise of heart spun with their hands’ (v25), and again, ‘all the women whose heart stirred them in wisdom spun the goat hair’ (v26). The phrase ‘wise of heart’ (chakham-lev) is the identical qualification the text uses for the skilled craftsmen commissioned back at Exodus 28:3, already on these pages, and for ‘every man who is wise of heart’ called to build at v10 above — the same word for skill applied without distinction to men and women alike.

The promise from before the golden calf, restated word for word

Moses’ introduction of Bezalel and Oholiab (vv30–35) repeats, almost verbatim, Jehovah’s own words naming them on the mountain, before any of this book’s crisis had happened (Exodus 31:2–6, already on these pages). The golden calf, the broken tablets, the plea to see God’s glory, the covenant remade — four chapters of rupture and repair sit between the promise and its restatement, and when the text finally returns to it, nothing about the promise itself has changed. Bezalel was named before the crisis and is commissioned after it in the identical words, as if the interruption left no mark on what God intended to build.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. ‘Wise of heart’ kept for chakham-lev (v10, 25, 30 [as ‘wisdom of heart’ in its noun form at v35]), matching this translation’s own earlier choice at Exodus 28:3 and 31:6. ‘Nose-rings, ear-rings, signet-rings, and bracelets’ follows the ASV/NASB line for the four disputed jewelry terms of v22 — see the note above for the real fork in the shelf. ‘Tachash’ kept transliterated rather than guessed at (vv7, 23), matching this translation’s own earlier choice at Exodus 25:5. The chapter’s two paragraph breaks ({פ} at v3, {פ} at v29) mark the seam between the sabbath command and the call for materials, and between the people’s gifts and Bezalel’s commissioning; v35 closes with the Torah’s own {ס}. Verse numbering follows the Hebrew exactly; Hebrew and English Exodus 35 both run 1–35.

A note on order: Exodus stands on these pages at chapters 1–40 — the covenant remade, and now the people themselves respond: the sabbath restated first, a freewill offering of the very gold once stripped for the calf, and Bezalel and Oholiab commissioned exactly as promised before the crisis. Exodus 36, the craftsmen at work and the people’s giving finally restrained, and Exodus 37, the ark and the table and the lampstand actually built, and Exodus 38, the bronze altar and the courtyard built at last, and Exodus 39, the priestly garments finally sewn, are now on these pages too. Exodus 40 — the tabernacle raised and the glory of Jehovah filling it, the book’s own last chapter — is now on these pages too. Exodus stands complete here, all forty chapters; the story continues in Leviticus, already begun on these pages.
