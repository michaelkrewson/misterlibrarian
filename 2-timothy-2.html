Paul in chains writes to Timothy: the soldier, the athlete and the farmer; 'Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead'; a faithful saying; rightly handling the word of truth against talk that spreads like gangrene; vessels for honor; and the Lord's slave who must not quarrel.
Undated by the text; on the traditional reading, Paul's last imprisonment at Rome, mid-60s AD; many scholars date the Pastoral letters later.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · “Be empowered by the grace” — and four links in a chain

“You then, my child, be empowered by the grace that is in Christ Jesus.” “My child” is the address of 2 Timothy 1:2. “Be empowered” is endynamoō, the verb of Paul’s thanks “to the one who empowered me” at 1 Timothy 1:12 and of “in the one who empowers me” at Philippians 4:13 (dictionary). The KJV reads “be strong in the grace”; the ASV reads “be strengthened in the grace”; the NWT 1984 reads “keep on acquiring power in the undeserved kindness.”

⚠ “The things you heard from me through many witnesses, entrust these to faithful people who will be able to teach others also.” “Entrust” is the verb of the “deposit” of 2 Timothy 1:12 and 2 Timothy 1:14 and of 1 Timothy 6:20 (dictionary). The chain has four links: Paul, Timothy, faithful people, and others. “Through many witnesses” is read of the public occasion on which Timothy heard the teaching, the “good confession before many witnesses” of 1 Timothy 6:12, or of the many who can vouch for what Paul taught. Churches have read the chain as a pattern for handing on the teaching, some through an ordered succession of office, some through faithful teachers in each generation; this page records the readings and does not choose. “People” is anthrōpois; the KJV reads “commit thou to faithful men,” the NIV “entrust to reliable people.”

Verses 3–7 · The soldier, the athlete and the farmer

“Suffer along with me as a good soldier of Christ Jesus.” Synkakopatheō, “to suffer hardship along with,” stands in two verses, both in this letter: “suffer along with me for the gospel” at 2 Timothy 1:8, and this one (dictionary). The three pictures that follow, soldier, athlete and farmer, are the three of 1 Corinthians 9:7, “Who ever serves as a soldier at his own expense? Who plants a vineyard and does not eat its fruit?” and of the runners at 1 Corinthians 9:24. The Byzantine text reads “You therefore suffer hardship,” and so the KJV: “Thou therefore endure hardness, as a good soldier of Jesus Christ.” The ASV reads “Suffer hardship with me.”

“Serving as a soldier … entangled in the affairs of everyday life.” Pragmateia, “business, affairs,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does stratologeō, “to enlist a soldier.” The Vulgate adds “to God,” and so the Douay: “No man, being a soldier to God, entangleth himself with secular businesses.” The NIV reads “gets entangled in civilian affairs”; the NWT 1984 reads “the commercial businesses of life.”

“He is not crowned unless he competes lawfully.” Nomimōs, “lawfully, according to the rules,” stands in two verses: the law that is good “if one uses it lawfully” at 1 Timothy 1:8, and the athlete here (dictionary). The crown is the victor’s wreath of 1 Corinthians 9:25, “they do it to receive a perishable crown.” The KJV reads “except he strive lawfully”; the NWT 1984 reads “unless he has contended according to the rules.”

⚠ “The farmer who labors ought to be the first to partake of the fruits.” Readers take it two ways: the worker’s right to the first share, as at 1 Corinthians 9:10, “the one who threshes in hope of a share”; or the order of things, labor first and fruit after. The Geneva takes the second, “The husbandman must labor before he receive the fruits”; the NWT 1984 reads “The hardworking farmer must be the first to partake of the fruits.” “Think over what I say, for the Lord will give you understanding”: the Byzantine text makes the second half a prayer, and so the KJV, “and the Lord give thee understanding in all things.”

Verses 8–10 · “Remember Jesus Christ” — “the word of God is not chained”

“Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead, from the seed of David, according to my gospel.” The two phrases are those of Romans 1:3 and Romans 1:4, “who came from the seed of David according to the flesh” and “appointed Son of God in power” by the resurrection; “according to my gospel” is the phrase of Romans 2:16. The order here puts the resurrection first. The KJV reads “Remember that Jesus Christ of the seed of David was raised from the dead”; the ASV reads “Remember Jesus Christ, risen from the dead, of the seed of David.”

“Even to the point of chains, as an evildoer; but the word of God is not chained.” Kakourgos, “evildoer,” stands in four verses: the two crucified with Jesus at Luke 23:32, 23:33 and 23:39, and Paul here (dictionary). The chained prisoner and the unchained word are the pair of Philippians 1:14, the brothers “made confident in the Lord by my chains,” and of the last words of Acts, Paul teaching “with all boldness, unhindered” at Acts 28:31. The KJV reads “Wherein I suffer trouble, as an evil doer, even unto bonds; but the word of God is not bound.”

“Therefore I endure everything for the sake of the chosen.” “The chosen” is eklektoi, the word of “the chosen angels” at 1 Timothy 5:21. “Endure” is hypomenō, and it returns in the saying of verse 12. The KJV reads “for the elect’s sakes”; the NWT 1984 reads “for the sake of the chosen ones.”

Verses 11–13 · “If we died with him” — “if we are unfaithful, he remains faithful”

“Faithful is the saying: For if we died with him, we will also live with him.” The fourth of the “faithful sayings,” after 1 Timothy 1:15, 1 Timothy 3:1 and 1 Timothy 4:9; its four balanced lines read like a hymn. The first is Paul’s at Romans 6:8, “if we died with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with him”; “reign with him” is the verb of 1 Corinthians 4:8, “so that we could reign with you”; and suffering and glory together are the pair of Romans 8:17. Symbasileuō, “to reign with,” stands in two verses: 1 Corinthians 4:8 and this one (dictionary). The KJV reads “For if we be dead with him, we shall also live with him”; the NWT 1984 reads “if we died together, we shall also live together.”

⚠ “If we deny him, he also will deny us; if we are unfaithful, he remains faithful; he cannot deny himself.” The third line is Jesus’ saying at Luke 12:9, “the one who denies me before men will be denied before the angels of God.” The fourth breaks the pattern: “unfaithful … faithful” is the pair of Romans 3:3, “will their unfaithfulness abolish the faithfulness of God?” Readers take it two ways: as comfort, God’s faithfulness outlasting ours; or as warning, God faithful to his own word, including the denial of the line before. This page sets out both and does not choose. The Nestle-Aland text adds “for” before the last clause, and so the ASV, “for he cannot deny himself”; the KJV, without it, reads “If we believe not, yet he abideth faithful: he cannot deny himself.”

Verses 14–18 · “Rightly handling the word of truth” — talk that spreads like gangrene

“Solemnly charging them before the Lord not to quarrel about words.” The Greek printed on this page reads “before the Lord”; the Nestle-Aland text reads “before God,” and so the NWT 1984, “charging them before God as witness,” where the KJV reads “charging them before the Lord.” “Solemnly charging” is diamartyromai, as at 1 Timothy 5:21 (dictionary). “Quarrel about words” is the verb of the noun at 1 Timothy 6:4, “quarrels about words” (dictionary). “Ruin” is katastrophē, “overturning,” the word behind “catastrophe,” found in two verses: here and 2 Peter 2:6, of Sodom and Gomorrah.

⚠ “A worker with nothing to be ashamed of, rightly handling the word of truth.” Orthotomeō stands in one verse of the New Testament; it is “to cut straight,” and the Greek Old Testament uses it of cutting straight paths at Proverbs 3:6 and 11:5 (yet to come in this translation; dictionary). Readers picture a road cut straight, a furrow ploughed straight, or a stone or hide cut true; each means the word handled without swerving. The KJV reads “rightly dividing the word of truth,” and in 1888 C. I. Scofield took that wording for the title of his pamphlet Rightly Dividing the Word of Truth, which divides Scripture into dispensations; the Greek pictures a straight cut, not a division into parts. The ASV reads “handling aright the word of truth”; the NIV reads “who correctly handles the word of truth.” Anepaischyntos, “with nothing to be ashamed of,” stands in one verse too (dictionary); “worker” is the ergatēs of 1 Timothy 5:18.

“Stand clear of profane empty chatter, for they will make further progress in ungodliness.” “Empty chatter” is the kenophōnia of 1 Timothy 6:20; “make progress” is the verb of Timothy’s “progress” at 1 Timothy 4:15, turned to irony (dictionary). Periistaso, “stand clear,” stands in two verses: here and Titus 3:9. The KJV reads “But shun profane and vain babblings: for they will increase unto more ungodliness”; the NWT 1984 reads “for they will advance to more and more ungodliness.”

“Their word will spread like gangrene. Among them are Hymenaeus and Philetus.” Gangraina, “gangrene,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); “will spread” is “will have nomē,” the word for pasture, “and find pasture,” at John 10:9: the talk grazes outward. Hymenaios stands in two verses: here and 1 Timothy 1:20, “Hymenaeus and Alexander, whom I handed over to Satan”; Philetus is named only here. The KJV reads “their word will eat as doth a canker”; the ASV reads “their word will eat as doth a gangrene.”

⚠ “Saying that the resurrection has already happened.” “Missed the mark” is the verb of 1 Timothy 1:6 and 1 Timothy 6:21. The teaching is not explained. Readers take it as a resurrection understood only spiritually, already given in baptism, reading too much into words like Colossians 2:12, “buried with him in baptism, in which you were also raised with him”; it stands beside the Corinthians who said “there is no resurrection of the dead” at 1 Corinthians 15:12. The idea lived on: the Treatise on the Resurrection, a letter of the later second century found at Nag Hammadi, teaches its reader to count himself already risen. The KJV reads “saying that the resurrection is past already; and overthrow the faith of some”; the NWT 1984 reads “saying that the resurrection has already occurred.”

Verses 19–21 · “Jehovah knows those who are his” — vessels for honor

⚠ “God’s firm foundation stands, having this seal: ‘Jehovah knows those who are his.’” The first line of the seal is Moses’ word to Korah at Numbers 16:5, “Jehovah will make known who is his”; the Hebrew there has the divine name, and the Greek of 2 Timothy, kyrios, stands for it, so this translation prints “Jehovah.” The Greek Old Testament reads “God knew those who are his”; the letter’s wording is nearer the Hebrew. A seal marks ownership, as the Spirit seals believers at 2 Corinthians 1:22 and Ephesians 1:13. The KJV reads “The Lord knoweth them that are his”; the NWT 1984 reads “Jehovah knows those who belong to him.”

⚠ “Let everyone who names the name of the Lord turn away from unrighteousness.” The second line is not a quotation of one verse. It recalls the same story, Moses telling the people to “turn aside … from the tents of these wicked men” at Numbers 16:26, and its phrase “names the name of the Lord” stands in the Greek of Leviticus 24:16. Because the line may mean God or Christ, this translation keeps “the Lord”; the Byzantine text reads “Christ,” and so the KJV, “Let every one that nameth the name of Christ depart from iniquity”; the NWT 1984 reads “Let everyone naming the name of Jehovah renounce unrighteousness”; the ASV reads “the name of the Lord.”

“Vessels of gold and silver, but also of wood and clay, some for honor and some for dishonor.” The honor and dishonor are those of the potter’s vessels at Romans 9:21, “one vessel for honor and another for dishonor.” Ostrakinos, “of clay,” stands in two verses: “this treasure in clay jars” at 2 Corinthians 4:7, and here (dictionary); “of wood” is the idols “of wood” at Revelation 9:20. The KJV reads “also of wood and of earth; and some to honour, and some to dishonour”; the NIV reads “some are for special purposes and some for common use.”

⚠ “If anyone cleanses himself from these, he will be a vessel for honor, sanctified, useful to the master.” “From these” is read of the vessels for dishonor, the teachers of verses 16 to 18, or of the things they teach. Ekkathairō, “to clean out,” stands in two verses: “Clear out the old leaven” at 1 Corinthians 5:7, and this one (dictionary). Euchrēstos, “useful,” stands in three verses: Onesimus, “now useful both to you and to me,” at Philemon 1:11, Mark at 2 Timothy 4:11, and this one (dictionary). “The master” is despotēs, the name for God at Luke 2:29; Paul himself was “a chosen vessel” at Acts 9:15. The KJV reads “meet for the master’s use”; the NIV reads “useful to the Master.”

Verses 22–26 · “The Lord’s slave must not quarrel” — “caught alive”

“Flee youthful desires, and pursue righteousness, faith, love, peace.” Neōterikos, “youthful,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). ⚠ Readers take the desires as the passions of the body, or, from the setting, the young man’s love of argument and novelty. “Flee … pursue” is the pair of 1 Timothy 6:11; “those who call on the Lord” are those of 1 Corinthians 1:2, “all those who in every place call on the name of our Lord”; “out of a pure heart” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 1:5. The KJV reads “Flee also youthful lusts”; the NWT 1984 reads “flee from the desires incidental to youth.”

“Refuse foolish and ignorant controversies … the Lord’s slave must not quarrel, but be gentle toward all, able to teach, patient under wrong.” “Refuse” is the verb of 1 Timothy 4:7; “controversies” are those of 1 Timothy 6:4. Apaideutos, “ignorant, untaught,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. “Gentle” is ēpios, the nurse’s gentleness of 1 Thessalonians 2:7 (dictionary); “able to teach” is the overseer’s didaktikos at 1 Timothy 3:2 (dictionary). Anexikakos, “bearing evil without resentment,” stands in one verse too (dictionary). The KJV reads “the servant of the Lord must not strive; but be gentle unto all men, apt to teach, patient”; the NWT 1984 reads “a slave of the Lord does not need to fight.”

“Instructing his opponents with gentleness; perhaps God will grant them repentance leading to the knowledge of the truth.” “Instructing” is paideuō, the verb of Hymenaeus and Alexander handed over “so that they may be taught” at 1 Timothy 1:20. Antidiatithēmi, “to set oneself against,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. Repentance is God’s gift, as at Acts 11:18, “to the nations also God gave the repentance that leads to life”; “the knowledge of the truth” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 2:4. The KJV reads “if God peradventure will give them repentance to the acknowledging of the truth.”

⚠ “They will come back to their senses out of the snare of the devil, having been caught alive by him for that one’s will.” Ananēphō, “to sober up, to come to one’s senses,” stands in one verse of the New Testament; it is built on the “be sober” of 1 Thessalonians 5:6 (dictionary). “The snare of the devil” is that of 1 Timothy 3:7. Zōgreō, “to catch alive,” stands in two verses: Jesus to Simon, “from now on you will be catching people alive,” at Luke 5:10, and this one (dictionary). The Greek has two pronouns, “by him” and “that one’s,” and readers take them three ways: caught by the devil to do the devil’s will; caught by the devil, and now brought back to do God’s will; or caught alive by the Lord’s slave for God’s will, as Simon was to catch people alive. This page keeps both pronouns and does not choose. The KJV reads “who are taken captive by him at his will”; the NIV reads “who has taken them captive to do his will”; the NWT 1984 reads “caught alive by him for the will of that one.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “be empowered,” as at 1 Timothy 1:12; “entrust,” the verb of the deposit; “suffer along with me,” as at 1:8; “lawfully,” as at 1 Timothy 1:8; “evildoer,” as at Luke 23:32; “chains” and “chained” for one root; “the chosen”; “unfaithful” and “faithful,” as at Romans 3:3; “before the Lord,” with “God” in the note; “progress,” the verb of 1 Timothy 4:15; “missed the mark,” as at 1 Timothy 1:6; “Jehovah” in the quotation of Numbers 16:5 and “the Lord” in the line after it; “the master,” the despotēs of Luke 2:29; “slave”; and “caught alive,” as at Luke 5:10.

Echoes paid. “My child” and the deposit of 2 Timothy 1:2, 2 Timothy 1:12 and 2 Timothy 1:14; suffer along with me from 2 Timothy 1:8; the soldier and the farmer of 1 Corinthians 9:7; lawfully from 1 Timothy 1:8; the faithful saying of 1 Timothy 4:9; quarrels about words and controversies from 1 Timothy 6:4; empty chatter and missed the mark from 1 Timothy 6:20 and 1 Timothy 6:21; Hymenaeus from 1 Timothy 1:20; Korah’s story at Numbers 16:5; and the snare of the devil from 1 Timothy 3:7.

Echoes planted. The last days and the lovers of money at 3:1–2; Jannes and Jambres at 3:8; and the Scripture breathed out by God, and the man of God, at 3:16–17.

Next in this book: 2 Timothy 3, already on these pages. Hard times in the last days, a long list of vices, Jannes and Jambres, persecution, and the Scripture that is God-breathed.
