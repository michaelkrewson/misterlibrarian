Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–6 · “Wives, be subject” — won “without a word,” the hidden person of the heart, and Sarah who called Abraham “lord”

“Likewise, wives, be subject to your own husbands, so that even if some disobey the word, they may be won without a word through the conduct of their wives, as they watch your pure conduct in fear.” “Likewise” carries on the instructions of 1 Peter 2:13 and 2:18; the same instruction to wives stands at Colossians 3:18 and Titus 2:5. ⚠ These verses, with Ephesians 5:22 and Colossians 3:18, have been used to tell wives to stay with and obey husbands who abused them; the note at Ephesians 5:21 sets out that use and the readings of the household instructions, and does not choose between the readings. Peter speaks to the wives directly, and the purpose he gives is that unbelieving husbands may be won by their wives’ conduct. “Disobey the word” is the phrase of 1 Peter 2:8, and “without a word” plays on it: the husbands who will not listen to the word may be won with no words at all. Paul asks the same question from the other side at 1 Corinthians 7:16, “how do you know, wife, whether you will save your husband?” “Watch” is the verb of 1 Peter 2:12, and “conduct” is anastrophē (dictionary). The KJV reads “by the conversation of the wives”; the NWT 1984 reads “they may be won without a word through the conduct of [their] wives”; the NIV reads “they may be won over without words.”

“Let your adornment not be the outward kind — braiding of hair and putting on of gold, or wearing of clothes — but the hidden person of the heart, in the imperishable quality of a meek and quiet spirit, which is of great worth in God’s sight.” “Adornment” is kosmos, the word that also means “world” (dictionary). Emplokē, “braiding,” stands in one verse (dictionary), and so do perithesis, “putting on,” and endysis, “wearing.” Paul’s list is the same at 1 Timothy 2:9, “not with braided hair and gold or pearls or costly garments.” “The hidden person” is the inward self of Romans 2:29 and 2 Corinthians 4:16; “imperishable” is the inheritance of 1 Peter 1:4. The adjective praus, “meek,” stands in four verses, among them “Happy the meek” at Matthew 5:5 and “I am meek and humble in heart” at Matthew 11:29 (dictionary). Hēsychios, “quiet,” stands in two verses, the other “a calm and quiet life” at 1 Timothy 2:2 (dictionary). Polyteles, “of great worth,” stands in three verses, the others the costly ointment of Mark 14:3 and the “costly garments” of 1 Timothy 2:9 (dictionary). The KJV reads “the hidden man of the heart”; the NWT 1984 reads “the secret person of the heart”; the NIV reads “your inner self, the unfading beauty of a gentle and quiet spirit.”

“For this is how the holy women of old who hoped in God used to adorn themselves, being subject to their own husbands, as Sarah obeyed Abraham, calling him lord. You have become her children if you do good and do not fear any terror.” Sarah is named in four verses of the New Testament, among them Romans 4:19 and Hebrews 11:11 (encyclopedia). “Calling him lord” is the word of the Greek of Genesis 18:12, “my lord” (checked), printed “and my lord so old?” at Genesis 18:12, where Sarah says it to herself as she laughs. Westcott–Hort reads “used to obey” (the apparatus). “Children” is tekna; some versions print “daughters.” Ptoēsis, “terror,” stands in one verse, and echoes the Greek of Proverbs 3:25, “you shall not fear terror” (yet to come in this translation, checked) (dictionary). The KJV reads “whose daughters ye are”; the ASV reads “and are not put in fear by any terror”; the NWT 1984 reads “not fearing any cause for terror”; the NIV reads “do not give way to fear.”

Verse 7 · “A weaker vessel” — husbands, understanding, honor, and “fellow heirs of the grace of life”

⚠ “Husbands, likewise, live with them with understanding, as with a weaker vessel, the female one, showing them honor as fellow heirs of the grace of life, so that your prayers may not be hindered.” Synoikeō, “live with,” stands in one verse (dictionary). “Vessel” is skeuos, as in the debated “his own vessel” of 1 Thessalonians 4:4 (dictionary). The comparative asthenesteros, “weaker,” stands in two verses, the other “the members of the body that seem to be weaker are necessary” at 1 Corinthians 12:22. Gynaikeios, “female,” stands in one verse (dictionary). Readers take “weaker” of bodily strength, of a wife’s vulnerability in that society, or of both; readings differ. The sentence sets the word between “showing them honor” and “fellow heirs”: synklēronomos, “fellow heir,” stands in four verses, among them “heirs together with Christ” at Romans 8:17 and the nations as “co-heirs” at Ephesians 3:6 (dictionary); Robinson–Pierpont makes it agree with the husbands, “as being fellow heirs” (the apparatus). The verb enkoptō, “hinder,” stands in five verses, among them “I was often hindered from coming to you” at Romans 15:22 (dictionary). The KJV reads “giving honour unto the wife, as unto the weaker vessel”; the NWT 1984 reads “as to a weaker vessel, the feminine one”; the NIV reads “as the weaker partner.”

Verses 8–12 · “Not repaying evil for evil” — five words for a congregation, and Psalm 34

“Finally, all of you, be of one mind, sympathetic, loving the brothers, tenderhearted, humble in mind, not repaying evil for evil or reviling for reviling, but on the contrary blessing, because to this you were called, so that you may inherit a blessing.” Five adjectives, three of them found nowhere else in the New Testament: homophrōn, “of one mind,” stands in one verse (dictionary); philadelphos, “loving the brothers,” in one verse, the adjective of the “sincere brotherly love” of 1 Peter 1:22; and tapeinophrōn, “humble in mind,” in one verse, where Robinson–Pierpont reads “friendly” (the apparatus) (dictionary). Sympathēs, “sympathetic,” with its verb stands in two verses, the other the high priest “able to sympathize with our weaknesses” at Hebrews 4:15 (dictionary); eusplanchnos, “tenderhearted,” stands in two verses, the other Ephesians 4:32 (dictionary). “Not repaying evil for evil” is Paul’s at Romans 12:17 and 1 Thessalonians 5:15; loidoria, “reviling,” stands in two verses, the other 1 Timothy 5:14 (dictionary), and the example is Christ’s at 1 Peter 2:23, “When he was reviled, he did not revile in return.” “Blessing” answers “bless those who curse you” at Luke 6:28. The KJV reads “be pitiful, be courteous”; the ASV reads “tenderhearted, humbleminded”; the NWT 1984 reads “not paying back injury for injury”; the NIV reads “Do not repay evil with evil or insult with insult.”

“For ‘whoever wants to love life and see good days, let him keep his tongue from evil and his lips from speaking deceit; let him turn away from evil and do good; let him seek peace and pursue it. For the eyes of Jehovah are on the righteous, and his ears are open to their supplication; but the face of Jehovah is against those who do evil.’” The lines are the Greek of Psalm 34:12–16 (33:13–17 in the Greek numbering, checked). Peter turns the psalm’s question, “Who is the man who wants life…?”, into “whoever wants,” turns its commands to “you” into “let him,” and stops before its last line, “to cut off the remembrance of them from the earth” (checked). The Hebrew has the name Jehovah where the Greek has kyrios (checked); at 1 Peter 2:3 the next verse made the Lord of the psalm Christ, but here the eyes and the face are God’s, as in the psalm, so this translation prints “Jehovah.” “Deceit” is the dolos of 1 Peter 2:1 and 2:22, and the tongue held back recalls James 1:26. Robinson–Pierpont adds “his” to “tongue” and “lips” (the apparatus). The KJV reads “let him refrain his tongue from evil”; the ASV reads “And let him turn away from evil, and do good”; the NWT 1984 reads “[the] eyes of Jehovah are upon the righteous ones”; the NIV reads “the face of the Lord is against those who do evil.”

Verses 13–17 · “Sanctify Christ as Lord” — Isaiah 8:12–13, and “ready to make a defense” for the hope

“And who is the one who will harm you if you become zealous for what is good? But even if you should suffer for righteousness’ sake, you are happy. ‘Do not fear what they fear, and do not be troubled.’” Zēlōtēs, “zealous one,” stands in eight verses, two of them the name of Simon the Zealot, and among the rest “zealous for good works” at Titus 2:14 (dictionary); Robinson–Pierpont reads “imitators” (the apparatus). “Suffer for righteousness’ sake, you are happy” is the beatitude of Matthew 5:10, “Happy those who have been persecuted for righteousness’ sake,” with “happy” as there. The quotation is the Greek of Isaiah 8:12, “do not fear its fear, nor be troubled”; the Hebrew has “neither fear their fear” (yet to come in this translation; both checked). The KJV reads “happy are ye”; the ASV reads “blessed are ye: and fear not their fear”; the NWT 1984 reads “the object of their fear do not YOU fear”; the NIV reads “Do not fear their threats.”

⚠ “But in your hearts sanctify Christ as Lord, always ready to make a defense to everyone who asks you for a reason for the hope that is in you.” Isaiah goes on, in the Hebrew, “Jehovah of armies, him you shall sanctify,” and in the Greek, “sanctify the Lord himself” (both checked); Peter writes “sanctify Christ as Lord,” putting Christ where Isaiah names Jehovah of armies. Robinson–Pierpont reads “the Lord God” (the apparatus), the text behind the KJV. Because the referent here is Christ, this translation keeps “Lord”; the words can also be read “sanctify the Lord, the Christ,” and readings differ. Apologia, “defense,” stands in eight verses, among them “listen now to my defense” at Acts 22:1 and “the defense of the gospel” at Philippians 1:16 (dictionary). “The hope” is the “living hope” of 1 Peter 1:3. The KJV reads “But sanctify the Lord God in your hearts”; the ASV reads “but sanctify in your hearts Christ as Lord”; the Douay reads “But sanctify the Lord Christ in your hearts”; the NIV reads “in your hearts revere Christ as Lord.”

“Yet with meekness and fear, keeping a good conscience, so that, in the very thing in which you are spoken against, those who mistreat your good conduct in Christ may be put to shame. For it is better, if the will of God should will it, to suffer for doing good than for doing evil.” “Meekness” is prautēs, as at James 3:13 (dictionary). “Spoken against” is the verb of 1 Peter 2:12, where Robinson–Pierpont here also adds “as evildoers” (the apparatus). The verb epēreazō, “mistreat,” stands in two verses, the other “pray for those who mistreat you” at Luke 6:28 (dictionary). “If the will of God should will it” keeps the Greek doubling of verb and noun. Suffering for doing good was the word at 1 Peter 2:20. The KJV reads “that falsely accuse your good conversation in Christ”; the ASV reads “who revile your good manner of life in Christ”; the NWT 1984 reads “if the will of God wishes it”; the NIV reads “to suffer for doing good than for doing evil.”

Verse 18 · “Christ also suffered once for sins” — the righteous for the unrighteous, “made alive in the spirit”

“For Christ also suffered once for sins, the righteous for the unrighteous, so that he might bring you to God, put to death in the flesh but made alive in the spirit.” “Christ also suffered” repeats 1 Peter 2:21. Hapax, “once,” stands in fourteen verses, among them “having been offered once to bear the sins of many” at Hebrews 9:28 (dictionary); its longer form is the “once for all” of Romans 6:10 (dictionary). Westcott–Hort and Tregelles read “died” for “suffered,” and Tregelles “us” for “you” (the apparatus). “The righteous for the unrighteous” is the one who “committed no sin” of 1 Peter 2:22. ⚠ “In the flesh … in the spirit” is read as two spheres of Christ’s existence, his bodily death and his new life, or as “by the Spirit,” the Holy Spirit who raised him, as at Romans 8:11; the Greek has no article and no capital, and readings differ. The KJV reads “but quickened by the Spirit”; the ASV reads “but made alive in the spirit”; the NWT 1984 reads “Christ died once for all time concerning sins”; the NIV reads “but made alive in the Spirit.”

Verses 19–20 · ⚠ “The spirits in prison” — three readings, the days of Noah, and “eight souls”

⚠ “In which also he went and made proclamation to the spirits in prison, who had once been disobedient, when the patience of God waited in the days of Noah while the ark was being built.” One of the most debated sentences in the New Testament, and this translation does not decide it. “Made proclamation” is kēryssō, to announce as a herald (dictionary), not the “proclaim good news” of 4:6. Three readings are held. First, between his death and resurrection Christ went to the dead and proclaimed to them, as “he also went down into the lower parts of the earth” at Ephesians 4:9 and “you will not abandon my soul to Hades” at Acts 2:27 are read, with 4:6, “the good news was proclaimed also to the dead”; this reading stands behind the creed’s “he descended into hell” (reported, not checked here). Second, Christ, in the spirit, proclaimed through Noah to the people of Noah’s day, who are now spirits in prison; 2 Peter 2:5 calls Noah “a herald of righteousness”, and the reading is associated with Augustine (reported, not checked here). Third, the risen Christ, on his way to heaven, proclaimed his victory to angels imprisoned since Noah’s time, the “sons of God” of Genesis 6:2 as some Jewish writings such as 1 Enoch read them (reported, not checked here), compare “the angels who did not keep their own domain” at Jude 6; on this reading “he went” is the same participle as “having gone into heaven” in verse 22. Readings differ. The verb apekdechomai, “wait,” stands in eight verses, among them “those who eagerly await him” at Hebrews 9:28 (dictionary); “patience” is makrothymia (dictionary). The KJV reads “By which also he went and preached unto the spirits in prison”; the NWT 1984 reads “In this [state] also he went his way and preached to the spirits in prison”; the NIV reads “After being made alive, he went and made proclamation to the imprisoned spirits.”

“… in which a few, that is, eight souls, were brought safely through water.” Noē, Noah, is named in eight verses, among them “the days of Noah” at Matthew 24:37 and “built an ark to save his household” at Hebrews 11:7 (encyclopedia); “ark” is kibōtos, the word also used of the ark of the covenant at Hebrews 9:4 (dictionary). The eight are Noah, his wife, his three sons and their three wives at Genesis 7:13, and 2 Peter 2:5 calls Noah “the eighth.” The verb diasōzō, “bring safely through,” stands in eight verses, among them the shipwrecked “all were brought safely through to the land” at Acts 27:44 (dictionary). “Through water” is read as through the water they passed or by means of the water that bore the ark up; readings differ. The KJV reads “eight souls were saved by water”; the NWT 1984 reads “were carried safely through the water”; the NIV reads “eight in all, were saved through water.”

Verses 21–22 · Baptism, “the appeal of a good conscience to God” — the counterpart, and Christ at the right hand

⚠ “Which, as its counterpart, now saves you too: baptism — not the putting off of the filth of the flesh but the appeal of a good conscience to God — through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.” “Which” points back to the water. Antitypos, “counterpart,” stands in two verses, the other “a counterpart of the true one” at Hebrews 9:24 (dictionary). “Baptism” is baptisma, as “one baptism” at Ephesians 4:5 and “buried with him through baptism” at Romans 6:4 (dictionary). Apothesis, “putting off,” stands in two verses, the other “the putting off of my tent” at 2 Peter 1:14; rhypos, “filth,” stands in one verse, kin to the “filth” of James 1:21 (dictionary). Eperōtēma stands in one verse, and its sense is disputed: an appeal or request to God for a good conscience, a pledge made to God from a good conscience, or the answer given in the baptismal questioning (reported, not checked here); readings differ (dictionary). “Through the resurrection of Jesus Christ” is the phrase of 1 Peter 1:3. The KJV reads “but the answer of a good conscience toward God”; the ASV reads “the interrogation of a good conscience toward God”; the NWT 1984 reads “the request made to God for a good conscience”; the NIV reads “the pledge of a clear conscience toward God.”

“Who is at the right hand of God, having gone into heaven, with angels and authorities and powers made subject to him.” “At the right hand of God” is Psalm 110:1 as Paul uses it at Romans 8:34, and the powers made subject are “every rule and authority and power” of Ephesians 1:21. “Having gone” is the participle of “he went” in verse 19. NA28 and Robinson–Pierpont add the article before “right hand” (the apparatus). The Douay has a clause not in the Greek, from the Latin: the Douay reads “swallowing down death, that we might be made heirs of life everlasting”; the KJV reads “Who is gone into heaven, and is on the right hand of God”; the NWT 1984 reads “angels and authorities and powers were made subject to him.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “likewise,” as at 2:13 and 2:18; “without a word,” against “the word”; “conduct” and “watch,” as at 2:12; “adornment”; “the hidden person of the heart”; “meek,” as at Matthew 11:29; “lord,” as Sarah says it at Genesis 18:12; “a weaker vessel”; “fellow heirs”; “reviling,” as at 1 Timothy 5:14; “Jehovah” in Psalm 34 and “Lord” for Christ in Isaiah 8; “happy,” as at Matthew 5:10; “defense”; “mistreat,” as at Luke 6:28; “once,” as at Hebrews 9:28; “made proclamation”; “brought safely through,” as at Acts 27:44; “counterpart,” as at Hebrews 9:24; and “appeal,” with the other readings in the note.

Echoes paid. The household instructions begun at 1 Peter 2:13; “as they watch” from 1 Peter 2:12; the imperishable of 1 Peter 1:4; reviling from 1 Peter 2:23; the deceit of 1 Peter 2:1; suffering for doing good from 1 Peter 2:20; “Christ also suffered” from 1 Peter 2:21; the living hope and the resurrection of 1 Peter 1:3; and the Lord of 1 Peter 2:3, now set beside the Lord of Psalm 34.

Echoes planted. “Since Christ suffered in the flesh, arm yourselves with the same way of thinking” at 4:1; the good news “proclaimed also to the dead” at 4:6; “love covers a multitude of sins” at 4:8; and “the burning among you” and sharing in the sufferings of Christ at 4:12–13.

Next in this book: 1 Peter 4, already on these pages. “Arm yourselves with the same way of thinking”; the good news proclaimed to the dead; “the end of all things has drawn near”; “love covers a multitude of sins”; and the burning among you, “if you are insulted for the name of Christ, you are happy.”
