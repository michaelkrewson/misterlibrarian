🕰
Six days before the final Passover, Jesus returns to Bethany for a dinner at which Mary anoints his feet; the next day he enters Jerusalem on a donkey to a crowd waving palm branches, fulfilling Zechariah's oracle of a humble king. Some Greeks ask to see him, and Jesus announces 'the hour has come' for the Son of Man to be glorified, comparing his coming death to a grain of wheat that must fall into the ground to bear fruit. A voice from heaven answers his prayer; he tells the crowd 'the ruler of this world will be cast out' and that he will be 'lifted up' to draw all people to himself. The chapter closes on the chief priests' continuing unbelief, read against Isaiah's own prophecy of blinded eyes, and Jesus' own last public summary of his mission.
c. AD 30, six days before the final Passover (v1), with the entry into Jerusalem the next day (v12) — the same Passion-week entry told in Matthew 21, Mark 11 and Luke 19, though this chapter places the Bethany dinner before the entry rather than after it, unlike Mark's and Matthew's own placement of their version of the same anointing.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–8 · ⚠ a dinner this Gospel already assumed the reader knew, a textual fork over a single verb, and the half-sentence everyone misquotes

‘Six days before the Passover’ places this dinner BEFORE the triumphal entry (vv12–19), which Mark and Matthew’s own parallel anointing does not: both place their version of this scene after the entry, in the final two days before the Passover, at the house of ‘Simon the leper’ (Mark 14:3–9; Matthew 26:6–13, both already on these pages). This Gospel never mentions Simon at all for this scene, places it in an unnamed house at Bethany six days out rather than two, and names the woman, Mary, where Mark and Matthew leave her anonymous. A different anointing again, at a Pharisee’s table in Galilee, by an unnamed ‘sinner,’ stands earlier in the ministry in Luke’s own account (Luke 7:36–50, already on these pages) — whether that is a third event or the same one remembered a third way is a question this library has already flagged as unsettled (see the entry on Simon the leper). What is not in question is that THIS chapter's own narrator had already assumed the reader knew this scene two chapters earlier, introducing Mary at Lazarus's sickbed as ‘the one who anointed the Lord with perfume and wiped his feet with her hair’ (John 11:2, already on these pages) — a flat future-tense fact, stated as settled history, before this chapter has told it even once.

⚠ Verse 7 carries a real textual fork, and the two readings change what Jesus is actually telling Judas to let her do. The critical Greek text this translation follows reads a subjunctive, hina…tērēsē, ‘so that she MAY KEEP it’ — permission to keep what remains of the perfume for later, matching NWT’s ‘that she may keep this observance’ and, in its own different wording, ASV’s ‘suffer her to keep it against the day of my burying.’ A different, later Greek tradition instead has a perfect indicative, ‘she HAS KEPT this’ — a claim about what she already did, not a permission about what happens next — which is the reading KJV follows: ‘against the day of my burying hath she kept this.’ One reading describes an instruction going forward; the other, a decision already made in the past. This translation follows the earlier reading and states the fork rather than silently picking a side.

‘You always have the poor with you’ (v8) is the FIRST half of a two-part Deuteronomy sentence, and the half that gets quoted alone has a long history of being read backward. The whole verse Jesus is drawing on runs: ‘the needy will not cease from within the land — therefore I am commanding you — you shall surely open your hand’ (Deuteronomy 15:11, already on these pages) — the permanence of poverty stated as the PREMISE of a command to give, not an excuse to stop. Mark's own parallel keeps a clause the other Gospels drop, ‘and whenever you want, you can do good to them’ (Mark 14:7, already on these pages), which is Deuteronomy's own ‘therefore’ restated in Jesus's mouth; this chapter states the bare half-sentence and lets the fuller sense stand on the Deuteronomy note it now links back to.

Verses 9–11 · a death sentence that spreads from one man to the witness who proves him

The chief priests' plan to kill Lazarus (v10) is, on its own cold logic, the most practical response available to them: a dead man walking around Bethany is a standing, un-arguable advertisement for exactly the claim they most need to suppress. It is also the furthest this Gospel's hostility has gone so far — not silencing a preacher, but trying to erase the evidence a resurrection already left in the world. The irony the text does not spell out is that Lazarus's danger is itself the clearest proof this sign ever worked: ‘many of the Jewish people were going away and believing in Jesus’ because of him (v11), the very outcome a second killing is meant to reverse.

Verses 12–16 · ⚠ palm branches only one Gospel names, a royal title only one Gospel adds to the psalm, and a disciples' memory this book keeps returning to

⚠ The palm branches of v13 belong to John's account alone; none of the three Synoptic Gospels mentions them. Matthew and Mark both have the crowd spread ‘branches’ of some kind without naming the tree; Luke's own version of this scene has no branches at all, only cloaks spread on the road (Luke 19:36, already on these pages). This Gospel is also the only one of the four to specify PALM branches by name — the detail that gave the day its familiar modern title, ‘Palm Sunday,’ a name none of the four Gospel writers ever uses.

⚠ ‘Even the King of Israel’ (v13) is this Gospel's own addition to the crowd's quotation of Psalm 118:26 (not yet on these pages), and no other Gospel's version of the shout inserts a royal title directly into the psalm quotation itself. Matthew has the crowd add ‘to the Son of David’ as a separate acclamation alongside the psalm quote, not inside it (Matthew 21:9, already on these pages); Mark's crowd blesses ‘the coming kingdom of our father David’ rather than naming Jesus a king at all (Mark 11:10, already on these pages); Luke's crowd drops ‘Hosanna’ entirely and never mentions a king in its own shout, saving ‘king’ for a separate line two verses later (Luke 19:38, already on these pages). Four Gospels, four different ways of handling the one moment the crowd calls Jesus king out loud, and only this one lets the title sit inside the psalm's own words.

The quotation at v15 is a loose paraphrase, not a verbatim quotation of any single verse, and this translation states that rather than pretending it resolves to one source. ‘Do not be afraid, daughter of Zion’ echoes the ‘fear not’ oracles addressed to Zion elsewhere in the prophets (compare Zephaniah 3:14–16, not yet on these pages); ‘your king is coming... sitting on a donkey's colt’ draws on Zechariah 9:9 (not yet on these pages) without reproducing its wording exactly — this Gospel compresses and recombines rather than citing a single verse the way its own double Isaiah quotation later in this chapter does (vv38, 40).

‘His disciples did not understand these things at first’ (v16) is a confession this Gospel has made about itself once already, in almost the same words, after an earlier action of Jesus's they also failed to grasp until afterward. Compare John 2:17, 22 (already on these pages): the disciples ‘remembered’ the zeal-for-your-house psalm only once Jesus was ‘raised from the dead,’ and here they ‘remembered’ the royal entry's own fulfillment only once he ‘was glorified’ — the identical device, understanding arriving only in hindsight, used of two different events many chapters apart.

Verses 17–19 · two crowds and a complaint that concedes everything it is afraid of

This chapter is careful to separate two different crowds by what each one is responding to: one ‘kept bearing witness’ because it had SEEN Lazarus called out of the tomb (v17), the other ‘went out to meet him’ because it had only HEARD a report of the sign (v18). Eyewitness and rumor are kept distinct in the Greek's own tenses — one crowd testifying from firsthand memory, the second drawn by secondhand news of exactly what the first one is now telling everyone.

The Pharisees' own complaint (v19) — ‘the whole world has gone after him!’ — concedes, in the same breath as despair, the very scale of the problem their side keeps insisting is manageable. ‘World’ here is the ordinary human sense of kosmos, not a theological claim about the universe, but the hyperbole is the point: a faction that still thinks it can control this outcome would not phrase its own alarm this way.

Verses 20–26 · the Gentiles this Gospel has been promising since chapter 4, an hour named at last, and a seed that has to die to do the one thing it is for

⚠ ‘We want to see Jesus’ (v21) is the single most ordinary request in this entire Gospel, and it is also the request that finally triggers the announcement ‘the hour has come’ (v23) — a sentence this Gospel has delayed and delayed since its very first sign (‘my hour has not yet come,’ John 2:4, already on these pages). Nothing marks these Greeks out as remarkable: no name, no further word from them, no recorded meeting with Jesus at all. What triggers the hour is not a miracle or a confrontation but Gentiles simply wanting in — the exact widening this Gospel has been pointing toward since the Samaritan woman's own townspeople believed ‘this man really is the Savior of the world’ (John 4:39–42, already on these pages) and since the shepherd promised ‘other sheep that are not of this fold’ (John 10:16, already on these pages).

The grain of wheat (v24) is this Gospel's own version of a claim every Synoptic Gospel makes in different words — that a life saved by clinging to it is lost, and a life given up is kept (compare Mark 8:35, Matthew 16:25, Luke 9:24, all already on these pages) — but here it arrives as agricultural image rather than paradox stated flatly. A seed kept whole, unplanted, stays exactly one seed forever; only the seed that is buried and, in the plainest sense, destroyed as a seed produces a harvest. The image answers its own moment precisely: Jesus is explaining, to Greeks who have just asked to see him, why seeing him is about to require watching him die.

‘Life’ and ‘soul’ in v25 translate the identical Greek word, psychē, used twice in one sentence for two sides of the same paradox — this translation keeps both English words rather than forcing one, the same choice this project has already made of the identical word elsewhere (see the psychē dictionary entry). ‘The one who loves his psychē loses it’ and ‘the one who hates his psychē... will keep it’ are the same noun, pulling in opposite directions, inside a single Greek sentence.

Verses 27–30 · a prayer that sounds like Gethsemane months early, and a crowd that cannot agree on what it just heard

‘Now my soul is troubled... Father, save me from this hour’ (v27) is this Gospel's own version of a prayer the Synoptic Gospels place only later, in Gethsemane, the night before the crucifixion — the identical anguish, the identical address to the Father, voiced here in public, at the temple, well before any garden scene this Gospel never separately narrates. Where the Synoptic Jesus asks that the cup pass from him and then submits (compare Mark 14:36, already on these pages), this chapter's Jesus answers his own question in the same breath: ‘it is for this very reason that I have come to this hour’ — the petition and its own refusal spoken by the identical voice, with no narrated struggle in between.

The voice from heaven (v28) answers Jesus directly but is heard by the standing crowd only as noise, and the crowd itself cannot agree even on what KIND of noise it was — thunder, or an angel speaking (v29). This is the only time in this Gospel a voice from heaven speaks during Jesus's ministry, and the text is explicit that its content was audible to Jesus and unintelligible, by the crowd's own admission, to everyone else; Jesus himself states the reason a moment later — ‘this voice has come not for my sake, but for yours’ (v30) — making the sign a public event whose significance even its own hearers could not agree on while it was happening.

Verses 31–34 · a ruler cast out, a lifting-up this Gospel has been building toward twice already, and a crowd that answers one title with a question about another

‘The ruler of this world’ (v31) is this Gospel's own name for the hostile power Jesus says is about to be ‘cast out’ — the same phrase recurs twice more, later in the Farewell Discourse, already on these pages out of this chapter's own sequence (John 14:30; John 16:11, already on these pages). All three uses describe the identical defeat from three different angles across the book, with this chapter's own use the earliest one a reader meets in the text as it now stands, even though two of the three were translated first.

⚠ ‘Lifted up’ (v32) is the THIRD and final use of this exact verb, hypsoō, in this Gospel, and this chapter is where the ambiguity the earlier two uses built finally gets stated outright. Jesus first used it of himself at John 3:14 (already on these pages), comparing his own coming death to Moses lifting up the bronze serpent in the wilderness, and again at John 8:28 (already on these pages) without yet explaining what kind of lifting he meant. Only here does the narrator step outside the dialogue and say so directly: ‘he said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die’ (v33) — the word's double sense, an exaltation achieved by way of a crucifixion, stated as the Gospel's own deliberate pun rather than left for a reader to guess.

The crowd's question — ‘who is this Son of Man?’ (v34) — answers a claim about the Christ with a question about an entirely different title, and the mismatch is the crowd's own confusion, not a scribal error. They have heard from the Law that ‘the Christ remains forever’ and cannot square that with a Son of Man who is ‘lifted up’ in the sense Jesus has just made unmistakably fatal; Jesus does not resolve which title covers which claim, and the chapter moves on without the crowd getting an answer to the question it actually asked.

Verses 35–36 · the last public words of Jesus's ministry, in an image this Gospel opened with

‘Walk while you have the light’ is Jesus's own last recorded public statement to a crowd anywhere in this Gospel — everything from chapter 13 on is spoken privately, to his own disciples alone, until the arrest. That it closes on light and darkness is not incidental: the same opposition opened this Gospel's own prologue (‘the light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it,’ John 1:5, already on these pages) and has recurred at every turn since — ‘light of this world’ (John 8:12, John 9:5, both already on these pages), ‘walking in the day’ against stumbling in the night (John 11:9–10, already on these pages). This chapter's own closing image — Jesus going away and hiding himself from a crowd that has just been told its window is closing — enacts, rather than merely states, the warning it just gave.

Verses 37–43 · two Isaiah quotations under one name, a prophet credited with seeing a glory his own book never says whose it was, and the first count of secret believers this Gospel gives a reason for

⚠ Verses 38 and 40 quote two different parts of the book of Isaiah, decades apart in the traditional dating of its own two halves, and this chapter cites both under the single name ‘Isaiah the prophet’ without distinguishing them — the identical move this project has already flagged where Isaiah's authorship question is actually discussed (see the opening note at Isaiah 40, already on these pages). Verse 38 quotes Isaiah 53:1 (already on these pages, ‘who has believed what we heard? And the arm of Jehovah — upon whom has it been revealed?’); verse 40 adapts Isaiah 6:10 (not yet on these pages), the prophet's own commissioning vision. Whatever a reader concludes about the book's composition, this Gospel's own usage treats ‘Isaiah’ as one voice across both halves.

⚠ Verse 38's opening word is a vocative address, ‘Lord,’ that the underlying Hebrew of Isaiah 53:1 does not actually have — the Greek Old Testament adds it, and this chapter's quotation inherits the addition. This translation therefore keeps ‘Lord’ at the start of the verse (matching the Greek Kyrie, an address rather than a translated name) while rendering the verse's SECOND occurrence of the word, ‘the arm of kyriou,’ as ‘Jehovah’ — because that second word does correspond to an actual instance of the Hebrew divine name in Isaiah's own Hebrew text, consistent with how this project has already rendered that exact clause at Isaiah 53:1. Two Greek words for ‘lord’ in one verse, doing two different jobs: the first an address the Septuagint supplied, the second a translation of a name that was always there.

Verse 40's quotation is a free adaptation of Isaiah 6:10, not a word-for-word rendering, and this translation states what changed rather than letting the quotation marks imply an exactness the Greek itself does not claim. The Hebrew is an imperative addressed to the prophet (‘make the heart of this people fat... shut their eyes’) and names three organs — eyes, ears, heart; this chapter's quotation recasts it as something already accomplished (‘he HAS blinded... HAS hardened’) and drops the ears entirely, keeping only eyes and heart. The sense survives the compression; the exact wording does not.

⚠⚠ ‘Isaiah said these things because he saw HIS glory’ (v41) leaves the pronoun's referent exactly as ambiguous in the Greek as the English here keeps it — and the two shelves checked split on resolving it. The vision in question is Isaiah's own throne-room commissioning (checked against the Hebrew directly, since this translation does not yet carry that chapter): the prophet reports seeing the Lord seated on a throne and says his own eyes have seen the King, Jehovah of Hosts (Isaiah 6:1, 5, not yet on these pages) — a vision of GOD's own glory, stated on its own terms with no mention of a second figure. This chapter's claim that Isaiah ‘saw HIS glory’ and ‘spoke about HIM,’ placed immediately after two verses about Jesus, reads as an identification of that enthroned glory with Christ's own pre-existent glory — a very high claim this translation states rather than softens. NIV resolves the ambiguity explicitly, ‘he saw Jesus' glory’; ASV, KJV and NWT all keep the bare, unresolved ‘his glory,’ the reading this translation follows. The Spanish shelf splits the identical way, in its own words (see the Spanish edition's own note).

The ‘many even of the rulers’ who secretly believed (v42) are the first count this Gospel gives a stated REASON for — fear of being ‘put out of the synagogue,’ aposynagōgos, the identical rare word this Gospel used of the healed blind man's own parents three chapters earlier (John 9:22, already on these pages). The threat the parents feared in private is now stated as the reason a whole class of leaders stays silent in public — the same word tracking the identical fear from one social rank to a much higher one, and due to recur once more, openly predicted by Jesus himself to his own disciples (John 16:2, already on these pages).

Verses 44–50 · Jesus's own last public sentence, restating a promise this Gospel made in its very first half

This closing speech has no stated audience and no narrated response — the text simply says ‘Jesus cried out and said,’ with no crowd described as present, no question answered, no reaction recorded afterward. Coming immediately after v36's report that Jesus had already ‘gone away and hidden himself,’ this reads less like a scene than a summary: the narrator's own closing statement of what this whole public ministry had been proclaiming, placed after the ministry itself is already over.

‘I did not come to judge the world, but to save the world’ (v47) restates, almost word for word, a claim this Gospel made in its own second chapter of public ministry: ‘God did not send the Son into the world to judge the world, but so that the world might be saved through him’ (John 3:17, already on these pages). The two statements bookend everything this chapter and the ten before it have narrated — one near the very beginning of the public ministry, this one at its very end — both making the identical claim about what Jesus's own coming was and was not for.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Kyrios in the crowd's own Psalm quotation (v13) is kept as ‘Jehovah,’ matching this project's standing practice for a New Testament quotation of an Old Testament verse that carries the Hebrew divine name, and matching the identical choice already made at the Synoptic parallels (Matthew 21:9, Mark 11:9, Luke 19:38, all already on these pages). The SAME Greek word four verses into the Isaiah quotation (v38) splits instead — ‘Lord’ for the Septuagint's own added vocative, ‘Jehovah’ for the word translating an actual instance of the Hebrew name — a deliberate distinction stated in the note rather than flattened to one English word either way. Psychē (v25) keeps both ‘life’ and ‘soul’ for the identical Greek noun within one sentence, printing the paradox rather than resolving it to a single English word.

Echoes paid. The ‘other sheep... one flock, one shepherd’ promise of John 10:16 (already on these pages) is answered by the Greeks who come asking to see Jesus (v20). ‘Lifted up,’ hypsoō, reaches its third and final use (v32) after John 3:14 and John 8:28 (both already on these pages), with the narrator finally stating outright the double sense the earlier two uses only implied. The disciples' hindsight ‘remembering’ (v16) repeats the identical device already used of the temple-cleansing's own aftermath (John 2:17, 22, already on these pages). Aposynagōgos (v42) returns from the healed blind man's parents (John 9:22, already on these pages) to a whole class of secretly-believing rulers. And ‘I did not come to judge the world but to save the world’ (v47) restates John 3:17 (already on these pages) almost word for word, at the opposite end of the public ministry.

Echoes planted. ‘The ruler of this world will be cast out’ (v31) is the first of three uses of the identical phrase, the other two already on these pages out of order (John 14:30; John 16:11, already on these pages). The ‘little while longer’ the light remains (v35) anticipates the Farewell Discourse's own repeated ‘little while’ sayings, already on these pages ahead of this chapter (John 16:16–19, already on these pages, though the phrase itself recurs at John 14 and John 15's own surrounding material). And the secret believers afraid of expulsion (v42) anticipate Jesus's own open prediction of that same threat to his disciples (John 16:2, already on these pages).

Left open on purpose. Whose glory, exactly, Isaiah saw (v41) — God's own, on the throne-vision's own terms, or Christ's pre-existent glory, the reading this chapter's placement of the claim invites but does not state outright; the Greek pronoun does not settle it, and two shelf versions resolve it where the rest do not. Which title, Christ or Son of Man, the confused crowd's own question (v34) was actually asking about — a question this chapter raises through the crowd and never has Jesus answer directly. And whether the real-world event behind the Lazarus plot (v10) and the chief priests' fear ever reached the length of an actual attempt on his life — the text states the plan and never again mentions Lazarus by name.
