Jesus crosses the Kidron to a garden, where Judas arrives with an armed party and Jesus identifies himself, 'I am he,' causing them to fall back; Peter strikes off a slave's ear, which Jesus rebukes. Jesus is bound and led first to Annas, then to Caiaphas, while Peter — following at a distance — denies knowing him three times before a rooster crows, exactly as predicted. At dawn the Jewish leaders hand Jesus to Pilate, unwilling to enter the governor's residence themselves before the Passover; Pilate questions Jesus privately ('My kingdom is not of this world... What is truth?'), finds no basis for a charge, and offers to release him under the Passover amnesty custom — but the crowd demands Barabbas instead.
c. AD 30, continuing the same night as chapters 13-17 into the following morning — the arrest, Peter's three denials, and the opening half of the trial before Pilate, ending with the choice of Barabbas.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–3 · the garden across the Kidron, finally named — Gethsemane's own prayer skipped entirely, and a Roman cohort among the arresting party

⚠ This chapter opens exactly where last chapter's own closing note said it would — the garden ‘across the Kidron,’ not yet linked when John 17 named it (already on these pages). This Gospel never narrates the agonized prayer the Synoptic accounts set in this same garden — ‘let this cup pass from me… yet not as I want, but as you’ (Matthew 26:39; Mark 14:36; Luke 22:42, all already on these pages) — moving straight from the supper's last words to the arrest itself. The one trace of that scene that survives into this Gospel is the single word ‘cup’ at v11, below.

The Kidron itself (cheimarrou, ‘winter-stream’ — a wadi that runs only in the rainy season) is the valley separating the Temple Mount from the Mount of Olives, and it already carried history before this night: David crossed it weeping, fleeing his own son (2 Samuel 15:23, not yet on these pages), and three different reforming kings dumped the idols they tore out of the temple into it (1 Kings 15:13; 2 Kings 23:4, 6, 12; 2 Chronicles 30:14, none yet on these pages). A valley already associated with a king in flight and with false worship swept out of the temple; now it is where the true king is taken instead.

⚠ v3's arresting party carries real Roman vocabulary — a speira, ‘cohort,’ and, one verse later, a chiliarchos, a Roman tribune (v12) — and none of the three Synoptic accounts of this same arrest mentions a single Roman soldier. Matthew's ‘a great crowd, with swords and clubs, from the chief priests and elders of the people’ (26:47), Mark's nearly identical wording (14:43) and Luke's bare ‘chief priests and officers of the temple and elders’ (22:52, all already on these pages) each describe a temple-authority operation. This Gospel alone names Roman rank and unit terms at the arrest itself; the texts simply differ on who made it, and this translation states the difference rather than harmonizing it away.

‘Torches and lamps and weapons’ (v3) is itself a detail none of the other three Gospels gives at all — ordinary enough for a night arrest, but arriving in a book that has spent seventeen chapters calling Jesus the light the darkness could not overcome (1:5, already on these pages): the men coming to seize the light bring their own, much smaller ones. ⚠ The shelf does not even agree on the ORDER: ASV and KJV both read ‘lanterns and torches,’ NIV reverses it to ‘torches, lanterns,’ and NWT 1984 prints ‘torches and lamps,’ the word order this translation follows.

Verses 4–9 · ⚠ ‘I am he’ said three times, soldiers falling backward, and Jesus's OWN earlier word given the identical fulfillment formula this Gospel otherwise reserves for Scripture

⚠ ‘Ego eimi,’ bare, is said three times in five verses (vv5, 6, 8) — the identical construction, no predicate supplied, this Gospel already used of Jesus's claim to stand outside time itself: ‘before Abraham was, I AM’ (8:58, already on these pages). Whether the arresting party hears a plain ‘it is I’ or something nearer that same claim, the text does not say — but v6's reaction is stronger than a confirmed name would call for. Compare the seer's own collapse before the risen Christ, one book later in this library: ‘I fell at his feet like a dead man… I am the first and the last’ (Revelation 1:17, already on these pages) — the identical pairing of a bare self-declaration and a body hitting the ground, there in reverence, here in apparent shock.

⚠ v9's ‘that the word HE spoke might be fulfilled’ quotes Jesus's OWN earlier sentence, not a prophet's — ‘those you gave me, I have not lost a single one’ answers both 6:39 (‘I should lose nothing of all that he has given me’) and, closer still, last chapter's ‘not one of them was destroyed’ (17:12, both already on these pages). The formula itself, ‘hina plērōthē ho logos,’ is otherwise reserved in this Gospel for quoting the Hebrew Scriptures (12:38's Isaiah, 13:18's Psalm 41, both already on these pages) — here, for the only time in this book, applied to Jesus's own prior word instead.

Verses 10–11 · Peter, a sword, a servant named only here in any Gospel, and a cup this Gospel never shows Jesus praying over

⚠ Simon Peter is named here as the swordsman; every Synoptic account of this same blow withholds his name — Matthew's bare ‘one of those with Jesus’ (26:51), Mark's identical anonymity (14:47), Luke's ‘a certain one of them’ (22:50, all already on these pages). Only this Gospel names both the striker and the struck — the servant is ‘Malchus,’ named nowhere else in the New Testament. John and Luke alone specify which ear, the RIGHT (22:50, already on these pages; Matthew and Mark leave the side unstated), and only Luke records that Jesus healed it (22:51, already on these pages) — this Gospel reports the wound and moves straight to the rebuke.

‘The cup the Father has given me’ (v11) is this Gospel's only reference to the image the Synoptics build an entire prayer scene around in this same garden — see v1's note, above. One line survives into this Gospel of a prayer it has already skipped entirely, spoken here not in private anguish but in public rebuke of the one disciple who tried to prevent the very thing that prayer was wrestling with.

Verses 12–14 · ⚠ Annas first — a detail no other Gospel gives — and Caiaphas's OWN prophecy from four chapters ago, quoted back almost word for word

⚠ Only this Gospel sends Jesus to ANNAS before Caiaphas at all; the Synoptic accounts go straight to Caiaphas, or simply ‘the high priest’ (Mark 14:53, already on these pages), with no mention of Annas anywhere in their own arrest-night narratives. Annas was no longer the acting high priest — Rome had deposed him years before — but as Caiaphas's father-in-law and the patriarch of a family that would go on supplying high priests for decades, an unofficial first stop made sense to anyone who knew whose household actually ran things.

⚠ v14's description of Caiaphas repeats, almost verbatim, his own prophecy from the council convened after Lazarus's resurrection: ‘it is better for you that one man should die for the people than that the whole nation perish’ (11:50, already on these pages) answered here by ‘it was better for one man to die for the people’ — the same verb (sympherei), the same preposition (hyper), the same object (heis anthrōpos), the same beneficiary (tou laou). The council's private calculation from months earlier is now simply reported as settled policy, the ‘one man’ standing in this high priest's own courtyard.

Verses 15–18 · the other disciple no one names, a first denial from the one who swore he'd die first, and a charcoal fire that returns in this Gospel's own last chapter

⚠ ‘Another disciple… known to the high priest’ (v15) is never named in this chapter, and this Gospel's own convention elsewhere — leaving its favored disciple unnamed, calling him only ‘the disciple whom Jesus loved’ (13:23, already on these pages) — is the reason tradition has long identified the two. How a Galilean fisherman came to be ‘known to the high priest’ at all is a detail this chapter raises and never explains.

Peter's first denial (v17) is the first of three, and this Gospel already told its reader exactly how many were coming and under what sign: ‘the rooster will not crow until you have denied me three times’ (13:38, already on these pages) — spoken to the same man who had just insisted, in the same conversation, ‘I will lay down my life for you.’

⚠ The ‘charcoal fire’ (anthrakia, v18) this Gospel names here is a true rarity — it recurs exactly once more in the whole New Testament, and the only other occurrence is in this same Gospel's own final chapter, where the risen Jesus has one burning on the shore before he restores this same Peter three times, once for each denial (John 21:9, not yet on these pages). The two fires bracket Peter's whole failure and its undoing.

Verses 19–23 · ‘spoken openly,’ a slap that answers no actual insolence, and a question Jesus turns back on his own questioners

‘I have spoken openly to the world’ (v20) uses the identical adverb, parrēsia, this Gospel has already used of Jesus's own guarded relationship to publicity — withheld at the Feast of Tabernacles (7:26, already on these pages) and finally granted to the disciples at the Farewell Discourse's close, ‘I will no longer speak to you in figures, but will tell you plainly’ (16:25, already on these pages). By this night the claim is retrospective and total: a public ministry in synagogue and temple, nothing taught in secret, now recited to a court convened at night, in secret, to try him for it. ASV reads ‘spoken openly,’ matching this translation; KJV keeps the same adverb but the older verb form, ‘spake openly’; NWT 1984 prints ‘spoken to the world publicly’ — a legal register (open to public scrutiny) the plainer ‘openly’ does not foreground as sharply.

⚠ The officer's slap (v22) answers a question Jesus never actually asks improperly — v21 only requests that the court call its own witnesses rather than interrogate the defendant directly, which the legal procedure of the period generally required anyway. The blow punishes the TONE the officer hears, not anything improper in the content, and Jesus's own answer (v23) does what the court itself should have done: asks for a specific charge rather than a generic grievance.

Verses 24–27 · sent on to Caiaphas with no hearing narrated at all, two more denials, and a rooster that answers a prophecy from five chapters and one long night ago

v24 finally supplies the transfer the narrative has been assuming since v13 — Annas sends Jesus on to Caiaphas, already named the ACTING high priest (v13). The verse interrupts Peter's own scene to report it, then returns to the courtyard without ever narrating whatever happened in Caiaphas's own presence — the Synoptic hearing before Caiaphas (Matthew 26:57–68, already on these pages) has no parallel anywhere in this Gospel.

⚠ The second and third denials (vv25, 27) complete, exactly, the three 13:38 promised — and the rooster crows on cue, with no comment from Peter, no weeping, no narrator's aside at all. Luke alone adds that ‘the Lord turned and looked at Peter’ in the same instant (22:61, already on these pages) and that Peter wept bitterly (22:62); this Gospel gives its reader only the sound of the bird and moves on — this same Peter's own restoration waits for the shore of chapter 21, not yet on these pages.

Verses 28–32 · ⚠ the governor's residence, a ritual-purity law that opens the oldest chronology puzzle in the Gospels, and Jesus's OWN prediction of how he would die, quoted back word for word

⚠ v28's ‘so that they would not be defiled but could eat the Passover’ sits at the center of the oldest chronological disagreement among the four Gospels: if the leaders who arrested Jesus have not yet eaten the Passover meal on this same morning, this Gospel's own timeline puts the meal of chapters 13–17 BEFORE the Passover — while the Synoptics are explicit that the Last Supper WAS that meal (‘I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you,’ Luke 22:15, already on these pages). Proposed harmonizations include two calendars running that year (a reckoning one day apart), pascha here naming the wider festival week's offerings rather than the Seder itself, or a genuine disagreement between the traditions. This translation states the difficulty rather than resolving it.

⚠ v32's ‘to signify by what kind of death he was going to die’ repeats, in the identical Greek words, this Gospel's own earlier aside at the triumphal entry — ‘he said this to show by what kind of death he was going to die’ (12:33, already on these pages) — the only two places in this Gospel the sentence occurs. The first time named it in the abstract, tied to ‘being lifted up’ (12:32–33); this time the Jewish leaders' own refusal to execute anyone themselves (v31) supplies the mechanism — Rome crucifies, the Sanhedrin could at most have stoned, and the specific death Jesus foretold requires exactly the court now standing in front of him.

Verses 33–38 · ⚠ ‘my kingdom is not of this world,’ said in a Gospel that almost never uses the word ‘kingdom’ at all, and a question Pilate never waits to hear answered

⚠ Basileia, ‘kingdom,’ is said five times in these six verses (v36 three times, 36–37) — and the ONLY other place this whole Gospel uses the noun at all is near its very beginning, twice in one breath to Nicodemus by night: ‘no one can see the kingdom of God’ (3:3, already on these pages) and ‘…enter the kingdom of God’ (3:5). Three uses in twenty-one chapters, the first pair in a private night conversation with a sympathetic Pharisee, the last in a private night conversation with the Roman governor who will condemn him — a word this Gospel otherwise avoids, each time addressed to exactly one man at a time.

⚠ The shelf's usual rendering, ASV, KJV and NIV all ‘not of this world,’ is sharpened by NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 alike to ‘no PART of this world’ — a territorial claim (not sourced from here) rather than a merely qualitative one (not of this world's kind); this translation follows the shelf majority.

⚠ ‘Everyone who belongs to the truth hears my voice’ (v37) answers, two words later, the question this scene is best remembered for. ‘What is truth?’ (v38) is one of the most quoted lines in literature, and this Gospel gives it no narrated answer — not because the book has none, but because Jesus has already given it, to the one person present not asking: he came into the world specifically ‘to testify to the truth’ (v37). Pilate's own question gets no reply at all; he simply turns and walks back outside.

v38's ‘I find no basis for a charge against him’ is the first of a verdict this trial will repeat twice more, word for word, at 19:4 and 19:6 (not yet on these pages, verified against the Greek) — a governor who states the defendant's innocence three times and crucifies him anyway.

Verses 39–40 · Barabbas, already catalogued on these pages, and a cry one chapter away from a very different shout and a title nailed above a cross

This chapter's Barabbas is already in this library's own record (Barabbas, already on these pages, from Matthew 27:16) — this Gospel adds nothing beyond the single word lēstēs, ‘bandit,’ the identical word the Synoptic accounts use of the two men crucified on either side of Jesus (27:38, already on these pages). The crowd releases one bandit; the state will execute Jesus between two more.

⚠ The crowd's shout, ‘Not this man, but Barabbas’ (v40), is the first of this trial's two competing chants — the second, ‘Crucify, crucify him’ (John 19:6, not yet on these pages, verified against the Greek), is only one chapter away. So is Pilate's own closing title for the man this crowd has just rejected in favor of a bandit, nailed above his head in three languages (19:19–20, not yet on these pages) — the very title, ‘King of the Jews,’ Pilate has been asking about since v33.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Hoi Ioudaioi splits THREE ways in this chapter, not the usual two: the hostile authorities who arrest and prosecute Jesus are ‘the Jewish leaders’ (vv12, 14, 31, 35, 36, 38), the generic public Jesus describes teaching in synagogue and temple is ‘the Jewish people’ (v20, the sense already distinguished at 1:19 and 11, both already on these pages), and the fixed royal title ‘King of the Jews’ (vv33, 39) is kept in its conventional English form rather than folded into either of the other two, since it names a political office and not an acting group. Basileia is kept as ‘kingdom’ throughout, tracking its own rarity in this Gospel (see n18-33). Aletheia is kept as ‘truth’ (see the dictionary entry). Ego eimi (vv5, 6, 8) is kept as the bare ‘I am he,’ rather than a supplied ‘I am Jesus’ or similar, since the bare construction is the whole point (see n18-4).

Echoes paid. The garden across the Kidron (v1) lands last chapter's own forward-pointing close (17:24–26). ‘Not one of them… lost’ (v9) answers both 17:12 and 6:39. The Caiaphas prophecy (v14) repeats 11:50 almost verbatim. Peter's three denials (vv17, 25, 27) fulfill 13:38's own rooster-prediction exactly. ‘Spoken openly’ (v20) answers both 7:26 and 16:25. And ‘what kind of death’ (v32) repeats 12:33 word for word (all already on these pages).

Echoes planted. ‘I find no basis for a charge’ (v38) will repeat twice more, at John 19:4 and 19:6 (not yet on these pages). The charcoal fire (v18) returns once more, at this same Peter's own restoration, in this Gospel's final chapter (21:9, not yet on these pages). ‘King of the Jews’ (v33) becomes the inscription nailed over the cross in three languages at once (19:19–20, not yet on these pages). And the ritual-purity/Passover timing crux (v28) is stated as a genuine difficulty, not a problem this translation promises to resolve later.

This chapter completes the arrest and the first half of Jesus's trial; the book's own sequence runs unbroken from chapter 1 through this chapter — next in sequence is John 19, the scourging, the crown of thorns, and the crucifixion itself.
