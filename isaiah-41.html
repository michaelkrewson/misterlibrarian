The trial of the nations and their gods: Jehovah rouses a conqueror from the east and the north, the idol-makers nail their god so it will not totter, Israel the servant, seed of Abraham the friend, is told “do not be afraid”, the worm Jacob becomes a threshing sledge, rivers run on bare heights, and the gods are found to be wind and empty chaos.
⚠ Disputed as for chapter 40: the conqueror is Cyrus, who took Babylon in 539 BC; the traditional reading hears the eighth-century Isaiah foretelling him, the critical reading a prophet of the exile announcing him as he rises.

הַחֲרִישׁוּ אֵלַי אִיִּים, וּלְאֻמִּים יַחֲלִיפוּ כֹחַ; יִגְּשׁוּ אָז יְדַבֵּרוּ, יַחְדָּו לַמִּשְׁפָּט נִקְרָבָה.

מִי הֵעִיר מִמִּזְרָח, צֶדֶק יִקְרָאֵהוּ לְרַגְלוֹ; יִתֵּן לְפָנָיו גּוֹיִם, וּמְלָכִים יַרְדְּ--יִתֵּן כֶּעָפָר חַרְבּוֹ, כְּקַשׁ נִדָּף קַשְׁתּוֹ.

Las islas lo han visto, y temen; los confines de la tierra tiemblan; se acercaron y vinieron.

No tengas miedo, gusano Jacob, hombres de Israel. Yo te ayudo, declara Jehová, tu Redentor, el Santo de Israel.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · The one from the east — the islands summoned to court, “righteousness calls him”, and “I am he”

Renew their strength. “Keep silence before me, islands, and let the peoples renew their strength”: the chapter opens as a court, with the far coastlands told to fall silent and then make their case, “let us come near together for judgment”. “Renew their strength” is the last promise of chapter 40, “those who wait for Jehovah shall renew their strength” (Isaiah 40:31, now on these pages); the phrase stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, these two, and what was promised to the weary is now offered to the nations as a challenge. The iyyim, “islands”, are the coasts and isles of the Mediterranean, the edge of the known world, as in 40:15. The Greek has “be renewed toward me, islands, for the rulers will change their strength”. The ASV has “Keep silence before me, O islands; and let the peoples renew their strength”; the NWT 1984 “let national groups themselves regain power”; the TLB “O lands beyond the sea”.

Righteousness calls him. “Who has roused one from the east? Righteousness calls him to its feet”: tsedeq yiqra’ehu le-raglo. The words allow “righteousness calls him to its feet”, a servant summoned to attend, or “victory meets him at every step”. The chapter does not name him; later in the book he is Cyrus, “my shepherd” and “his anointed” (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1), the Persian king whose decree ends the exile (Ezra 1:2, now on these pages; Cyrus). Josephus says Cyrus learned of his calling by reading the book Isaiah had left two hundred and ten years before (Antiquities 11.5, numbered 11.1.2 in Whiston). The Latin read the word as a person, and so the Douay has “Who hath raised up the just one from the east”, a line long read of Christ; the Greek keeps the abstract “righteousness”. The KJV has “the righteous man from the east”; the ASV “whom he calleth in righteousness to his foot”; the NIV “calling him in righteousness to his service”.

A path his feet have not traveled. “He makes them like dust to his sword, like driven stubble to his bow”: the stubble that the storm carried off in 40:24. “He pursues them, he passes on in peace, by a path his feet have not traveled”: the Hebrew is spare, “a path with his feet he does not come”, either new ground or a march so fast his feet scarcely touch the road. The ASV has “even by a way that he had not gone with his feet”; the Douay “no path shall appear after his feet”; the NIV “by a path his feet have not traveled before”.

The first, and with the last — I am he. “Who has worked and done it, calling the generations from the beginning? I, Jehovah, the first, and with the last — I am he”: ani hu, the words of the Song of Moses, “See now that I — I am he” (Deuteronomy 32:39, now on these pages). As God’s word about himself, ani hu stands in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible: that one in Deuteronomy and six in Isaiah, of which this is the first. The Greek renders it egō eimi, “I am”, the words Jesus uses in John, “unless you believe that I am he” and “before Abraham was, I am” (John 8:24, 58); and “the first and the last” is the title of the one who speaks in Revelation, “I am the first and the last” (Revelation 1:17, all now on these pages). The ASV has “I, Jehovah, the first, and with the last, I am he”; the NWT 1984 “I, Jehovah, the First One; and with the last ones I am the same”; the Douay “I am the first and the last”.

Verses 5–7 · The idol workshop — “Be strong!”, the craftsman and the goldsmith, and nails so it will not be moved

They saw, and were afraid. “The islands have seen and are afraid”: ra’u … ve-yira’u, “they saw … and they feared”, two verbs one letter apart. The same play returns in verse 23, where the written text has “and we will fear” and the reading is “and we will see”. “The ends of the earth” are the ones whose Creator is Jehovah (40:28). The ASV has “The isles have seen, and fear; the ends of the earth tremble”; the NWT 1984 “The islands saw and began to fear”; the KJV “The isles saw it, and feared”.

Be strong. “Each helps his fellow, and says to his brother, ‘Be strong!’ And the craftsman strengthens the goldsmith … and he strengthens it with nails, so that it will not be moved”: the frightened nations answer the summons by making a god. The verb is chazaq three times, and it runs on into Jehovah’s answer: “you whom I took hold of” (v9) and “I … take hold of your right hand” (v13) are the same verb. The nations strengthen their god with nails; Jehovah takes hold of his people. The craftsman and goldsmith are the ones of 40:19, and “so that it will not be moved” is the poor man’s idol of 40:20 (all now on these pages). The ASV has “So the carpenter encourageth the goldsmith” and “he fasteneth it with nails, that is should not be moved”; the NWT 1984 “So the craftsman went strengthening the metalworker”; the NIV “so it will not topple”.

Verses 8–13 · “Do not be afraid, for I am with you” — Israel my servant, Abraham my friend, and the right hand of righteousness

My servant, Abraham my friend. “But you, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen, the seed of Abraham my friend”: this is the first time in these chapters that Israel is called “my servant”, the title that will carry the servant passages to their height in chapter 53 (Isaiah 53, now on these pages). “My friend” is ohavi, “the one who loves me”: “Abraham, the one who loves me” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and 2 Chronicles 20:7 has “Abraham your friend”. James draws the title from these verses, “he was called a friend of God” (James 2:23, now on these pages), and the Qur’an too calls Abraham God’s friend (4:125). The Greek has “Abraham, whom I loved”. The ASV has “the seed of Abraham my friend”; the TLB “he was my friend”; the NWT 1984 “the seed of Abraham my friend”.

Chosen, not rejected. “You whom I took hold of from the ends of the earth … I have chosen you and have not rejected you”: ma’as, the verb of the king who was rejected (1 Samuel 15:23, now on these pages). The Greek of verses 8–9, “seed of Abraham” and “whom I took hold of”, is behind the line of Hebrews, “he takes hold of the seed of Abraham” (Hebrews 2:16, now on these pages). The ASV has “I have chosen thee and not cast thee away”; the NWT 1984 “I have chosen you, and I have not rejected you”; the KJV “called thee from the chief men thereof”.

Do not look anxiously about. “Do not be afraid, for I am with you; do not look anxiously about, for I am your God”: “do not be afraid” is the word to Abram, “Do not be afraid, Abram” (Genesis 15:1), and to Ahaz (Isaiah 7:4, both now on these pages), and it comes three times in five verses here (vv10, 13, 14). Tishta is from sha’ah, to gaze about, the look of someone searching for help in every direction; the same verb returns in verse 23. “The right hand of my righteousness” answers verse 2: the righteousness that summons the conqueror upholds Israel. The NWT 1984 has “Do not gaze about, for I am your God”; the ASV “be not dismayed, for I am thy God”; the Douay “turn not aside”.

As nothing. “The men who contend with you shall be as nothing and shall perish … as nothing and as nought”: ayin and efes, the words chapter 40 used of the nations, “as nothing before him … less than nothing” (40:17). “I take hold of your right hand” reverses the usual image: here God grips the hand of his servant. The ASV has “shall be as nothing, and as a thing of nought”; the NWT 1984 “as something nonexistent and as nothing”; the NIV “will be as nothing at all”.

Verses 14–16 · “Worm Jacob” — the few men of Israel, your Redeemer, and the threshing sledge with double edges

Worm Jacob, men of Israel. “Do not be afraid, worm Jacob, men of Israel”: tola’at, the grub that bred in the manna, and the word turns feminine here, Jacob addressed as a woman, as Zion is elsewhere in these chapters. Metei Yisrael is “men of Israel”, but the noun is the one of “few in number”, metei mispar, as when Jacob says “I am few in number” (Genesis 34:30) and Moses warns “you will be left few in number” (Deuteronomy 4:27, both now on these pages); the Greek has “few Israel”. The Latin read it as metim, “dead men”, and the Douay has “you that are dead of Israel”. The ASV has “Fear not, thou worm Jacob, and ye men of Israel”; the NIV “you worm Jacob, little Israel”.

Your Redeemer. “I help you, declares Jehovah, your Redeemer, the Holy One of Israel”: go’el, the next of kin whose duty is to buy back what a poor relative has lost (Leviticus 25:25), the role Boaz takes up (Ruth 4, both now on these pages). “Your Redeemer” in this spelling stands in seven verses of Isaiah, and this is the first. “The Holy One of Israel” is Isaiah’s own title, here three times (vv14, 16, 20); it stands in twenty-four verses of Isaiah, against six in the rest of the Bible. The NWT 1984 has “even your Repurchaser, the Holy One of Israel”; the ASV “and thy Redeemer is the Holy One of Israel”; the KJV “and thy redeemer, the Holy One of Israel”.

A threshing sledge with double edges. “Look — I make you a threshing sledge, sharp, new, with double edges”: a morag is a heavy board studded underneath with stones or iron, dragged over the sheaves by oxen, as in the engraving on this page. Ba’al pifiyyot, “owner of mouths”, is “double-edged”, and the word stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and the “two-edged sword” of Psalm 149:6. The worm becomes the instrument that grinds mountains to chaff, and then the storm of 40:24 blows the chaff away. The NWT 1984 has “a new threshing instrument having double-edged [teeth]”; the ASV “a new sharp threshing instrument having teeth”; the Douay “a new thrashing wain, with teeth like a saw”.

Verses 17–20 · Rivers on the bare heights — the afflicted and the needy, seven trees in the desert, and “the Holy One of Israel has created it”

The afflicted and the needy. “The afflicted and the needy seek water, and there is none”: aniyyim and evyonim, the pair of the law’s care for the poor. The way home through the desert becomes a land of water: “rivers on the bare heights, and springs in the midst of the valleys”. Shefayim are the bare, wind-swept hilltops, the last place water would be. The ASV has “I will open rivers on the bare heights”; the NWT 1984 “Upon bare hills I shall open up rivers”; the KJV “I will open rivers in high places”.

Seven trees. “I will put in the wilderness the cedar, the acacia, the myrtle and the oil tree; I will set in the desert the juniper, the ash and the cypress together”: seven trees, the first four well known and the last three uncertain. The acacia is the wood of the ark (Exodus 25:10, now on these pages); the myrtle returns in Isaiah’s promise “instead of the brier, the myrtle” (55:13); the sixth tree, tidhar, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and 60:13, where the same last three are listed again. The Greek has five trees. The ASV has “the fir-tree, the pine, and the box-tree together”; the NWT 1984 “the juniper tree, the ash and the cypress”; the Douay “the fir tree, the elm, and the box tree”.

Has created it. “That the hand of Jehovah has done this, and the Holy One of Israel has created it”: bara, the verb of “In the beginning God created” (Genesis 1:1, now on these pages), used here of a garden grown in the desert. The four verbs of seeing and knowing, “see and know, and consider and understand”, end in yaskilu, from sakal, to have insight. The ASV has “and the Holy One of Israel hath created it”; the NWT 1984 “and the Holy One of Israel has himself created it”.

Verses 21–24 · The trial of the gods — “the King of Jacob”, the former things and the things to come, and “less than nothing”

The King of Jacob. “Bring near your case, says Jehovah; bring forward your strong arguments, says the King of Jacob”: the court of verse 1 now hears the other gods. “The King of Jacob” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Atsumot, “strong arguments”, is from the root of strength and of bone. The ASV has “Produce your cause, saith Jehovah; bring forth your strong reasons, saith the King of Jacob”; the NWT 1984 “Produce YOUR arguments”; the NIV “says Jacob’s King”.

The former things and the things to come. “The former things, what were they? … or let us hear the things to come”: ha-rishonot and ha-ba’ot, the test these chapters will set again and again, that a god who cannot tell what is coming is no god. “Do good or do harm, that we may look anxiously about and see it together”: anything at all, as Jeremiah says of idols that “cannot do harm, nor is it in them to do good” (Jeremiah 10:5). Nishta’ah is the verb of verse 10, so the gods are asked to make their worshipers anxious, the very thing Jehovah forbids his servant. The written text has ve-nira, “and we will fear”, read ve-nir’eh, “and we will see”. The ASV has “yea, do good, or do evil, that we may be dismayed, and behold it together”; the NWT 1984 “that we may gaze about and see [it] at the same time”.

Less than nothing. “Look — you are less than nothing, and your work less than naught; an abomination is whoever chooses you”: me-ayin, as in 40:17, and me-afa, a word found nowhere else. The form stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible; its letters elsewhere spell ef’eh, the viper (30:6), and many take it as a variant of efes, “nought”. The Greek has “from where are you, and from where your work? From the earth”. “Abomination” is to’evah. The ASV has “Behold, ye are of nothing, and your work is of nought; an abomination is he that chooseth you”; the NWT 1984 “YOU are something nonexistent, and YOUR achievement is nothing”.

Verses 25–29 · The one from the north — governors like clay, “Look, here they are!”, a herald of good news, and wind and empty chaos

From the north, from the rising of the sun. “I have roused one from the north, and he has come; from the rising of the sun, one who calls on my name”: the verb of verse 2, he’ir, closes a frame around the chapter, and the conqueror comes from both quarters, as Cyrus, king of Media and Persia, did. The Greek has “they shall be called by my name”. “He will come on governors as on mortar, as a potter treads clay”: seganim, a word borrowed from Akkadian for provincial officials. The potter is Jeremiah’s, “as the clay in the potter’s hand, so are you in my hand” (Jeremiah 18:6, now on these pages). The ASV has “and he shall come upon rulers as upon mortar, and as the potter treadeth clay”; the NWT 1984 “he will come upon deputy rulers as [if they were] clay”; the NIV “He treads on rulers as if they were mortar”.

Look, here they are. “Who told it from the beginning, that we might know? … There was none who told, none who announced, none who heard your words. First to Zion — ‘Look, here they are!’ — and to Jerusalem I give a herald of good news”: rishon le-Tsiyyon hinneh hinnam, as compressed as Hebrew gets. Most take it as Jehovah’s claim to be the first to tell Zion, “Look, here they are”, the exiles coming home. The mevasser, “herald of good news”, is the word of 40:9, and of the feet on the mountains that Paul quotes (Romans 10:15, both now on these pages; mevasser). The Latin has evangelistam, and so the Douay reads “to Jerusalem I will give an evangelist”. The Greek reads “I will give Zion a beginning, and comfort Jerusalem for the way”. The ASV has “I am the first that saith unto Zion, Behold, behold them”; the NWT 1984 “There is one first, [saying] to Zion”.

Wind and empty chaos. “And I look, and there is not a man; and among these there is no counselor”: the gods have no counselor to give, and chapter 40 asked who had ever been Jehovah’s counselor (40:13). “Look — they are all iniquity; their works are nought; their cast images are wind and empty chaos”: aven, iniquity; efes, nought; and tohu, “empty chaos”, as in 40:17 and 40:23, the word for the earth before the first day (Genesis 1:2, now on these pages). The ASV has “Behold, all of them, their works are vanity and nought; their molten images are wind and confusion”; the NWT 1984 “Their molten images are wind and unreality”; the KJV “Behold, they are all vanity”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “renew their strength”, as in 40:31; “islands” for iyyim, as in 40:15; “Righteousness calls him”, without supplying a person; “I am he” for ani hu, as in Deuteronomy 32:39; “strengthen” and “take hold” for chazaq; “craftsman” and “goldsmith”, as in 40:19; “do not be afraid”, as in Genesis 15:1 and Isaiah 7:4; “look anxiously about” for sha’ah, in verses 10 and 23 alike; “as nothing” and “empty chaos”, as in 40:17; “Redeemer” for go’el; “men of Israel” for metei Yisrael; and “herald of good news”, as in 40:9.

Echoes paid. The renewed strength, the stubble and storm, the idol-makers, “as nothing” and “empty chaos”, the ends of the earth and the herald of good news, all from chapter 40; the “do not be afraid” of Genesis 15 and Isaiah 7; and the “I am he” of Deuteronomy 32.

Echoes planted. Cyrus named (Isaiah 44:28; 45:1); the servant called by name (Isaiah 42:1); “I am the first and I am the last” (Isaiah 44:6; 48:12); and the feet of the herald on the mountains (Isaiah 52:7). None is translated here yet.

⚠ A note on order: Isaiah stands on these pages at chapters 1, 7, 40, 41 and 53. Isaiah 2–6, 8–39, 42–52 and 54–66 remain open; next in this part of the book is Isaiah 42 — “Look, my servant, whom I uphold”: the first of the servant songs, a bruised reed he will not break, and a new song to Jehovah.
