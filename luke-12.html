Warning his disciples first, before an enormous crowd, against the leaven of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy, Jesus tells them to fear God rather than those who can only kill the body, and not to fear confessing him even under interrogation, though blasphemy against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven. Asked to settle an inheritance, he refuses the role of judge and tells the parable of a rich fool who plans years of ease and dies that same night. He tells the disciples not to be anxious about food or clothing, promises a 'little flock' the kingdom, and describes slaves who stay ready for a returning master as blessed, with Peter drawing out the parable of the faithful and unfaithful manager. He warns that he brings fire and division, not the peace some expect, and rebukes the crowds for reading the weather but not the times, closing with the parable of settling with a creditor before it is too late.
c. AD 29, continuing the same journey-to-Jerusalem sequence opened at 9:51 and carried through chapters 10-11; Matthew scatters this same material across several discourses of his own (the sparrows and confession sayings and the anxiety discourse in the Sermon and the mission charge, the faithful-and-unfaithful-slave parable in Matthew 24, the settling-with-an-accuser parable in the Sermon), while the parable of the rich fool and Peter's interrupting question are found only in Luke.

Luke 12 — The Mister Translation

1
Ἐν οἷς ἐπισυναχθεισῶν τῶν μυριάδων τοῦ ὄχλου, ὥστε καταπατεῖν ἀλλήλους, ἤρξατο λέγειν πρὸς τοὺς μαθητὰς αὐτοῦ πρῶτον· Προσέχετε ἑαυτοῖς ἀπὸ τῆς ζύμης, ἥτις ἐστὶν ὑπόκρισις, τῶν Φαρισαίων.
Meanwhile, when the crowd had gathered by the tens of thousands, so that they were trampling one another, he began to say to his disciples first, “Watch out for yourselves, because of the yeast of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy.”note
Mientras tanto, cuando la multitud se había reunido por decenas de miles, tanto que se atropellaban unos a otros, comenzó a decir primero a sus discípulos: «Cuídense de la levadura de los fariseos, que es la hipocresía.»
2
οὐδὲν δὲ συγκεκαλυμμένον ἐστὶν ὃ οὐκ ἀποκαλυφθήσεται, καὶ κρυπτὸν ὃ οὐ γνωσθήσεται.
But there is nothing covered up that will not be uncovered, and nothing hidden that will not become known.note
Pero no hay nada encubierto que no se vaya a descubrir, ni oculto que no se vaya a saber.
3
ἀνθʼ ὧν ὅσα ἐν τῇ σκοτίᾳ εἴπατε ἐν τῷ φωτὶ ἀκουσθήσεται, καὶ ὃ πρὸς τὸ οὖς ἐλαλήσατε ἐν τοῖς ταμείοις κηρυχθήσεται ἐπὶ τῶν δωμάτων.
Therefore, whatever you have said in the darkness will be heard in the light, and what you have spoken in the ear inside a storeroom will be proclaimed from the housetops.note
Por eso, todo lo que han dicho en la oscuridad se oirá en la luz, y lo que han hablado al oído en los cuartos internos se proclamará desde las azoteas.
4
Λέγω δὲ ὑμῖν τοῖς φίλοις μου, μὴ φοβηθῆτε ἀπὸ τῶν ἀποκτεινόντων τὸ σῶμα καὶ μετὰ ταῦτα μὴ ἐχόντων περισσότερόν τι ποιῆσαι.
But I say to you, my friends: do not fear those who kill the body, and after that have nothing more they can do.note
Pero les digo a ustedes, amigos míos: no teman a los que matan el cuerpo, y después de eso ya no pueden hacer nada más.
5
ὑποδείξω δὲ ὑμῖν τίνα φοβηθῆτε· φοβήθητε τὸν μετὰ τὸ ἀποκτεῖναι ἔχοντα ἐξουσίαν ἐμβαλεῖν εἰς τὴν γέενναν· ναί, λέγω ὑμῖν, τοῦτον φοβήθητε.
But I will show you whom to fear: fear the one who, after killing, has authority to throw into Gehenna. Yes, I tell you, fear this one.note
Sino que les mostraré a quién deben temer: teman al que, después de matar, tiene autoridad para arrojar en el Guehená. Sí, les digo, a este teman.
6
οὐχὶ πέντε στρουθία πωλοῦνται ἀσσαρίων δύο; καὶ ἓν ἐξ αὐτῶν οὐκ ἔστιν ἐπιλελησμένον ἐνώπιον τοῦ θεοῦ.
Are not five sparrows sold for two pennies? And not one of them is forgotten before God.note
¿No se venden cinco gorriones por dos monedas pequeñas? Y ni uno solo de ellos está olvidado delante de Dios.
7
ἀλλὰ καὶ αἱ τρίχες τῆς κεφαλῆς ὑμῶν πᾶσαι ἠρίθμηνται· μὴ φοβεῖσθε· πολλῶν στρουθίων διαφέρετε.
But even the hairs of your head are all numbered. Do not be afraid: you are worth more than many sparrows.note
Pero hasta los cabellos de la cabeza de ustedes están todos contados. No teman: valen más que muchos gorriones.
8
Λέγω δὲ ὑμῖν, πᾶς ὃς ἂν ὁμολογήσῃ ἐν ἐμοὶ ἔμπροσθεν τῶν ἀνθρώπων, καὶ ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ὁμολογήσει ἐν αὐτῷ ἔμπροσθεν τῶν ἀγγέλων τοῦ θεοῦ·
And I tell you, everyone who acknowledges me before men, the Son of Man will also acknowledge him before the angels of God.note
Y les digo: todo el que me reconozca delante de los hombres, también el Hijo del Hombre lo reconocerá delante de los ángeles de Dios.
9
ὁ δὲ ἀρνησάμενός με ἐνώπιον τῶν ἀνθρώπων ἀπαρνηθήσεται ἐνώπιον τῶν ἀγγέλων τοῦ θεοῦ.
But the one who denies me before men will be denied before the angels of God.note
Pero al que me niegue delante de los hombres se le negará delante de los ángeles de Dios.
10
καὶ πᾶς ὃς ἐρεῖ λόγον εἰς τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου, ἀφεθήσεται αὐτῷ τῷ δὲ εἰς τὸ ἅγιον πνεῦμα βλασφημήσαντι οὐκ ἀφεθήσεται.
And everyone who speaks a word against the Son of Man will be forgiven; but the one who blasphemes against the Holy Spirit will not be forgiven.note
Y a todo el que diga una palabra contra el Hijo del Hombre se le perdonará; pero al que blasfeme contra el Espíritu Santo no se le perdonará.
11
ὅταν δὲ εἰσφέρωσιν ὑμᾶς ἐπὶ τὰς συναγωγὰς καὶ τὰς ἀρχὰς καὶ τὰς ἐξουσίας, μὴ μεριμνήσητε πῶς ἢ τί ἀπολογήσησθε ἢ τί εἴπητε·
And when they bring you before the synagogues, the rulers, and the authorities, do not be anxious about how you should defend yourselves or what you should say.note
Y cuando los lleven ante las sinagogas, los gobernantes y las autoridades, no se preocupen por cómo van a defenderse o qué van a decir;
12
τὸ γὰρ ἅγιον πνεῦμα διδάξει ὑμᾶς ἐν αὐτῇ τῇ ὥρᾳ ἃ δεῖ εἰπεῖν.
For the Holy Spirit will teach you, in that very hour, what you must say.note
porque el Espíritu Santo les enseñará, en esa misma hora, lo que deben decir.
13
Εἶπεν δέ τις ἐκ τοῦ ὄχλου αὐτῷ· Διδάσκαλε, εἰπὲ τῷ ἀδελφῷ μου μερίσασθαι μετʼ ἐμοῦ τὴν κληρονομίαν.
Then someone from the crowd said to him, “Teacher, tell my brother to divide the inheritance with me.”note
Entonces alguien de la multitud le dijo: «Maestro, dile a mi hermano que reparta conmigo la herencia.»
14
ὁ δὲ εἶπεν αὐτῷ· Ἄνθρωπε, τίς με κατέστησεν κριτὴν ἢ μεριστὴν ἐφʼ ὑμᾶς;
But he said to him, “Man, who appointed me judge or arbitrator over you?”note
Pero él le dijo: «Hombre, ¿quién me nombró juez o repartidor entre ustedes?»
15
εἶπεν δὲ πρὸς αὐτούς· Ὁρᾶτε καὶ φυλάσσεσθε ἀπὸ πάσης πλεονεξίας, ὅτι οὐκ ἐν τῷ περισσεύειν τινὶ ἡ ζωὴ αὐτοῦ ἐστιν ἐκ τῶν ὑπαρχόντων αὐτῷ.
And he said to them, “Watch out, and guard yourselves against every kind of greed, because a person’s life does not consist in the abundance of his possessions.”note
Y les dijo: «Miren, y guárdense de toda clase de codicia, porque la vida de una persona no consiste en la abundancia de sus bienes.»
16
εἶπεν δὲ παραβολὴν πρὸς αὐτοὺς λέγων· Ἀνθρώπου τινὸς πλουσίου εὐφόρησεν ἡ χώρα.
And he told them a parable, saying, “The land of a certain rich man produced good crops.note
Y les contó una parábola, diciendo: «Las tierras de cierto hombre rico dieron una buena cosecha.
17
καὶ διελογίζετο ἐν ἑαυτῷ λέγων· Τί ποιήσω, ὅτι οὐκ ἔχω ποῦ συνάξω τοὺς καρπούς μου;
And he reasoned within himself, saying, ‘What shall I do, since I have no place to store my crops?’note
Y él razonaba consigo mismo, diciendo: ‘¿Qué haré, ya que no tengo dónde guardar mis cosechas?’
18
καὶ εἶπεν· Τοῦτο ποιήσω· καθελῶ μου τὰς ἀποθήκας καὶ μείζονας οἰκοδομήσω, καὶ συνάξω ἐκεῖ πάντα τὸν σῖτον καὶ τὰ ἀγαθά μου,
And he said, ‘This is what I will do: I will tear down my barns and build bigger ones, and there I will store all my grain and my goods.note
Y dijo: ‘Esto es lo que haré: derribaré mis graneros y construiré otros más grandes, y ahí guardaré todo mi grano y mis bienes,
19
καὶ ἐρῶ τῇ ψυχῇ μου· Ψυχή, ἔχεις πολλὰ ἀγαθὰ κείμενα εἰς ἔτη πολλά· ἀναπαύου, φάγε, πίε, εὐφραίνου.
and I will say to my soul: “Soul, you have many goods laid up for many years; rest, eat, drink, be merry.”’note
y le diré a mi alma: “Alma, tienes muchos bienes guardados para muchos años; descansa, come, bebe, alégrate.”’
20
εἶπεν δὲ αὐτῷ ὁ θεός· Ἄφρων ταύτῃ τῇ νυκτὶ τὴν ψυχήν σου ἀπαιτοῦσιν ἀπὸ σοῦ· ἃ δὲ ἡτοίμασας, τίνι ἔσται;
But God said to him, ‘Fool! This very night your soul is demanded back from you — and the things you prepared, whose will they be?’note
Pero Dios le dijo: ‘¡Necio! Esta misma noche te reclaman el alma —¿y lo que preparaste, de quién será?’
21
οὕτως ὁ θησαυρίζων ἑαυτῷ καὶ μὴ εἰς θεὸν πλουτῶν.
So it is with the one who stores up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God.”note
Así es con el que atesora para sí mismo, y no es rico para con Dios.»
22
Εἶπεν δὲ πρὸς τοὺς μαθητὰς αὐτοῦ· Διὰ τοῦτο λέγω ὑμῖν, μὴ μεριμνᾶτε τῇ ψυχῇ τί φάγητε, μηδὲ τῷ σώματι τί ἐνδύσησθε.
Then he said to his disciples, “For this reason I tell you: do not be anxious about your life, what you will eat, nor about your body, what you will wear.note
Y dijo a sus discípulos: «Por eso les digo: no se preocupen por su vida, qué van a comer, ni por su cuerpo, qué van a vestir.
23
ἡ γὰρ ψυχὴ πλεῖόν ἐστιν τῆς τροφῆς καὶ τὸ σῶμα τοῦ ἐνδύματος.
For life is more than food, and the body more than clothing.note
Porque la vida es más que la comida, y el cuerpo más que la ropa.
24
κατανοήσατε τοὺς κόρακας ὅτι οὐ σπείρουσιν οὐδὲ θερίζουσιν, οἷς οὐκ ἔστιν ταμεῖον οὐδὲ ἀποθήκη, καὶ ὁ θεὸς τρέφει αὐτούς· πόσῳ μᾶλλον ὑμεῖς διαφέρετε τῶν πετεινῶν.
Consider the ravens: they neither sow nor reap; they have no storeroom or barn, and God feeds them. How much more are you worth than the birds!note
Fíjense en los cuervos: no siembran ni cosechan; no tienen despensa ni granero, y Dios los alimenta. ¡Cuánto más valen ustedes que las aves!
25
τίς δὲ ἐξ ὑμῶν μεριμνῶν δύναται ἐπὶ τὴν ἡλικίαν αὐτοῦ προσθεῖναι πῆχυν;
And which of you, by being anxious, can add a cubit to his span of life?note
¿Y quién de ustedes, por preocuparse, puede añadir un codo a su estatura?
26
εἰ οὖν οὐδὲ ἐλάχιστον δύνασθε, τί περὶ τῶν λοιπῶν μεριμνᾶτε;
If, then, you cannot do even the smallest thing, why are you anxious about the rest?note
Si, pues, no pueden ni con lo más pequeño, ¿por qué se preocupan por lo demás?
27
κατανοήσατε τὰ κρίνα πῶς αὐξάνει· οὐ κοπιᾷ οὐδὲ νήθει· λέγω δὲ ὑμῖν, οὐδὲ Σολομὼν ἐν πάσῃ τῇ δόξῃ αὐτοῦ περιεβάλετο ὡς ἓν τούτων.
Consider the lilies, how they grow: they neither toil nor spin; yet I tell you, not even Solomon in all his glory was clothed like one of these.note
Fíjense en los lirios, cómo crecen: no trabajan ni hilan; pero les digo, ni Salomón, con toda su gloria, se vistió como uno de ellos.
28
εἰ δὲ ἐν ἀγρῷ τὸν χόρτον ὄντα σήμερον καὶ αὔριον εἰς κλίβανον βαλλόμενον ὁ θεὸς οὕτως ἀμφιέζει, πόσῳ μᾶλλον ὑμᾶς, ὀλιγόπιστοι.
But if God so clothes the grass, which is in the field today and tomorrow is thrown into the oven, how much more will he clothe you, you of little faith!note
Y si Dios viste así a la hierba, que hoy está en el campo y mañana es echada al horno, ¡cuánto más los vestirá a ustedes, hombres de poca fe!
29
καὶ ὑμεῖς μὴ ζητεῖτε τί φάγητε καὶ τί πίητε, καὶ μὴ μετεωρίζεσθε,
And do not seek what you will eat and what you will drink, and stop being tossed about with worry,note
Y ustedes, no busquen qué comer y qué beber, ni sigan inquietándose,
30
ταῦτα γὰρ πάντα τὰ ἔθνη τοῦ κόσμου ἐπιζητοῦσιν· ὑμῶν δὲ ὁ πατὴρ οἶδεν ὅτι χρῄζετε τούτων.
for all these things the nations of the world seek after, and your Father knows that you need them.note
porque todas estas cosas las buscan las naciones del mundo; pero el Padre de ustedes sabe que las necesitan.
31
πλὴν ζητεῖτε τὴν βασιλείαν αὐτοῦ καὶ ταῦτα προστεθήσεται ὑμῖν.
But seek his kingdom, and these things will be added to you.note
Más bien, busquen su reino, y estas cosas se les añadirán.
32
Μὴ φοβοῦ, τὸ μικρὸν ποίμνιον, ὅτι εὐδόκησεν ὁ πατὴρ ὑμῶν δοῦναι ὑμῖν τὴν βασιλείαν.
Do not be afraid, little flock, for your Father has been well pleased to give you the kingdom.note
No teman, pequeño rebaño, porque a su Padre le ha placido darles el reino.
33
πωλήσατε τὰ ὑπάρχοντα ὑμῶν καὶ δότε ἐλεημοσύνην· ποιήσατε ἑαυτοῖς βαλλάντια μὴ παλαιούμενα, θησαυρὸν ἀνέκλειπτον ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς, ὅπου κλέπτης οὐκ ἐγγίζει οὐδὲ σὴς διαφθείρει·
Sell your possessions, and give to charity. Make for yourselves purses that do not wear out, a treasure in the heavens that never fails, where no thief comes near and no moth destroys.note
Vendan lo que tienen y den limosna. Háganse bolsas que no se desgasten, un tesoro inagotable en los cielos, donde ningún ladrón se acerca ni la polilla destruye;
34
ὅπου γάρ ἐστιν ὁ θησαυρὸς ὑμῶν, ἐκεῖ καὶ ἡ καρδία ὑμῶν ἔσται.
For where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.”note
porque donde está su tesoro, allí estará también su corazón.»
35
Ἔστωσαν ὑμῶν αἱ ὀσφύες περιεζωσμέναι καὶ οἱ λύχνοι καιόμενοι,
“Let your waists be dressed for action, and your lamps burning,note
«Que sus cinturas estén ceñidas, y sus lámparas encendidas,
36
καὶ ὑμεῖς ὅμοιοι ἀνθρώποις προσδεχομένοις τὸν κύριον ἑαυτῶν πότε ἀναλύσῃ ἐκ τῶν γάμων, ἵνα ἐλθόντος καὶ κρούσαντος εὐθέως ἀνοίξωσιν αὐτῷ.
and you like men waiting for their master, whenever he returns from the wedding feast, so that when he comes and knocks they may open to him at once.note
y sean ustedes como hombres que esperan a su señor, cuando regrese de la fiesta de bodas, para que, cuando llegue y llame, le abran de inmediato.
37
μακάριοι οἱ δοῦλοι ἐκεῖνοι, οὓς ἐλθὼν ὁ κύριος εὑρήσει γρηγοροῦντας· ἀμὴν λέγω ὑμῖν ὅτι περιζώσεται καὶ ἀνακλινεῖ αὐτοὺς καὶ παρελθὼν διακονήσει αὐτοῖς.
Happy are those slaves whom the master finds awake when he comes. Truly I tell you, he will dress himself, have them recline at the table, and come and serve them.note
Felices aquellos siervos a quienes el señor, al llegar, encuentre despiertos. En verdad les digo que se ceñirá, los hará reclinarse a la mesa, y vendrá a servirles.
38
κἂν ἐν τῇ δευτέρᾳ κἂν ἐν τῇ τρίτῃ φυλακῇ ἔλθῃ καὶ εὕρῃ οὕτως, μακάριοί εἰσιν ἐκεῖνοι.
And if he comes in the second watch, or the third, and finds them so, happy are those slaves.”note
Y si llega en la segunda vigilia, o en la tercera, y los encuentra así, felices son aquellos siervos.»
39
Τοῦτο δὲ γινώσκετε ὅτι εἰ ᾔδει ὁ οἰκοδεσπότης ποίᾳ ὥρᾳ ὁ κλέπτης ἔρχεται, οὐκ ἂν ἀφῆκεν διορυχθῆναι τὸν οἶκον αὐτοῦ.
“But know this: if the master of the house had known at what hour the thief was coming, he would not have let his house be broken into.note
«Pero sepan esto: que si el dueño de la casa hubiera sabido a qué hora vendría el ladrón, no habría dejado que forzaran su casa.
40
καὶ ὑμεῖς γίνεσθε ἕτοιμοι, ὅτι ᾗ ὥρᾳ οὐ δοκεῖτε ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἔρχεται.
You also, be ready, because the Son of Man is coming at an hour you do not expect.”note
Ustedes también estén listos, porque el Hijo del Hombre viene a la hora que no esperan.»
41
Εἶπεν δὲ ὁ Πέτρος· Κύριε, πρὸς ἡμᾶς τὴν παραβολὴν ταύτην λέγεις ἢ καὶ πρὸς πάντας;
Peter said, “Lord, are you telling this parable to us, or to everyone?”note
Pedro dijo: «Señor, ¿nos dices esta parábola a nosotros, o a todos?»
42
καὶ εἶπεν ὁ κύριος· Τίς ἄρα ἐστὶν ὁ πιστὸς οἰκονόμος, ὁ φρόνιμος, ὃν καταστήσει ὁ κύριος ἐπὶ τῆς θεραπείας αὐτοῦ τοῦ διδόναι ἐν καιρῷ τὸ σιτομέτριον;
And the Lord said, “Who then is the faithful and sensible manager, whom the master will put in charge of his household, to give them their measure of food at the right time?note
Y el Señor dijo: «¿Quién es, entonces, el administrador fiel y sensato, a quien el señor pondrá a cargo de su casa, para dar a tiempo la ración de alimento?
43
μακάριος ὁ δοῦλος ἐκεῖνος, ὃν ἐλθὼν ὁ κύριος αὐτοῦ εὑρήσει ποιοῦντα οὕτως·
Happy is that slave whom his master, when he comes, finds doing so.note
Feliz aquel siervo a quien su señor, al llegar, encuentre haciéndolo así.
44
ἀληθῶς λέγω ὑμῖν ὅτι ἐπὶ πᾶσιν τοῖς ὑπάρχουσιν αὐτοῦ καταστήσει αὐτόν.
Truly I tell you, he will put him in charge of all his possessions.note
En verdad les digo que lo pondrá a cargo de todos sus bienes.
45
ἐὰν δὲ εἴπῃ ὁ δοῦλος ἐκεῖνος ἐν τῇ καρδίᾳ αὐτοῦ· Χρονίζει ὁ κύριός μου ἔρχεσθαι, καὶ ἄρξηται τύπτειν τοὺς παῖδας καὶ τὰς παιδίσκας, ἐσθίειν τε καὶ πίνειν καὶ μεθύσκεσθαι,
But if that slave says in his heart, ‘My master is delayed in coming,’ and begins to beat the menservants and the maidservants, and to eat and drink and get drunk,note
Pero si aquel siervo dice en su corazón: ‘Mi señor se tarda en venir’, y comienza a golpear a los siervos y a las siervas, y a comer y beber y embriagarse,
46
ἥξει ὁ κύριος τοῦ δούλου ἐκείνου ἐν ἡμέρᾳ ᾗ οὐ προσδοκᾷ καὶ ἐν ὥρᾳ ᾗ οὐ γινώσκει, καὶ διχοτομήσει αὐτὸν καὶ τὸ μέρος αὐτοῦ μετὰ τῶν ἀπίστων θήσει.
the master of that slave will come on a day he does not expect, and at an hour he does not know, and will cut him in two, and assign him a place with the unfaithful.note
el señor de aquel siervo llegará en un día que no espera, y a una hora que no sabe, y lo cortará en dos, y le asignará un lugar con los infieles.
47
ἐκεῖνος δὲ ὁ δοῦλος ὁ γνοὺς τὸ θέλημα τοῦ κυρίου αὐτοῦ καὶ μὴ ἑτοιμάσας ἢ ποιήσας πρὸς τὸ θέλημα αὐτοῦ δαρήσεται πολλάς·
And that slave who knew his master’s will, and did not prepare or act according to his will, will be beaten with many blows;note
Y aquel siervo que conocía la voluntad de su señor, y no se preparó ni actuó según esa voluntad, recibirá muchos golpes;
48
ὁ δὲ μὴ γνοὺς ποιήσας δὲ ἄξια πληγῶν δαρήσεται ὀλίγας. παντὶ δὲ ᾧ ἐδόθη πολύ, πολὺ ζητηθήσεται παρʼ αὐτοῦ, καὶ ᾧ παρέθεντο πολύ, περισσότερον αἰτήσουσιν αὐτόν.
but the one who did not know, and did things deserving of blows, will be beaten with few. Everyone to whom much was given, much will be required from him; and from the one who was entrusted with much, even more will be asked.”note
pero el que no la conocía, e hizo cosas dignas de golpes, recibirá pocos. Y a todo el que se le dio mucho, mucho se le exigirá; y al que se le confió mucho, más se le pedirá.»
49
Πῦρ ἦλθον βαλεῖν ἐπὶ τὴν γῆν, καὶ τί θέλω εἰ ἤδη ἀνήφθη;
“I came to cast fire on the earth, and how I wish it were already kindled!note
«Fuego vine a echar sobre la tierra, ¡y cómo quisiera que ya estuviera encendido!
50

But I have a baptism to be baptized with, and how I am hard-pressed until it is accomplished!note

Verses 1–3 · τῆς ζύμης … ἥτις ἐστὶν ὑπόκρισις … κρυπτὸν ὃ οὐ γνωσθήσεται tēs zymēs … hētis estin hypokrisis … krypton ho ou gnōsthēsetai — the biggest crowd this Gospel has counted yet, and a warning aimed past it, at the Twelve first

⭐⭐ Tens of thousands, trampling each other — and Jesus speaks to twelve of them first. The crowd, myriadōn (“by the tens of thousands,” the same word behind “myriad”), out-hyperboles even the five thousand fed at Luke 9:14 (already on these pages) — yet his first sentence is addressed pointedly “to his disciples first” (prōton), a word that all but promises a second audience. That promise is only partly kept: the crowds get the weather proverb at v54, but the sternest material here — fearing God rather than executioners, confessing under interrogation, the blasphemy that cannot be forgiven — stays private the whole chapter.

The yeast of the Pharisees, named hypocrisy outright, is the same image and the same negative sense Matthew 16:6 (already on these pages) gives it against the identical pairing of rivals — and the same word Matthew 13:33 turns completely around, into a picture of the kingdom’s own hidden growth. Luke will make that identical about-face in its own words two chapters ahead (13:20–21, not yet on these pages) — so that by then this Gospel too will have used zymē for both the corruption and the kingdom, exactly as Matthew already has.

⚠ A small textual wrinkle sits inside the sentence itself: the earlier witnesses this translation follows put “which is hypocrisy” right after “yeast,” qualifying it immediately; a later strand of the tradition moves the clause to the very end, after “of the Pharisees,” so that “hypocrisy” arrives as an afterthought rather than the sentence’s own hinge. The sense survives either order.

“Nothing covered that will not be uncovered” repeats, almost word for word, a sentence Mark 4:22 (already on these pages) already used to explain why Jesus speaks in parables at all — there, a saying about the kingdom’s own secrecy having an expiration date; here, the identical sentence turned into a warning that a hypocrite’s staged decency has one too. One sentence doing two different jobs in two different Gospels — the same “portable material” already flagged twice in the dinner-table scene one chapter back (11:33–36).

Verses 4–7 · μὴ φοβηθῆτε … ἐμβαλεῖν εἰς τὴν γέενναν … πέντε στρουθία … ἀσσαρίων δύο mē phobēthēte … embalein eis tēn geennan … pente strouthia … assariōn dyo — the identical sparrow argument Matthew already made, priced at a better rate

⭐⭐ The math changes, and the deal gets better. This is Matthew 10:28–31’s own argument (already on these pages, with its own note on the assarion and Gehenna) — the same two fears distinguished, the same sparrows, the same numbered hairs — but the coin does different work. Matthew’s arithmetic is two sparrows for one assarion; Luke’s is five for two. Recomputed: Matthew’s rate is two birds per coin; Luke’s is two and a half — the fifth sparrow is, in effect, thrown in for nothing, a bulk discount that makes the “not one is forgotten” argument even cheaper to buy than Matthew’s version of the same saying.

Gehenna is the Valley of Hinnom this project has already followed from Topheth’s ash-heap altars to Matthew 5:22’s own warning — named here as the ONE thing worth fearing instead of an executioner who, this chapter insists, “has nothing more” to do once the body is dead.

Verses 8–10 · ὁμολογήσῃ … ἀρνησάμενός … εἰς τὸ ἅγιον πνεῦμα βλασφημήσαντι homologēsē … arnēsamenos … eis to hagion pneuma blasphēmēsanti — the unforgivable sin, spoken here with no exorcism, no accusation, and no Beelzeboul in sight

⭐⭐⭐ The identical saying, with its trigger removed. Matthew 12:31–32’s own version of this saying (already on these pages) is the direct climax of a specific dispute: a crowd has just watched Jesus cast out a demon and heard it called Satan’s own work, and THAT particular act of calling God’s power satanic is what the blasphemy names there. Luke keeps the confess/deny formula almost word for word (cross Matthew 10:32–33, already on these pages) but detaches the unforgivable-sin saying completely from any triggering incident — no exorcism, no crowd calling it Beelzeboul’s doing, not even a link back to this Gospel’s OWN Beelzeboul scene one chapter earlier (11:14–23). Read without Matthew’s narrative anchor, the saying is harder to pin to one specific act and easier to hear as a general warning — a difference of placement, not of wording, and a caution against reading either Gospel’s setting into the other’s.

The mirror-built grammar Matthew 10:32’s own note already flagged — acknowledge/acknowledged, deny/denied, the verb handed back to the speaker — is intact here too, almost to the letter.

Verses 11–12 · εἰσφέρωσιν ὑμᾶς ἐπὶ τὰς συναγωγὰς … τὸ γὰρ ἅγιον πνεῦμα διδάξει ὑμᾶς eisperōsin hymas epi tas synagōgas … to gar hagion pneuma didaxei hymas — a promise this Gospel has now heard delivered in three different rooms

Mission charge, end-times warning, or neither — the same promise fits all three. The assurance that the Spirit will supply the words “in that very hour” is Mark 13:11’s own warning to the Twelve about a coming tribulation (already on these pages), and Matthew 10:19–20’s charge to them before their first mission (also already on these pages). Three settings for one promise: a future crisis in Mark, a specific sending-out in Matthew, and here, general teaching to a crowd with no mission and no crisis named at all — the clearest sign yet that this saying, like the leaven and the hidden-things sayings already flagged this chapter, traveled loose enough for each Gospel to set it wherever its own argument needed it.

Verses 13–15 · τίς με κατέστησεν κριτὴν ἢ μεριστὴν … ἀπὸ πάσης πλεονεξίας tis me katestēsen kritēn ē meristēn … apo pasēs pleonexias — asked to judge an inheritance, he refuses the one title later preaching will give him without his ever having claimed it

⭐⭐ “Judge or arbitrator” (kritēs ē meristēs) names the very role the lawyers of 10:25 and 11:45–52 (already on these pages) hold as a guild — and Jesus refuses the office outright, mid-crowd, over the most ordinary of legal disputes. The refusal has an edge later readers of this book would recognize: the earliest Christian preaching will call the risen Jesus God’s own appointed judge “of the living and the dead” (Acts 10:42, not yet on these pages) — a title handed to him here, in the one scene where a stranger tries to give it to him directly, and he will not take it.

“Every kind of greed” (pasēs pleonexias) sets up the parable that follows as an illustration, not an unrelated second saying — the man demanding his share of an inheritance and the man in the parable hoarding grain are answered by the identical sentence: life does not consist in the abundance of one’s possessions.

Verses 16–21 · Τί ποιήσω … Ψυχή, ἔχεις πολλὰ ἀγαθὰ … Ἄφρων Ti poiēsō … Psychē, echeis polla agatha … Aphrōn — a monologue with no room in it for anyone else

⭐⭐⭐ Twelve first-person words in three verses, and not one other person in the room. Counted directly from the Greek, vv17–19 use a first-person verb or possessive twelve times — poiēsō (I will do), echō (I have), synaxō (I will gather), mou (my crops), poiēsō again, kathelō (I will tear down), mou (my barns), oikodomēsō (I will build), synaxō again, mou (my goods), erō (I will say), mou (my soul) — a monologue with no other person in it at all, not even the God who is about to speak the only other line in the scene. He talks to his own soul as though it were a separate guest at his own table; God’s answer addresses that same soul directly and takes it back that same night.

“Eat, drink, and be merry” (phage, pie, euphrainou) is the classic ancient formula for living entirely for today — the parable’s whole irony is that the man plans YEARS (“pollá etē”) of it on the one night he has none left.

⚠ “Fool” translates aphrōn — not moral wickedness but a diagnosed failure to reckon with the one variable that was never his to plan around. The word choice matters: nothing in the parable accuses him of having gained his wealth wrongly, only of forgetting whose it would become.

Verses 22–31 · μὴ μεριμνᾶτε τῇ ψυχῇ … κατανοήσατε τοὺς κόρακας … οὐδὲ Σολομὼν mē merimnate tē psychē … katanoēsate tous korakas … oude Solomōn — the identical sermon Matthew already preached, with one bird swapped for a worse one

⭐⭐⭐ The identical “do not be anxious” sermon — with an unclean bird where Matthew had a generic one. Verse for verse this is Matthew 6:25–33’s own argument (already on these pages, down to the anxiety drumbeat merimnaō and the disciples addressed as oligopistoi), but Luke replaces Matthew’s generic “birds of the heaven” with a specific, named bird: the raven (korax) — one of the UNCLEAN birds this project has already listed at Leviticus 11:15 and Deuteronomy 14:14 (already on these pages), a scavenger with no place on anyone’s table. Readers who knew their Scriptures would also know the raven from a specific story: Elijah, starving in the wilderness, was fed BY ravens (1 Kings 17:4–6, not yet on these pages). Here the direction reverses — God simply feeds the ravens themselves, unclean and unproductive exactly as they are, asking nothing of them in return.

⚠ The “cubit” argument (v25) carries its own small addition in the manuscripts: the earlier witnesses this translation follows read simply “a cubit”; a later strand adds “one” (hena), pulling Luke’s wording one notch closer to a debate that does not exist in the Greek here — the measurement is already singular without it. NIV sidesteps the ambiguity noted at Matthew 6:27’s own parallel by reading “a single hour” instead of a literal cubit — the identical choice made there for the identical Greek phrase.

Verses 32–34 · Μὴ φοβοῦ, τὸ μικρὸν ποίμνιον … ὅπου ὁ θησαυρός Mē phobou, to mikron poimnion … hopou ho thēsauros — the smallest flock in the New Testament, named with a word already built to sound small

⭐⭐ Poimnion is already a diminutive of poimnē, “flock” — and Luke doubles down by calling it “the little poimnion,” a tenderness with no real parallel elsewhere in the Gospels for the whole body of disciples (John’s Good Shepherd chapter will speak of “one flock,” not yet on these pages, but never a SMALL one). Set against a chapter that has just measured “the nations of the world” and Solomon’s own glory, the smallness is the point: this is the group great empires and rich kings are implicitly being contrasted with, and it is handed a kingdom anyway.

“Treasure in the heavens … where no thief comes near” restates Matthew 6:19–21’s own treasure saying (already on these pages) almost clause for clause — moth and thief both carried over unchanged — but drops Matthew’s parallel warning about rust, keeping the two thefts (natural decay, deliberate theft) a purse of coins, unlike a barn of grain, is actually vulnerable to.

Verses 35–38 · αἱ ὀσφύες περιεζωσμέναι … περιζώσεται καὶ ἀνακλινεῖ αὐτοὺς καὶ διακονήσει hai osphyes periezōsmenai … perizōsetai kai anaklinei autous kai diakonēsei — a master who comes home and puts on an apron

⭐⭐⭐ The master girds himself — with the same verb the slaves were just told to keep girded. Stay dressed, keep the lamps lit, be ready to open the door at a knock — the same wakefulness Mark 13:33–37 (already on these pages) commands of a doorkeeper who cannot know when the master returns. But Luke’s version turns the reward into something no other version of this scene has: the master, finding them ready, “will dress HIMSELF” (perizōsetai — the identical verb the slaves were just told to keep girded with) and serve THEM at table. A master girding himself like a slave to wait on the household he owns is a role reversal this Gospel does not explain here — it will not need to, once its own Last Supper reaches the same gesture in the first person: “I am among you as the one serving” (22:27, not yet on these pages).

KJV, ASV and the Geneva all print “Blessed” for makarios at v37; this translation follows the NWT’s “Happy” instead, matching the choice already fixed at 6:20’s own beatitudes.

Verses 39–40 · εἰ ᾔδει ὁ οἰκοδεσπότης … οὐκ ἂν ἀφῆκεν διορυχθῆναι ei ēdei ho oikodespotēs … ouk an aphēken diorychēnai — a clause the manuscripts import straight out of Matthew, for the third time this project has caught the habit

⚠⚠ “He would have stayed awake” — Matthew’s own wording of this same saying, imported wholesale. A large part of the manuscript tradition — including the readings behind KJV (“he would have watched, and not have suffered his house to be broken through”), ASV (“he would have watched, and not have left his house to be broken through”), and even the NWT 1984 (“he would have kept watching and not have let his house be broken into”) — adds a clause with no equivalent in the shorter text this translation follows: the master “would have STAYED AWAKE and” not allowed the break-in. That added clause is, word for word, Matthew’s OWN wording of this identical thief-in-the-night saying (24:43, already on these pages: “he would have stayed awake and would not have let his house be broken into”). This is the third time in two chapters this project has caught a copyist importing Matthew’s exact phrasing into Luke’s version of a shared saying — after the Lord’s Prayer and the “scribes and Pharisees, hypocrites” refrain at 11:2–4 and 11:44 (already on these pages) — and tellingly, the NWT’s own 2013 revision quietly drops the clause its 1984 edition had kept, moving to the shorter reading this translation follows.

NIV already reads the shorter text here too, agreeing with this translation’s choice.

Verses 41–48 · πρὸς ἡμᾶς … ἢ καὶ πρὸς πάντας; … τίς ἄρα ἐστὶν ὁ πιστὸς οἰκονόμος pros hēmas … ē kai pros pantas? … tis ara estin ho pistos oikonomos — Peter interrupts a parable Matthew's Gospel lets run without him, and the household gets a manager instead of a slave

⭐⭐ Matthew’s version of this scene runs straight through, uninterrupted. Matthew 24:45–51 (already on these pages) tells this same thief-and-servant material without a break; Luke alone gives Peter the interruption, “are you telling this to us, or to everyone?”, and Jesus answers with a parable rather than a yes or no. Where Matthew calls the man left in charge simply “the faithful and sensible SLAVE” (doulos), Luke’s word is oikonomos, “manager” — a distinct role with real delegated authority over a household’s resources, not just its chores. The swap fits a pattern already visible in this Gospel’s own vocabulary: a lawyer at 10:25, a debt-collector three verses from this chapter’s own close (v58), and now a household manager — Luke’s Jesus keeps reaching for the language of administration and finance where Matthew’s reaches for the language of plain service.

“Everyone to whom much was given, much will be required” closes the parable as a stand-alone maxim, quoted so often on its own that its origin here — a warning to a manager entrusted with OTHER people’s food rations, not a general statement about talent or opportunity — is easy to lose.

Verses 49–53 · Πῦρ ἦλθον βαλεῖν … βάπτισμα δὲ ἔχω βαπτισθῆναι … ἀλλʼ ἢ διαμερισμόν Pyr ēlthon balein … baptisma de echō baptisthēnai … all’ ē diamerismon — the same warning Matthew gives with a sword, given here with fire and a name for what fire does

⭐⭐ Fire and “division,” where Matthew’s version of this warning never leaves the sword. Matthew 10:34–36 (already on these pages) uses one image throughout — a sword — for the identical five-way household split named here. Luke never uses that word at all: fire, then “division” (diamerismon) by name, doing the work Matthew’s sword does. Two different pictures attached to what appears to be the identical five-person headcount (three against two, two against three) — which argues for one saying remembered in two images, rather than two separate warnings that happened to agree on a number.

⭐⭐ “Baptism” as a figure for his own death is not a new image invented here. It is the same word Jesus uses to James, John and Peter: “are you able to be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with?” (Mark 10:38–39, already on these pages, with its own note on the paired cup). Luke’s Jesus applies the same figure to himself in the first person, naming the thing he is “hard-pressed” (synechomai — held together under pressure from both sides) to see through to its end.

Verses 54–56 · νεφέλην ἀνατέλλουσαν ἐπὶ δυσμῶν … τὸν καιρὸν τοῦτον πῶς οὐκ οἴδατε δοκιμάζειν nephelēn anatellousan epi dysmōn … ton kairon touton pōs ouk oidate dokimazein — a saying this project already flagged, three chapters into the other Gospel, as probably borrowed from here

⭐⭐⭐ The payoff of a promise this project made at Matthew 16. Matthew 16:1–4’s own note (already on these pages) already named this exact passage as the likely source of a puzzle in ITS Gospel: Matthew’s parallel version of this weather proverb (16:2b–3 — a red sky at evening, a red and lowering sky at morning) is missing from Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Vaticanus and a run of other early witnesses — missing so consistently that a number of textual critics have proposed the passage was never Matthew’s at all, but a scribe’s import of a nearly identical saying from LUKE, the version printed here, adjusted from Luke’s west-wind-and-south-wind pairing to Palestine’s more famous evening-red-sky omen. If that is right, the direction of borrowing usually assumed between the Synoptic Gospels runs backward for this one case: not Luke smoothing Matthew, but Matthew’s manuscript tradition smoothing itself out of Luke.

Whichever direction the borrowing ran, the point survives untouched in both settings: eyes that can read a sunset for tomorrow’s weather, and call it hypocrisy not to read the age itself.

Verses 57–59 · μετὰ τοῦ ἀντιδίκου σου ἐπʼ ἄρχοντα … ὁ πράκτωρ σε βαλεῖ εἰς φυλακήν … τὸ ἔσχατον λεπτόν meta tou antidikou sou ep’ archonta … ho praktōr se balei eis phylakēn … to eschaton lepton — the identical parable Matthew tells about anger, told here about a clock already running out

⭐⭐ Same parable, different point — and a different officer at the end of the chain. Matthew 5:25–26 (already on these pages) sets this exact scene inside the Sermon on the Mount’s teaching on anger and reconciliation — settle with a brother before the court does it for you. Luke’s version, closing a chapter about weather signs and a present time “this generation” keeps failing to read, reframes the identical parable as urgency about TIME running out generally, not reconciliation specifically. The officer at the end of the chain is also a different word in each Gospel: Matthew’s hypēretēs is a generic “attendant” (the same term used elsewhere for a synagogue or high-priestly assistant); Luke’s praktōr names something more specific — a debt-enforcement officer in Greco-Roman administration, the professional who actually collects what a court has ordered paid. It is the same financial vocabulary this chapter has already reached for with oikonomos at v42.

The coin at the very end — lepton, the smallest denomination in circulation — answers exactly the barns full of grain this chapter opened with: KJV and ASV both print “mite,” a genuinely tiny English coin in 1611 gone the way of the farthing already flagged at Matthew 10:29’s own note; the NWT 1984 spells the smallness out directly, “the last small coin of very little value,” which this translation’s own “small coin” follows in spirit rather than borrowing whole.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: manager for oikonomos (vv42–48), kept distinct from Matthew’s plain “slave” at its own version of this parable (24:45); ravens (v24) kept as Luke’s own specific, unclean bird rather than smoothed into Matthew’s generic “birds of the heaven”; Happy for makarios (vv37, 43), matching the choice already fixed at 6:20’s beatitudes; and little flock (v32) left with its double diminutive intact rather than flattened to a plain “flock.”

Echoes paid. Matthew 16:1–4’s own note, which already guessed this exact passage was the probable source Matthew’s weather-proverb was borrowed from, back when these pages had not yet reached it, is completed here (vv54–56). Matthew 10:29’s note on the assarion is answered by this chapter’s own better bulk rate (v6). And the mirror-built confess/deny formula planted at Matthew 10:32’s note recurs almost unchanged at vv8–9.

Echoes planted. This chapter’s own negative use of “yeast” (v1) will be answered by its opposite, the kingdom’s own hidden growth, two chapters ahead (13:20–21, not yet on these pages) — completing, on Luke’s own pages, the same two-sided use of the image Matthew’s Gospel already carries between 13:33 and 16:6. The master who “dresses himself” to serve his own household (v37) anticipates this Gospel’s own Last Supper, where Jesus will say the identical thing of himself in the first person (22:27, not yet on these pages). And the debt-collecting praktōr of v58, paired with this chapter’s own oikonomos at v42, is a vocabulary this Gospel is not finished with — Luke 16’s parable of the unjust STEWARD (not yet on these pages) will put the identical word back to work.

Next in sequence: Luke 13 — Galileans whose blood Pilate mixed with their own sacrifices, a tower that falls on eighteen people at Siloam, and the very echo this chapter’s yeast note just promised: a mustard seed and a woman’s yeast, both turned into pictures of the kingdom itself.
