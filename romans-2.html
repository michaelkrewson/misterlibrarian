Romans 2 — The Mister Translation

1
Διὸ ἀναπολόγητος εἶ, ὦ ἄνθρωπε πᾶς ὁ κρίνων· ἐν ᾧ γὰρ κρίνεις τὸν ἕτερον, σεαυτὸν κατακρίνεις, τὰ γὰρ αὐτὰ πράσσεις ὁ κρίνων·
Therefore you are without a defense, O man — every one of you who judges; for in that in which you judge another, you condemn yourself, for you who judge practice the same things.note
Por lo cual eres inexcusable, oh hombre, quienquiera que seas tú que juzgas; pues en lo que juzgas a otro, te condenas a ti mismo, porque tú que juzgas practicas las mismas cosas.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–5 · the trap springs

⚠ This is the sentence Romans 1's own closing note called the single most important thing to carry into this chapter. The list of Gentile vice in chapter 1 was bait, delivered in the voice of standard Jewish preaching — the sort of thing a Jewish reader would nod along to. The moment the nodding is loudest, Paul turns the sentence around: you are without a defense, whoever you are who judges. Chapter 1's note already flagged the trap; this is where it closes. “Without a defense” keeps anapologētous, the same courtroom word used there, rather than switching to “without excuse” for variety.

The accusation is not that the judge is wrong about the sin. It is that the judge is guilty of it — “you who judge practice the same things” (v1), stated as fact, not hypothesis.

Verses 6–11 · to the Jew first — twice

“To the Jew first and also to the Greek” (v9, v10) repeats, word for word, the phrase chapter 1's thesis statement already used (1:16) — and this time it cuts both ways. There the phrase named who hears the gospel first; here it names who is judged first. Paul will not let the priority run in only one direction. The same two words return as the letter's unfinished business at chapters 9-11, exactly as chapter 1's note anticipated.

⚠ “No partiality” (v11, prosōpolēmpsia) is literally “receiving the face” — judging by appearance, status, or favor rather than by fact. KJV and ASV both use the older idiom “no respect of persons”; NIV reads “no favoritism”; ESV reads “no partiality.” RV60 keeps the old idiom too, “no hay acepción de personas”; NVI modernizes to “no hay favoritismos.” This translation follows the modern sense, since the KJV-era idiom no longer means what it once did to an English ear.

Verses 12–16 · a law with no lawgiver in reach

⚠ “A law to themselves” (v14) and the work of the law “written on their hearts” (v15) is one of the most consequential sentences Paul ever wrote for the history of ethics outside the church — the closest thing in the New Testament to a doctrine of natural law or universal conscience, cited for centuries by philosophers who never opened the rest of the letter. Gentiles who never received Torah can still, Paul says, do what Torah requires, because the requirement is written into them by some means other than revelation.

Conscience (syneidēsis, v15) is not itself the law — it is the witness that testifies to how a person measures up against a standard already present. Paul’s own picture is a courtroom running inside a single person: accusing thoughts and defending thoughts, arguing the case, with no verdict reached until “the day when God judges the secrets of men” (v16).

Verses 17–24 · the teacher who cannot teach himself

The four rhetorical questions (vv21-22) are built to fall like blows: steal, adultery, idols, temple robbery — each one paired with the very credential just claimed a verse earlier. A guide to the blind who cannot see his own path; a teacher of infants who has not taught himself.

⚠ “The name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you” (v24) is presented as a citation — “just as it is written” — and the closest match on the page is Isaiah 52:5, where the Greek Old Testament has God's name “blasphemed” among the nations continually because of what has happened to Israel; Ezekiel 36:20-23, where Israel's exile causes the nations to see God's name “profaned,” carries the same charge in different words and may stand behind it as well. Either way the accusation is not new with Paul: he is telling a Jewish reader that their own scripture already convicted them of exactly this, centuries earlier.

Verses 25–27 · the sign reversed

Circumcision was Abraham's own sign of the covenant (Genesis 17:11), and Paul does not dispute its value — he disputes what it is worth WITHOUT the obedience it was meant to mark. A lawbreaking circumcised man has, in Paul's arithmetic, subtracted the sign back to zero; an uncircumcised man who keeps the law's requirements has the thing the sign was for, without the sign itself. The reversal in v27 is stark on purpose: the uncircumcised outsider becomes the judge, and the circumcised insider the one judged.

Verses 28–29 · the hardest sentence Paul wrote to his own people

⚠ This is the sentence chapter 1's own closing note called out by name in advance as the hardest thing Paul says to a Jewish reader in this letter: the real Jew is one inwardly, and real circumcision is of the heart, “by spirit, not by letter.”

“By spirit, not by letter” (v29, en pneumati ou grammati) becomes one of Paul's standing pairs, restated at 7:6 and 2 Corinthians 3:6. ⚠ Whether “spirit” here means the Holy Spirit specifically or the inward disposition generally is a real question the Greek's lowercase-or-capital ambiguity cannot settle by itself. RV60 keeps it lowercase and general, “en espíritu, no en letra”; NVI capitalizes and personifies it, “la que realiza el Espíritu” — the very interpretive choice this note is flagging, made silently inside a single Spanish translation's punctuation. This translation keeps the ambiguity, lowercase, unresolved.

The chapter that opened with a Gentile indictment closes by handing the hardest word to the reader who thought this letter was about somebody else.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: "without a defense" for anapologētous (v1), matching chapter 1's rendering rather than varying it; "no partiality" for prosōpolēmpsia (v11), the modern sense rather than the KJV-era idiom; "a law to themselves" and "written on their hearts" (vv14-15), kept literal rather than paraphrased; and "by spirit, not by letter" (v29), left lowercase and ambiguous rather than resolved toward either reading.

Echoes paid. The trap — chapter 1's closing note named this chapter's opening verse as the single most important thing to carry forward, and v1 is that promise cashed. "To the Jew first and also to the Greek" (vv9-10) — the identical phrase from 1:16, now cutting toward judgment rather than toward the gospel's advance. And the closing sentence on spirit and letter (v29) — flagged in advance by chapter 1's own preview as the hardest line Paul writes to his own people in this letter.

Echoes planted. "To the Jew first" (vv9-10), repeated now twice in two chapters, building toward the unfinished argument of chapters 9-11. By spirit, not by letter (v29), restated at 7:6 and in 2 Corinthians 3:6. And the circumcision of the heart (v29), which chapter 4 will need before it can argue that Abraham was justified before he was ever circumcised at all.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether "spirit" in v29 names the Holy Spirit specifically or an inward disposition generally — RV60 and NVI silently make opposite calls in their own punctuation. And the precise Old Testament source behind v24's citation, Isaiah 52:5 or Ezekiel 36:20-23 or both at once — the library sets out the candidates and does not choose.

Next in this book: the argument chapter 2 has been building toward — if circumcision and law-keeping do not finally save, what advantage is there in being a Jew at all?
