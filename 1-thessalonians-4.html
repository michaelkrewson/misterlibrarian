Instructions on holiness, brotherly love and a quiet working life, and then comfort for the grieving: the dead in Christ will rise first, and the living will be snatched up with them to meet the Lord.
c. AD 50–51, from Corinth, a few months after the founding of the congregation; some of its members had already died.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · walking to please God, and overflowing still more

“How you ought to walk and to please God — as you are in fact walking — you overflow still more.” “Finally” turns the letter from memory to instruction, though two chapters remain. “Walk” and “please God” take up 2:12, “walk worthily of God,” and 2:4, “not as pleasing men but God”; “overflow” is the verb of the prayer at 3:12. The Byzantine text lacks “as you are in fact walking,” and so the KJV: “Furthermore then we beseech you, brethren, and exhort you by the Lord Jesus”; the ASV reads “even as ye do walk”; the NIV reads “As for other matters.”

Verses 3–8 · sanctification, “his own vessel,” the brother wronged, and the Holy Spirit given

“This is the will of God, your sanctification: that you keep away from sexual immorality.” Hagiasmos, “sanctification,” the making holy, stands three times in vv3–7, as at 1 Corinthians 1:30 and Romans 6:19 (dictionary); the prayer of 3:13 asked for hearts “blameless in holiness.” Porneia is the “sexual immorality” of Colossians 3:5 (dictionary). The KJV reads “even your sanctification, that ye should abstain from fornication”; the NIV reads “that you should avoid sexual immorality.”

⚠ “That each one of you know how to acquire his own vessel in sanctification and honor.” Ktasthai is the infinitive of Acts 8:20, “to acquire the gift of God” (dictionary); skeuos is a vessel, a jar, any article (dictionary). Both main readings are old. Some take the vessel as one’s own body, to be brought under control, as Paul speaks of “clay jars” at 2 Corinthians 4:7. Others take it as a wife, to be taken in marriage, comparing 1 Peter 3:7, “a weaker vessel,” and the sense “acquire”; against it, some object that it would speak of a wife as an article. This page sets out both and does not choose, and keeps “vessel” so that neither is decided for the reader. The KJV reads “how to possess his vessel in sanctification and honour”; the NIV reads “learn to control your own body”; the TLB reads “so that each of you will marry in holiness and honor.”

“Not in the passion of desire, like the nations that do not know God.” Pathos is the “passion” of Colossians 3:5. “The nations that do not know God” is how the Thessalonians themselves once were: Galatians 4:8, “when you did not know God.” The KJV reads “Not in the lust of concupiscence”; the ASV reads “not in the passion of lust”; the NWT 1984 reads “not in covetous sexual appetite.”

“… overstep and take advantage of his brother in the matter, because the Lord is an avenger.” Hyperbainō, “overstep,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); pleonekteō is “take advantage,” as at 2 Corinthians 7:2. “The matter” is read as the matter in hand, so that adultery is a wrong done to a brother, or as business dealings, with the Vulgate’s negotium. “Avenger” is the ekdikos of Romans 13:4. Readers take “the Lord” as the Lord Jesus of vv1–2 or as God; this translation keeps “Lord.” The KJV reads “go beyond and defraud his brother in any matter”; the Douay reads “nor circumvent his brother in business”; the NWT 1984 reads “because Jehovah is one who exacts punishment.”

“The one who sets this aside sets aside not a man but God, who also gives his Holy Spirit to you.” “This” is supplied; the Greek says only “the one who sets aside.” Atheteō is “set aside,” as at Galatians 2:21, “I do not set aside the grace of God,” and Mark 7:9. “Gives” is present; the Byzantine text reads “gave,” and so the KJV: “who hath also given unto us his holy Spirit”; the ASV reads “who giveth his Holy Spirit unto you”; the NWT 1984 reads “who puts his holy spirit in YOU.”

Verses 9–12 · taught by God to love, a quiet life, and work with your hands

“About brotherly love … you yourselves are taught by God to love one another.” Philadelphia, “brotherly love,” is the word of Romans 12:10 (dictionary). Theodidaktoi, “taught by God,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); the Greek of Isaiah 54:13 says that all Zion’s sons will be “taught of God”. They show it “in all Macedonia” (encyclopedia). The KJV reads “for ye yourselves are taught of God to love one another”; the TLB reads “For God himself is teaching you to love one another.”

“Make it your aim to live quietly, and to attend to your own affairs, and to work with your hands.” Philotimeomai, “make it one’s aim,” the love of honor turned to ambition, stands in three verses: Romans 15:20, 2 Corinthians 5:9 and this one (dictionary); here the ambition is to be quiet. Hēsychazō, “live quietly,” is the verb of Acts 11:18, “they quieted down” (dictionary). Working with their hands is what the missionaries did, “night and day” (2:9). Readers ask why the instruction was needed: some connect it with idleness in expectation of the end, which 2 Thessalonians 3:6–12 addresses; others with ordinary life in a society of patrons and dependants. Nestle–Aland and the Byzantine text read “your own hands,” and so the KJV: “that ye study to be quiet, and to do your own business, and to work with your own hands”; the NIV reads “make it your ambition to lead a quiet life.”

“So that you may walk properly toward those outside.” Euschēmonōs, “properly,” as at Romans 13:13, “Let us walk properly, as in the day”; “those outside” as at Colossians 4:5. The KJV reads “That ye may walk honestly toward them that are without”; the NWT 1984 reads “walking decently as regards people outside.”

Verses 13–15 · those who are sleeping, grief without hope, and a word of the Lord

“About those who are sleeping, so that you do not grieve as the rest do, who have no hope.” Koimaomai, “fall asleep,” is the word for the dead at 1 Corinthians 15:6 and 1 Corinthians 15:20, “the firstfruits of those who have fallen asleep.” Paul does not forbid grief, only grief of that kind. “Having no hope” is said of the readers’ past at Ephesians 2:12; the Greek poet Theocritus, in the third century BC, has a herdsman say, “hopes are among the living; the dead are without hope” (Idyll 4.42). The Byzantine text reads the perfect, “those who have fallen asleep,” and so the KJV: “concerning them which are asleep, that ye sorrow not, even as others which have no hope”; the NWT 1984 reads “those who are sleeping [in death],” and the NIV reads “those who sleep in death,” each adding the explanation.

“God will bring with him those who have fallen asleep through Jesus.” “Through Jesus” can go with “fallen asleep,” those who died as his, as at 1 Corinthians 15:18, “fallen asleep in Christ”; or with “will bring,” God bringing them through Jesus. This translation keeps the Greek order. The KJV reads “them also which sleep in Jesus will God bring with him”; the NWT 1984 reads “those who have fallen asleep [in death] through Jesus God will bring with him”; the NIV reads “God will bring with Jesus those who have fallen asleep in him.”

“By a word of the Lord: that we who are living, who remain until the presence of the Lord.” Readers take “a word of the Lord” as a saying of Jesus not recorded in the Gospels, as Jesus’ teaching such as Matthew 24:31 in Paul’s own words, or as a word given by the risen Lord through a prophet. The NWT 1984 reads “by Jehovah’s word”; this translation keeps “Lord,” since the Lord of these verses is the Lord Jesus who comes down in v16. “We who are living” counts the writers among the living: readers take it that Paul expected to be alive, or that he speaks for whoever is alive then, as at 1 Corinthians 15:51, “we will not all fall asleep,” beside 2 Corinthians 4:14, “will raise us also.” “Go ahead of” is phthanō (dictionary). The KJV reads “shall not prevent them which are asleep,” in the old sense of “go before”; the ASV reads “shall in no wise precede them that are fallen asleep.”

Verses 16–18 · a shout of command, the archangel and the trumpet, snatched up to meet the Lord, and the word “rapture”

“With a shout of command, with an archangel’s voice and with God’s trumpet.” Keleusma, a call of command, the word for an order shouted to troops or to rowers, stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). Archangelos stands in two verses: this one and Jude 1:9, “Michael the archangel” (dictionary). The trumpet sounded at Sinai (Exodus 19:16) and sounds at 1 Corinthians 15:52, “the last trumpet,” and Matthew 24:31. “The dead in Christ will rise first”: the living have no head start. The KJV reads “with a shout, with the voice of the archangel, and with the trump of God”; the NWT 1984 reads “with a commanding call”; the NIV reads “with a loud command.”

⚠ “We … will be snatched up together with them in clouds to meet the Lord in the air.” Harpazō is “snatch up,” as at 2 Corinthians 12:2, where Paul was “snatched up as far as the third heaven” (dictionary). The Latin Vulgate renders it rapiemur, “we shall be caught up,” and from that Latin root comes English “rapture.” The teaching that believers will be snatched away before a time of tribulation, with the Lord’s visible coming to follow later, was spread in the 1830s chiefly by John Nelson Darby and the Plymouth Brethren and then widely by the Scofield Reference Bible of 1909; those who hold it find it here. Others read one public coming, and point to apantēsis, “to meet,” the word for people going out from a city to meet an arriving guest and escort him in, as the virgins go out to the bridegroom at Matthew 25:6 and the brothers come out from Rome to meet Paul at Acts 28:15 (dictionary). This page sets out both and does not choose. Clouds go with him at Acts 1:9, “a cloud took him up,” and Mark 13:26. The KJV reads “shall be caught up together with them in the clouds, to meet the Lord in the air”; the NWT 1984 reads “be caught away in clouds to meet the Lord in the air”; the Douay reads “to meet Christ, into the air,” following the Vulgate’s obviam Christo.

“And so we will always be with the Lord. So comfort one another with these words.” The end of the passage is not a timetable but company: to be “with the Lord,” as Paul wishes “to be with Christ” at Philippians 1:23. “Comfort” is parakaleō (dictionary), and the passage was written to be read to the grieving. The KJV reads “Wherefore comfort one another with these words”; the NIV reads “Therefore encourage one another with these words”; the TLB reads “So comfort and encourage each other with this news.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “walk” and “please God,” as at 2:12 and 2:4; “overflow,” as at 3:12; “sanctification,” as at 1 Corinthians 1:30; “sexual immorality” and “passion,” as at Colossians 3:5; “vessel,” undecided, as at Romans 9:21; “acquire,” as at Acts 8:20; “take advantage,” as at 2 Corinthians 7:2; “avenger,” as at Romans 13:4; “set aside,” as at Galatians 2:21; “brotherly love,” as at Romans 12:10; “make it your aim,” as at 2 Corinthians 5:9; “properly,” as at Romans 13:13; “fallen asleep,” as at 1 Corinthians 15:20; “presence,” as at 2:19; “snatched up,” as at 2 Corinthians 12:2; “to meet,” as at Acts 28:15; and “comfort” for parakaleō.

Echoes paid. The holiness of 1 Thessalonians 3:13, now spelled out; the love for one another of 1 Thessalonians 3:12; work night and day from 1 Thessalonians 2:9; the holy ones who come with the Lord at 1 Thessalonians 3:13, and the dead God brings with him; the presence of 1 Thessalonians 2:19; waiting for his Son “from the heavens” at 1 Thessalonians 1:10, and the Latin rapiemur promised there.

Echoes planted. “About the times and the seasons” at 5:1, again with “no need to write”; the day of the Lord “like a thief in the night” at 5:2; and “comfort one another” again at 5:11.
