“I will bless Jehovah at all times”: an alphabetic psalm of David, headed by his escape from the Philistine king by feigning madness, in which the afflicted man calls and is heard, the angel of Jehovah camps around those who fear him, the hearer is told “Taste and see that Jehovah is good”, and of the righteous man “not one of his bones is broken”.
The heading sets it after David’s flight to Gath (1 Samuel 21:11–16 in the Hebrew numbering), in the years he was a fugitive from Saul, before his reign; the psalm itself names no event.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verse 1 · “When he changed his behavior before Abimelech” — David at Gath, a word for taste and good sense, and an alphabet with a letter missing

The heading and its story. “Of David, when he disguised his sanity before Abimelech, who drove him out, and he went away”: the heading is verse 1 in the Hebrew, so every verse on this page is one number ahead of an English Bible. It points to the story of David at Gath, where he “changed his good sense in their eyes, and played the madman in their hands”, scrawling on the doors of the gate and letting his spittle run down his beard (1 Samuel 21:14 in the Hebrew numbering). Be-shannoto et-ta‘mo, “when he changed his ta‘am”, repeats that story’s words exactly. Ta‘am is taste, and then the judgment a person tastes things with, good sense; and verse 9 uses its verb, “Taste and see that Jehovah is good”. The NWT 1984 has “at the time of his disguising his sanity”; the ASV “when he changed his behavior before Abimelech, who drove him away, and he departed”; the Douay, following the Greek, in which he changes his face, “when he changed his countenance before Achimelech”.

Abimelech or Achish? The story calls the king of Gath Achish; the heading calls him Abimelech, “my father is king”, the name of the king of Gerar in the days of Abraham and Isaac (Abimelech, Genesis 20 and 26, already on these pages). Readings differ: some take Abimelech as a title of Philistine kings, as the Geneva does in its own summary, “which was a general name to all the Kings of the Philistines”; others take the heading to preserve a different form of the tradition. The Greek reads Abimelech, and the Latin Achimelech. Two words of the story come back in the psalm: David “played the madman”, va-yitholel, and in verse 3 his soul “will make its boast”, tithallel, from the same letters; and Achish asks “Do I lack madmen?”, with the verb of verse 11, “will not lack any good thing”.

An alphabet. From verse 2 the psalm is an acrostic: each verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, marked at the start of each verse, from alef in verse 2 to tav in verse 22. Two things break the pattern. There is no line for vav, the sixth letter: he in verse 6 is followed by zayin in verse 7. And after tav comes one more line, verse 23, beginning with pe, podeh, “redeems”. Psalm 25 has the same two features, and its extra last line begins “Redeem Israel, O God”, pedeh (Psalm 25:22). One proposal, reported here rather than adopted, notes that the first letter, the middle one (lamed, verse 12) and the last spell alef, the name of the first letter.

Verses 2–7 · “I will bless Jehovah at all times” — a boast, terrors, radiant faces, and “this poor man cried”

At all times. “I will bless Jehovah at all times; his praise will be continually in my mouth”: the verb of “Bless Jehovah, O my soul” (Psalm 103:1, already on these pages), and tehillah, praise. “In Jehovah my soul will make its boast; let the humble hear and be glad”: tithallel, praise turned back on oneself, a boast; and the anavim, the humble, as in “the humble will possess the land” (Psalm 37:11, already on these pages). The ASV has “I will bless Jehovah at all times: His praise shall continually be in my mouth”; the NIV “I will extol”; the NWT 1984 “In Jehovah my soul will make its boast”.

Terrors. “Magnify Jehovah with me, and let us exalt his name together”: the imperative is plural, said to the humble of the verse before. “I sought Jehovah, and he answered me, and from all my terrors he delivered me”: darash, to seek or inquire of, and natsal, to snatch away. The terrors are megurot, from gur, to dread; the same letters also make a verb “to sojourn”, and the Greek read “from all my sojournings”, a fitting word for a refugee in Gath. The ASV has “And delivered me from all my fears”; the NWT 1984 “And out of all my frights he delivered me”; the Douay “from all my troubles”.

Radiant faces. “They looked to him and became radiant, and their faces will not be abashed”: naharu, to shine, to beam; and yechparu, from the root of being abashed that shares its letters with chafar, to dig. The Greek reads both verbs as commands, “Come to him and be enlightened”, and makes the faces “yours”. The NWT 1984 has “They looked to him and became radiant”; the ASV “They looked unto him, and were radiant”; the KJV “and were lightened”; the Douay “Come ye to him and be enlightened: and your faces shall not be confounded”.

This afflicted man. “This afflicted man called, and Jehovah heard, and saved him from all his distresses”: zeh ani, “this afflicted one”, the speaker pointing at himself as an example. Ani is the afflicted and the poor in one word (oni); the distresses are tsarot, tight places. The NWT 1984 has “This afflicted one called”; the ASV “This poor man cried, and Jehovah heard him, And saved him out of all his troubles”.

Verses 8–11 · “The angel of Jehovah camps around” and “Taste and see that Jehovah is good” — a camp of angels, a taste, and lions that go hungry

A camp around them. “The angel of Jehovah camps around those who fear him, and rescues them”: choneh, from chanah, the root of machaneh, a camp. When the angels of God met Jacob, he said “This is God’s camp!” and named the place Mahanaim (Genesis 32:3, already on these pages). The mal’akh is a messenger; va-yechalletsem, “and rescues them”, is to draw out. The ASV has “The angel of Jehovah encampeth round about them that fear him, And delivereth them”; the NWT 1984 “is camping all around those fearing him”.

Taste and see. “Taste and see that Jehovah is good; happy is the man who takes refuge in him”: ta‘amu, the verb of the heading’s ta‘am. The man who once made his good sense look like madness now asks others to taste. The Greek has chrēstos, “kind”, and Peter uses those words of Christ: “if you have tasted that the Lord is kind” (1 Peter 2:3, already on these pages). Ashrei is “happy”, the Psalter’s first word; the man is a gever, a strong man; and to take refuge is chasah, the verb behind machseh, a refuge. The ASV has “Oh taste and see that Jehovah is good: Blessed is the man that taketh refuge in him”; the NWT 1984 “Happy is the able-bodied man that takes refuge in him”; the Douay, from the Latin, “O taste, and see that the Lord is sweet”.

No lack. “Fear Jehovah, you his holy ones, for those who fear him lack nothing”: qedoshav, his holy ones, and the root chaser, to lack, the verb of “Jehovah is my shepherd; I lack nothing” (Psalm 23:1, already on these pages). “Young lions go in want and hunger, but those who seek Jehovah will not lack any good thing”: kefirim, lions in their prime, who can still go hungry. The Greek reads “the rich” in place of the lions, and so the Douay has “The rich have wanted, and have suffered hunger”. The ASV has “The young lions do lack, and suffer hunger; But they that seek Jehovah shall not want any good thing”; the NWT 1984 “The maned young lions themselves have had little on hand and gone hungry”.

Verses 12–17 · “Come, ye children, hearken unto me” — the fear of Jehovah, “Who is the man who desires life?”, and the lines Peter quotes

A teacher’s voice. “Come, children, listen to me; I will teach you the fear of Jehovah”: banim, sons, the address of a wisdom teacher, as in “Hear, my son, your father’s discipline” (Proverbs 1:8, already on these pages). The second half of the psalm turns from thanks to teaching here, at lamed, and the verb of the line, alammedkhem, “I will teach you”, is spelled with the same three letters as the letter’s own name; the lesson is yir’at YHVH, the fear of Jehovah. The ASV has “Come, ye children, hearken unto me: I will teach you the fear of Jehovah”; the NWT 1984 “Come, YOU sons, listen to me”.

Who wants life? “Who is the man who desires life, who loves days in which to see good?”: the question is answered in four commands. “Keep your tongue from evil, and your lips from speaking deceit”: mirmah, the deceit with which Jacob took his brother’s blessing. “Turn away from evil and do good; seek peace and pursue it”: shalom, and radaf, the verb for chasing an enemy, here turned on peace. The ASV has “What man is he that desireth life, And loveth many days, that he may see good?”; the NWT 1984 “Who is the man that is delighting in life”; the ASV again “Depart from evil, and do good; Seek peace, and pursue it”; the NIV “Turn from evil and do good; seek peace and pursue it”.

The eyes and the face. “The eyes of Jehovah are toward the righteous, and his ears toward their cry for help. The face of Jehovah is against those who do evil, to cut off the memory of them from the earth”: shav‘atam, their cry for help. Peter quotes these lines from the Greek, from “who wants to love life” to “the face of Jehovah is against those who do evil”, and stops before the cutting off (1 Peter 3:10–12, already on these pages); there the cry for help is deēsis, “supplication”, the Greek word in this verse. The NWT 1984 has “And his ears are toward their cry for help”; the ASV “The eyes of Jehovah are toward the righteous, And his ears are open unto their cry”, and “To cut off the remembrance of them from the earth”; the NIV “to blot out their name from the earth”.

Verses 18–21 · “Jehovah is near to the broken of heart” — a cry with no named subject, many evils, and “not one of them is broken”

Who cried out? “They cried out, and Jehovah heard, and from all their distresses he delivered them”: the Hebrew names no subject, and the nearest plural is “those who do evil” in the verse before. The Greek supplies “the righteous”, from verse 16, and most of the shelf follows. The ASV has “The righteous cried, and Jehovah heard”; the Douay “The just cried”; the NWT 1984 keeps the Hebrew, “They cried out, and Jehovah himself heard”.

Broken and crushed. “Jehovah is near to the broken of heart, and he saves the crushed in spirit”: nishberei-lev and dakke’ei-ruach, the pair of Psalm 51, “The sacrifices of God are a broken spirit; a heart broken and crushed” (Psalm 51:19, already on these pages). The verb is shavar, to break. The ASV has “Jehovah is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart, And saveth such as are of a contrite spirit”; the NWT 1984 “those that are broken at heart; And those who are crushed in spirit he saves”; the NIV “close to the brokenhearted”.

Many evils, no broken bone. “Many are the evils that befall the righteous, but from them all Jehovah delivers him”: ra‘ot, the word for the evil the psalm tells its pupils to turn from (verses 14, 15, 17), here for what happens to a good man. He is not spared the evils; he is delivered from them. “He keeps all his bones; not one of them is broken”: the same verb as the broken of heart in verse 19, so that hearts may break and bones will not. The etsem is a bone; John reads “Not one of his bones will be broken” at the cross (John 19:36, already on these pages), where the law of the Passover lamb, “you shall not break a bone of it” (Exodus 12:46, already on these pages), stands behind it as well. The ASV has “Many are the afflictions of the righteous”, and “He keepeth all his bones: Not one of them is broken”; the NWT 1984 “Many are the calamities of the righteous one”; the Douay, with the Greek plural, “keepeth all their bones”.

Verses 22–23 · “Evil will put the wicked to death” and “Jehovah redeems the life of his servants” — evil as executioner, guilt, and the extra line

Evil that kills. “Evil will put the wicked to death, and those who hate the righteous will be held guilty”: the evil the righteous are delivered from in verse 20 is the evil that kills the wicked here. Yeshamu is from asham, guilt, to be guilty and to bear what guilt costs. The KJV has “Evil shall slay the wicked”, and at the end of the verse “shall be desolate”; the ASV “And they that hate the righteous shall be condemned”; the NWT 1984 “will be held guilty”; the Douay “The death of the wicked is very evil”, after the Greek.

The extra line. “Jehovah redeems the life of his servants, and none who take refuge in him will be held guilty”: the line after tav, beginning with pe, podeh, from padah, to buy back at a price. The life is nefesh. The same verb closes both verses: those who hate the righteous are held guilty, and those who take refuge are not, and the refuge is the word of verse 9. The ASV has “Jehovah redeemeth the soul of his servants; And none of them that take refuge in him shall be condemned”; the NWT 1984 “Jehovah is redeeming the soul of his servants”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: the heading counted as verse 1, as the Hebrew counts it; “taste” in verse 9 kept beside the heading’s ta‘am; “the humble” for anavim, as in Psalm 37:11; “lack nothing” for chaser, as in Psalm 23:1; “deceit” and “cry for help” in the lines 1 Peter 3 quotes; “evil” for ra and ra‘ah throughout, including the “evils that befall the righteous”; “broken” for shavar in verses 19 and 21 alike; “held guilty” for asham in verses 22 and 23 alike; and the alphabet marked letter by letter, with no vav and an extra pe, as the Hebrew has it.

Echoes paid. “Bless Jehovah” from Psalm 103; the humble of Psalm 37; God’s camp at Mahanaim (Genesis 32); the Abimelech of Genesis 20 and 26; “I lack nothing” from Psalm 23; Jacob’s deceit (Genesis 27); the broken and crushed heart of Psalm 51; the Passover bone of Exodus 12; and this psalm heard again in 1 Peter 2:3, 1 Peter 3:10–12 and John 19:36.

Echoes planted. David at Gath, playing the madman before Achish (1 Samuel 21:11–16 in the Hebrew numbering); and the other alphabet with no vav and an extra pe, Psalm 25. Neither is translated here yet.
