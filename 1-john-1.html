The letter opens like the Gospel of John — 'that which was from the beginning… the word of life' — but reaches out and TOUCHES it: 'our hands handled', against those who denied Christ came in the flesh. Then its first declaration, 'God is light, and in him is no darkness at all', the blood that continually cleanses, and the honest confession of sin set against the opponents' three proud denials.
c. AD 85–95, on the traditional reading — from Ephesus, by the same hand as the Gospel and the two shorter letters, in old age, to churches torn by secessionists who had 'gone out from us' (2:19). ⚠ Authorship is debated with the whole Johannine corpus: tradition names the apostle John son of Zebedee; many critics posit a 'Johannine school' or an elder distinct from the apostle. The library reports the positions and casts no vote. The letter is undatable with precision; it clearly answers a crisis already unfolding, the proto-docetic denial of Christ's flesh.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · a prologue that has handled what it preaches

⚠ The letter opens like the Gospel of John — and then puts hands on it. “That which was from the beginning…” deliberately re-sounds the Gospel’s “In the beginning was the Word” (John 1:1). But where the Gospel soars, the letter reaches out and touches: a rising chain of the senses — we have heard, we have seen with our eyes, we looked upon, and our hands handled. ⚠ The last verb, psēlaphō (“to grope, to feel, to handle”), is the risen Christ’s own word — “handle me and see” (Luke 24:39) — and it is chosen as a hammer against the secessionists this letter fights, who denied that the divine Christ had truly come in flesh. The Word of life was not a phantom; the writer felt him under his hands.

“The WORD of life… the life was made manifest.” The Greek opens on a series of neuter pronouns — “that which” (ho), not “he who” — so the object is at once the message about life and the person who is life, and the sentence deliberately refuses to separate them. ⚠ It is grammatically the roughest opening in the New Testament: the main verb does not arrive until verse 3, and verse 2 breaks in as a parenthesis, the writer unable to say “the word of life” without stopping to add that “the life” is a person who “was with the Father” — the same pros ton patera, “with the Father,” as John 1:1’s “the Word was with God.”

Verses 3–4 · fellowship, and a joy filled to the brim

“So that you too may have FELLOWSHIP with us.” Koinōnia — sharing, having-in-common, partnership — is the aim of the whole letter, and the writer builds a chain: the eyewitnesses share what they saw so the readers may share with them, and that shared life is itself a sharing “with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ.” ⚠ The horizontal and the vertical are one thing: to be joined to the apostolic testimony is to be joined to God. Against a splinter-group that had “gone out from us” (2:19), the word is pointed — there is one fellowship, and it runs through the witnesses.

⚠ “So that OUR joy may be complete.” A real textual split: the earliest and best manuscripts read “our joy” (hēmōn) — the writers’ own joy is completed when the readers come in — while a large part of the tradition reads “your joy” (hymōn), the two words differing by a single letter. This translation follows the critical text’s “our.” KJV “that your joy may be full”; NIV “to make our joy complete.” The verb is peplērōmenē, “filled full,” the same fullness Jesus promises in the upper room (John 15:11).

Verses 5–7 · God is light, and the blood that cleanses

“God is LIGHT, and in him is no darkness at all.” The letter’s first great declaration — ho theos phōs estin — and one of three “God is” statements the Johannine writings give (with “God is spirit,” John 4:24, and “God is love,” 4:8). ⚠ Note it is not “God is a light” but light itself, absolute, with a double negative piled on for force: “darkness in him there is not, none” (ouk… oudemia). And “light” here is moral before it is metaphysical — it means holiness and truth, so that to “walk in the light” is to live truthfully, and the test of fellowship is conduct, not claim.

⚠ “The BLOOD of Jesus his Son CLEANSES us from all sin.” The verb katharizei is present tense — a continual cleansing, not a one-time scrub — and it lands in the same breath as walking in the light, which cuts against any idea that light means sinlessness. ⚠ The one who walks in the light is not the one who has no sin (verse 8 forbids that) but the one whose sin is being continually washed. It is the letter’s answer to the perfectionists: fellowship is for the honest, not the flawless. NWT “cleanses us from all sin”; KJV “cleanseth us from all sin.”

Verses 8–10 · three things the liars say, and the honesty that heals

⚠ Three “if we say” claims — the slogans of the opponents, quoted and demolished. The writer sets up three boasts, almost certainly the secessionists’ own words: “we have fellowship… yet walk in darkness” (v6), “we have no sin” (v8), “we have not sinned” (v10). Each is met with a blunt verdict — we lie, we deceive ourselves, we make him a liar. ⚠ The people denying their sin were evidently the “spiritual” ones who felt beyond it; the letter calls the denial not humility but self-deception, and worse, a charge of falsehood laid against God, “whose word is not in us.”

“If we CONFESS our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive.” Against the three denials stands one admission. Homologeō means to “say the same thing” as God about our sin — to agree with his verdict rather than argue it — and the promise attached is startlingly legal: God is “faithful and just” (dikaios) to forgive, not merely merciful. ⚠ Forgiveness is owed to his own faithfulness and secured by the just satisfaction the next verse names (the blood, 2:2’s propitiation) — so that pardon is an act of God’s righteousness, not a relaxing of it. “To cleanse us from all unrighteousness” closes the paragraph where verse 7 opened it: the same cleansing, now tied to the confessing mouth. NIV “faithful and just and will forgive.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: the neuter “that which” of the prologue, message and person held together as the Greek holds them; “handled” for psēlaphō, the anti-docetic touch; “the word of life” for logos tēs zōēs, left as ambiguous as it stands; “fellowship” for koinōnia throughout; “God is light” with the piled double negative kept; the present-tense “cleanses” for the continual katharizei; and “confess” for homologeō, to say the same thing as God. The “our joy” of the critical text is printed with the “your joy” variant flagged.

Echoes paid. The “in the beginning… the Word” of John 1:1, and the “with the Father” that matches John’s “the Word was with God”; the “life” that “was the light of men” (John 1:4); the risen Christ’s “handle me and see” (Luke 24:39); and the “blood” that frees from sin, met from the other end at Revelation 1:5.

Echoes planted. The “antichrists” who “went out from us” (2:18-19), the secessionists whose slogans this chapter is already answering; the “advocate” and the “propitiation for our sins” (2:1-2) that the “faithful and just to forgive” of 1:9 leans on; the test of walking “as he walked” (2:6); and the letter’s summit, “God is love” (4:8, 16), the third of the great “God is” sayings after “God is light” here.

1 John is new to the library — the warmest and most searching of the letters, written to a church torn by people who had walked out of it, and the source of “God is light” and “God is love.” It reads less like a letter than a sermon: no greeting, no name, no address, just a voice that has seen and touched what it preaches. Next in this book: chapter 2 — “my little children, I write these things so that you may not sin; but if anyone does sin, we have an ADVOCATE with the Father”; the new-and-old commandment to love; the “antichrists” who deny the Son; and “do not love the world.”
