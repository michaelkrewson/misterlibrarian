Unable to bear the separation, Paul stays at Athens and sends Timothy back to Thessalonica; Timothy returns with good news of their faith and love, and the chapter ends in a prayer that God and the Lord Jesus will guide the way back and strengthen their hearts for his presence.
c. AD 50–51, from Corinth, soon after Timothy's return (Acts 18:5).

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · left at Athens alone, and Timothy, God’s co-worker

“When we could bear it no longer, we thought it good to be left behind at Athens alone.” Stegō is to cover, to hold out, the verb of 1 Corinthians 9:12, “we endure everything” (dictionary). Acts tells it differently: Paul waits at Athens for Silas and Timothy (Acts 17:16), and they rejoin him only in Corinth, coming “down from Macedonia” (Acts 18:5) (encyclopedia). Readers take it that Timothy did reach Athens and was sent back, which Acts does not mention, or that Acts has shortened the story. Whether the “we” is Paul alone or Paul and Silvanus is also read both ways; in v5 he changes to “I.” The KJV reads “when we could no longer forbear, we thought it good to be left at Athens alone”; the NWT 1984 reads “when we could bear it no longer, we saw good to be left alone in Athens.”

⚠ “Our brother and God’s co-worker in the gospel of Christ.” The copies differ. The text this translation follows and Nestle–Aland read synergon tou theou, “God’s co-worker,” with the first hand of Codex Claromontanus, minuscule 33 and some Old Latin copies; Codex Sinaiticus, Codex Alexandrinus and the Vulgate read “God’s servant”; Codex Vaticanus has only “co-worker”; and the Byzantine text joins the two, “servant of God and our co-worker.” Readers who prefer “co-worker” hold that copyists softened a bold phrase; the same phrase stands at 1 Corinthians 3:9, “we are God’s co-workers” (dictionary). The KJV reads “and minister of God, and our fellowlabourer”; the ASV reads “and God’s minister in the gospel of Christ”; the NIV reads “co-worker in God’s service.” “To strengthen you” is stērizō, as at Romans 1:11; it returns in v13 (dictionary).

Verses 3–5 · shaken by afflictions, appointed for this, and the tempter

“Shaken by these afflictions.” Sainesthai stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). The verb is used of a dog wagging its tail, and so of fawning and coaxing; readers take it here as “be shaken, disturbed,” or as “be coaxed away,” won over by soft words in a hard time. “Afflictions” is the thlipsis of 1:6, “having received the word in much affliction” (dictionary). The KJV reads “be moved by these afflictions”; the NWT 1984 reads “be swayed by these tribulations”; the NIV reads “unsettled by these trials.”

“We are appointed for this … we told you beforehand that we were going to suffer affliction.” Keimetha, “we are appointed,” is the verb of Philippians 1:16, “I am appointed for the defense of the gospel.” At Acts 14:22 Paul and Barnabas tell the new disciples that “through many tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of God,” with the same noun. The KJV reads “we told you before that we should suffer tribulation”; the NWT 1984 reads “we used to tell YOU beforehand that we were destined to suffer tribulation.”

“For fear that somehow the tempter had tempted you and our labor would come to be empty.” “I too”: Paul speaks for himself again, as at 2:18, “I, Paul.” Ho peirazōn, “the tempter,” is the title of Matthew 4:3, “And the tempter came” (dictionary); in 2:18 it was Satan who cut in. “Labor” and “empty” are the words of 2:9 and 2:1. The KJV reads “lest by some means the tempter have tempted you, and our labour be in vain”; the NWT 1984 reads “the Tempter might have tempted YOU”; the TLB reads “I was afraid that perhaps Satan had gotten the best of you.”

Verses 6–10 · Timothy’s good news, “now we live,” and mending what is lacking

“Timothy has come to us from you and has brought us the good news of your faith and love.” “Brought the good news” is euangelizomai, the verb of preaching the gospel, used here of a report about the readers (dictionary). Faith and love are two of the three of 1:3, without hope, which returns at 5:8. Epipotheō, “long for,” is the word of Philippians 1:8, “how I long for you all.” The NWT 1984 reads “the good news about YOUR faithfulness and love,” printing “faithfulness” for pistis in vv5–7 and “faith” in vv2 and 10; the KJV reads “brought us good tidings of your faith and charity”; the NIV reads “has brought good news about your faith and love.”

“We were comforted over you … because now we live, if you stand firm in the Lord.” “Comforted” is parakaleō, the verb of v2’s “exhort,” now said of what the readers did for the writers (dictionary). “Need” is anankē, as at 2 Corinthians 6:4. “Stand firm in the Lord” is the phrase of Philippians 4:1. The KJV reads “For now we live, if ye stand fast in the Lord”; the NIV reads “For now we really live, since you are standing firm in the Lord”; the TLB reads “We can bear anything as long as we know that you remain strong in him.”

“What thanks can we repay to God … praying beyond all measure … to mend what is lacking in your faith?” Antapodidōmi, “repay,” is the verb of Romans 12:19, “I will repay.” Hyperekperissou, a triple compound, “beyond all measure,” stands in three verses: Ephesians 3:20, “far more abundantly than what we ask or think,” this one and 5:13. “Night and day” is the phrase of 2:9: they worked night and day, and they pray night and day. Katartizō, “mend,” is the word for mending nets at Mark 1:19 and for mending a brother caught in a trespass at Galatians 6:1 (dictionary). Hysterēma, “what is lacking,” stands in eight verses, among them Colossians 1:24, “what is lacking of the afflictions of Christ” (dictionary). The KJV reads “and might perfect that which is lacking in your faith”; the NWT 1984 reads “we make more than extraordinary supplications”; the NIV reads “supply what is lacking in your faith.”

Verses 11–13 · God and the Lord Jesus guide our way, love that overflows, and the presence with all his holy ones

⚠ “May our God and Father himself, and our Lord Jesus, guide our way to you.” The verb, kateuthynai, is singular, though it has two subjects; English “may … guide” cannot show the number, and the Spanish of this translation keeps the singular. Readers explain the singular by the grammar, a verb agreeing with the subject nearest it or with a pair taken as one, or as a statement that the Father and the Lord Jesus act as one; the same construction returns at 2 Thessalonians 2:16–17. This page sets out both and does not choose. Kateuthynō, “guide,” stands in three verses, among them Luke 1:79, “to guide our feet into the way of peace” (dictionary). The KJV reads “Now God himself and our Father, and our Lord Jesus Christ, direct our way unto you”; the NWT 1984 reads “direct our way prosperously to YOU”; the NIV reads “clear the way for us to come to you.”

“May the Lord make you increase and overflow in love … blameless in holiness.” “Overflow in love” is the prayer of Philippians 1:9, “that your love may overflow still more and more.” “Blameless” answers 2:10, “how … blamelessly we behaved.” Hagiōsynē, “holiness,” stands in three verses: Romans 1:4, “the Spirit of holiness,” 2 Corinthians 7:1, “perfecting holiness,” and this one (dictionary). The KJV reads “To the end he may stablish your hearts unblameable in holiness”; the NIV reads “May he strengthen your hearts so that you will be blameless and holy.”

⚠ “At the presence of our Lord Jesus with all his holy ones.” “Presence” is the parousia of 2:19 (dictionary). “With all his holy ones” echoes the Greek of Zechariah 14:5, “and Jehovah my God will come, and all the holy ones with him” (yet to come in this translation; the Greek reads kyrios), where the one who comes is Jehovah; here it is the Lord Jesus. Readers take the “holy ones” as angels, as at Mark 8:38, “with the holy angels,” or as believers, “his holy ones” as at Colossians 1:26, or as both. The NWT 1984 reads “with all his holy ones”; the KJV reads “with all his saints”; the TLB reads “with all those who belong to him,” choosing the believers. Nestle–Aland adds “Amen,” and so the Douay: “with all his saints. Amen.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “bear” for stegō, in v1 and v5; “God’s co-worker,” as at 1 Corinthians 3:9; “strengthen,” as at Romans 1:11, in v2 and v13; “exhort,” as at 2:12; “afflictions,” as at 1:6; “appointed,” as at Philippians 1:16; “the tempter,” as at Matthew 4:3; “labor” and “empty,” as at 2:9 and 2:1; “long for,” as at Philippians 1:8; “stand firm in the Lord,” as at Philippians 4:1; “repay,” as at Romans 12:19; “mend,” as at Mark 1:19 and Galatians 6:1; “guide,” as at Luke 1:79; “overflow,” as at Philippians 1:9; and “presence” and “holy ones,” as at 2:19 and Colossians 1:26.

Echoes paid. Satan’s obstacle of 1 Thessalonians 2:18, answered by sending Timothy; Timothy, the co-sender of 1 Thessalonians 1:1; the afflictions of 1 Thessalonians 1:6, foretold; labor and empty from 1 Thessalonians 2:9 and 1 Thessalonians 2:1; faith and love from 1 Thessalonians 1:3; night and day from 1 Thessalonians 2:9; the wish to see their face from 1 Thessalonians 2:17; blameless from 1 Thessalonians 2:10; and the presence of 1 Thessalonians 2:19.

Echoes planted. The holy ones who come with the Lord, and the dead whom God will bring with Jesus, at 4:14; holiness, the theme with which chapter 4 opens its instructions at 4:3; and love for one another, which 4:9 says they need no one to write to them about.

Next in this book: 1 Thessalonians 4, already on these pages. Holiness in the body, love of the brothers, and those who have fallen asleep.
