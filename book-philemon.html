📖 Philemon

Πρὸς Φιλήμονα (Pros Philēmona) — 'To Philemon' — a Christian slave-owner of Colossae, and the only private individual to receive a surviving letter from Paul about a purely personal matter.

Paul's shortest letter and his only truly private one — a single page of pure tact about a runaway slave. Onesimus fled his master Philemon, met Paul in prison and was converted; Paul sends him home carrying this, asking that he be received 'no longer as a slave but… a beloved brother'. It is the New Testament book that has weighed most in the long argument over slavery.

Chapter by chapter

Philemon 1The shortest of Paul’s letters and the only truly private one — a single page of pure tact, written to one man about one man. Onesimus, a slave, has run from his master Philemon (a well-off believer of Colossae), reached Paul in prison, and been converted; now Paul sends him home carrying this. ⚠ The strategy is in the first word: not “apostle” but “PRISONER of Christ Jesus” — Paul has every right to command and spends the whole letter refusing to, appealing “for love’s sake” instead, because a free choice is worth what an order is not. He puns on the slave’s name (Onesimus, “useful”): “once USELESS to you, now USEFUL.” He offers to pay any debt in his own hand — “charge it to my account; I, Paul, will repay” — and reminds Philemon that he owes Paul “your very self.” ⚠ And at the centre is the sentence that has weighed most in the long argument over slavery: receive him “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a BELOVED BROTHER.” Paul never quite commands manumission — readers have debated the silence for two thousand years — but the word “brother” quietly dissolves the word “slave” without ever attacking it head-on. The library sets the sentence down and lets it do its slow work.

About the book

Where it sits
The EIGHTEENTH book of the New Testament, placed last among Paul's letters (after the Pastorals, before Hebrews) — the tail of the collection, and by far its smallest item. That so slight a note was preserved and canonised at all is part of its interest.
Genre
A PERSONAL LETTER — a private note of appeal, not a treatise, and a small classic of persuasion: warm, playful (it puns on the slave's name), and relentlessly gentle, refusing to command what it could command so that the reader will choose it freely. At twenty-five verses it is the shortest book Paul wrote.
Author
PAUL, and undisputed — one of the seven letters no serious scholar questions. Its brevity and private subject are part of the reason: no one forges a single-page note about one man's household slave. Timothy is named as co-sender; the letter says 'I' throughout, in Paul's own voice, and at verse 19 he breaks in to write an IOU 'with my own hand'.
Date written
c. AD 60–62, from the Roman imprisonment, written together with COLOSSIANS — the two letters share Timothy and the whole company of Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke, and Onesimus carried both home. (Some place the shared imprisonment at Caesarea or Ephesus instead of Rome, which would move the date a little.)
Place
Written from prison (vv1, 9, 13, 23) — Rome on the traditional reading — to PHILEMON at Colossae, in the Lycus valley of the province of Asia.
Audience
PHILEMON, a well-off believer of Colossae converted through Paul, whose house held a congregation; with APPHIA (probably his wife) and ARCHIPPUS (probably his son) named alongside, and the house-church made witness. The real subject is ONESIMUS, Philemon's slave, who had run away — perhaps after theft (v18) — reached Paul, and been converted in prison. The letter equips no one and instructs on nothing general; it asks one man for one act of grace.

How it's laid out

1–3Greeting — 'Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus', to Philemon, Apphia, Archippus and the house-church.
4–7Thanksgiving that lays the ground: Philemon's love refreshes the hearts of the saints.
8–16The appeal: not a command but a request, for Onesimus — 'useless once, now useful' — to be received 'no longer as a slave but a beloved brother'.
17–20'Receive him as you would me'; charge any debt to Paul's account, signed in his own hand.
21–25Confidence of 'even more than I say', a request for a guest room, and greetings from the company in the cell.

What it's about

  • SLAVERY — the letter's afterlife: cited on both sides of abolition, and read ever since for what Paul does (send the slave back) and does not (command his freedom); the sentence 'a beloved brother' quietly undoes the institution it never attacks.
  • APPEAL, NOT COMMAND — Paul lays down his apostolic right to order Philemon (vv8-9, 14) so that the good done will be free, not compelled; the whole letter is a study in persuasion over power.
  • USEFULNESS — the pun on Onesimus ('useful'): 'once useless, now useful' (v11), and 'let me have benefit of you' (v20, the same root); a man restored to what his name promised.
  • SUBSTITUTION — 'charge it to my account… I will repay' (vv18-19), long read as a small living picture of the gospel: another's debt assumed and charged to an innocent.
  • BROTHERHOOD across status — Onesimus received 'both in the flesh and in the Lord' (v16); the gospel reordering the most fixed of ancient social bonds.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. The text of Philemon is exceptionally clean; its variants are trivial (a few points of word order, and 'grace' vs 'joy' at v7). ⚠ The letter's real interpretive weight is not textual but ethical and historical: whether Paul is asking Philemon to FREE Onesimus or only to forgive and receive him; whether presbytēs at v9 should be read 'old man' or 'ambassador' (presbeutēs, one letter apart); and what 'even more than I say' (v21) leaves unsaid. None of these is settled by the manuscripts.

Looking forward

Christ is barely named as a subject here and everywhere as the medium: everything happens 'in Christ' and 'in the Lord' — Onesimus is a brother 'in the Lord' (v16), the appeal is made 'in Christ' (vv8, 20), the benefit sought is 'in the Lord' (v20). ⚠ The letter's deepest Christ-content is structural rather than stated: Paul standing surety for a debtor, taking another's wrong onto his own account and signing it in his own hand (vv18-19), has been read since the Fathers as an unplanned parable of the atonement — the mediator who says 'charge it to me'. What is asserted is only the frame, the grace of 'the Lord Jesus Christ' with which it opens and closes; the theology is enacted, not argued.

Where the debates are

Above all the SLAVERY question — what exactly Paul asks, and why he does not simply demand manumission; the letter's use and misuse in the abolition debate. Whether presbytēs (v9) is 'old man' or 'ambassador'. What 'even more than I say' (v21) hints at. Where the imprisonment was (Rome, Caesarea, or Ephesus), which sets the date. And the attractive but unprovable tradition that Onesimus became bishop of Ephesus and gathered Paul's letters. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
