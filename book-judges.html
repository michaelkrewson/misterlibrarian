📖 Judges

שׁוֹפְטִים Shofetim — 'Judges' — but the Hebrew SHOFET is wider than a courtroom judge. A shofet DELIVERS as much as he decides: the 'judges' of this book are chiefly military rescuers raised up to save Israel from oppressors, and only secondarily settlers of disputes. 'Chieftain' or 'deliverer' catches the sense as well as 'judge.'
Κριταί (Kritai) — 'Judges' — the Greek and Latin (Iudices) title, a direct translation of the Hebrew, carrying the same courtroom narrowing that the Hebrew word itself does not intend.

What happened after the conquest: a downward spiral of a people who forgot, a God who kept rescuing them anyway, and a refrain that ends the book — 'in those days there was no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes.'

Chapter by chapter

Judges 1The conquest, told from underneath. It opens well — Israel asks God, Judah goes up, cities fall — and then, tribe by tribe marching north up the map, the same phrase tolls seven times: DID NOT DRIVE OUT. A king mutilated as he mutilated others, a woman who negotiates for water, iron chariots offered as an excuse, and a redeemed people putting the Canaanites to forced labour instead of removing them.Judges 2The angel of Jehovah comes up from Gilgal to Bochim and reads the verdict the first chapter has been building toward: the people weep, Joshua's generation dies, and a generation arises that did not know Jehovah. The judges come, and the cycle begins: deliverance, relapse, and a worse corruption after each judge dies. Then Jehovah keeps the nations in the land on purpose, as a test.Judges 3Israel is left with nations it should have driven out, marries into them, and serves their gods. Othniel is raised up, then Ehud, who kills the fat king of Moab with a hidden sword and walks out past the locked doors. Shamgar kills six hundred with an oxgoad.Judges 4Ehud is dead and Israel does evil again under Jabin of Hazor, whose commander Sisera has nine hundred iron chariots. Deborah, a prophetess, judges under her palm tree and sends for Barak, who will not go to battle without her. The victory goes to a woman: Jael drives a tent peg into Sisera's temple.Judges 5Deborah and Barak sing the victory. The stars fight from heaven, the brook Kishon sweeps Sisera's army away, Jael is blessed above women, and Sisera's mother waits at the window for a chariot that will not come. The song also calls the tribes to account: some came down to the battle, and some stayed by their sheepfolds and ships.Judges 6Midian raids Israel for seven years, and Israel hides in the hills. An angel sits under a terebinth in Ophrah while Gideon threshes wheat in a winepress. He tests God twice with a fleece, and the sign comes true. The chapter ends with the altar of Jehovah-shalom and the name Jerubbaal.Judges 7Gideon sends the fearful home until three hundred remain, chosen by the way they drink. Torches hide in jars and horns sound in the night, and the Midianites turn their swords on each other. Two princes, named for a raven and a wolf, are run down at a rock and a winepress.Judges 8Gideon takes the kings of Midian across the Jordan, punishes Succoth and Penuel for refusing his men bread, captures Zebah and Zalmunna at Karkor, and makes an ephod of the plunder that becomes a snare. He refuses kingship, and when he dies Israel turns to Baal-berith.Judges 9Abimelech, a son of Gideon by a concubine at Shechem, has the seventy sons of Jerubbaal killed on one stone, and the men of Shechem make him king. Jotham, the one who hid, speaks the parable of the trees from Mount Gerizim, and the curse he calls down comes true: fire between Abimelech and Shechem, and a woman's millstone at Thebez.Judges 10Tola judges Israel from Shamir for twenty-three years, and Jair the Gileadite for twenty-two, with thirty sons who ride thirty young donkeys. Israel serves the gods of its neighbours again, and Ammon oppresses it for eighteen years. When Israel confesses and puts away the foreign gods, God's soul is cut short by its misery, and the princes of Gilead ask who will lead the war against Ammon.Judges 11Jephthah the Gileadite, the son of a prostitute, is driven out by his brothers and brought back by the elders of Gilead to lead the war against Ammon. He answers Ammon with the history of the land, makes a vow before Jehovah, and wins. His only child comes out to meet him, and he keeps the vow; the daughters of Israel go each year to commemorate her.Judges 12Ephraim crosses to Zaphon and threatens to burn Jephthah's house over him for not calling them to the war on Ammon. 'I cried to you, and you did not save me': the men of Gilead hold the fords of the Jordan, and one word, Shibboleth, decides who crosses. Then three quieter judges: Ibzan of Bethlehem, Elon the Zebulunite, buried in a town spelled like his own name, and Abdon, whose seventy sons and grandsons ride seventy young donkeys.Judges 13Israel does evil again and gets forty years of the Philistines, and this time nobody cries out. An angel comes instead to Manoah's barren wife, twice, and leaves her husband catching up: a son, a Nazirite of God from the womb, no razor on his head, who will only begin to save Israel. He will not eat, he will not give his name ('It is wonderful'), and he goes up in the flame of the altar. Samson, the sun's boy, is born, and the spirit begins to stir him.Judges 14Samson goes down to Timnah for a Philistine wife because she is right in his eyes. The spirit of Jehovah rushes on him and he tears a lion apart barehanded; later he eats honey out of its carcass and tells nobody. At his seven-day drinking feast he bets thirty companions on a riddle only he can answer, and the guests answer it by threatening his bride with fire. He pays the bet with thirty dead men of Ashkelon, and his wife goes to the best man.Judges 15Samson comes back with a young goat and finds his wife given away, so three hundred foxes carry torches through the Philistine harvest; the Philistines burn the woman and her father. Judah binds its own judge and hands him over at Lehi, the spirit rushes on him, the ropes melt like burnt flax, and a fresh donkey's jawbone makes 'a heap, two heaps' of a thousand men. Then, dying of thirst, he prays for the first time, and God splits a rock for the spring of the one who called.Judges 16Samson carries the gates of Gaza to a hill facing Hebron, then loves Delilah in the valley of Sorek, where each Philistine lord offers her eleven hundred pieces of silver. Three lies — bowstrings, new ropes, the loom — and then all his heart; the razor, and 'he did not know that Jehovah had turned away from him.' Blind and grinding in Gaza, he is brought out to make sport at Dagon's feast, prays 'remember me', and pulls the house down: more dead at his death than in his life.

About the book

Where it sits
The second book of the FORMER PROPHETS in the Hebrew Bible (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings), the great history that runs from entering the land to losing it. In the Christian Old Testament it is the seventh book, grouped with the 'historical books.' It bridges Joshua's conquest and the rise of the monarchy under Samuel.
Genre
Narrative history, but shaped as a THEOLOGY OF THE CYCLE: a repeating pattern (Israel does evil → is oppressed → cries out → God raises a deliverer → the land has rest → the deliverer dies → Israel does evil again) that spirals downward rather than round, so each turn is worse than the last. It contains some of the Bible's most vivid — and most disturbing — storytelling.
Author
Anonymous. Ancient Jewish tradition (the Talmud) credits SAMUEL, and the book's repeated refrain, 'in those days there was no king in Israel,' is written from a standpoint that already has one — so a monarchy-era hand, plausibly early, is the traditional and natural reading. Critical scholarship sees it as part of a larger 'Deuteronomistic History' (Joshua through Kings) shaped in the seventh-sixth centuries BC around older hero-stories. See 'Where the debates are.'
Date written
The EVENTS fall in the roughly two centuries between the conquest and the monarchy — on the traditional chronology c. 1380–1050 BC, a period of tribal, decentralised Israel with no central government. The COMPOSITION is later: monarchy-era on any view, whether early (near Samuel) or, on the Deuteronomistic view, considerably later.
Place
The land of Canaan itself, tribe by tribe — the hill country, the valleys, the coastal plain held by the Philistines, and the Transjordan. The book is intensely geographical: its disasters and rescues are local, tied to particular tribes and particular towns.
Audience
Later Israel — a people who DID have a king, reading the story of what life was like when there was none. The book is, among other things, an argument about leadership: what happens to a covenant people with no centre to hold them.

How it's laid out

1:1–3:6The double introduction — the incomplete conquest (ch. 1) and its theological diagnosis (ch. 2): a generation arose 'who did not know Jehovah,' and the cycle begins.
3:7–16:31The judges themselves — Othniel, Ehud and the fat king, Deborah and Barak and Jael with her tent-peg, Gideon and his fleece, the tragedy of Jephthah's vow, and the long ruin of Samson. Each cycle darker than the last.
17–21The double epilogue — Micah's idol and the migration of Dan, and the horror at Gibeah and the near-destruction of Benjamin: two stories with no judge at all, showing how far the rot has gone. The refrain closes it: 'everyone did what was right in his own eyes.'

What it's about

  • The CYCLE — sin, servitude, supplication, salvation, and back to sin — spiralling DOWN. The land gets less rest and the deliverers get more flawed as the book goes on.
  • God's exhausting FAITHFULNESS. He keeps rescuing a people who keep forgetting him, raising deliverer after deliverer for a nation that does not deserve one — grace stretched to the point of grief.
  • The FLAWED deliverer. These are not saints: a left-handed assassin, a reluctant thresher, a man who sacrifices his own daughter to a rash vow, a womanising strongman undone by his own appetites. God works through deeply compromised people, and the book never pretends otherwise.
  • The danger of the UNFINISHED task — Judges 1's 'did not drive out' becomes the root of everything that follows: the peoples left in the land become the snare that pulls Israel into their gods.
  • The question of a KING. The refrain 'no king in Israel; everyone did what was right in his own eyes' frames the anarchy as a leadership vacuum — setting up the books of Samuel and the demand for a monarchy.
  • Some of the Bible's most prominent WOMEN — Deborah the prophet-judge, Jael the killer of Sisera, Achsah who negotiates for water, Jephthah's unnamed daughter, Samson's Delilah, and the horrific fate of the Levite's concubine — for good and for terrible ill.

The source text

Translated from the Hebrew MASORETIC TEXT — the digital Hebrew of Mechon-Mamre (the Leningrad/Aleppo tradition), consonants with the Masoretes' vowel-points and cantillation, and its scribal peculiarities kept. The scroll's own paragraph breaks are shown as it marks them: petuchah {פ} (open) and setumah {ס} (closed). Judges also contains one of the oldest passages in the Hebrew Bible — the SONG OF DEBORAH (ch. 5), archaic poetry many scholars date close to the events it celebrates, and a place where the Masoretic text is famously difficult. The seven-version shelf under every chapter compares the NIV, KJV, Douay-Rheims, The Living Bible, the 1599 Geneva, ASV, and NWT.

Looking forward

Judges is the Bible's long argument that flawed human deliverers are not enough. Every judge rescues, and every rescue fails to last, because the deliverer dies and the people relapse — and the book ends crying out, in effect, for a king who will not. The New Testament reads the whole pattern as pointing past itself: Hebrews 11 lists Gideon, Barak, Samson and Jephthah among the heroes of faith, flaws and all, 'of whom the world was not worthy' — people who 'were commended through their faith, yet did not receive what was promised,' because the something better was still ahead.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP AND DATE, as with the other Former Prophets: the traditional ascription to Samuel (monarchy-era, early) versus the critical view of a Deuteronomistic History edited in the seventh-sixth centuries around older sources — the recurring refrain about 'no king' clearly post-dates the events either way. CHRONOLOGY: the judgeships and their round numbers (many periods of 40 or 80 years) add up to more time than the span between conquest and monarchy allows, so most read the judges as partly OVERLAPPING regional figures rather than a single national succession. And the HARD ETHICS — the ban, Jephthah's vow, the atrocity at Gibeah — which the book reports with striking refusal to tidy up; it shows the horror without endorsing it, and readers have always argued over how much is description and how much is verdict. This library lays the readings out with their pedigrees and does not cast a vote.
