Jesus' brothers urge him to go publicly to the Feast of Tabernacles in Jerusalem; he goes secretly instead, then teaches openly in the temple midway through the feast, provoking a divided crowd — some call him the Christ, others a deceiver. Officers sent by the chief priests to arrest him return without him, unable to explain why: 'no one ever spoke like this man.'
c. AD 29, the Feast of Tabernacles (Sukkot) in the autumn — the last full year of Jesus' ministry, the opposition in Jerusalem now sharpening toward the final Passover.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–9 · ‘my time has not yet come’ — and a Greek variant a pagan critic used to call Jesus a liar

The brothers’ challenge (vv3–4) is edged: go public, prove yourself, stop hiding — and John states plainly why it cuts as it does, ‘for not even his own brothers believed in him’ (v5). Jesus’ answer turns on a distinction this Gospel keeps making between his TIME (kairos, the appointed moment) and theirs, always available because nothing hangs on it.

⚠ Verse 8 carries one of this Gospel’s most consequential single-word textual variants. The earliest and best manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and the reading followed by Tregellius and NA28) read ouk, a flat ‘I am NOT going up to this feast’ — and Jesus goes up anyway, secretly, nine verses later (v10). The majority Byzantine text, and the earliest surviving papyrus witnesses, instead read oupō, ‘I am not YET going up,’ which removes the apparent contradiction outright. The shelf splits accordingly: KJV reads ‘I go not up YET’; ASV prints the shorter ‘I go not up’ with a footnote — ‘many ancient authorities add yet’; NIV and ESV both read the plain ‘I am not going up.’ ⚠ This is not a modern discovery: the third-century pagan philosopher Porphyry cited this exact verse to accuse Jesus of inconsistency, and Jerome, writing against him, already knew and discussed both readings. This translation follows the earlier, harder text — ‘I am not going up’ — and lets the tension it creates stand, rather than resolving a difficulty ancient scribes may themselves have smoothed away.

Verses 10–13 · secretly, after all

Jesus does go up — just not the way his brothers demanded. ‘Not openly but in secret’ (v10) answers their ‘show yourself to the world’ (v4) with almost the opposite posture, and the chapter spends its next fourteen verses on a crowd that cannot settle what he is: ‘a good man’ against ‘he deceives the crowd’ (v12, planaō) — the identical verb the Pharisees will use of the crowd itself at v47, closing a frame around the whole chapter with the one charge everybody keeps handing to somebody else.

Verses 14–24 · unschooled learning, and a healing answered with a legal technicality

‘How does this man know his letters, when he has never studied?’ (v15) is a question about credentials — rabbinic teaching authority normally required formal discipleship under a recognized teacher, which Jesus visibly lacked. His answer relocates the test entirely: not lineage but obedience, ‘if anyone is willing to do his will, he will know’ (v17) — a claim that the teaching authenticates itself to anyone actually willing to live by it, not to a credentialing committee.

⚠ The Sabbath argument (vv21–23) reaches back to a specific, already-told story. ‘I did one work, and you are all astonished’ is the Bethesda healing of John 5 (already on these pages), and Jesus answers the charge with a rabbinic argument from lesser to greater: circumcision, which itself overrides the Sabbath and which the law traces to the patriarchs rather than to Moses (v22), is routinely performed on the eighth day even when it falls on a Sabbath; if a single body part may be legally treated on the Sabbath, how much more the whole man made well? ‘Stop judging by appearance’ (v24) closes the argument by naming exactly what the objection had done — judged the act by its calendar rather than its content.

Verses 25–31 · does anyone actually know where the Christ is from?

The Jerusalem crowd’s reasoning in v27 draws on a real strand of Jewish expectation — that the Messiah’s origin would remain hidden until the moment of his sudden revealing, so a man everyone already knows (a Galilean, Joseph’s son) is disqualified by the very fact of being known. Jesus’ reply plays the same word two ways: yes, they know where he is from in the surface sense (v28, ‘you know me and you know where I am from’), and no, they do not, in the sense that actually matters — ‘he who sent me is true, and him you do not know’ (v28b). ⚠ This is dramatic irony aimed at a reader this Gospel assumes already knows what this crowd does not: that this same Galilean was in fact born in Bethlehem, David’s own village — the very objection raised again at v42, and never answered within this chapter at all.

Verses 32–36 · officers sent, and a saying nobody in the crowd can parse

The temple authorities escalate from watching to acting — officers dispatched to seize him (v32) — and Jesus answers with a saying that lands as pure riddle: ‘where I am, you cannot come’ (v34). The Jewish leaders’ guess, that he might mean teaching ‘the Dispersion among the Greeks’ (v35), is wrong about his own immediate meaning (he means his death and return to the Father) but not wrong, in the larger shape of this Gospel’s own future, about where the message actually goes.

Verses 37–39 · streams from within — the cry at the water-pouring, and a Spirit not yet given

The setting is a real, specific Temple ritual, not a generic feast-day backdrop. On each of the seven days of Tabernacles, a priest drew water from the Pool of Siloam in a golden vessel and poured it out at the altar while pilgrims sang the Hallel — a water-libation ceremony tied to prayer for rain and, in later tradition, to the outpouring of God’s Spirit. Jesus stands and cries out during exactly this ritual, on its climactic last day, and redirects its whole action onto himself: not water drawn and poured, but water flowing FROM the believer.

⚠ The Greek of v38 is genuinely ambiguous about one thing English punctuation has to decide: whose heart the rivers flow from. Read one way, ‘as the Scripture has said’ belongs with what follows and the rivers flow from the BELIEVER’s own heart; read the other way, the clause about believing stands alone and the rivers flow from CHRIST, cited as the true rock of Exodus 17 or Ezekiel’s temple-river (Ezekiel 47, not yet on these pages). No single verse in the Old Testament matches the citation exactly either way, which is itself part of the puzzle. This translation follows the more common punctuation (the believer’s own heart) without silencing the alternative.

The parenthetical in v39 is the narrator, not Jesus, glossing the image directly: this is about the Spirit, not yet given because Jesus is not yet glorified. It answers, in advance, the promise this Gospel keeps at John 14 and 15 (already on these pages) — the Spirit-Paraclete who comes only after Jesus goes. And ‘living water’ itself is not a new image here: John 4 (already on these pages) already gave the phrase to a Samaritan woman at a well, who heard it, correctly, as running water before hearing it as anything more.

Verses 40–53 · a Bethlehem child mistaken for a Galilean, and a passage that begins here without ending here

The crowd splits three ways over a single question — prophet, Christ, or neither, on the grounds that ‘the Christ’ cannot come from Galilee (v41) because Scripture places him in Bethlehem, David’s village (v42) — and this Gospel, which never narrates a birth at all, lets the objection stand completely unanswered on the page, trusting a reader who already knows the nativity traditions this book itself never tells.

The officers’ report is the chapter’s plainest line: sent to arrest a man, they return empty-handed and explain themselves with ‘no one has ever spoken like this man’ (v46) — testimony from men with no stake in believing anything, which is exactly why it counts. The Pharisees’ retort dismisses the whole crowd as ‘accursed’ for its ignorance of the law (v49), the same contempt for the ordinary and unlearned this Gospel keeps setting the actual evidence against.

⚠ Nicodemus, returning from John 3 (already on these pages), asks a narrow, careful, entirely defensible legal question — does the law convict a man unheard? (v51) — and gets mocked for it rather than answered: ‘are you from Galilee too?’ The Pharisees’ own proof text, ‘no prophet arises from Galilee’ (v52), is worth checking against the record: Jonah, one of the few named writing prophets with a stated hometown, came from Gath-hepher in Galilee (2 Kings 14:25, not yet on these pages) — a claim made with total confidence, in the middle of a chapter about knowing where things are actually from, that does not survive its own test.

⚠ Verse 53 is where one of the New Testament’s most famous textual questions begins, and this translation states the problem rather than hiding it. ‘Then each of them went home’ is, in the earliest and best Greek manuscripts (including the two oldest complete New Testaments, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and the earliest papyri that cover this stretch of text), followed immediately by the start of chapter 8's Sermon on the Mount material with no gap at all — the story of the woman caught in adultery (traditionally John 7:53–8:11) is simply absent from them. It appears first in manuscripts of the Western textual tradition, then spreads into the Byzantine text that underlies the King James Version, and in some manuscript families it floats to other locations entirely — after Luke 21, or at the very end of this Gospel. Nearly every modern translation prints it, because it is old and its vocabulary reads as genuinely Johannine in style and it may well preserve a real, early tradition about Jesus — but nearly all of them also mark it off with brackets or a footnote explaining that it is not present in the earliest witnesses. This translation follows that near-universal practice: the passage appears at the start of John 8, already on these pages, clearly marked, reported rather than either suppressed or silently absorbed into the text as though its transmission history were simple.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Oupō tracked consistently as ‘not yet’ everywhere it is textually secure in this chapter (vv30, 39 twice) so that its ABSENCE at the disputed v8 stays visible to an English reader rather than being harmonized away. Planaō (vv12, 47) kept as ‘deceive’ both times it is used, preserving the frame between the crowd's early whisper and the Pharisees' late insult. And the feast’s own name — skēnopēgia, ‘tent-pitching,’ v2 — kept as ‘Tabernacles’ rather than the more literal ‘Booths,’ matching this shelf's own majority practice.

Echoes paid. Chapter 6’s own closing note named this chapter by number and previewed exactly this — a crowd divided over whether Jesus is the Christ — now shown in full rather than miniature. Chapter 4’s living water, first offered privately to one Samaritan woman at a well, is here offered publicly, at the Temple's own water-ritual, to everyone within earshot. And chapter 5’s Bethesda healing, left as an open charge at the end of that chapter, finally gets its Sabbath defense argued out loud.

Echoes planted. The Spirit not yet given (v39) is the marker this Gospel will not resolve until the Farewell Discourse promises the Paraclete by name (John 14 and 15, already on these pages) and, beyond that, until 20:22. The hidden-origin Christ expectation (v27) and the Bethlehem objection (v42) are both left standing, unresolved within this chapter, for a reader to carry forward. And v34’s riddle, ‘where I am you cannot come,’ recurs almost word for word at 13:33, spoken this time to his own disciples rather than to hostile crowds.

Left open on purpose. Whether v38’s rivers flow from the believer or from Christ himself — a live and old dispute this translation reports rather than resolves. Whether Jesus’ own words at v8 originally read ‘not’ or ‘not yet’ — a real textual fork with real theological weight riding on it, not a rounding error. And the hidden origin of the Christ (v27) against the Bethlehem tradition (v42) — two pieces of messianic expectation this chapter lets sit unreconciled, in the mouths of characters who never learn they are talking about the same man twice.

Next installment in this book, whenever it is translated: John 8, opening with the woman caught in adultery — the passage this chapter's own last verse leads directly into, textual questions and all.
