Women find the tomb empty and are told by two messengers that Jesus has risen, a report the Eleven dismiss as nonsense. Two disciples walking to Emmaus are joined by the risen Jesus, unrecognized, who rebukes their slowness to believe the Scriptures and is known to them at last in the breaking of bread. He appears to the assembled Eleven, invites them to touch him and eats before them to prove he is not a spirit, opens their minds to understand the Scriptures, and commissions them to preach repentance and forgiveness to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem — then parts from them at Bethany.
c. AD 30, Sunday, the day of the resurrection through that same evening for the Emmaus road and the appearance to the Eleven; the tomb and the Emmaus scenes run in close parallel with the resurrection-day material of Matthew 28, Mark 16, and John 20, though Luke alone narrates the Emmaus road at length and compresses the ascension (v51) into the close of the very day the other Gospels leave open, where Acts 1:3 will later state explicitly that forty days actually separated the two.

Verses 1–3 · τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων … οὐχ εὗρον τὸ σῶμα tē mia tōn sabbatōn … ouch heuron to sōma — the day named the same way in every Gospel, and a phrase the text printed here leaves shorter than the shelf prints it

⭐⭐ “The first day of the week” is, word for word, how all four Gospels date the resurrection — the single point where Matthew, Mark, Luke and John never vary. Tē mia tōn sabbatōn, literally “on day one of the sabbaths,” uses sabbaton loosely for the whole seven-day week, a usage already seen at 18:12 (already on these pages, “I fast twice a week”). Matthew 28:1 and Mark 16:2 (both already on these pages) date the women’s visit with the identical phrase; John's own account (20:1, not yet on these pages) is reported to do the same. This is the day this project's own English calls SUNDAY, and its Christian observance as the day of worship, in place of the Sabbath, traces to nothing more elaborate than this sentence, repeated four times.

⚠ “The body” — not “the body of the Lord Jesus.” A large family of later manuscripts adds “of the Lord Jesus” after “body” in v3, printed as plain running text by the KJV (‘the body of the Lord Jesus’) and the ASV alike. The earliest witnesses read simply “the body,” and that is what is printed here — the shorter reading, letting the subject stay implicit rather than supplying, twice over, a title the sentence does not need for its sense.

Verses 4–7 · Τί ζητεῖτε τὸν ζῶντα μετὰ τῶν νεκρῶν Ti zēteite ton zōnta meta tōn nekrōn — a question with no parallel in any other Gospel’s account of the tomb, and a verse this translation keeps though the oldest complete Bible in Greek does not have it

⭐⭐⭐ “Why do you seek the living among the dead?” is unique to Luke’s own account — neither messenger in Matthew 28:5–7 nor the young man of Mark 16:6–7 (both already on these pages) asks anything; both simply announce. Only here does the question itself carry the whole argument: ho zōn, “the living one,” is a title this translation has already used once, of God himself at the burning bush's own argument for the resurrection of the dead (“he is not a God of the dead but of the living,” 20:38, already on these pages) — now applied to Jesus directly, at the one moment in the story where it could be proven true or false by simply opening a tomb.

⚠⚠ V6’s whole first sentence, “He is not here, but has been raised,” is absent from Codex Bezae and the Old Latin manuscripts — but present, unbracketed, in the earliest Greek witnesses this project follows, including Codices Sinaiticus and Vaticanus. This is one of a small cluster of places late in Luke’s own Gospel (see also vv12, 36, 40, 51–52 below) where the nineteenth-century editors Westcott and Hort judged the SHORTER reading original — the opposite of the usual pattern, where a later scribe is more often caught ADDING material than removing it, which is exactly why these few verses earned the name “Western non-interpolations” in the textual-criticism literature that followed them. Modern critical editions from the mid-twentieth century on have mostly reversed Westcott and Hort's judgment at this particular verse and print the sentence as running text; the library follows that later consensus here, keeping the sentence, while flagging the two or three places below where the text used for this translation keeps a shorter reading that most of the shelf does not.

The “dazzling clothing” (v4) recurs in nearly the same words from the mountain seven chapters earlier — Jesus’s own garment “became dazzling white” at the Transfiguration (9:29, already on these pages), the same rare adjective, exastraptō/astraptō, used there of Jesus himself and here of the two messengers at his tomb.

Verses 8–9 · τῶν ῥημάτων αὐτοῦ tōn rhēmatōn autou — a memory this Gospel has already told the reader to expect

“They remembered his words” answers a promise this Gospel planted at the annunciation and the nativity: Mary, told things she does not yet understand, “kept all these things, pondering them in her heart” (2:19, already on these pages); here, for the first time in the narrative, a group of disciples does the same with a prediction of Jesus's own, and the remembering itself becomes the hinge that turns eleven verses of confusion into a report.

Verses 10–11 · λῆρος lēros — a word that occurs exactly once in the whole New Testament, and the verdict the first resurrection witnesses are met with

⭐⭐⭐ Only Luke’s own account names the women who carried the first report — and only Luke's own narrator states, in one blunt word, exactly how the Eleven received it. See the new leros dictionary entry: the noun, translated “nonsense,” is a hapax legomenon — this is its one and only appearance anywhere in the Greek New Testament. Neither Matthew nor Mark's own account of the women's report uses any single word this harsh; Luke's narrator does, and applies it to testimony from witnesses whose word, under the law of the period, could not by itself establish a fact in a Jewish court. This translation states the word plainly rather than softening it, since the plainness is the point: the first response to the resurrection, inside this Gospel's own narrative, is disbelief, not faith.

Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Mary the mother of James were named once before, together, as three of “many others” who provided for Jesus and the Twelve “out of their own means” (8:3, already on these pages) — the same three women, the only three Luke's own Gospel names twice.

Verse 12 · παρακύψας βλέπει τὰ ὀθόνια μόνα parakypsas blepei ta othonia mona — a verse some of the oldest Greek manuscripts leave out, and a detail John's own Gospel, not yet on these pages, is widely read as expanding

⚠⚠ Like v6, this verse is one of the “Western non-interpolations” — absent from Codex Bezae and the Old Latin, but kept here on the strength of the earliest Greek witnesses that do have it. The NIV prints it as plain text with a footnote naming the omission in “some manuscripts”; the NWT 1984 prints it without any bracket at all. Some scholars have argued the verse was imported from John's own fuller account of Peter (and the beloved disciple) running to the tomb (John 20:3–10, not yet on these pages); others take Luke's brief version as the earlier report John later expanded. The library states both readings and does not decide between them.

Peter running rather than walking is this Gospel's own second use of the verb trechō, run — the first is the father of the prodigal son, who “ran” to meet a son he had given up for dead (15:20, already on these pages). Both runs answer a loss the runner did not expect to be reversed.

Verses 13–16 · οἱ ὀφθαλμοὶ αὐτῶν ἐκρατοῦντο hoi ophthalmoi autōn ekratounto — a divine passive that supplies no explanation, and a distance the manuscripts themselves cannot agree on

⭐⭐ “Their eyes were kept” is a passive verb with no stated agent — the ordinary grammar scholars call a “divine passive,” where God (or here, plausibly, the risen Jesus himself) is understood to be the one acting, without being named. The text does not say Jesus disguised himself, wore other clothing, or was simply unrecognized in ordinary grief; it says their eyes were kept — the same passive construction v31 will use in reverse, when their eyes were opened. Whatever kept them from seeing him is the same kind of thing that will, six verses on, let them see him: not a change in his appearance, on this reading, but a change worked on them. The TLB alone fills the gap with an explanation the Greek does not supply, ‘they didn’t recognize him at first because he had changed his appearance’ — a guess borrowed from the disputed “other form” of Mark 16:12 (already on these pages, itself part of a passage the earliest manuscripts do not contain), not a reading either passage's own Greek requires.

⚠ Sixty stadia, or a hundred and sixty — the manuscripts disagree on the distance itself, which is one reason Emmaus’s exact location has never been settled. See the new Emmaus encyclopedia entry: the text followed here reads “sixty stadia,” about seven miles (eleven kilometers), the figure behind the KJV's, ASV's, and NWT's ‘threescore furlongs’/‘sixty stadia’/‘eleven kilometers’. A minority of manuscripts, including Codex Sinaiticus in one correction, read “a hundred and sixty stadia,” about eighteen miles — a reading no version on either shelf checked here adopts, but one some geographers have used to argue for Emmaus Nicopolis, a site otherwise too far for a round trip made twice in one evening (v33).

Verses 17–24 · Κλεοπᾶς Kleopas — the only one of the two Emmaus travelers given a name, and a name this Gospel never explains

⭐ Cleopas is named once, here, and never again in the New Testament — his companion is never named at all. See the new Cleopas encyclopedia entry: tradition since the early church has guessed the unnamed second traveler was his wife, on the strength of a “Mary the wife of Clopas” named at the cross in John 19:25 (not yet on these pages) — a DIFFERENT Greek name, Klōpas rather than Kleopas, though the two are frequently conflated in later tradition. The text itself states neither the companion's identity nor the relationship; this translation reports the guess as a guess.

⚠ “Jesus the Nazarene” uses the spelling this Gospel's own narrator otherwise avoids. Cleopas's own word here, Nazarēnos, matches the unclean spirit's address at 4:34 (already on these pages); elsewhere in this Gospel where the same idea appears in the narrator's or another speaker's own voice, it is the alternate form Nazōraios instead — the spelling the crowd used at 18:37 (already on these pages). Both are translated ‘Nazarene’ here; the two forms point at the same town without any difference this translation can detect in meaning.

“We were hoping he was the one who was going to redeem Israel” (v21) is the same verb, lytroō, this Gospel's own opening songs used of God: Zechariah blessed the God of Israel “because he has visited and brought about redemption for his people” (1:68, already on these pages). Cleopas states the hope in the past tense — ēlpizomen, “we WERE hoping” — grammar that marks a hope already given up, spoken, without either traveler realizing it, directly to the man the hope was about.

Verses 25–27 · βραδεῖς τῇ καρδίᾳ … διερμήνευσεν bradeis tē kardia … diermēneusen — a rebuke sharper than anything this Gospel has Jesus say to a stranger elsewhere, and a Bible-length lesson given to precisely two people

⭐⭐ “Slow of heart” is Jesus’s own verdict on unbelief that follows evidence rather than a lack of it — the two travelers have already heard the women's report (v22–24) and still do not connect it to what “the prophets have spoken.” The rebuke is not aimed at doubting a rumor; it is aimed at not having read the Scriptures rightly in the first place. Diermēneusen, “interpreted” (v27), is a compound built on the same root that gives English “hermeneutics,” the discipline of interpretation itself — and this is the verse it is named for.

⭐⭐⭐ “Beginning from Moses and from all the prophets… in all the Scriptures” is the single most sweeping claim of scriptural fulfillment anywhere in the four Gospels, and the text never once states which specific verses Jesus used. This is, by design, the one messianic argument in the New Testament the reader cannot check: every other citation of “what is written” in this Gospel names or quotes a specific text; here the content of a seven-mile walk's worth of teaching is summarized in a single clause. The NWT reads ‘he interpreted to them things pertaining to himself in all the Scriptures’; the KJV ‘he expounded unto them in all the scriptures the things concerning himself’ — neither version, nor any other on this shelf, supplies what this translation's own notes cannot supply either: the verses themselves are Luke's silence, not this library's.

Verses 28–29 · προσεποιήσατο πορρώτερον πορεύεσθαι prosepoiēsato porrōteron poreuesthai — a stranger who pretends to keep walking, and two travelers who will not let him

“He acted as if he were going farther” states, without apology, that Jesus's own action here was a kind of performance — prospoieomai is the ordinary verb for pretending, and every version on this shelf translates it the same way. The verse does not resolve the discomfort some readers feel at a deception, however small, staged by Jesus himself; ancient hospitality convention read a guest's token reluctance as the polite move that let a host insist, and Cleopas and his companion do exactly that — ‘urged him strongly’, parebiasanto, the same verb this Gospel used of the parable-master’s servants compelling the poor and lame to come in off the roads (14:23, already on these pages). Whichever reading is preferred, this translation states the plain sense of the verb rather than softening it.

Verses 30–32 · διηνοίχθησαν … διήνοιγεν diēnoichthēsan … diēnoigen — one verb, opening three different things in one chapter, and the exact four verbs of the Last Supper repeated over a different table

⭐⭐⭐ The same bread-blessing is recognized rather than repeated — and this translation keeps the identical four verbs, in the identical order, that opened the Last Supper two nights earlier. “Took… blessed… broke… gave” (v30) reproduces, verb for verb, “he took bread, gave thanks, broke it, and gave it to them” (22:19, already on these pages) — a meal Cleopas and his companion, on the text's own silence, were almost certainly not present for. What they recognize is not a private gesture they once witnessed but, this translation's own note on 22:19 having already discussed, a pattern already becoming the church's own remembered shape for the meal Paul will later call “the Lord's Supper” (1 Corinthians 11:20, not yet on these pages).

⭐⭐⭐ The new dianoigō dictionary entry: the same Greek verb, “to open thoroughly,” does three different kinds of opening in this one chapter — EYES (v31), SCRIPTURES (v32), and, seventeen verses on, a MIND (v45). The verb appears in only two other New Testament verses: earlier in this same Gospel, of a firstborn son who “opens the womb” (2:23, already on these pages, a wholly unrelated legal sense), and once more in Luke's own second volume, where Paul in a synagogue is “opening and demonstrating that the Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead” (Acts 17:3, not yet on these pages) — the identical verb, over the identical argument, in the identical author's own later chapter. One word does the seeing, the understanding, and, later in Acts, the arguing.

The “burning heart” (v32) names, after the fact, what this translation's own note at v25 called slowness — the same two travelers, recognizing in hindsight that the rebuke and the exposition had already begun to work on them before they could see who was speaking it.

Verses 33–35 · ὤφθη Σίμωνι ōphthē Simōni — an appearance this Gospel never narrates, confirmed only by a single clause, and matched by the earliest written list of resurrection witnesses in the whole New Testament

⭐⭐⭐ “Appeared to Simon” is the only mention anywhere in the four Gospels of a private resurrection appearance to Peter alone — and this Gospel never narrates it, only reports that the Eleven were already saying it when the two Emmaus travelers arrived. Paul's own creedal formula, likely older than any Gospel and probably already fixed by the time he received it, opens its list of witnesses the same way: “he appeared to Cephas, then to the Twelve” (1 Corinthians 15:5, not yet on these pages) — the identical verb, ōphthē, and the identical priority given to Peter over the wider group, from an independent written source. Two separate strands of New Testament tradition agree that Peter was appeared to first, or at least named first, and neither tells the reader what was said.

Verses 36–40 · ἔστη ἐν μέσῳ αὐτῶν estē en mesō autōn — a greeting every other version on both shelves prints, and this translation's own text does not

⚠⚠⚠ “Peace to you” is missing outright from the text followed here — not bracketed, simply absent — against every other version checked, including the NWT, which brackets the clause rather than dropping it. The earliest Greek witness (Codex Vaticanus among them) lacks “and he said to them, ‘Peace to you’” entirely; the great majority of later manuscripts add it, and nearly every printed Bible follows the majority — the KJV and ASV both print it as plain running text, ‘Peace be unto you’. The NWT 1984 is the one shelf version that flags real doubt here, printing the clause inside double brackets, ‘[[May YOU have peace]]’ — visibly less confident in it than the KJV or ASV, but still more confident than the shorter text followed on this page, which omits it altogether. If the shorter reading is right, Jesus's first act among the assembled Eleven is not a greeting at all, but simply standing there — and their terror (v37) follows a silence, not a word of peace that should have calmed them.

⚠⚠ Verse 40, showing the hands and feet a second time (after v39 already invited them to look), is itself one of the “Western non-interpolations” — kept here, in brackets, as the critical text prints it. Absent from Codex Bezae and the Old Latin; present in the earliest complete Greek Bibles, Sinaiticus and Vaticanus among them. Unlike vv6 and 12 above, where this translation follows the fuller reading unbracketed, the text followed here keeps v40 flagged — a genuine, and on strict manuscript grounds slightly arbitrary-looking, inconsistency in how these few disputed verses are each weighed, which this note states rather than smooths over.

“Flesh and bones,” not “flesh and blood.” Paul will later write that “flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom of God” (1 Corinthians 15:50, not yet on these pages) of the resurrection body; Luke's own risen Jesus offers the opposite pairing, sarka kai ostea, flesh and BONES, to prove he is not pneuma, a spirit. Whether the two statements describe the same kind of body from two different angles, or whether Luke and Paul are simply making two different points — one arguing against a ghost, the other against a merely physical resurrection — is a real question the shelf and the commentaries answer differently; this translation states both texts and does not force one to explain the other away.

Verses 41–43 · ἀπιστούντων … ἀπὸ τῆς χαρᾶς apistountōn … apo tēs charas — a disbelief this translation's own dictionary entry now tracks across three appearances, and joy itself named as its cause

See the apistia dictionary entry, extended here: the noun and its verb tracked the Eleven's refusal to believe the women (v11) and now track a second, gentler kind of not-believing — apistountōn apo tēs charas, disbelieving FROM joy, a doubt caused by happiness rather than opposed to it. The meal that follows is offered as evidence for the senses a joyful doubt cannot simply reason its way past; see the new synalizomai dictionary entry (already on these pages, from Acts 1:4) for this translation's fuller case that Luke's own risen Jesus is consistently shown eating, not merely appearing to.

Verses 44–49 · νόμῳ Μωϋσέως καὶ προφήταις καὶ ψαλμοῖς nomō Mōuseōs kai prophētais kai psalmois — the only place in the whole New Testament that names all three parts of the Hebrew Bible by their later-standard titles in one breath

⭐⭐⭐ “The Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms” is the New Testament's own closest approach to naming the three-part Hebrew canon — Torah, Nevi'im, and Ketuvim — that later Judaism would call the TaNaKh. Elsewhere the New Testament's shorthand is the two-part “the Law and the Prophets” (used already in this Gospel at 16:16, already on these pages); here alone a third category is named, and “Psalms” stands, on the most common reading, as a stand-in for the whole third division of writings the book most naturally represents. This translation keeps the specific noun rather than generalizing it to “the Writings,” since that is what the Greek actually says.

⚠ “Repentance AND forgiveness,” not “repentance FOR forgiveness” — a real textual fork with a real difference in what is claimed. The text followed here reads metanoian kai aphesin hamartiōn, “repentance and forgiveness of sins,” two things proclaimed side by side — the reading behind the KJV's and ASV's own ‘repentance and remission of sins’. A strong alternative tradition reads eis in place of kai, “repentance FOR/UNTO the forgiveness of sins” — repentance as the thing that leads to forgiveness rather than a second, separate proclamation — which is what the NWT prints, ‘repentance for forgiveness of sins’. The difference is small in English and real in Greek: one reading proclaims two gifts, the other proclaims one gift reached through a condition.

⭐⭐ “Power from on high” is a promise this Gospel's own second volume answers by name, twice, within its own first chapter. Luke's second volume opens with the identical command to wait in the city (Acts 1:4, already on these pages) and the identical promise, now given its own word — “you will receive POWER when the Holy Spirit comes upon you” (Acts 1:8, already on these pages). The two-volume work's own hinge is built here: this chapter closes a Gospel on an unfulfilled promise, and Luke's second book opens by fulfilling it.

Verses 50–53 · ἀνεφέρετο … προσκυνήσαντες anephereto … proskynēsantes — two bracketed clauses, a different verb than Luke's own second volume uses for the identical event, and a word this Gospel has used only once before, of a very different request

⚠⚠⚠ Both bracketed clauses in these verses — “and was carried up into heaven” and “having worshiped him” — are, once again, “Western non-interpolations”: absent from Codex Bezae and the Old Latin, present in Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, and kept here in brackets as the critical text prints them. If the shorter reading is original, Luke's Gospel ends with Jesus simply parting from the disciples at Bethany, with neither an explicit ascension nor an explicit act of worship stated in the verse itself — the whole scene read, on that reading, through the interpretive lens of the two added clauses rather than stated outright by them. Every version on both shelves checked here prints both clauses as plain running text.

⭐⭐⭐ “Was carried up” — anaphereō — is NOT the verb Luke's own second volume uses for the same event. Acts uses a different verb three times for the ascension, analambanō, “taken up” (Acts 1:2, 1:9, 1:11, already on these pages) — the verb the Greek Old Testament uses of Elijah, taken up in the whirlwind. This verse's own anaphereō is a different compound, used elsewhere in the New Testament of Jesus being led UP a mountain (the Transfiguration, 9:28, already on these pages, of the disciples) and of sin-bearing (“he himself bore our sins,” 1 Peter 2:24, not yet on these pages). One author, writing the same event twice in two different books, reaches for two different verbs — a small inconsistency this translation states rather than harmonizes away.

⭐⭐ “Having worshiped him” — proskynēsantes — is only the third use of this verb anywhere in this Gospel, and the other two are the devil's own demand, refused. Proskyneō, to worship, to bow down to, occurs in exactly three verses of Luke: twice at the temptation, where the devil demands “if you worship before me” and is refused with “you shall worship the Lord your God” (4:7–8, already on these pages), and here, in a bracketed clause, as the one thing the disciples do before returning to Jerusalem. If this clause is original, the same verb that opened this Gospel refused to Satan closes it given to Jesus, the temptation's own refusal now completed rather than merely stated; if the bracket marks a later addition, the echo is the later church's own reading of the ascension rather than Luke's own sentence. Either way, this is the only worship the word is ever given in Luke's own Gospel.

The Gospel that opened with a priest struck silent inside the temple, unable to bless the people waiting outside (Zechariah, 1:22, already on these pages) closes with ordinary disciples “continually in the temple, blessing God” — the same verb, eulogeō, that opens the book now closes it, moved from a priest who could not speak to a whole community that will not stop.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation follows the shorter reading, and where it keeps the fuller one, among Luke's own cluster of “Western non-interpolations”: v6's “he is not here, but has been raised” and v12's account of Peter at the tomb are both kept as plain, unbracketed text; v40's second showing of the hands and feet, v51's “and was carried up into heaven,” and v52's “having worshiped him” are all kept but bracketed; and v36's “Peace to you” is left out of the running text altogether, the one point in this chapter where the text followed here is shorter than every other version on both shelves.

Echoes paid. The “dikaios” verdict this Gospel's closing chapter promised would be “tested once more… against two men who do not yet believe what they have seen” (last chapter's own closing note) is paid across this whole chapter: the Eleven call the women's report leros (v11), and Cleopas and his companion are “slow of heart to believe” a risen man walking beside them (v25) before either “dikaios” verdict from the cross is ever vindicated by a single eyewitness seeing him alive. And the bread “taken, blessed, broken, and given” at Emmaus (v30) is the identical four-verb sequence this Gospel used once before, at the meal Jesus himself called his own body two nights and one execution earlier (22:19).

Echoes planted. “Power from on high” (v49) and the command to “stay in the city” are both picked up, by name, in the opening chapter of this same author's second volume — a promise this Gospel closes on and does not itself fulfill, left open for its own sequel. And dianoigō, the one verb this chapter uses of an eye, a scripture, and a mind (vv31, 32, 45), returns once more in that same second volume, of a different apostle opening the identical argument in a synagogue — not yet linked on these pages, but the same author's own hand visible in advance.
