Jethro brings Zipporah and her two sons back to Moses at the mountain of God, hears the whole story, offers sacrifice, and — seeing Moses judging the people alone from morning to evening — advises the delegation structure Moses then adopts.
Placed here, immediately before Sinai — but the chapter's own language of Moses already teaching 'the statutes of God and his laws' (v16, v20) reads to many readers as later than its position in the text, since the law proper is not given until chapter 20. This library keeps the narrative's own placement and flags the puzzle rather than resolving it.

Translator’s Notes

A name that is thirty years overdue

Jethro brings back the family Moses had sent away — Zipporah and the two sons, whose names are given here as a matched pair the text has been sitting on. Gershom was already named at his birth (2:22): ‘a sojourner there.’ Eliezer never was — this is his first appearance anywhere in the Bible, his birth never narrated, his name only now supplied, retroactively, decades after the fact: ‘the God of my father was my help, and delivered me from the sword of Pharaoh.’ ⚠ Read together the two names summarize the whole story so far in six words: exile, then rescue.

A father-in-law who knows more than the news

The welcome (v7) runs through every gesture a reunion needs — going out to meet him, bowing, kissing, asking after each other’s welfare — before a word of the actual news is exchanged. Only then does Moses tell the whole story (v8), and Jethro’s response is not simply relief but a theological verdict.

⚠ ‘Jehovah is greater than all gods’ (v11) is a striking line from a Midianite priest, and worth reading exactly as it is rather than smoothing it into something it does not quite say: not ‘the only God’ but the greatest among the gods he already reckoned with — a comparative claim, made by a man the text never asks to renounce his own priesthood or convert in any formal sense. He reaches this conclusion by argument, not by decree: ‘in the very thing in which they dealt arrogantly’ — Pharaoh’s own cruelty became the proof.

A meal presided over by a priest who is not Israel’s

⚠ Jethro, not Aaron, is the one who takes the offering — the Hebrew’s subject stays Jethro across the whole verse, with Aaron and Israel’s elders arriving afterward, as guests at a meal someone else has provided. This is worth pausing on precisely because Aaron has no priesthood yet: the office is not installed until Exodus 28-29, chapters still ahead of this one. A Midianite priest presides over a sacrificial meal that the future priesthood of Israel simply attends, ‘before God’ — the text does not treat the irregularity as a problem worth commenting on.

Morning until evening, and no one thinks to ask why

The picture is almost cinematic in its plainness: one man seated, an entire people standing, ‘from the morning until the evening’ (v13) — a detail repeated verbatim in Jethro’s question one verse later (v14), as if the repetition is itself the complaint. Nothing about the system is presented as malicious or even unusual; it is simply what happens when one person is the only channel a whole nation has to God’s answers.

‘Not good,’ and a structure built to outlast one man

⚠ ‘The thing that you are doing is not good’ (v17) is a rare thing in this book: outside counsel to Moses, offered plainly and accepted without argument. Jethro’s diagnosis is not that Moses is doing the job badly but that no single person can do this particular job at this particular scale — v18’s prediction is stated as fact, not held out as a possibility. KJV and ASV both read the older idiom ‘wear away’; NIV and ESV both modernize to ‘wear yourself/yourselves out.’ RV60 keeps the stronger ‘desfallecerás del todo’ (you will utterly collapse); NVI reads the milder ‘te cansas’ (you grow tired) — the same warning, two very different registers of urgency.

The four qualifications for the new judges (v21) — able, God-fearing, truthful, hating unjust gain — describe character rather than credentials; no tribe, family, or prior office is named as a qualifier. KJV and ASV both open the list with the older ‘able men’; NIV modernizes to ‘capable men,’ matching NVI’s ‘hombres capaces’ against RV60’s older ‘varones de virtud.’ The resulting structure, thousands down to tens, is pure administrative math: a chain that lets one dispute travel only as far up the chain as its difficulty requires, and no farther.

Implemented exactly as advised, and a puzzle in the calendar

Verses 24-26 report the plan carried out in almost the same words Jethro used to propose it (compare v21 with v25, v22 with v26) — a rare case in this book of counsel given and then followed to the letter, with no editorializing about the outcome.

⚠ This chapter sits in an odd place on the calendar. It is told here, before Sinai (chapter 19 is the next scene), yet v16 and v20 already have Moses making known ‘the statutes of God and his laws’ — language that reads more naturally as describing a body of law Israel already has than one not yet given at the mountain a chapter away. Many readers, ancient and modern, have proposed that this episode chronologically belongs later (Jethro and his kinsman Hobab are still with Israel much further on, in Numbers 10:29-32) and is placed here by topic rather than by date — closing the wilderness-journey material before Sinai opens a wholly different kind of chapter. The text itself gives no date for this scene and does not resolve the question.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. V11’s ‘greater than all gods’ kept as the comparative claim it is in Hebrew, rather than softened toward exclusive monotheism the verse does not itself state. V18’s idiom rendered ‘wear yourself out’ rather than the older ‘wear away,’ for a modern reader's ear. V12 keeps Jethro as the offering's subject throughout, since the Hebrew never shifts it to Aaron.

⚠ A note on order: translated in sequence — Jethro’s visit is the last stop before Sinai; the mountain itself is reached at Exodus 19.
