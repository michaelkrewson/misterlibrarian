The seventh seal opens in silence — 'about half an hour' of it, the only silence recorded in the whole vision. Seven angels are given seven trumpets; an angel offers incense with the prayers of the saints, then hurls the censer's fire to the earth. The first four trumpets sound in turn: hail and fire mixed with blood burn a third of the earth; a burning mountain thrown into the sea kills a third of sea life; a star named Wormwood poisons a third of the rivers; a third of the sun, moon, and stars are struck dark.
c. AD 95, opening the trumpet sequence that the seventh seal contained all along — the seals and the trumpets are widely read as concentric rather than strictly sequential, each ending in the next stage of judgment.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · a half hour the whole book never explains · ἐγένετο σιγὴ ἐν τῷ οὐρανῷ ὡς ἡμιώριον egeneto sigē en tō ouranō hōs hēmiōrion

⚠ This half hour of silence is one of the least explained moments in the whole book, and the library reports several old guesses rather than one answer. Some read it against Jewish temple practice, where incense was offered in silence while the congregation prayed outside; some read it as heaven itself pausing in suspense before the seventh seal's contents — the seven trumpets — are revealed; some simply take it as dramatic pacing, a held breath between the sixth seal's terror and what comes next. Nothing in the text explains the pause. It only marks that there was one, and for how long.

The seventh seal does not contain a single new judgment, the way seals one through six each did. It contains the seven trumpets — the seals do not stand apart from the trumpets; the seventh seal is the trumpets, the next cycle nested inside the last.

Verses 3–5 · the same fire that carries prayers up throws judgment down · θυμιάματα… ταῖς προσευχαῖς τῶν ἁγίων thymiamata… tais proseuchais tōn hagiōn

The chapter pairs two uses of the same golden altar deliberately. First, an angel offers incense together with the prayers of the saints — likely the very prayers heard already, the martyrs under the altar crying ‘how long’ at 6:10 — and the smoke rises to God. Then the same censer is refilled, not with incense this time but with fire from the altar, and thrown back down to earth. The prayer goes up; the answer comes down, from the same vessel. The text does not spell out the connection between the two directions. It only places them back to back.

Verses 6–9 · a fourth becomes a third · χάλαζα καὶ πῦρ μεμιγμένα ἐν αἵματι chalaza kai pyr memigmena en haimati

⚠ The trumpets escalate past the seals in scale, not just in kind. The fourth seal's horseman was given power over a fourth of the earth (6:8); the first four trumpets each strike a third. The same fraction — a third — repeats seven times across this chapter alone (earth, trees, grass; sea, sea creatures, ships; rivers and springs; sun, moon, stars, day, night), a deliberate drumbeat of partial, not total, destruction. Whatever is coming, this is still not everything.

Hail and fire, mixed with blood (v7) rewrites the seventh plague of Egypt, where hail and fire came down together and struck ‘every plant of the field’ (Exodus 9:23-25) — the same combination, centuries later, aimed now at the whole earth rather than one nation.

Verses 10–13 · a bitter name, and a bird whose identity the manuscripts dispute · τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ ἀστέρος λέγεται· ὁ Ἄψινθος to onoma tou asteros legetai; ho Apsinthos

Wormwood is a real, bitter plant (Artemisia absinthium), and long before this chapter it was already the Hebrew Bible's standing image for the bitterness that follows broken covenant and coming judgment — a root bearing wormwood and gall (Deuteronomy 29:18), justice turned to wormwood (Amos 5:7), a people fed wormwood and given poisoned water to drink (Jeremiah 9:15, 23:15). This chapter does not invent a new symbol; it names a star after an old one and lets it poison what should have given life.

⚠ NVI does not just translate the name; it changes it. KJV, ASV and ESV all keep ‘Wormwood’ as a proper name, and RV60 keeps the matching Spanish botanical name, ‘Ajenjo.’ NVI alone renames the star itself ‘Amargura,’ Bitterness — trading the specific plant for the general quality it stood for, a translation choice rather than a different Greek text.

⚠ ‘Eagle’ or ‘angel’? A real, well-attested textual variant, not a translation choice. KJV and RV60 both read ‘angel’, following the Textus Receptus; ASV, NIV, ESV and NVI all read ‘eagle’, following the earlier manuscripts — the reading kept here. An eagle crying human words in midheaven is, if anything, the stranger image, and stranger is usually the harder reading to have invented by accident.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation follows the earlier manuscripts: v13's ‘eagle’ over KJV/RV60's ‘angel’ — a real textual split, flagged rather than silently resolved either way.

Echoes paid. The golden altar and the prayers of the saints (v3) recall the martyrs' own cry, ‘how long,’ from 6:10 — the same prayers, now carried up by name. The ‘third’ that structures every trumpet in this chapter answers, and intensifies, the fourth seal's reach over a fourth of the earth at 6:8.

Echoes planted. The seven trumpets (v2), only now handed out, will structure every chapter through 11, whenever they are translated — this chapter sounds the first four; the fifth and sixth, and the delay before the seventh, are still ahead. And the eagle's own ‘woe, woe, woe’ (v13) names the shape the next two trumpets will take before either one sounds.

⚠ Left open on purpose. Why heaven falls silent for half an hour, and what exactly that half hour was for — the library reports the leading guesses and settles on none of them. The text gives a duration. It withholds the reason.
