Joshua, old and well advanced in years, calls all Israel's elders, heads, judges and officers and tells them that Jehovah has fought for them and given them the land. He warns that to cleave to the nations that remain, to intermarry with them and to serve their gods will bring the loss of the land itself. Every good promise has come true, and the covenant works in both directions.
Like chapters 13-22, a narrative and legal chapter with no date of its own. It comes long after the rest of Joshua 21:44 and the eastern tribes' return (22), and before the assembly at Shechem (24). The book does not say how old Joshua was, so the early/late conquest question applies unchanged (c. 1406 BC on the early date this project foregrounds, c. 1230-1210 BC on the late).

Verses 1–2 · The old man calls the nation, Joshua 23:1–2: the rest of v1 is Joshua 21:44 said again, and the audience is every level of the people

★★★ The chapter opens by repeating the rest the book has already reported. V1 says Jehovah had given rest to Israel from all their enemies round about, the same rest Joshua 21:44 (already on these pages) gave, and the verb ‘had given rest’ is the one Joshua 22:4 used of the eastern tribes’ brothers (already on these pages). The chapter begins where the book’s last land-division left off, and the speech follows from that rest.

★★ Joshua 13:1 said the same of him, and the phrase returns here. The Hebrew says Joshua was zaqen, ‘old’, and that he had come ba ba-yamim, ‘into the days’, a phrase for advanced age (Joshua 13:1, already on these pages). The ASV reads ‘well stricken in years’, the NWT 2013 ‘advanced in years’, and the NWT 1984 ‘advanced in days’; this translation keeps the older English phrase.

★ Joshua calls everyone, and the list of offices is the chapter’s first structure. V2 names elders, heads, judges and officers: the whole civil and religious apparatus of the people, the same four groups Deuteronomy 16:18 gives when it orders judges and officers in every town (Deuteronomy 16:18, already on these pages). The speech is for everyone who is responsible for keeping the law.

Verses 3–5 · What Jehovah did, and what he will still do, Joshua 23:3–5: the nations to be driven out, and the land as an inheritance by lot

★★ V3 gives the history: ‘You have seen all that Jehovah your God has done’. The verb ‘you have seen’ is the eyewitness verb, and the sentence sends the audience back over the whole conquest they took part in. The verb nilcham, ‘fought’, is the same one Joshua 10:14 and 10:42 use of God fighting for Israel (both already on these pages).

★★ V4 says Joshua has allotted the remaining nations to the tribes as an inheritance, by lot. The verb hipal is to cast or fall, the verb for the lot. The nations that remain are part of the inheritance, which turns the unconquered peoples into the lot of Israel’s tribes. The boundaries run from the Jordan to the Great Sea, the western sea of Joshua’s own map.

★ V5 promises that Jehovah will drive them out, and that Israel will possess their land. The verb yarash is the one used in Joshua 21:43 (already on these pages) for the land given and possessed. The promise is conditional on nothing in v5; the conditions come in v6 onward.

Verses 6–8 · Be strong, keep the law, and cleave to Jehovah, Joshua 23:6–8: three negatives and one verb, and the verb is dabaq again

★★ V6 says ‘be very strong’, and it then turns the strength into obedience. The strength is for ‘keeping and doing all that is written in the book of the law of Moses’. The phrase sefer torat Moshe is the book’s own name for the law (already on these pages in Joshua 8:34). The command is not to turn aside to right or left, the same image as Deuteronomy 5:32 (already on these pages).

★★ V7 names three prohibitions: do not mention their gods, do not swear by them, do not serve or bow down to them. The verbs are the ones the second commandment uses (Exodus 20:5, already on these pages). The chapter has moved from the nations’ land to their gods’ names, and the list of verbs follows the order of the decalogue.

★★ V8 turns the prohibitions into the single positive command: cleave to Jehovah. Dabaq is the verb for holding fast, the verb of Genesis 2:24 (already on these pages) for a man joined to his wife, and the translation keeps it as ‘cleave’, the same verb as Joshua 22:5.

Verses 9–10 · The one who chases a thousand, Joshua 23:9–10: no man has stood against you, and one of you puts a thousand to flight

★★ V9 says no one has stood before Israel to this day. The sentence repeats the claim of Joshua 21:44 (already on these pages), now stated as the people’s own experience. The verb amad, ‘stand’, is the verb of resistance: no one has stood in front of you.

★★ V10 says ‘one of you chases a thousand’, and the sentence echoes Leviticus 26:8. The Leviticus verse promises that five will chase a hundred and a hundred will chase ten thousand, and this verse makes the same promise of divine fighting in a single sentence. Jehovah is the one who fights, and the arithmetic is his. This translation keeps ‘puts a thousand to flight’ for the verb yirdof, which means to chase or pursue.

Verses 11–12 · Take heed, and the warning about marriage, Joshua 23:11–12: intermarriage as the way back, in a verb that appears in one verse

★★★ The verb in v12 for intermarriage stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The form hithchattem, ‘you will intermarry’, is found only here, checked consonantally across the whole corpus, and the verb’s prohibition in the law is Deuteronomy 7:3 (already on these pages), which this verse is written against. The warning is therefore the book’s own, in the Hebrew form it used before.

★ V12 says ‘if you turn back’, and the warning is about turning, not about the nations. The verb shuv, ‘turn back’, is the verb of repentance and apostasy in the prophets. The warning is that the people will go back to the nations they were told to leave; the verb is used of the return, not of the arrival.

Verse 13 · Snare, trap, whip, thorns, Joshua 23:13: the nations as a trap that stays after the victory

★★ V13 gives four images for the nations that remain: a snare, a trap, a whip in your sides, thorns in your eyes. The first two are the hunter’s words. The form lefach, ‘for a snare’, stands in fifteen verses of the Hebrew Bible and the noun moqesh in twenty-one, both checked consonantally; together they are the book’s pair of words for a trap set for the unwary.

★★ The last two images are about pain, not capture. ‘Scourge in your sides’ and ‘thorns in your eyes’ describe a living irritation. The thorn word is written with a doubled nun, a spelling that the corpus search does not reach with the usual form, so this note does not claim a parallel for it.

★ The threat is that the land will be lost, not that the nations will be destroyed. The verb is abad (‘perish’), and the clause ‘until you perish from off this good land’ makes the land itself the subject of the loss.

Verse 14 · ‘The way of all the earth’ and ‘not one word has failed’, Joshua 23:14: the formula of the book’s own farewell, and a sentence the shelf reads two ways

★★★ ‘Not one word has failed’ stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible. The clause lo-nafal davar is found in Joshua 21:43 (already on these pages), here, and 1 Kings 8:56 (not yet on these pages). Here it is the last of Joshua’s three uses and the one that closes his speech. The Hebrew repeats the clause twice in the verse, and the translation keeps the repetition.

★★★ ‘The way of all the earth’ is a phrase for death, and it stands in two verses. Bederek kol-ha-aretz stands here and in 1 Kings 2:2 (not yet on these pages), where David says the same thing before he dies. The NWT 1984, the ASV, the KJV and the TNM 1987 keep the phrase; the NWT 2013 and the TNM 2019 both reword it as ‘about to die’.

⚠ The shelf does not agree with itself on this sentence, and the note says which edition says what. The NWT 1984 reads ‘I am going today in the way of all the earth’, and the NWT 2013 reads ‘I am about to die’. The TNM 1987 reads ‘me voy por el camino de toda la tierra’, and the TNM 2019 reads ‘estoy a punto de morir’. This translation keeps the Hebrew phrase; the revisions move the idiom toward the sense, which is a decision about English and Spanish, not about the text.

Verses 15–16 · The reverse of the promise, Joshua 23:15–16: all the good things came, so all the evil things will come, and the covenant is the hinge

★★ V15 turns the promise round: ‘as all the good things have come upon you, so the LORD will bring all the evil things’. The grammar is a conditional mirror: the good that came is the reason for the evil that will come. The sentence is the chapter’s most exact statement of the covenant curses, and it reuses the verb of v14 for coming to pass.

★★ V16 names the breach as the covenant: ‘when you transgress the covenant of Jehovah your God’. The word berit is the book’s own term for the bond between Israel and God, and the verse is the last mention of it in the speech. The next chapter returns to the covenant as the subject of the assembly at Shechem.

Echoes paid. Joshua 21:43–45 (already on these pages) gave the land and the rest that v1 begins from, and the ‘not one word failed’ clause of v14 closes the same formula the book opened in Joshua 21:45. The people of Joshua 22 (already on these pages) are told in v5 to love and cleave, and the verb is repeated here at v8.

Echoes planted. Joshua 24 (not yet on these pages) is the assembly at Shechem, where the covenant of v16 is renewed. The gods Joshua names in v7 return as the gods Israel is told to put away in Joshua 24:14. The ‘way of all the earth’ of v14 has its match in David’s farewell at 1 Kings 2:2 (not yet on these pages).

Decisions. The Hebrew has no verse gap; the English and the Masoretic numbering agree for all sixteen verses. The ‘way of all the earth’ sentence is kept as the Hebrew phrase, with the revisions named in the note, and the Hebrew mokesh and pach are given their own entries.

Library. Two new dictionary entries are needed in both languages: pach (the snare) and moqesh (the trap), which the chapter’s v13 uses as a pair. The other terms (torah, dabaq, berit) already have entries.

⚠ A note on order: Joshua stands on these pages at chapters 1–23. Joshua 24 remains the open sequence, the assembly at Shechem and Joshua’s death.
