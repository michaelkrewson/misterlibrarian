The same letter, still written from Macedonia, and its last chapter. Paul announces his third visit to Corinth, quotes the law of two or three witnesses, warns those who sinned before that he will not spare, tells the Corinthians to test themselves, and closes with five commands, a holy kiss and a three-part blessing.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year for the letter. It looks ahead to a third visit; at Acts 20:2–3 Paul spends three months in Greece, a stay commonly identified with that visit, though Luke does not name Corinth.

Verses 1–2 · the third visit, two or three witnesses, and “I will not spare”

“This is the third time I am coming to you.” Triton touto, “this third time,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: 12:14, where Paul was “ready to come,” and this one, where he is coming. The letter counts the visits without describing them. The first founded the congregation, and Acts says Paul stayed in Corinth “a year and six months” (Acts 18:11). The second is the one this chapter calls being “present the second time,” most likely the visit “in sorrow” of 2:1; Acts does not mention it. At Acts 20:2–3 Paul comes “into Greece” and spends three months there, and that stay is commonly taken to be the third visit, though Luke does not name Corinth. The KJV and the ASV both read “This is the third time I am coming to you”; the NIV reads “This will be my third visit to you.”

⚠ “Two or three witnesses” — Deuteronomy 19:15, with “and” where the Hebrew has “or.” The second sentence is the law of witnesses, quoted without a formula. In the Hebrew of Deuteronomy 19:15 “a matter shall stand” at the mouth of two witnesses “or” at the mouth of three. The Greek Old Testament reads kai, “and,” for that “or,” and adds pan, “every,” which the Hebrew does not have: stēsetai pan rhēma, “every matter shall stand.” Paul shortens the sentence and keeps both. When Jesus quotes the same law for a brother who will not listen, the Greek has “or” (Matthew 18:16). All seven versions on the English shelf print “or” here, and so does the NWT’s 2013 revision; this translation prints the “and” that is in the Greek, and keeps “at the mouth of” and “shall stand” from its own Deuteronomy. Rhēma is a thing said, and so a word, but in the Greek Old Testament it also stands for the Hebrew davar, which is both a word and a matter. The KJV reads “In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word be established”; the NWT 1984 reads “At the mouth of two witnesses or of three every matter must be established”; the Douay and the Geneva both read “In the mouth of two or three witnesses shall every word stand,” the Douay from the Vulgate’s vel, “or.”

⚠ Who are the witnesses? A hearing at Corinth, or the visits themselves. The quotation can be read two ways. On one reading it promises due process: when Paul arrives, charges will be settled as the law requires, on the word of two or three witnesses and not on rumor. On the other, the visits and warnings are themselves the witnesses: a second visit, a warning now in writing, and a third visit on the way. The text does not say which, and this page does not choose. The TLB chooses inside the verse: “Well, this is my third warning as I come now for this visit.”

“I said it before, and I say it beforehand” — three words with pro-. Proeirēka kai prolegō, “I have said before and I say beforehand,” and then tois proēmartēkosin, “those who sinned before.” Proamartanō stands in two verses of the New Testament, 12:21 and this one, and the second picks up the first: the people Paul feared he would have to mourn over are the people he now warns. “As when I was present the second time and now that I am absent” is the letter’s pairing of present and absent, as at 10:11. The Byzantine text adds graphō, “I write,” after “now”; Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th do not have it. That is why the KJV reads “and being absent now I write to them which heretofore have sinned” and the Geneva reads “so write I now being absent”; the NWT 1984, without it, reads “I say in advance to those who have sinned before.”

“I will not spare.” Ou pheisomai. The verb is pheidomai, and the letter has used it twice before: at 1:23 Paul did not come again to Corinth “to spare you,” and at 12:6 he held back from boasting, which this translation printed “I hold back.” Here the sparing stops. At Romans 11:21 the same verb, “God did not spare the branches according to nature,” stands one verse before apotomia, “severity”; this chapter sets the two side by side again, “I will not spare” here and “deal sharply” at v10. The NWT 1984 reads “if ever I come again I will not spare”; the TLB reads “this time I come ready to punish severely and I will not spare them.”

Verses 3–4 · proof of the Christ who speaks in me, and crucified out of weakness

“You are seeking proof” — the test that runs to verse 7. Dokimē, “proof,” is the first of a family of words that fills vv3–7: dokimē here, the verb dokimazō at v5, adokimos, “unapproved,” at vv5, 6 and 7, and dokimos, “approved,” at v7. The noun has come before in this letter: Paul wrote “to know whether you are proved” (2:9), the Macedonians gave “during a great test of affliction” (8:2), and the collection itself was a “proof” (9:13). Now the Corinthians want proof of Paul. The Douay makes the verse a question, “Do you seek a proof of Christ that speaketh in me,” following the Vulgate’s An experimentum quaeritis; the Geneva reads “Seeing that ye seek experience of Christ”; the NIV reads “since you are demanding proof that Christ is speaking through me.”

“Not weak toward you, but powerful in you.” Paul’s critics said his bodily presence “is weak” (10:10); the Christ who speaks in him, he answers, is not weak toward them. Dynateō stands in three verses of the New Testament: Romans 14:4, where “the Lord is able to make him stand,” 9:8, where “God is able,” and this one. Here it is set against “weak,” so this translation prints “is powerful.” The ASV reads “who to you-ward is not weak, but is powerful in you”; the NWT 1984 reads “is not weak toward YOU but is powerful among YOU.”

⚠ “Crucified out of weakness” — and alive out of the power of God. Estaurōthē ex astheneias, alla zē ek dynameōs theou: the same preposition twice, ek, “out of,” naming what each thing came from. This translation keeps it both times, and a third time in “we will live with him out of the power of God.” Weakness was the condition of the cross; the power of God is the source of the life. It is the sentence of 12:9, “power is made perfect in weakness,” told now of Christ and then of the apostles. The Byzantine text adds ei, “if,” which makes the first clause a concession: the KJV reads “For though he was crucified through weakness.” Without it, the ASV reads “for he was crucified through weakness”; the NIV reads “he was crucified in weakness.” The NWT 1984 reads “he was impaled owing to weakness,” its rendering of stauroō, in keeping with its teaching that Jesus died on an upright stake and not on a cross; this translation prints “crucified,” as at 1 Corinthians 1:23.

“We will live with him … toward you.” The future “we will live” can point to the resurrection. But the sentence ends eis hymas, “toward you,” the same words as v3’s “not weak toward you,” and that ties the life and the power to the coming visit: the Corinthians will meet them at Corinth. The Greek allows both, and so does this translation. The NWT 1984 reads “we are weak with him” where the Greek printed here has “in him”; the NIV reads “we will live with him in our dealing with you.”

Verses 5–7 · “examine yourselves,” approved and unapproved

⚠ “Examine yourselves” — test yourselves, put yourselves to the proof. The two commands use two verbs. Peirazete is the verb of testing and of tempting, the one at 1 Corinthians 10:13, where God will not let you “be tested beyond what you are able to bear” (dictionary); this translation prints “test yourselves.” Dokimazete is the verb of the assayer, who puts metal or coin to the proof, and it belongs to the family of v3’s “proof,” so this translation prints “put yourselves to the proof.” In both clauses the Greek puts heautous, “yourselves,” first: the Corinthians asked for proof of Christ in Paul, and he hands the test back to them. The KJV reads “Examine yourselves, whether ye be in the faith; prove your own selves”; the ASV reads “Try your own selves, whether ye are in the faith; prove your own selves”; the NIV reads “Examine yourselves to see whether you are in the faith; test yourselves”; the NWT 1984 reads “Keep testing whether YOU are in the faith, keep proving what YOU yourselves are.”

“Unapproved” — adokimos, three times in three verses. Adokimos stands in eight verses of the New Testament, and three of them are in this chapter, vv5, 6 and 7. It is what fails the test (dictionary). This translation prints “unapproved,” as at Romans 1:28, “an unapproved mind,” so that the pair in v7, “approved” and “unapproved,” stays visible; at 1 Corinthians 9:27 the same word was Paul’s fear for himself, “disqualified,” and at Titus 1:16 it is “worthless” for any good work. “Approved,” dokimos, is the word of 10:18: not the one who commends himself is approved, “but the one whom Jehovah commends.” The KJV reads “except ye be reprobates?” and the ASV reads “unless indeed ye be reprobate,” English forms of the Vulgate’s reprobi; the NWT 1984 reads “Unless YOU are disapproved”; the NIV reads “unless, of course, you fail the test.”

“Jesus Christ is in you” — in each of you, or among you? The “you” is plural, as in v3’s “powerful in you,” and en hymin can mean within each of them or among them as a congregation; the English “in you” keeps both. The NWT 1984 reads “Jesus Christ is in union with YOU.” Tregelles and the Greek text behind the NIV read “Christ Jesus,” and so does the Vulgate, which is why the NIV and the Douay both read “Christ Jesus is in you.” The Byzantine text adds estin, “is,” which the sense supplies anyway.

“Even if we are like those who are unapproved.” Verse 7 prays the opposite of what a man defending himself would pray. Paul asks that they do nothing wrong, “not so that we may appear approved.” One way to read the reasoning: if they put things right before he comes, he will have nothing to punish, the power promised in v4 will have no occasion to show itself, and to his critics he may still look like a man with no proof to give. He prays for that outcome anyway. The critical text reads “we pray”; the Byzantine reads euchomai, “I pray,” and so the KJV reads “Now I pray to God that ye do no evil” and the Geneva “Now I pray unto God,” while the ASV reads “Now we pray to God that ye do no evil.” The Douay, following the Vulgate’s nos autem ut reprobi simus, turns the last clause into part of the prayer: “and that we may be as reprobates.” The TLB reads “not because that will be a feather in our caps.”

Verses 8–10 · nothing against the truth, “your mending,” and authority for building up

“We can do nothing against the truth, but for the truth.” The Greek has no “only”; the NWT 1984 and the NIV both supply it, “but only for the truth,” while the ASV reads “For we can do nothing against the truth, but for the truth.” Verse 9 then turns 12:10 around. There it was “when I am weak, then I am powerful”; here Paul rejoices “whenever we are weak and you are powerful.” Dynatoi, “powerful,” is the adjective of 12:10, and the power now belongs to them.

⚠ “Your mending” — katartisis, the net-mender’s word. Katartisis stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and its verb comes back two verses later in the commands of v11, “be mended.” The verb is katartizō, which this translation printed “mending the nets” at Mark 1:19, and which Paul used to these same people near the start of his first letter: no tears among you, “but that you be mended together in the same mind” (1 Corinthians 1:10). The word comes early in the first letter to Corinth and at the end of the second. It is a repair, not a polish, and the older “perfection” reads it as a finish: the KJV reads “even your perfection,” the ASV “even your perfecting,” and the Douay “your perfection,” from the Vulgate’s consummationem. The NWT 1984 reads “YOUR being readjusted”; the NIV reads “that you may be fully restored” (dictionary).

“Deal sharply” — apotomōs, and authority for building up. Apotomōs stands in two verses of the New Testament: Titus 1:13, “rebuke them sharply,” and this one. Its noun is the “severity” of God at Romans 11:22 (dictionary). Paul writes while absent so that he will not have to use it when present: the letter goes ahead of him to make the severity unnecessary. The authority is the one “the Lord gave” “for building you up and not for tearing you down” at 10:8, said again here without the “you”; the building up came back at 12:19 too, “for your building up.” Kathairesis, “tearing down,” stands in three verses of the New Testament, and all three are in this letter: “tearing down strongholds” at 10:4, 10:8, and this one. The KJV reads “lest being present I should use sharpness”; the ASV reads “that I may not when present deal sharply”; the NWT 1984 reads “I may not act with severity”; the NIV reads “I may not have to be harsh.”

Verses 11–12 · “rejoice” or “farewell,” five commands, the God of love, and a holy kiss

⚠ “Rejoice” or “farewell”? Chairete. Loipon, “finally,” is literally “as for the rest.” Then chairete, which in Greek is two things at once: the command “rejoice” and the everyday word for greeting someone or taking leave. When the risen Jesus meets the women at Matthew 28:9, it is the word this translation printed “Greetings”; at Philippians 4:4 it is “Rejoice in the Lord always.” Here it heads four more commands, so this translation prints “rejoice,” and the farewell is still in it. The KJV and the ASV both read “Finally, brethren, farewell”; the Geneva reads “Finally brethren, fare ye well”; the NWT 1984 reads “continue to rejoice”; the Douay reads “For the rest, brethren, rejoice,” the Vulgate’s gaudete.

Be mended, be comforted, be of the same mind, be at peace. “Be mended” is the word of v9 made a command. Parakaleisthe can be passive, “be comforted,” or middle, “heed my appeal” or “encourage one another”; this translation prints “be comforted,” because it is the verb that sounds through 1:4, “who comforts us in all our affliction,” and a letter that opened with comfort ends by telling its readers to be comforted. “Be of the same mind,” to auto phroneite, is what Paul asked of Euodia and Syntyche at Philippians 4:2; the phrase stands in four verses of the New Testament. Eirēneuō, “be at peace,” stands in four verses: Mark 9:50, “be at peace with one another,” Romans 12:18, this one, and 1 Thessalonians 5:13. The NWT 1984 reads “to be readjusted, to be comforted, to think in agreement, to live peaceably”; the KJV reads “Be perfect, be of good comfort, be of one mind, live in peace”; the NIV reads “Strive for full restoration, encourage one another”; the Douay reads “take exhortation”; the TLB reads “Pay attention to what I have said.”

“The God of love and peace.” “The God of peace” comes in other letters of Paul (Romans 15:33, Philippians 4:9), but “the God of love” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The Douay reverses the pair, “the God of peace and of love shall be with you,” following the Vulgate’s Deus pacis et dilectionis; the KJV and the ASV keep the Greek order, “the God of love and peace shall be with you.”

“A holy kiss” — and “all the holy ones.” En hagiō philēmati: the kiss was the ordinary greeting of family and friends, and among believers it is called holy (dictionary). The holy kiss stands in four verses of the New Testament, all near the end of a letter of Paul: Romans 16:16, 1 Corinthians 16:20, this one, and 1 Thessalonians 5:26; 1 Peter 5:14 has “a kiss of love.” Then “all the holy ones greet you”: hagios again, and this translation keeps the echo by printing “holy ones,” as at 1:1. The NWT 1984 keeps it too, “Greet one another with a holy kiss,” and then, in its own verse, “All the holy ones send YOU their greetings.” The KJV loses it: “All the saints salute you.” The TLB drops the kiss: “Greet each other warmly in the Lord.” The holy ones who send greetings are the believers with Paul as he writes; the letter places him in Macedonia (7:5).

Verse 13 · the grace, the love and the fellowship — the blessing that closes the letter

⚠ 2 Corinthians 13:14 or 13:13? Where the verse numbers part. The Greek printed here divides this chapter into thirteen verses, with the kiss and the greeting together as v12 and the blessing as v13, and the Vulgate, the Douay and the Geneva Bible divide it the same way. The King James Version divides v12 in two, so that “All the saints salute you” is its v13 and the blessing is its 13:14; the ASV, the NIV and the NWT 1984 do the same. Among Greek editions, the Textus Receptus behind the KJV has fourteen verses and the Byzantine text of Robinson–Pierpont thirteen. This translation follows the Greek it prints, so the blessing is 13:13 here and 13:14 in five of the seven English versions on the shelf. Nothing in the text changes.

The only three-part grace in Paul’s letters. Paul’s letters close with a wish of grace, and everywhere else it has one member: “The grace of the Lord Jesus be with you” (1 Corinthians 16:23); “The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with you” (Romans 16:20); “The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit” (Philippians 4:23). Of those grace-wishes, this is the only one with three members: the grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, the love of God, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit. The grace is the last echo of 12:9, “My grace is enough for you,” and the love is that of v11’s “God of love.” The NWT 1984 renders charis “undeserved kindness,” as it usually does: “The undeserved kindness of the Lord Jesus Christ.” The Douay reads “The grace of our Lord Jesus Christ,” with the Vulgate’s nostri, “our,” which the Greek here does not have.

⚠ Three names in one sentence — the verse in the Trinity debate. The verse sets the Lord Jesus Christ, God and the Holy Spirit side by side in one blessing, and it has had a long life in Christian worship, where it is still spoken as a blessing. Readers who hold the doctrine of the Trinity, formulated in the creeds of the fourth century, have long cited it as one of the clearest places where the New Testament names the three together. Readers who do not hold that doctrine point out that the verse calls only one of the three “God” and says nothing about how the three are related. This page reports both and does not vote. What a reader can check in the verse itself: it names the three in one breath, it gives each a gift (grace, love, fellowship), and it calls the second simply “God.” Capital letters are an editor’s decision, since the oldest Greek manuscripts are written entirely in capitals; this translation prints “Holy Spirit,” as it prints “the Spirit” at 1:22 and 3:17. The NWT 1984 prints “the sharing in the holy spirit,” in lower case, as it does throughout, in keeping with its teaching that the holy spirit is God’s active force and not a person; the KJV reads “the communion of the Holy Ghost.”

“The fellowship of the Holy Spirit” — given by the Spirit, or shared in the Spirit? Koinōnia is sharing, partnership, having in common (dictionary); this translation has printed it “partnership” at 1 Corinthians 1:9 and “a sharing in the blood of Christ” at 1 Corinthians 10:16. With a genitive it can mean the fellowship the Spirit creates among believers, or the share believers have in the Spirit; “the fellowship of” keeps both, as the “of” does in “the grace of the Lord” and “the love of God” beside it. The phrase stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The ASV reads “the communion of the Holy Spirit”; the NIV reads “the fellowship of the Holy Spirit”; the Douay reads “the communication of the Holy Ghost,” the Vulgate’s communicatio.

No “Amen,” and a postscript that is not part of the letter. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th end with “you”; the Byzantine text adds Amēn, which is why the KJV and the Douay both end “be with you all. Amen.” The Textus Receptus then adds a subscription that the critical editions do not print: “The second to the Corinthians was written from Philippi of Macedonia, through Titus and Luke.” The Geneva prints it after the letter: “written from Philippi, a city in Macedonia, and sent by Titus and Lucas.” The letter itself places Paul in Macedonia (7:5) and has Titus on his way to Corinth (8:17), but it names no city of writing, and Luke’s name does not appear in it.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “approved” and “unapproved” for dokimos and adokimos at 13:5, 13:6 and 13:7, with “proof” for dokimē at 13:3 and “put to the proof” for dokimazō at 13:5; “mending” and “be mended” for the katartizō family at 13:9 and 13:11, as at Mark 1:19 and 1 Corinthians 1:10; “out of” three times for ek at 13:4; “spare” for pheidomai at 13:2, as at 1:23; “sharply” for apotomōs at 13:10, as at Titus 1:13; “for building up and not for tearing down” at 13:10, as at 10:8; “be comforted” at 13:11, the comfort of 1:3–7; “holy kiss” and “holy ones” for hagios at 13:12; and “and” between the two witnesses and the three at 13:1, where the shelf prints “or.”

Echoes paid. The third visit of 12:14 is announced (13:1); those who sinned before at 12:21 are warned (13:2); the sparing of 1:23 ends (13:2); power in weakness from 12:9 is told of Christ crucified (13:4); the building up of 10:8 and 12:19 returns (13:10); the comfort of 1:4 becomes a command (13:11); and the grace of 12:9 opens the closing blessing (13:13). Outside the letter: the two or three witnesses of Deuteronomy 19:15 and Matthew 18:16; the mending of Mark 1:19 and 1 Corinthians 1:10; the unapproved mind of Romans 1:28; the severity of Romans 11:22; the same mind at Philippians 4:2; and the holy kiss of Romans 16:16 and 1 Corinthians 16:20.

Echoes planted. The mending of a brother caught in a fault at Galatians 6:1; be at peace and the holy kiss at 1 Thessalonians 5:13 and 5:26; the kiss of love at 1 Peter 5:14; the same mind at Philippians 2:2; the unapproved at 2 Timothy 3:8 (there “disqualified”) and Hebrews 6:8; and the one-member grace that closes Galatians and the letters to the Thessalonians.

This is the last chapter of 2 Corinthians. The next letter is Galatians 1, already on these pages.
