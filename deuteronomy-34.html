The last chapter of the Torah: Moses climbs Mount Nebo, is shown the whole land, and dies at a hundred and twenty with his eye undimmed and his vigor unspent — buried by a hand the grammar never names, in a grave no one has ever found. Thirty days of mourning; Joshua, filled with the spirit of wisdom, takes up a hand-off two hands strong though only one was commanded; and the Torah’s own closing verdict, spoken nowhere else in the whole Hebrew Bible: there has not arisen a prophet since in Israel like Moses, whom Jehovah knew face to face.
Still the plains of Moab in the fortieth year, closing the weekly portion Vezot Haberakhah and the annual reading cycle itself. Hebrew and English verse numbers align throughout, and the Masoretic text carries no internal paragraph break at all — one unmarked unit until the layout mark that closes the whole Torah after v12.

Verses 1–4 · The oath that closed Genesis closes the Torah too, and a sea named without its own name splits the shelf three ways

★★★ ‘To Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob’ is Deuteronomy’s own refrain, and v4 is its seventh and last performance. Searched across the whole archived Hebrew Bible, the exact three-name formula stands in eleven verses. Seven of the eleven are in Deuteronomy alone — 1:8, 6:10, 9:5, 9:27, 29:12, 30:20 (all already on these pages), and this verse. Deuteronomy’s own opening use, at 1:8, already named where the formula came from: Genesis 50:24 (already on these pages), Joseph’s dying words — ‘the land which he swore to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob’ — quoted there as a promise about to be kept at the border. Here it recurs a seventh time, from God’s own mouth instead of Joseph’s report of it, first person instead of third (nishbati, ‘I swore,’ against Genesis’s nishba, ‘he swore’), spoken over a second dying man at the opposite end of the same five books. Genesis ends with the man who asked to be carried up out of Egypt into that land; Deuteronomy ends with the man who is shown it and dies outside it.

★★ The sea in v2 is named by direction, not by name, and the shelf splits three ways over what to do with that. Ha-yam ha-acharon, literally ‘the hindmost sea’ — acharon being ‘last, rearmost,’ which is what the Mediterranean is from someone facing the customary east — is a different construction from the bare directional yam this project’s own dictionary entry already tracks from Deuteronomy 33:23 (already on these pages), where the word alone stands for ‘west’ with no literal sea intended. Here the sea IS intended, and three tiers of commitment show up on the shelf: ASV keeps the literal image, ‘the hinder sea’; KJV and Geneva 1599 read ‘the utmost sea’, and the Douay-Rheims, following the Vulgate, ‘the furthermost sea’ — three renderings of distance without direction. NWT 1984 and its 2013 revision both cash the direction out plainly, ‘the western sea’, joined in Spanish by the TNM 1987 and TNM 2019 («el mar occidental») and by RV60 («el mar occidental»). NIV and TLB go a step further still and name the body of water outright, ‘the Mediterranean Sea’, joined in Spanish by the NVI, «el mar Mediterráneo». This translation follows the direction — ‘the western sea’ — extending the same convention v33:23 already established, short of naming a sea the Hebrew itself declines to name.

⚠ V1’s ‘as far as Dan’ is very likely the same later-editor’s update this project has already flagged once before. The Dan entry, built at Genesis 14:14 (already on these pages), already notes that the town was not called Dan until the tribe of Dan captured and renamed it centuries later (Judges 18:29, not yet on these pages) — so a name Israel does not yet possess is used here to describe the panorama Moses is shown at the very moment the Torah's own traditional author dies. Whether that is a later scribe's small update to an old text, or something else, is exactly the kind of seam this project reports rather than resolves; both instances of the anachronism now stand on these pages.

Verse 4 · The last verb the whole Torah applies to Moses is a WARNING, not a threat, and it is aimed at a promise he will not personally collect

★ ‘I have let you see it with your eyes, but you shall not go over there’ changes nothing the previous chapter did not already say. Deuteronomy 32:52 (already on these pages) already told Moses, in almost the same words, ‘you will see the land only from a distance; you will not go there.’ This verse is Jehovah keeping that word exactly, verse for verse, with nothing added and nothing softened at the last possible moment. The mercy and the sentence arrive together a second time, and neither one is renegotiated.

Verses 5–6 · The commanded formula for Aaron’s death is never spoken over Moses’ own, and twelve of thirteen versions leave unnamed who buries him

★★★ Moses is commanded to be ‘gathered to his people,’ exactly as Aaron was — and when the moment finally comes, the narrative never says it. Deuteronomy 32:50 (already on these pages) ordered Moses to ‘die on the mountain… and be gathered to your people, as Aaron your brother died… and was gathered to his people.’ Numbers 20:24, 26 (already on these pages) faithfully uses the idiom of Aaron, twice. This verse does not: vayamat sham Mosheh… al-pi Adonai, ‘Moses died there… by the mouth of Jehovah’ — the promised formula from 32:50 is simply not repeated at the moment it was written to describe. The dictionary’s own asaf entry counts six verses using the idiom across the whole Hebrew Bible, Aaron among them; this would have been a seventh, and it is not one.

★★ ‘By the mouth of Jehovah’ is a title this book has not used of a death before, and thirteen shelf witnesses leave its counterpart in v6 grammatically unclaimed. V6’s vayikbor oto, ‘and HE buried him,’ names no subject at all — the closest available antecedent, by the Hebrew’s own grammar, is Jehovah, the last named actor in v5. KJV, ASV, Geneva 1599, NIV, NWT (both editions), TNM (both editions), NVI, RV 1909 and RV60 all keep the same unclaimed ‘he’. ⚠ Only the Living Bible resolves it outright — ‘THE LORD buried him in a valley’ — naming, in a paraphrase, exactly the reading the other twelve leave to the reader’s own inference.

⚠ A tension worth showing, not smoothing: the grave this chapter puts ‘opposite Beth-peor’ sits in territory this project has already given to Reuben, not Gad. Deuteronomy 33:21’s own note reported a later tradition reading Gad’s ‘portion of the lawgiver’ as Moses’ own unmarked grave, in Gad’s Transjordan allotment. But Numbers 32:37–38 (already on these pages) has the children of REUBEN, not Gad, building and naming Nebo itself among their own cities — the same summit this chapter opens on. The tradition and the tribal map pull two different directions; this translation reports both rather than picking a winner.

Verse 7 · Moses is the first person in the whole Torah to die at the number Genesis fixed for every one after him, and the Douay-Rheims reads his teeth instead of his strength

★★★ A hundred and twenty is not a round number here either — it is the ceiling Genesis set for the whole human race, and Moses is the first person the Torah records reaching it exactly. Genesis 6:3 (already on these pages) reported two old readings of Jehovah’s ‘their days shall be 120 years’ — a lifespan cap, or a grace period before the flood — and left the fork open, noting plainly that people go on living past 120 for many chapters after it. None of them lands on the number exactly: Abraham dies at 175, Isaac at 180, Jacob at 147, Joseph at 110, Aaron at 123 (Numbers 33:39, already on these pages). Moses is the first, and across the whole Torah the only, death recorded at exactly 120 — whichever of Genesis 6’s two readings is right, this is the number's first perfect match.

⚠ ‘His vigor had not fled’ renders a genuine hapax, and the Douay-Rheims reads an entirely different part of the body. Lechoh’s root and sense are disputed, fixed by no other biblical occurrence; this translation follows the mainstream ‘vigor, freshness’ sense the rest of the shelf shares. KJV, ASV and Geneva 1599 all read ‘nor his natural force abated’; NWT 1984 ‘his vital strength had not fled’; NIV ‘nor his strength gone’. The Douay-Rheims alone breaks from all of them — ‘neither were his TEETH moved’ — the same translation-trail this project has met before, where an obscure Hebrew word reaches English by way of a Latin Vulgate that read the difficulty differently at the source.

★ ‘His eye had not grown dim’ uses a verb form the whole Hebrew Bible keeps to exactly two verses. Searched across the whole archived Hebrew Bible, kahatah stands in exactly two verses: this one, and Ezekiel 21:12 (not yet on these pages), where it is not an eye that grows dim but ‘every spirit’ that faints at approaching news — the same rare verb, applied once to a hundred-and-twenty-year-old man’s eyesight and once, centuries later, to a whole nation’s courage.

Verse 8 · The mourning Numbers already promised, paid to the day

★★★ This is a debt this project’s own notes have been carrying since Numbers 20. Numbers 20:29’s own note, on Aaron’s death, said outright: ‘it is the same mourning Moses will be given at Deuteronomy 34:8, and the only two people in the Torah who get it are the two who were told at this rock that they would not arrive.’ It arrives here exactly as promised — thirty days, the whole house of Israel, the identical span — closing a debt this project has owed for thirty-two chapters.

Verse 9 · One hand was commanded at Numbers 27; two were used there, and this verse still calls it two

★★★ This is the third time this project has had to report how many hands Moses laid on Joshua, and the answer has not changed since Numbers 27. Numbers 27:18 (already on these pages) commanded Moses to lay his HAND — singular — on Joshua; v23 reported that he laid his HANDS — plural — on him, an unauthorized addition the whole shelf preserves without remark. Here, in the Torah’s own retrospective summary, the verb is samakh… et-yadav, ‘laid… HIS HANDS,’ plural a second time. Ten of the thirteen shelf witnesses keep the plural with the Hebrew — KJV, ASV, Geneva 1599, Douay-Rheims, NIV, TLB all ‘his hands’, RV 1909, RV60 and the NVI «sus manos», and even the TNM 2019 «las manos» — but NWT 1984 and its 2013 revision both read ‘his HAND,’ singular, against the Hebrew and against their own Spanish sibling’s later correction: the TNM 1987 followed its English parent into the singular («su mano»), and the TNM 2019 broke from it, restoring the plural its own 1984-line English original never did.

⚠ ‘The spirit of WISDOM’ specifies something Numbers 27 left general. Numbers 27:18’s own charge called Joshua only ‘a man in whom there is spirit’ — ruach alone, indefinite. Here the noun gains a genitive it did not carry there, ruach chochmah, ‘a spirit OF WISDOM’ — read most naturally as this book’s own report of what the laying on of hands actually produced, rather than a repetition of what Joshua already had going in.

Verses 10–12 · The Torah closes on the idiom from Exodus 33, not the rarer one from Numbers 12 — and no sentence like this one exists anywhere else in the Bible

★★★ ‘There has not arisen a prophet’ is not a formula — it is spoken exactly once in the whole Hebrew Bible. Searched across the whole archived Hebrew Bible, the clause stands in exactly ONE verse: this one. The Torah’s own closing verdict on Moses is not a recurring refrain being repeated one last time, the way the patriarchal oath at v4 is — it is a sentence written once, here, and nowhere else.

★★★ And the idiom that verdict reaches for is a deliberate choice between two this project has already shown are not the same phrase. Numbers 12:6–8 (already on these pages) drew a sharp line between peh el peh, ‘mouth to mouth’ — God’s own rarer, more literal description of how he spoke with Moses — and panim el panim, ‘face to face,’ the more common idiom used first at Exodus 33:11 (already on these pages) and at Genesis 32:30 (already on these pages) of Jacob at the Jabbok. Searched across the whole archived Hebrew Bible, the exact idiom stands in five verses; three are now on these pages (Genesis 32:30, Exodus 33:11, and this one), and two are not (Judges 6:22, Ezekiel 20:35). The Torah’s own last word on Moses reaches back past Numbers 12’s rarer, more startling ‘mouth to mouth’ and closes instead on the same idiom it used of him at the tent of meeting, many books earlier in this project’s own count — and of Jacob, before either Moses or the Torah existed.

⚠ The newest Spanish revision breaks the echo the older editions kept. TNM 1987 reads «a quien Jehová conoció cara a cara», matching its English parent and the idiom’s two earlier appearances letter for letter; the TNM 2019 replaces it with a paraphrase, «a quien Jehová conoció tan estrechamente», ‘whom Jehovah knew so closely’ — true to the sense, but severing the one Spanish thread that could have tied this verse back to its own two earlier Genesis and Exodus sightings.

★ The Living Bible compresses the whole closing catalogue of signs and terror into one sentence, and prints it twice. Fetched directly rather than assumed: the Living Bible’s own text gives vv11 and 12 the identical English sentence, ‘And at God’s command he performed amazing miracles that have never been equaled’ — a paraphrase spanning both verses rather than rendering each in turn, exactly the kind of verse-boundary compression this shelf’s freest member is known for elsewhere.

Echoes paid. Numbers 20:29’s own promised thirty days of mourning for Moses (v8). Numbers 27:18, 23’s two-hands-for-one-commanded detail, still standing at v9. Genesis 6:3’s open fork over ‘120 years,’ given its first exact match at v7. Numbers 27:12’s first charge and Deuteronomy 32:49’s later naming of Nebo, both finally carried out at v1. Numbers 12:6–8’s distinction between ‘mouth to mouth’ and ‘face to face,’ resolved at v10 in the Torah’s own last word. And Deuteronomy 1:8’s own first citation of Genesis 50:24’s patriarchal oath, completing its seventh and final Deuteronomy performance at v4.

Decisions. Hebrew and English verse numbers align through this whole chapter, and the Masoretic text carries no internal paragraph break at all — a single unmarked unit until the mark that closes it: Mechon-Mamre’s own {ש} after v12, which its own glossary describes as ‘an empty line at the end of some books’ — the physical layout of the scroll putting a blank space under the Torah’s own last line. ‘Jehovah’ for the tetragrammaton and every established place-name spelling continue this project’s own conventions; ‘the western sea’ at v2 extends v33:23’s own direction-word rendering rather than introducing a new one.

Library. Three new dictionary entries in both languages: eved Yehovah, the title this chapter gives Moses at the moment of his death; kahah, the two-verse verb behind ‘grown dim’; and lechoh, the disputed hapax the Douay-Rheims alone reads as teeth rather than strength. Two existing entries extended in both languages: panim, gaining this chapter’s own ‘face to face’ as its sixth documented use across the Torah; and asaf, noting that the ‘gathered to his people’ formula commanded for Moses at 32:50 is never actually spoken over his own death. Five encyclopedia entries updated: Beth-peor, Abarim and Pisgah, each resolving a forward-claim this project made from an earlier chapter; and Moses and Joshua, both gaining a closing reference to the hand-off this chapter completes.

⚠ A note on order: the Torah — Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, and now Deuteronomy — stands complete on these pages, all 187 chapters, for the first time.
