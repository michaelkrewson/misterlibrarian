Hebron and Jerusalem: all the tribes and then all the elders of Israel come to David at Hebron, make him their bone and flesh, and anoint him king over Israel; his years are counted, seven years and six months over Judah and thirty-three in Jerusalem; he takes the Jebusite stronghold of Zion and calls it the city of David, Hiram of Tyre sends cedar and craftsmen, more wives and eleven sons are listed, and the Philistines, who come up twice into the valley of Rephaim, are struck at Baal-perazim and then from Geba to Gezer.
After Ish-bosheth’s death (2 Samuel 4:7–12). David reigns seven years and six months at Hebron (2:11; 5:5), then thirty-three years at Jerusalem (5:5), forty in all (5:4); the chapter does not date the Philistine campaigns within them.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–5 · “Your bone and your flesh” — all Israel comes to David at Hebron, the elders make a covenant, and the years of his reign are counted

Bone and flesh. “And all the tribes of Israel came to David at Hebron, and spoke, saying: ‘Look — we are your bone and your flesh’”: “your bone and your flesh” (with “your” in the singular) stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and 1 Chronicles 11:1. The pair is the language of kinship: the first man’s “Bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh” (Genesis 2:23), Laban to Jacob, “Surely you are my bone and my flesh” (Genesis 29:14), and Abimelech to the men of Shechem, “I am your bone and your flesh” (Judges 9:2, all already on these pages). David will say it himself to the elders of Judah and to Amasa (2 Samuel 19:13–14). The tribes come with the words that Abner had been carrying to them, “Both yesterday and the day before you were seeking David as king over you” (2 Samuel 3:17, already on these pages). The ASV has “thy bone and thy flesh”; the KJV “thy bone and thy flesh” also; the NIV “your own flesh and blood”; the NWT 1984 “your bone and your flesh”; the Douay “thy bone and thy flesh”; the Geneva “thy bones and thy flesh”; the TLB “your blood brothers”. The Greek has both nouns in the plural, “bones” and “flesh”.

Led out and brought in. “Both yesterday and the day before, when Saul was king over us, it was you who led out and brought in Israel; and Jehovah said to you: ‘You shall shepherd my people Israel, and you shall be leader over Israel’”: “the one who led out and brought in” stands in two verses, this one and 1 Chronicles 11:2. The idiom is a commander’s: Joshua is asked for “who will go out before them and who will come in before them, who will lead them out and who will bring them in” (Numbers 27:17), Moses at the end cannot “go out and come in” (Deuteronomy 31:2), David “went out and came in before the people” under Saul (1 Samuel 18:13) and Israel and Judah loved him “for he went out and came in before them” (18:16), and Achish says of David’s “going out and coming in with me in the camp” that it is good (29:6, all already on these pages). “You shall shepherd my people” stands in two verses, again this one and 1 Chronicles 11:2. The words are quoted as Jehovah’s; the nearest on these pages are “commanded him to be leader over his people” (1 Samuel 13:14), Abigail’s “commands you to be leader over Israel” (25:30) and Abner’s “Jehovah has spoken of David” (2 Samuel 3:18, all already on these pages); “leader” is nagid. The NIV has “you were the one who led Israel on their military campaigns … You will shepherd my people Israel, and you will become their ruler”; the NWT 1984 “you yourself became the one leading Israel out and bringing it in … you yourself will become leader over Israel”; the ASV “thou shalt be prince over Israel”; the KJV “thou shalt be a captain over Israel”; the Geneva “thou leddest Israel in and out … thou shalt be a captain over Israel”; the Douay “thou shalt be prince over Israel”; the TLB “you were our real leader”.

A covenant before Jehovah. “And all the elders of Israel came to the king at Hebron, and King David made a covenant with them in Hebron before Jehovah; and they anointed David king over Israel”: the tribes speak in verse 1 and the elders act in verse 3, and the one who makes the covenant is the king. This is the third anointing the narrative tells: by Samuel “in the midst of his brothers” (1 Samuel 16:13), by the men of Judah (2 Samuel 2:4) and now by Israel (both already on these pages). The ASV has “made a covenant with them”; the KJV “made a league with them”; the NIV “made a covenant with them”; the NWT 1984 “concluded a covenant with them”; the Douay “made a league with them”; the TLB “made a contract before the Lord with the leaders of Israel”.

Thirty and forty. “David was thirty years old when he became king; he reigned forty years. In Hebron he reigned over Judah seven years and six months; and in Jerusalem he reigned thirty-three years over all Israel and Judah”: “seven years and six months” stands in three verses, the number at 2 Samuel 2:11 (already on these pages), this verse and 1 Chronicles 3:4. Seven years and six months and thirty-three years make forty years and six months, and the verse counts the whole as forty. Thirty is the age of Joseph before Pharaoh (Genesis 41:46) and of Jesus “when he began” (Luke 3:23, both already on these pages); Saul’s own verse of this kind has its number missing in the Hebrew (1 Samuel 13:1, already on these pages). The NIV and the NWT 1984 have “when he became king”; the ASV and the KJV have “when he began to reign”; the TLB folds verses 4 and 5 into one parenthesis, “He had already been the king of Judah for seven years, since the age of thirty.”

Verses 6–10 · The blind and the lame — David takes the Jebusite stronghold of Zion, the water-channel, the Millo, and the city of David

The Jebusite. “And the king and his men went to Jerusalem, to the Jebusite, the inhabitant of the land”: the Hebrew has the singular, “the Jebusite”, as in Joshua’s “the Jebusite has dwelt with the sons of Judah in Jerusalem to this day” (Joshua 15:63) and the book of Judges’ “the Jebusite who dwelt in Jerusalem the sons of Benjamin did not drive out” (Judges 1:21). Judah had once struck the city “with the edge of the sword” and sent it up in fire (Judges 1:8); a Levite would not lodge there, “opposite Jebus — that is, Jerusalem” (Judges 19:10); and the border of Judah ran by “the shoulder of the Jebusite” (Joshua 15:8, all already on these pages). See the Jebusites and Jerusalem.

The blind and the lame. “‘You shall not come in here, unless you remove the blind and the lame’ — thinking: ‘David shall not come in here’”: the Hebrew is literally “unless your removing the blind and the lame”, and readings differ on whether the blind and the lame are a demand, a garrison, or a jeer. The ASV has “Except thou take away the blind and the lame, thou shalt not come in hither”, and the KJV “Except thou take away the blind and the lame”, as does the Geneva (“Except thou take away the blind and the lame”); the NIV “even the blind and the lame can ward you off”; the NWT 1984 “the blind and the lame ones will certainly turn you away”; the TLB “Even the blind and lame could keep you out!”; the Douay “unless thou take away the blind and the lame”. The Greek has “the blind and the lame stood against him”. Verse 8 gives a saying that grew from it: “Blind and lame shall not come into the house”. The word for “the lame” is pisseach; the lame son of Jonathan, in the chapter before, is “lame in his feet” in other words (2 Samuel 4:4, already on these pages), and he will sit at the king’s table “continually … and he was lame in both his feet” with this word (2 Samuel 9:13). The law keeps “a man who is blind, or lame” from approaching (Leviticus 21:18, already on these pages). Which “house” the saying shuts them out of is not said: the ASV, KJV and NWT 1984 have “the house”, the NIV “the palace”, the Douay “the temple”, and the Greek “the house of the Lord”.

The stronghold of Zion. “And David captured the stronghold of Zion — that is, the city of David”: “the stronghold of Zion” stands in two verses, this one and 1 Chronicles 11:5. This is the first verse of the Hebrew Bible to name Zion (read from the hits); see Zion. “Stronghold” is metsudah, David’s own word for the places where he hid from Saul (1 Samuel 22:4; 24:22, already on these pages). In verse 9 he lives “in the stronghold” and gives it a name, “the city of David”. The ASV has “the stronghold of Zion”; the KJV “the strong hold of Zion”; the NIV “the fortress of Zion”; the Douay “the castle of Sion”; the Geneva “the fort of Zion”.

The water-channel. “Whoever strikes a Jebusite, let him reach the water-channel — and the lame and the blind, who are hated of David’s soul!”: the sentence breaks off, with no verb for “the lame and the blind”, and the written text has sene’u where the reading tradition says sin’ei (“hated of”). Tsinnor stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and the verse where deep calls to deep “at the sound of your tsinnorim” (Psalm 42:8), so the word belongs to water. Some readers connect it with a shaft or tunnel by which water came into the city; the Hebrew itself gives no description. The versions fill the break differently. The ASV has “let him get up to the watercourse, and smite the lame and the blind”; the KJV “Whosoever getteth up to the gutter … he shall be chief and captain”, a prize that the Hebrew here does not name and that stands in the Chronicles’ account (1 Chronicles 11:6); the NIV “will have to use the water shaft”; the NWT 1984 “let him, by means of the water tunnel, make contact”; the TLB “Go up through the water tunnel”; the Geneva “getteth up to the gutters … I will prefer him”; the Douay “offered that day a reward … get up to the gutters of the tops of the houses”. The Greek reads “let him lay hold with a short sword”.

The Millo, and Jehovah with him. “And David built all around, from the Millo and inward”: “the Millo” with the article stands in six verses. Besides this one they are 1 Kings 9:15, 24 and 11:27, 1 Chronicles 11:8 and 2 Chronicles 32:5; Shechem’s “Beth-millo” is another place with a word from the same root, “to fill” (Judges 9:6, already on these pages). The last word is bayta, “inward” or “toward the house”. The ASV has “from Millo and inward”; the NIV “from the terraces inward”; the NWT 1984 “from the Mound and inward”; the Douay “from Mello and inwards”; the TLB “beginning at the old Millo section of the city”. The Greek has “from the citadel”. “And David went on growing greater and greater”: “going and growing great” stands in two verses, this and 1 Chronicles 11:9, and it is the sequel of 3:1, where David “went on growing stronger and stronger” (already on these pages). “Jehovah, God of armies, was with him” stands in one verse; it joins “Jehovah of armies” at Goliath (1 Samuel 17:45) to “Jehovah was with him” (18:12, 14, all already on these pages).

Verses 11–12 · Hiram king of Tyre sends cedar and craftsmen, and David knows that Jehovah has established him — “for the sake of his people Israel”

Cedar from Tyre. “And Hiram king of Tyre sent messengers to David, and cedar trees, and craftsmen in wood and craftsmen in stone for the wall; and they built a house for David”: Hiram is a king of Tyre, a city that stands on these pages as a border town of Asher (Joshua 19:29, already on these pages). The word for “house” is the one that closes the saying of verse 8, “into the house”. David’s house of cedar and the Lord’s house are the subject of the next chapters (2 Samuel 7:2, now on these pages). The ASV has “cedar-trees, and carpenters, and masons”; the NIV “cedar logs and carpenters and stonemasons, and they built a palace for David”; the NWT 1984 “workers in wood and workers in stone for walls”; the TLB “cedar lumber, carpenters, and masons to build a palace”; the Douay “masons for walls: and they built a house for David”.

Established, and lifted up. “And David knew that Jehovah had established him king over Israel, and that he had lifted up his kingdom for the sake of his people Israel”: “lifted up his kingdom” stands in one verse. “Established” is hekhino, the verb of a thing set firmly in place; the reason given, “for the sake of his people Israel”, puts the kingdom under the people’s need rather than the king’s glory. The ASV has “Jehovah had established him king over Israel, and that he had exalted his kingdom for his people Israel’s sake”; the NIV “had established him as king over Israel and had exalted his kingdom for the sake of his people Israel”; the NWT 1984 “had firmly established him as king … exalted his kingdom for the sake of his people Israel”; the Douay “confirmed him king over Israel, and that he had exalted his kingdom over his people Israel”; the TLB “why the Lord had made him the king and blessed his kingdom so greatly—it was because God wanted to pour out his kindness on Israel”.

Verses 13–16 · More concubines and wives out of Jerusalem — eleven sons’ names, among them Nathan and Solomon

More concubines and wives. “And David took more concubines and wives from Jerusalem, after he came from Hebron; and more sons and daughters were born to David”: the Hebrew puts the concubines first, and says “from Jerusalem”, as the ASV does (“out of Jerusalem”); the NIV has “in Jerusalem”. The narrator gives no verdict. The law’s words for a king are “he shall not multiply wives for himself” (Deuteronomy 17:17, already on these pages), and the sons born in Hebron were six (2 Samuel 3:2–5, already on these pages). The KJV has “David took him more concubines and wives out of Jerusalem”; the NWT 1984 “David went on taking more concubines and wives out of Jerusalem”; the TLB “After moving from Hebron to Jerusalem, David married additional wives and concubines”.

Eleven names. “Shammua and Shobab and Nathan and Solomon, and Ibhar and Elishua and Nepheg and Japhia, and Elishama and Eliada and Eliphelet”: no mother is named here for any of them. 1 Chronicles 3:5 gives four, “Shimea, and Shobab, and Nathan, and Solomon”, as born to Bath-shua daughter of Ammiel, and the Chronicles’ other list has thirteen names, with Elpelet and Nogah added and “Beeliada” where this verse has Eliada (1 Chronicles 14:4–7); Eliada is “God knows” and Beeliada “Baal knows”, the pattern of Ish-bosheth and Eshbaal (see the note at Romans 11, already on these pages). Two of the names stand in the two genealogies of Jesus: “David fathered Solomon by the wife of Uriah” (Matthew 1:6) and “of Nathan, of David” (Luke 3:31, both already on these pages). The Douay closes verse 15 at Nepheg, “Jebahar, and Elisua, and Nepheg”, and opens verse 16 with “And Japhia”; the Hebrew closes verse 15 with Japhia, as do the ASV and the NWT 1984. The TLB prints all eleven names in each of the three verses. The KJV spells the last name “Eliphalet”.

Verses 17–21 · The Philistines seek David — the stronghold, the valley of Rephaim, “Jehovah has broken through”, and the idols left behind

The stronghold again. “And the Philistines heard that they had anointed David king over Israel, and all the Philistines went up to seek David; and David heard, and went down to the stronghold”: the chapters on these pages tell of no Philistine move after the anointing by Judah, and they come after the anointing by all Israel; Jehovah had spoken of saving Israel “from the hand of the Philistines” (2 Samuel 3:18, already on these pages). “to the stronghold” with the article stands in one verse. The Hebrew does not say which one: Zion is “the stronghold” in verse 9, and David’s hiding-place under Saul was “the stronghold” too (1 Samuel 22:5, already on these pages), and “went down” has been taken to point to that second place rather than to a hill. The ASV has “went down to the stronghold”; the KJV “went down to the hold”; the NWT 1984 “went down to the place hard to approach”; the Geneva “went down to a fort”; the NIV “went down to the stronghold”.

The valley of Rephaim. “And the Philistines came, and spread out in the valley of Rephaim”: “the valley of Rephaim” stands in eight verses, two on the border of Judah and Benjamin (Joshua 15:8; 18:16, already on these pages), verses 18 and 22 here, and four more (2 Samuel 23:13; Isaiah 17:5; 1 Chronicles 11:15; 14:9). “Spread out” is nittshu, from the verb natash, “to leave” or “to let loose”. The Greek has “the valley of the Titans”, translating Rephaim. The ASV has “spread themselves in the valley of Rephaim”; the NIV “spread out in the Valley of Rephaim”; the NWT 1984 “kept tramping about in the low plain of Rephʹa·im”; the Douay “the valley of Raphaim”.

Shall I go up? “And David inquired of Jehovah, saying: ‘Shall I go up against the Philistines? Will you give them into my hand?’ And Jehovah said to David: ‘Go up; for I will surely give the Philistines into your hand’”: David’s asking is the asking of Keilah and of the pursuit at Ziklag (1 Samuel 23:2, 4; 30:8, already on these pages), with a yes-or-no question and the answer. “I will surely give” (natón etten, the infinitive standing before the verb) stands in one verse. The ASV has “I will certainly deliver”; the KJV “I will doubtless deliver”; the NIV “I will surely deliver”; the NWT 1984 “I shall without fail give”; the TLB “I will give them to you”.

Baal-perazim. “And David came to Baal-perazim, and David struck them there; and he said: ‘Jehovah has broken through my enemies before me, like a breach of waters’”: the name means “lord of breaches”, and David gives its reason in the saying. “Baal-perazim” stands in two verses, this one and 1 Chronicles 14:11. The root parats is that of Perez (Genesis 38:29, “a breach upon you!”), the ancestor of David’s house (Ruth 4:18–22), and of the breach that Jehovah “had made in the tribes of Israel” (Judges 21:15, all already on these pages); see perets. “Jehovah has broken through” stands in five verses, and the next of them turns it against a man of Israel: David is angry “because Jehovah had broken out in a breach against Uzzah” (2 Samuel 6:8, now on these pages). The Greek translates the name, “the Upper Breaks”. The ASV has “like the breach of waters”; the KJV “hath broken forth upon mine enemies before me, as the breach of waters”; the NIV “As waters break out, the Lord has broken out against my enemies”; the NWT 1984 “like a gap made by waters”; the Douay “hath divided my enemies before me, as waters are divided”; the Geneva “divided mine enemies asunder”; the TLB “like a raging flood”.

Their idols. “And they left their idols there; and David and his men carried them away”: “their idols” in this spelling stands in six verses, among them the Philistines’ news carried “to the house of their idols” after Gilboa (1 Samuel 31:9, already on these pages). The Philistines had once carried Israel’s ark into the house of Dagon (1 Samuel 5:2, already on these pages); now Israel carries off what they leave. Shelves split on the last verb. The ASV has “took them away”; the NIV “carried them off”; the NWT 1984 “took them away”; the Douay “took away”; the TLB “confiscated”; the KJV “burned them”, the Geneva “burnt them”. The Chronicles’ account says “their gods … were burned with fire” (1 Chronicles 14:12), which is also the law’s word for the carved images of the nations (Deuteronomy 7:25, already on these pages); this verse’s Hebrew has vayyissa’em, “and he lifted them”. The Greek has “their gods”.

Verses 22–25 · The bakha trees — “You shall not go up”, the sound of marching in the treetops, and the Philistines struck from Geba to Gezer

Not up, but round. “And once more the Philistines went up, and spread out in the valley of Rephaim. And David inquired of Jehovah; and he said: ‘You shall not go up; turn round behind them’”: verse 22 repeats verse 18, and the answer reverses verse 19, “Go up”. The Douay puts the first question into verse 23 again, “Shall I go up against the Philistines, and wilt thou deliver them into my hands?”, which the Hebrew does not have. The ASV has “Thou shalt not go up: make a circuit behind them”; the KJV “fetch a compass behind them”; the NIV “Do not go straight up, but circle around behind them”; the NWT 1984 “You must not go up. Go around to the rear of them”; the TLB “Don’t make a frontal attack”.

The bakha trees. “Come upon them opposite the bakha trees … when you hear the sound of marching in the tops of the bakha trees”: “bakha trees” (bekha’im) stands in four verses, 23 and 24 here and 1 Chronicles 14:14–15. The tree is not known. The ASV, KJV and Geneva have “mulberry trees”; the NIV “poplar trees”; the TLB “balsam trees”; the Douay “pear trees”; the NWT 1984 “baʹca bushes”. The word is spelled like “weepers”, the root of bakah, “to weep” (Bochim, Judges 2:5, already on these pages), and the Greek reads it so, “the weeping valley”, with “the voice of the closing-in of the grove of the weeping valley” in verse 24.

The sound of marching. “Then you shall move decisively; for then Jehovah has gone out before you to strike the camp of the Philistines”: “the sound of marching” (qol tse’adah) stands in one verse; the Chronicles have the article, “the sound of the marching”. “You shall move decisively” (techerats) stands in one verse; see charats, and the Chronicles’ parallel has “you shall go out to battle”. “Jehovah has gone out before you” is Deborah’s word to Barak, “Has not Jehovah gone out before you?” (Judges 4:14, already on these pages). The ASV has “thou shalt bestir thyself”; the KJV “thou shalt bestir thyself”; the NIV “move quickly”; the NWT 1984 “you act with decision”; the Douay “shalt thou join battle”; the TLB “attack!”; the Geneva “then remove”.

From Geba to Gezer. “And David did so, as Jehovah commanded him; and he struck the Philistines from Geba until you come to Gezer”: Geba is the town where Jonathan struck the Philistine garrison (1 Samuel 13:3, already on these pages), in Benjamin’s hills; Gezer is the city whose king Joshua struck and whose Canaanites were never driven out (Joshua 10:33; 16:10, already on these pages). The Greek has “Gabaon” (Gibeon) for Geba, and so does the Chronicles’ Hebrew (1 Chronicles 14:16). The ASV has “from Geba until thou come to Gezer”; the NIV “from Gibeon to Gezer”; the NWT 1984 “from Geʹba to as far as Geʹzer”; the Douay “from Gabaa until thou come to Gezer”; the KJV “until thou come to Gazer”; the TLB “all the way from Geba to Gezer”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “your bone and your flesh”, as in Genesis 29:14 and Judges 9:2; “led out and brought in”, as in Numbers 27:17 and 1 Samuel 18:13; “shepherd” and “leader” for nagid; “covenant”; “the Jebusite” in the singular, as in Joshua 15:63; “the blind and the lame”; “the stronghold” for metsudah, as in 1 Samuel 22:4; “the water-channel” for tsinnor; “the Millo”; “lifted up his kingdom”; “concubines and wives”; “spread out” for natash; “broken through” for parats, as in Genesis 38:29; “idols” for atsabbim, as in 1 Samuel 31:9; “the bakha trees”; and “Jehovah has gone out before you”, as in Judges 4:14.

Echoes paid. Abner’s word to the elders (2 Samuel 3:17–18); Adam’s, Laban’s and Abimelech’s bone and flesh (Genesis 2:23; 29:14; Judges 9:2); the going out and coming in (Numbers 27:17; Deuteronomy 31:2; 1 Samuel 18:13, 16; 29:6); Jehovah’s word that David lead his people (1 Samuel 13:14; 25:30); the three anointings (1 Samuel 16:13; 2 Samuel 2:4); seven years and six months (2 Samuel 2:11); the age of Joseph and of Jesus (Genesis 41:46; Luke 3:23) and Saul’s missing number (1 Samuel 13:1); the Jebusite and Jerusalem (Joshua 15:8, 63; Judges 1:8, 21; 19:10); the lame son of Jonathan (2 Samuel 4:4); the law of the blemish (Leviticus 21:18); the stronghold (1 Samuel 22:4, 5; 24:22); “growing stronger and stronger” (2 Samuel 3:1); Jehovah of armies and Jehovah with him (1 Samuel 17:45; 18:12, 14); Tyre (Joshua 19:29); the king’s law (Deuteronomy 17:17); the sons born at Hebron (2 Samuel 3:2–5); Solomon and Nathan in the two genealogies (Matthew 1:6; Luke 3:31); the Keilah and Ziklag inquiries (1 Samuel 23:2, 4; 30:8); Perez and the breach (Genesis 38:29; Ruth 4:18–22; Judges 21:15); the idols of Gilboa, and the ark in Dagon’s house (1 Samuel 31:9; 5:2); the burning of images (Deuteronomy 7:25); Bochim (Judges 2:5); Deborah’s “gone out before you” (Judges 4:14); and Geba and Gezer (1 Samuel 13:3; Joshua 10:33; 16:10).

Echoes planted. “Bone and flesh” again, to the elders of Judah and to Amasa (2 Samuel 19:13–14); Mephibosheth, lame in both feet, at the king’s table (2 Samuel 9:13); “chief and captain” and the Chronicles’ account of Jerusalem (1 Chronicles 11:4–9); the psalm of the tsinnorim (Psalm 42:8); the Millo built again (1 Kings 9:15, 24; 11:27); the house of cedar (2 Samuel 7:2, now on these pages); the Chronicles’ lists of sons, with Beeliada (1 Chronicles 3:5; 14:4–7); the valley of Rephaim once more (2 Samuel 23:13); “Jehovah has broken through” turned on Uzzah (2 Samuel 6:8, now on these pages); the idols burned (1 Chronicles 14:12); and Gibeon to Gezer (1 Chronicles 14:16). None of these is translated here yet.
