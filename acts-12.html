Herod the king lays his hands on some of the congregation, kills James the brother of John with a sword and, seeing that it pleases the Jews, arrests Peter in the days of the Unleavened Bread and puts him under four groups of four soldiers. While the congregation prays, an angel of Jehovah wakes Peter between two soldiers, the chains fall off, and he is led past the first and second guard and out through the iron gate; at the house of Mary the mother of John called Mark the servant girl Rhoda will not open for joy, and Peter tells them how Jehovah led him out and asks that James and the brothers be told. Herod has the guards led away, goes down to Caesarea, and on a set day in royal apparel is hailed as ‘a god’s voice’; an angel of Jehovah strikes him and he is eaten by worms and breathes his last. The word of God keeps growing, and Barnabas and Saul, having fulfilled the service, return taking John called Mark.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. It dates Peter’s arrest ‘in the days of the Unleavened Bread’ (v3) and says Herod meant to bring him out ‘after the Passover’ (v4), and it opens ‘about that time’ (v1) beside the famine relief of 11:27–30 without a year. Josephus, in the Greek of the Antiquities (19.343–350), says a king Agrippa died at Caesarea in the seventh year of his reign and the fifty-fourth of his age; this library reports that and does not tie it to Luke’s verses or supply a year.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–4 · Ἡρῴδης ὁ βασιλεύς … ἐπέβαλεν … τὰς χεῖρας κακῶσαί … Ἰάκωβον … μαχαίρῃ … τετραδίοις … πάσχα Hērōdēs ho basileus … epebalen … tas cheiras kakōsai … Iakōbon … machairē … tetradiois … pascha — Herod, James and Peter, Acts 12:1–4: a king who lays his hands, a sword, four groups of four, and why the King James prints “Easter”

“Herod the king,” who lays his hands. Luke gives the name and the title and nothing more: Hērōdēs ho basileus. The name has already meant different men in these books: Herod “the king” of Matthew 2:1 and Luke 1:5, and the Herod of Luke 3:1, who is tetrarch of Galilee and not king (Herod Antipas). The surname Agrippa does not stand in this chapter. Reference works, following Josephus, call this king Herod Agrippa I (Herod Agrippa I), and Josephus’s account of the death of a king of that name at Caesarea is set beside vv20–23 below. Epebalen… tas cheiras, “laid his hands,” is how Acts tells an arrest: the rulers “laid hands on” Peter and John (4:3) and on the apostles (5:18), the Jews from Asia on Paul at 21:27 (not yet on these pages), and Herod here. The Greek puts the purpose next, kakōsai tinas tōn apo tēs ekklēsias, “to afflict some of those from the congregation.” Kakoō stands in five verses of Acts: Stephen’s word for what Egypt would do and did to Israel (7:6, 7:19, printed “afflict” there too), this verse, and 14:2 and 18:10 (not yet on these pages). The Greek gives no number and, in this chapter, names no victim but James. KJV “stretched forth his hands to vex certain of the church,” ASV “put forth his hands to afflict certain of the church,” Douay “stretched forth his hands, to afflict some of the church,” NWT 1984 “applied his hands to mistreating some,” NIV “arrested some who belonged to the church, intending to persecute them,” which supplies an arrest and a motive the verb does not carry, and TLB “moved against some of the believers,” which drops the hands.

“He killed James the brother of John with a sword.” Luke’s verb is aneilen, the plain verb of killing that Stephen uses of Moses and the Egyptian (7:28) and that stands in the plot against Saul (9:23). Machairē, “with a sword,” has no article, and the Greek does not say that the sword was a beheading; machaira stands in two verses of Acts, here and 16:27 (not yet on these pages), where a jailer draws one to kill himself (dictionary). The brothers are James and John the sons of Zebedee, who ask Jesus for the places at his right and left and are told, “The cup that I drink you will drink, and the baptism I am baptized with you will be baptized with” (Mark 10:39). Acts does not cite the saying, and this page does not tie the two together. Luke gives James a single clause: no speech, no charge, no burial, where Stephen had two chapters. KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva and NIV all print “with the sword” and so supply the article, NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 “by the sword”; TLB leaves the sword out and adds “the apostle” (its footnote marks the word as implied). The verb is KJV “killed,” NIV “put to death” and NWT 1984 “did away with.”

“Pleasing to the Jews,” and the days of the Unleavened Bread. Arestos, “pleasing,” stands in four verses of the New Testament: John 8:29, of what the Father is pleased by; Acts 6:2, where the Twelve say it is not pleasing that they leave the word of God to serve tables; this verse; and 1 John 3:22 (not yet on these pages). The Greek says tois Ioudaiois, “the Jews,” and does not say which: James, Peter and the whole congregation in Jerusalem were themselves Jews, and Luke has just named the target as “those from the congregation.” Prosetheto sullabein, literally “added to seize,” is the pattern of Luke 20:11 and 20:12, where the man who planted the vineyard “proceeded to send” a second and a third slave. KJV “proceeded further to take Peter also,” ASV “proceeded to seize Peter also,” Douay “proceeded to take up Peter also,” NWT 1984 “went on to arrest Peter also,” TLB “arrested Peter during the Passover celebration,” which folds the date into the arrest. The date is Luke’s parenthesis, ēsan de hēmerai tōn azymōn; the apparatus gives de to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles, and de hai, “the days,” to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. Azyma stands in nine verses of the New Testament: Matthew 26:17, Mark 14:1 and 14:12, Luke 22:1 and 22:7, this verse, Acts 20:6, and 1 Corinthians 5:7 and 5:8 (those three not yet on these pages). KJV “Then were the days of unleavened bread,” Douay “the days of the Azymes,” NIV “the Festival of Unleavened Bread,” NWT 1984 “days of the unfermented cakes.”

“Four groups of four,” and “Easter.” Tetradion names a unit of four and stands in this verse alone (dictionary); tessarsin tetradiois stratiōtōn is four such units, which makes sixteen men, and the Greek gives the number and no schedule. NIV “four squads of four soldiers each” and NWT 1984 “four shifts of four soldiers each” keep the grouping, and “shifts” reads a rotation into a word that says only “a four”; the usual explanation, one squad for each watch of the night, is a reading and is not stated in the verse. KJV, ASV and Geneva “four quaternions of soldiers,” Douay “four files of soldiers,” TLB “sixteen soldiers.” The plan is anagagein auton tō laō, “to bring him up to the people,” after the pascha. The word stands in twenty-seven verses of the New Testament and in Acts only here. The KJV prints “Easter” (“intending after Easter to bring him forth to the people”) for the word it prints “Passover” at Luke 22:1 (“the feast of unleavened bread drew nigh, which is called the Passover”) and “passover” at Luke 22:7 and Hebrews 11:28. The other versions on the shelf print “Passover” (NWT 1984 in lower case) and the Douay “pasch.” The Greek does not say what the people were to do with him: NIV “for public trial,” TLB “to deliver Peter to the Jews for execution.”

Verses 5–11 · ἐκτενῶς … ἁλύσεσιν δυσίν … ἄγγελος κυρίου ἐπέστη … πατάξας … ἤγειρεν … Ἀνάστα … Ζῶσαι … ὅραμα … αὐτομάτη … ἐν ἑαυτῷ γενόμενος … ὁ κύριος ἐξαπέστειλεν ektenōs … halysesin dysin … angelos kyriou epestē … pataxas … ēgeiren … Anasta … Zōsai … horama … automatē … en heautō genomenos … ho kyrios exapesteilen — Peter freed, Acts 12:5–11: earnest prayer, two chains, an angel who strikes his side, “Fasten your belt… follow me,” and Peter “coming to himself”

Earnest prayer, and the night before. Ektenōs, “earnestly,” is the adverb of Luke 22:44, where Jesus in agony “prayed more earnestly” (the comparative, ektenesteron); the root stands in five verses of the New Testament: that one, this verse, Acts 26:7 (not yet on these pages), 1 Peter 1:22 and 1 Peter 4:8 (the last not yet on these pages). The apparatus of the printed text gives the adverb ektenōs to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and the adjective ektenēs to the Robinson–Pierpont text; this page prints the adverb. The prayer is hypo tēs ekklēsias pros ton theon peri autou, “by the congregation, to God, concerning him,” and Luke does not say what they asked; the same apparatus gives peri, “concerning,” to the first three and hyper, “on behalf of,” to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “prayer was made without ceasing of the church unto God for him,” which makes the adverb a matter of duration, ASV “prayer was made earnestly of the church unto God for him,” NIV “the church was earnestly praying to God for him,” NWT 1984 “prayer to God for him was being carried on intensely by the congregation,” TLB “earnest prayer was going up to God from the church for his safety,” which names what was asked. This is Peter’s third custody in Acts (4:3, 5:18), and as at 5:19 it is an angel of Jehovah who acts at night. Halysesin dysin, “with two chains,” is the phrase used again of Paul at 21:33 (not yet on these pages), and halysis stands in four verses of Acts: vv6 and 7 here, 21:33 and 28:20 (not yet on these pages), where Paul says “I wear this chain.” NIV “The night before Herod was to bring him to trial” and TLB “The night before he was to be executed” read a night and a purpose into tē nykti ekeinē, “on that night,” and into proagagein, “bring forward” (v4 had anagagein, “bring up”). The apparatus gives proagagein auton to Nestle–Aland 28, prosagagein auton, “bring to,” to Westcott–Hort, proagein auton to Tregelles and auton proagein to Robinson–Pierpont; this page prints the first.

“An angel of Jehovah stood by” and struck his side. Angelos kyriou epestē is the clause of Luke 2:9, printed “stood over them” there; with no object it is printed “stood by” here. Angelos kyriou stands in four verses of Acts: 5:19, 8:26, this verse and v23, and this page prints “Jehovah” for kyrios in the formula (dictionary). Phōs elampsen en tō oikēmati is “a light shone in the cell”; oikēma, literally “a dwelling, a room,” stands in this verse alone, and the shelf prints KJV “prison,” Douay “room,” Geneva “house,” NIV “cell” and NWT 1984 “prison cell.” Pataxas, “striking,” is patassō, which stands in three verses of Acts: 7:24, where Moses strikes the Egyptian, this verse, and v23, where the verb is used of the same angel’s act on Herod. Pleura, “side,” stands in five verses of the New Testament: this one and four in John, where a spear pierces the side of the crucified (19:34) and the risen one shows it (20:20, 20:25, 20:27). Then ēgeiren auton legōn Anasta, “raised him up, saying, Rise”: these are two words Peter has himself used. At 10:26 he “raised up” Cornelius (ēgeiren auton in the printed text; Robinson–Pierpont have the two words in the other order) saying Anastēthi, “Stand up”; at 3:7 he raised the lame man with the same verb; and at 9:34 and 9:40 he says anastēthi to Aeneas and to Tabitha. Ēgeiren is also the verb Acts uses of God raising Jesus at 3:15, 4:10, 5:30 and 10:40; Luke draws nothing from that here. KJV and Geneva “smote Peter on the side, and raised him up,” ASV “smote Peter on the side, and awoke him,” Douay “he striking Peter on the side, raised him up,” NIV “struck Peter on the side and woke him up,” NWT 1984 “Striking Peter on the side, he roused him,” NWT 2013 “Hitting Peter on the side, he woke him,” TLB “slapped him on the side to awaken him.” The chains fall from his cheirōn, “hands”; NIV and TLB print “wrists.”

“Fasten your belt… and follow me.” Zōsai is zōnnymi, “to fasten the belt”; the simple verb stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and John 21:18 (twice), where Jesus tells Peter that when he was younger he “used to fasten your own belt” and walk where he wished, and when he is old “someone else will fasten your belt and carry you where you do not wish.” The apparatus gives the simple Zōsai to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and the compound Perizōsai to the Robinson–Pierpont text; this page prints the simple verb, and the compound is the verb of the girded loins of Luke 12:35 (periezōsmenai). The next verse ends with Akolouthei moi, “Follow me” (21:19), and the angel’s last command here is the same two words. John says what his saying signified (“by what kind of death he would glorify God”); Luke has no such comment, and nothing in Acts says he had that saying in view. The words are set side by side here and no further. Hypodēsai, “put on your sandals,” is a verb of three verses of the New Testament, Mark 6:9 (the Twelve are sent out “to wear sandals”), this verse and Ephesians 6:15 (not yet on these pages); sandalion, “sandal,” stands in Mark 6:9 and here alone. KJV “Gird thyself, and bind on thy sandals,” Douay “Gird thyself, and put on thy sandals,” NWT 1984 “Gird yourself and bind your sandals on,” NIV “Put on your clothes and sandals,” which drops the belt, and TLB “Get dressed and put on your shoes.” The picture of a night when one is told to be belted, shod and quick is the picture of Exodus 12:11, where the Passover meal is eaten with the loins girded and the sandals on the feet, in haste (Hebrew matnēkhem ḥagurim, na‘alēkhem beraglēkhem… beḥippāzon). Luke does not quote that verse and does not say the likeness is meant; it falls in the days of the Unleavened Bread (v3), and Herod means to bring him out “after the Passover” (v4); Luke 22:1 shows that the two names can name one festival, and the verse does not say whether the Passover meal itself is past.

“He thought he was seeing a vision.” Horama, “vision,” stands in eleven verses of Acts, and three of them are Peter’s own earlier vision: 10:17, where he is “at a loss within himself as to what the vision might be,” 10:19 and 11:5. There the vision was real and he took it as a puzzle; here the rescue is real and he takes it for a vision. KJV “wist not that it was true which was done by the angel,” ASV “he knew not that it was true which was done by the angel,” NIV “he had no idea that what the angel was doing was really happening,” NWT 1984 “did not know that what was happening through the angel was real,” TLB “dream or vision,” which adds a dream.

The gate that opened by itself, and the lane. Automatē, “by itself,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: Mark 4:28, where “the earth bears fruit by itself,” and this verse. KJV “of his own accord,” ASV, NWT 1984 and TLB “of its own accord,” Douay “of itself,” NIV “by itself.” The two guards are phylakē, the word printed “prison” at vv4, 5, 6 and 17 and printed “guard” here: prōtēn phylakēn kai deuteran, and the Greek leaves open whether the first and the second are posts or men on duty. KJV and Douay “ward,” ASV “guard,” NIV “guards,” NWT 1984 “sentinel guard,” Geneva “watch,” TLB “cell blocks.” The gate is pylē, kept apart in this translation from pylōn, the “gateway” of Mary’s house at vv13–14 (pylōn is printed “gate” at 10:17 and “gateway” at Matthew 26:71). Rhymē, “lane,” is the word of 9:11, “the lane called Straight” (dictionary); here it is rhymēn mian, “one lane.” KJV “through one street,” TLB “for a block.”

“Coming to himself,” and who sent the angel. En heautō genomenos has a partner in Peter’s own mouth, narrated by Luke at 10:17, where he was en heautō diēporei, at a loss “within himself” about a vision; these are the only two verses of Acts with en heautō. Ho kyrios is printed “Jehovah” here for the reason it is at 5:19: Peter names the one who sent ton angelon autou, “his angel,” and the angel of the Lord is God’s. The apparatus gives the article (ho kyrios) to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28, and kyrios without it to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont; Robinson–Pierpont also puts the words of “coming to himself” in the other order (genomenos en heautō). The two verbs of Peter’s sentence, exapesteilen, “sent out,” and exeilato, “rescued,” are the two of Nebuchadnezzar’s confession in Aramaic Daniel 3:28, “who sent his angel and delivered his servants” (shelaḥ mal’akhēh veshēzib; Daniel 3:28), and “sent his angel” is also Daniel 6:23 in the Hebrew numbering (not yet on these pages). Peter does not quote it, and whether Luke has the verse in view the text does not say. Exaireō, “to rescue,” is Stephen’s verb of Joseph (7:10) and of God’s coming down for Israel (7:34). KJV “the Lord hath sent his angel, and hath delivered me out of the hand of Herod,” and the other six versions on the shelf print “the Lord” here, while the NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah sent his angel forth” and does so at vv7, 11, 17 and 23. Prosdokia, “expectation,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and Luke 21:26, “fainting from fear and expectation of what is coming on the inhabited earth.” The Greek names an expectation and not its object. NIV “everything the Jewish people were hoping would happen,” TLB “what the Jews were hoping to do to me,” NWT 2013 “everything that the Jews were expecting to happen,” KJV “from all the expectation of the people of the Jews.”

Verses 12–17 · οἰκίαν τῆς Μαρίας … τοῦ ἐπικαλουμένου Μάρκου … πυλῶνος … παιδίσκη … Ῥόδη … ἀπὸ τῆς χαρᾶς … Μαίνῃ … διϊσχυρίζετο … Ὁ ἄγγελός ἐστιν αὐτοῦ … κατασείσας … ἐξήγαγεν … Ἰακώβῳ oikian tēs Marias … tou epikaloumenou Markou … pylōnos … paidiskē … Rhodē … apo tēs charas … Mainē … diēischurizeto … Ho angelos estin autou … kataseisas … exēgagen … Iakōbō — Rhoda at the gateway, Acts 12:12–17: “You are out of your mind,” “It is his angel,” and word to James

Mary’s house, and John called Mark. Synidōn, “realizing,” stands in this verse and 14:6 only. Tēs Marias tēs mētros Iōannou tou epikaloumenou Markou: the house is Mary’s, and the verse gives no husband and no other relation. Epikaloumenos is the participle printed “the one called” of Simon at 10:5, 10:18, 10:32 and 11:13, and here it is used of John. The name Markos stands in eight verses of the New Testament: this verse, v25, 15:37 and 15:39, and Colossians 4:10, 2 Timothy 4:11 and 1 Peter 5:13 (those three not yet on these pages) and Philemon 24; so the “Mark” here is the “John, who was called Mark” of 15:37 (John Mark, Mary). Hikanoi synēthroismenoi kai proseuchomenoi: hikanoi is “a considerable number,” the adjective of “a considerable crowd” at 11:24, with no count given, and synathroizō stands in this verse and 19:25 only (not yet on these pages). The Greek does not say that these are the whole congregation of v5; it says that many were there, and praying. KJV and ASV “whose surname was Mark,” Douay “who was surnamed Mark,” NIV “also called Mark,” NWT 1984 “who was surnamed Mark” and “quite a few were gathered together,” TLB “Mary, mother of John Mark.”

The gateway, the servant girl, and a name that means “rose.” Pylōn is the gateway of a house with a door of its own (tēn thyran tou pylōnos, “the door of the gateway”); at 10:17 Cornelius’s men stood at Simon’s, and here Peter stands at Mary’s. Paidiskē, “servant girl,” is the word for the first person who speaks to Peter in each of the four accounts of his denial (Matthew 26:69, Mark 14:66 and 14:69, Luke 22:56, John 18:17, where she is “the servant girl who was the doorkeeper”); in John Peter “stood outside at the door” (18:16), and at Matthew 26:71 he “had gone out to the gateway,” the same word as here. Luke does not draw the likeness, and the girl here accuses him of nothing: she knows his voice. Rhodē, the name, stands in this verse alone and is the Greek word for “rose”; the verse makes nothing of it. Hypakousai, “to answer,” is the verb of the priests “obeying the faith” at 6:7, and Acts has it in these two verses. KJV and Douay “came to hearken,” ASV “came to answer,” NIV “came to answer the door,” NWT 1984 “came to attend to the call,” TLB “came to open it,” which is what the Greek says she did not do. Apo tēs charas, “for joy,” are the words of Luke 24:41, where the disciples “still could not believe it for joy,” and they stand in Matthew 13:44 as well. Ouk ēnoixen, “she did not open,” is the middle one of three openings in the chapter: the gate of v10 “opened by itself” (ēnoigē), here the gateway stays shut, and at v16 “they opened” (anoixantes). KJV “opened not the gate for gladness,” ASV “opened not the gate for joy,” NWT 1984 “out of joy she did not open the gate,” NIV “so overjoyed she ran back without opening it,” TLB “so overjoyed that she ran back inside.”

“You are out of your mind,” she “insisted,” and “his angel.” Mainomai stands in five verses of the New Testament: John 10:20 (“He has a demon and is out of his mind”), this verse, Acts 26:24 and 26:25, where Festus says it to Paul, and 1 Corinthians 14:23 (those three not yet on these pages). Diēischurizeto, “insisted,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: Luke 22:59, where in the high priest’s courtyard “another insisted, saying, ‘Truly, this man also was with him,’” and this verse; in both the insisting is about Peter, and in both it is at first denied. The household’s answer to a girl’s report is the apostles’ answer to the women’s at Luke 24:11, where the words “appeared before them like nonsense, and they did not believe them”; the words are different and the refusal is the same. Ho angelos estin autou: angelos is “messenger” as well as “angel,” and the Greek does not say which sense the household means, or what they thought had happened: a messenger from Peter, a guardian (compare Matthew 18:10, “their angels in the heavens”), or something else. The sentence has no more in it. KJV “It is his angel,” NIV “It must be his angel,” and TLB “It must be his angel. They must have killed him,” whose last sentence is that version’s own (its footnote marks it as implied). The apparatus gives estin autou to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and autou estin to Robinson–Pierpont: the same words in the other order.

Knocking, amazement, a hand for silence, and James. Exestēsan, “were amazed,” is the verb of the believers at 10:45; it stands in eight verses of Acts (2:7, 2:12, 8:9, 8:11, 8:13, 9:21, 10:45 and here), and the Greek says only that they saw him and were amazed, not that they had not believed. Kataseisas tē cheiri sigan, “motioning with his hand to be silent”: kataseiō stands in four verses of Acts, all of a speaker who uses the hand to get quiet: here, 13:16 and 19:33, and 21:40 (the last two not yet on these pages), where Paul, Alexander and Paul do it before they speak. Ho kyrios auton exēgagen ek tēs phylakēs: exagō, “to lead out,” is the verb of the apostles’ release at 5:19 and of the Exodus in Stephen’s speech (7:36, 7:40) and in Paul’s at 13:17, “with a high arm he led them out.” The days are the days of the Unleavened Bread (v3), and Luke does not say that he means the likeness. “Jehovah” is printed as at v11, since the subject is the one Peter named there, and the Greek again has ho kyrios. Iakōbō kai tois adelphois: the James of v2 is dead, and this James is not described. “James” stands in Acts at 1:13, 12:2, 12:17, 15:13 and 21:18; at 15:13 he answers the council, and at 21:18 Paul goes in to him “and all the elders were present.” Reference works identify the James of 12:17, 15:13 and 21:18 as the Lord’s brother, the name Paul gives him at Galatians 1:19; Acts here says only “James” (James the Lord’s brother). NIV “Tell James and the other brothers and sisters,” which makes James one of them and adds the sisters, TLB “Tell James and the others.” Eis heteron topon, “to another place,” names no place; Peter is next in the book at Jerusalem (15:7), and Luke does not say where he has been. KJV “into another place,” TLB “left for safer quarters,” which gives a reason the Greek does not. KJV “beckoning unto them with the hand to hold their peace,” NIV “motioned with his hand for them to be quiet,” NWT 1984 “motioned to them with his hand to be silent.”

Verses 18–19 · τάραχος οὐκ ὀλίγος … τί ἄρα ὁ Πέτρος ἐγένετο … ἀνακρίνας τοὺς φύλακας ἐκέλευσεν ἀπαχθῆναι tarachos ouk oligos … ti ara ho Petros egeneto … anakrinas tous phylakas ekeleusen apachthēnai — the guards, Acts 12:18–19: “no small disturbance,” and what “led away” says

“No small disturbance.” Tarachos ouk oligos is a litotes, “not a little” for “a great deal,” and ouk oligos stands in eight verses of Acts: here, 15:2, and 14:28, 17:4, 17:12, 19:23, 19:24 and 27:20 (the last six not yet on these pages); tarachos itself stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 19:23, where the same two words are “no small disturbance about the Way.” Ti ara ho Petros egeneto, “what, then, had become of Peter,” is the question of 5:24, where the captain of the temple and the chief priests, told that the prison is locked and empty, “were perplexed about them, as to what this might become” (ti an genoito touto); there the officers report the guards still standing at the doors (5:23), and here Herod puts the guards to the question. KJV “no small stir among the soldiers, what was become of Peter,” NIV “no small commotion among the soldiers as to what had become of Peter,” NWT 1984 “no little stir among the soldiers over what really had become of Peter,” TLB “At dawn, the jail was in great commotion.”

“Ordered them to be led away.” Epizētēsas, “having sought,” and then anakrinas, “having examined,” the verb of a formal examination, which stands in five verses of Acts: 4:9, this verse, 17:11, 24:8 and 28:18 (the last three not yet on these pages). The guards are phylakes, the word of v6 for the men before the door; the “soldiers” of v18 are stratiōtai, the word of v4 for the sixteen, and the Greek does not say that all of them were put to the question. Then ekeleusen apachthēnai, “ordered [them] to be led away”: apagō says “lead away,” not where or to what, and the Greek does not say death. What makes the verse read as a death sentence is its setting: Acts 16:27 (not yet on these pages) shows a jailer who, finding the prison doors open, draws his sword to kill himself, supposing the prisoners had escaped. The shelf supplies the end: KJV, ASV and Douay “put to death,” NIV “executed” and TLB “sentenced to death,” five of the seven; Geneva “led to be punished” and NWT 1984 “led off [to punishment],” with the bracket marking the words as supplied. The TLB also has “sixteen guards” and “court-martialed,” neither of which is in the verse.

Down to Caesarea. Katelthōn apo tēs Ioudaias eis Kaisareian diētriben: Herod “goes down” to the city of Cornelius (10:1) and of Philip’s arrival (8:40), and diētriben is an imperfect, “was spending time,” with no length given (Caesarea). The Greek does not say why he goes, or what he does there until v20.

Verses 20–23 · θυμομαχῶν … ὁμοθυμαδὸν … Βλάστον … κοιτῶνος … Θεοῦ φωνὴ καὶ οὐκ ἀνθρώπου … ἐπάταξεν … οὐκ ἔδωκεν τὴν δόξαν τῷ θεῷ … σκωληκόβρωτος … ἐξέψυξεν thymomachōn … homothymadon … Blaston … koitōnos … Theou phōnē kai ouk anthrōpou … epataxen … ouk edōken tēn doxan tō theō … skōlēkobrōtos … exepsyxen — the death of Herod, Acts 12:20–23: “fighting mad” and “with one accord,” “a god’s voice,” the angel who strikes, and Josephus on the death of Agrippa

“Fighting mad,” and “with one accord.” Thymomachōn stands in this verse alone (dictionary): thymos, “anger,” plus machomai, “to fight,” so “fighting in anger.” The next clause answers it with homothymadon, “with one accord,” from the same thymos (dictionary), a word that stands in eleven verses of the New Testament, ten of them in Acts: of the believers at 1:14, 2:46, 4:24 and 5:12, of the crowd that rushes on Stephen (7:57), of Philip’s hearers (8:6), here, and at 15:25, 18:12 and 19:29 (those two not yet on these pages), with Romans 15:6 (not yet on these pages) the eleventh. The Greek puts the two words in one verse and says nothing of the likeness. KJV, ASV and TLB “highly displeased,” Douay “angry,” NIV “quarreling,” NWT 1984 “in a fighting mood” and NWT 2013 “in an angry mood.” Blaston ton epi tou koitōnos tou basileōs: Blastus, whose name stands here alone, is “the one over the king’s bedchamber,” and koitōn stands here alone too. The Tyrians and Sidonians “persuaded” him (peisantes): KJV and ASV “having made Blastus the king’s chamberlain their friend,” Douay “having gained Blastus,” NIV “securing the support of,” TLB “made friends with,” Geneva “persuaded.” “Their country was fed from the king’s” is trephesthai autōn tēn chōran apo tēs basilikēs, an adjective with its noun understood: KJV “nourished by the king’s country,” NIV “depended on the king’s country for their food supply,” NWT 1984 “supplied with food from that of the king.” The Hebrew Bible records the same dependence from the other side: Solomon gave Hiram twenty thousand kor of wheat for his household (1 Kings 5:25 in the Hebrew numbering, 5:11 in English versions), and Ezekiel 27:17 lists wheat, honey, oil and balm among what Judah and the land of Israel gave Tyre in trade (neither chapter yet on these pages).

The judgment seat, the speech, and “a god’s voice.” Taktē hēmera is “a set day,” and Luke gives no date. Bēma, the platform from which a judge sits, is “judgment seat” here as at Matthew 27:19; it stands in eight verses of Acts (7:5, where it is a foot’s breadth of ground, here, 18:12, 18:16, 18:17, 25:6, 25:10 and 25:17). KJV, ASV, NIV and TLB print “throne,” Douay “judgment seat” and NWT 1984 “judgment seat.” Edēmēgorei, “was making a public speech,” stands in this verse alone (dēmos plus agoreuō); those who answer are ho dēmos, a word that stands in four verses of Acts (here, 17:5, 19:30 and 19:33) and means a city’s assembled public, where laos is the word for the people of the Jews at vv4 and 11. Theou phōnē kai ouk anthrōpou has no article before theou, and is printed “a god’s” as the shout of a crowd that is not Israel’s. The apparatus gives Theou phōnē to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and Phōnē theou to Robinson–Pierpont: the same words in the other order, and in both theou has no article. KJV “It is the voice of a god, and not of a man,” ASV “The voice of a god, and not of a man,” Douay “voice of a god, and not of a man,” NIV “voice of a god, not of a man,” TLB “voice of a god and not of a man,” and NWT 1984 “A god’s voice, and not a man’s”: six of the seven print “a god.” Geneva “The voice of God, and not of man” is the one version on the shelf that does not. Compare Barnabas and Paul at Lystra, “we also are men of like nature with you” (14:15, not yet on these pages), and Peter to Cornelius, who had fallen at his feet: “Stand up; I myself also am a man” (10:26).

Josephus on the death of Agrippa. In Josephus’s Greek (Antiquities 19.343–352, numbered 19.8.2 in Whiston), a king Agrippa, when “the third year” of his reign “over all Judea” had been completed (Triton… etos… peplērōto), comes to Caesarea, “formerly called Strato’s Tower,” for spectacles in honor of Caesar. On the second day he puts on “a robe made entirely of silver” (stolēn ex argyrou pepoiēmenēn pasan) and goes into the theater as the day begins; his flatterers cry out “one from one place, one from another,” “addressing him as a god” (theon prosagoreuontes), and “the king did not rebuke them, nor put away the impious flattery.” Looking up, he sees on a rope above his head the bird the Romans call boubōn, an owl (so Josephus glosses the word at 18.195), takes it at once for an angelos, “a messenger of evils,” and is seized by a pain through the heart and then, “suddenly,” by a violent pain of the belly (koilias). “I, your god,” he says, “am now ordered to end my life.” After “five days” of the pain of the belly he dies, in the fifty-fourth year of his age and the seventh of his reign. Josephus’s word for the owl, angelos, is Luke’s word for the angel who strikes (dictionary). Set beside Luke’s verses, the two accounts agree on a king at Caesarea, a crowd that calls him a god, and a death that follows. They differ on the occasion (Luke names none), the clothing (Luke has “royal apparel” and no silver), the place (Luke has a judgment seat and no theater), the cause (an angel and worms in Luke; a bird taken for a messenger of evils, and a pain of the belly, in Josephus) and the time (Luke has “at once,” Josephus five days). This page reports both and does not harmonize them. The name Agrippa is Josephus’s, and the identification of Luke’s Herod with him is the reference works’ (Herod Agrippa I).

“Struck him,” “did not give the glory,” “eaten by worms,” “breathed his last.” Epataxen is the verb of v7: the angel of Jehovah pataxas Peter’s side and raised him up, and an angel of Jehovah epataxen Herod; “angel of Jehovah” stands in four verses of Acts, and two of them are in this chapter. At v23 the shelf prints KJV, ASV and Geneva “smote him,” NIV “struck him down,” Douay and NWT 1984 “struck him” and TLB “struck Herod.” Set against the renderings of v7 (in the note on vv5–11), six of the seven versions use the same English verb at both verses (smote and smote, struck and struck, striking and struck); the TLB has “slapped” at v7 and “struck” at v23, and the NWT 2013, a version outside the seven, “Hitting” and “struck.” Anth’ hōn, “because” (literally “in return for which”), stands in five verses of the New Testament: Luke 1:20, where Gabriel tells Zechariah he will be silent “because you did not believe my words,” Luke 12:3 and 19:44, this verse, and 2 Thessalonians 2:10 (the last not yet on these pages). The phrase “give glory to God” (dounai doxan tō theō, with no article) stands in Luke 17:18, where the one found to return and do it is “this foreigner,” in John 9:24, Romans 4:20 (not yet on these pages) and Revelation 11:13; this verse has “the glory,” tēn doxan, in the apparatus of Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, while the Robinson–Pierpont text has doxan without the article, the form of those four verses. Skōlēkobrōtos, “eaten by worms,” stands here alone (dictionary): skōlēx, “worm,” plus bibrōskō, “to eat,” and Luke names no illness. Exepsyxen is the verb of Ananias and Sapphira at 5:5 and 5:10; all three verses that use it are in Acts (dictionary), and the Greek says only that one verb, the breath going out, serves each. KJV and ASV “eaten of worms, and gave up the ghost,” NIV “eaten by worms and died,” NWT 1984 “eaten up with worms and expired,” TLB “filled with maggots and died,” and its “with a sickness” is its own addition.

Verses 24–25 · ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ ηὔξανεν καὶ ἐπληθύνετο … ὑπέστρεψαν, εἰς Ἰερουσαλὴμ πληρώσαντες τὴν διακονίαν … συμπαραλαβόντες Ἰωάννην τὸν ἐπικληθέντα Μᾶρκον ho logos tou theou ēuxanen kai eplēthyneto … hypestrepsan, eis Ierousalēm plērōsantes tēn diakonian … symparalabontes Iōannēn ton epiklēthenta Markon — the word grows, Acts 12:24–25: “word of God” or “word of the Lord,” and did Barnabas and Saul return to Jerusalem, from it, or after serving it?

“The word of God kept growing.” Ho logos tou theou ēuxanen kai eplēthyneto is the sentence of 6:7, ho logos tou theou ēuxanen, kai eplēthyneto ho arithmos, with the number left out: there the word grows and the number is multiplied, and here the word is both. The two verbs together are also Stephen’s of Israel in Egypt, “the people grew and were multiplied” (7:17), and the growth of the word comes again at 19:20 (not yet on these pages), “the word of the Lord kept growing and prevailing” (ēuxanen kai ischyen). The little de that opens the verse, “But,” sets the word’s growth against the king’s end. The apparatus gives theou to Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and kyriou, “of the Lord,” to Westcott–Hort alone: Douay prints “the word of the Lord increased and multiplied” and NWT 1984 “the word of Jehovah went on growing and spreading,” and the rest of the shelf has God. This page prints the word of the text it translates. KJV “the word of God grew and multiplied,” NIV “the word of God continued to spread and flourish,” TLB “God’s Good News was spreading rapidly and there were many new believers,” which expands both verbs.

“Returned, having fulfilled the service.” The printed text has a comma after hypestrepsan, “they returned,” and then eis Ierousalēm plērōsantes tēn diakonian, so that “to Jerusalem” belongs with “having fulfilled the service”: the service was for Jerusalem (11:29–30, where it is sent to the elders there), and the verse does not say where they returned to. Most of the shelf reads it the other way round: KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva and NIV “returned from Jerusalem,” and “from” is not the printed eis, which is “to, into” (the footnote of the NIV on this verse begins “Some manuscripts to”). NWT 1984 keeps the printed construction, “after having fully carried out the relief ministration in Jerusalem, they returned,” and so does NWT 2013, “after fully carrying out the relief work in Jerusalem, they returned.” TLB turns the errand round: “Barnabas and Paul now visited Jerusalem and as soon as they had finished their business, returned to Antioch,” and its footnote marks “to Antioch” as implied. The apparatus gives eis to Westcott–Hort, Robinson–Pierpont and Nestle–Aland 28, ex, “out of,” to Tregelles and apo, “from,” to the NIV, so that the shelf’s “from” is a reading and not a rendering of the printed word. Diakonia is the word of 11:29, “service,” here with plēroō, “to fulfill.” Symparalabontes, “taking along with them,” stands in four verses of the New Testament: here, 15:37, where Barnabas wants to take John called Mark along, 15:38, where Paul insists they should not take him along, and Galatians 2:1 (not yet on these pages); the apparatus gives symparalabontes to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and kai symparalabontes, “and taking along,” to Robinson–Pierpont. Ton epiklēthenta Markon, “the one named Mark,” is the aorist of v12’s present participle. The name in this verse is still Saul, which the TLB prints “Paul.”
