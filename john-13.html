At the Last Supper, before the feast of the Passover, Jesus rises mid-meal and washes his disciples' feet with a basin and a towel, telling them 'I have given you an example, that you also should do just as I have done to you.' He identifies his betrayer in a private signal to the disciple he loved, dips a piece of bread and hands it to Judas, who then goes out into the night. Jesus announces that the Son of Man is now glorified, gives the disciples 'a new commandment... that you love one another,' and tells Peter — who has just boasted he will lay down his life for him — that he will deny him three times before the rooster crows.
c. AD 30, the night of the Last Supper, before the feast of the Passover (v1) — the opening scene of the same night's extended private teaching this chapter shares with chapters 14-17, told only in John.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–3 · ⚠ a chronology this Gospel keeps on its own terms, a love stated in a tense that will not let go, and a supper a real textual fork either interrupts or has already finished

⚠ ‘Before the feast of the Passover’ opens this chapter on a timeline the Synoptic Gospels do not share. Matthew, Mark and Luke all identify this same meal AS the Passover itself, with the disciples sent ahead to ‘prepare the Passover’ (compare Matthew 26:17–19; Mark 14:12–16; Luke 22:7–13, all already on these pages). This Gospel instead places the supper BEFORE the feast, and will later keep the Jewish authorities not yet having eaten the Passover on the very day Jesus stands condemned (18:28; 19:14, both already on these pages) — the clearest sign in this whole book of a chronology running roughly a day out of step with the other three. This translation states the divergence rather than harmonizing it away; it comes up again, stated honestly rather than resolved, at John 18:28–32.

‘Loved them to the end’ (eis telos) carries a double sense English struggles to hold in one phrase — TEMPORALLY, ‘until the very end’ (he goes on loving them straight through the betrayal this same verse has already named), and QUALITATIVELY, ‘to the utmost,’ an exhausted extent rather than a stopping point. ASV and KJV both print ‘unto the end,’ which reads only the first sense; NWT (1984 and 2013 alike) keeps the identical ‘to the end.’ This translation follows the shelf’s own wording while flagging, rather than resolving, the second sense the Greek idiom also carries.

⚠ Verse 2 carries a real textual fork that changes the supper’s own timeline. The reading this translation follows has the Devil’s plan already fixed ‘while the evening meal was in progress’ — a present-tense participle, mid-meal — matching NWT’s ‘while the evening meal was going on’ and ASV’s ‘during supper.’ A different, later tradition instead has the meal already finished, the reading KJV follows: ‘and supper being ended.’ The two readings are not a small matter: on the earlier text, Jesus rises MID-MEAL to wash feet and, per v12, ‘reclined again’ afterward to keep eating — the foot-washing interrupts supper. On KJV’s text, the meal this chapter twice calls a deipnon (vv2, 4) is already over before its best-known scene even starts.

‘Knowing that the Father had given all things into his hands’ (v3) repeats, almost word for word, a claim this Gospel has already made once, in testimony about Jesus rather than from his own mouth: ‘the Father loves the Son and has given all things into his hand’ (John 3:35, already on these pages). There the claim stood as someone else’s report; here the narrator states the identical fact as Jesus’s own self-knowledge, immediately before the one who holds all things kneels to wash feet.

Verses 4–11 · ⚠ a basin and a towel neither word in this book needed again, a double negative Peter cannot make stronger, and a clause the earliest manuscripts may never have had

‘Laid aside his garments’ (himatia, v4) and ‘took his garments’ (v12) are the same noun, framing the whole scene like a pair of brackets — Jesus removes the outer layer that marks him as the one at the head of the table to do a slave’s task, and puts it back on the moment the task is finished. Niptēr, ‘basin’ (v5), is a true New Testament hapax, the only time this noun appears anywhere in this corpus; its partner two words earlier, lention, ‘towel’ — a loanword from the Latin linteum — occurs twice (vv4, 5) but both times in this same scene, and nowhere else in the New Testament at all. Two ordinary household objects, named with words this Gospel’s own author needed nowhere else in his book.

Peter’s refusal (v8) is built on a double negative construction Greek uses for its strongest possible denial — ou mē plus the subjunctive, ‘you will NEVER, under any circumstance, wash my feet’ — and Jesus’s answer matches its force rather than softening it: ‘unless I wash you, you have no meros, “part” or “share,” with me.’ The stakes Peter has not yet grasped are named in the very word he is refusing: a share in what is coming, not merely a courtesy he can decline.

⚠ Verse 10’s clause ‘except for his feet’ stands inside a recognized textual complication in the Greek tradition — the earliest witnesses are not unanimous on exactly how this sentence should read, and at least one early strand of transmission appears to have run the clause together differently than the text printed here. This translation follows the fuller reading, in which a bathed man needs ONLY his feet washed; a shorter reading some early copies carry removes the exception altogether, leaving simply ‘the one who has bathed has no need to wash — he is wholly clean,’ which states the same theological point (full cleanness already given) with no residual washing named at all. Either reading keeps Jesus’s larger claim intact; this translation flags the uncertainty rather than silently picking the smoother of the two.

Verses 12–17 · a title order Jesus himself reverses, a word for a pattern meant to be copied exactly, and the shelf’s own disagreement over what to call a teacher

‘Teacher and Lord’ (v13) becomes ‘Lord and Teacher’ (v14) one verse later — the same two titles, reversed, inside Jesus’s own single continuous speech. The chiasm is easy to read past in English and is doing real work in the Greek: the ascending order his disciples use (teacher first, lord second) becomes the descending order Jesus uses of himself (lord first, teacher second) at the exact moment he is drawing the lesson from what the lord has just done like the lowest slave in the house.

The shelf itself splits on how to render didaskalos here, and the split is not trivial. ASV and NWT both keep ‘Teacher’; KJV instead prints ‘Master,’ the word it also uses for kyrios two words later in the same verse — so KJV’s English reader meets ‘Master and Lord’ where the Greek names a TEACHER and an OWNER, two distinct relationships KJV’s own vocabulary does not keep apart.

‘An example’ (hypodeigma, v15) is not Greek’s word for a mere illustration — it names a pattern meant for literal repetition, and the chapter’s own grammar insists on it: ‘that you ALSO should do JUST AS I have done to you.’ The same noun recurs, in the negative, of a pattern worth refusing rather than copying (Hebrews 4:11, not yet on these pages) — one word doing service in both directions, depending entirely on whose conduct stands in front of it.

‘A slave is not greater than his master’ (v16) reuses the identical word, doulos, this project’s own dictionary entry has already flagged as the word Jesus retires two chapters ahead, out of this book’s own sequence: ‘no longer do I call you SLAVES… I have called you FRIENDS’ (John 15:15, already on these pages). Here the word still carries its full weight, unsoftened — the very verse that uses it is the one making the slave’s task, foot-washing, the standard every disciple now owes every other.

Verses 18–20 · a psalm about a trusted friend’s betrayal, the same crude verb for eating this Gospel has used of its hardest teaching, and one more absolute ‘I am’

‘The one who eats my bread has lifted up his heel against me’ draws on Psalm 41:9 (not yet on these pages), a lament in which the betrayer is not an enemy but a trusted friend — the psalm’s own next line, which this chapter does not quote, calls him ‘my close friend, in whom I trusted.’ Citing the psalm without its full context lets the chapter make its point — Scripture itself anticipated this — without forcing the psalmist’s own historical situation onto Judas directly.

⚠ ‘Eats’ here translates trōgō, the same unusually crude verb — properly ‘to gnaw, to munch’ — this Gospel already used six times running of its hardest teaching, the one that cost Jesus a portion of his own disciples (John 6:54–58, already on these pages). The word that once described eating Jesus’s own flesh and blood now describes the ordinary meal at which his betrayer sits beside him — the identical crude verb, carried from a scene about costly belief to a scene about the man who did not believe at all.

‘That you may believe that I AM’ (v19) is this Gospel’s own absolute ‘I am,’ egō eimi with no stated predicate — the identical construction already built toward across this book’s earlier chapters, most directly at ‘before Abraham was, I am’ (John 8:58, already on these pages). Here the claim is tied to a predicted event rather than a disputed past: believing ‘that I am’ is explicitly what a fulfilled prediction — the betrayal this chapter has just named — is supposed to produce.

Verses 21–30 · a verb for trouble sounding a third time, a title appearing here for the first time in the book, and a betrayal pointed to in private while the Synoptic Gospels leave it a riddle for everyone

‘He was troubled in spirit’ (v21) is the third time in this book’s own reading sequence that tarassō is used of Jesus, after two uses already on these pages — at Lazarus’s tomb (John 11:33) and in the temple two chapters back (John 12:27) — with two further uses already up out of order, in the Farewell Discourse this very supper is about to open into (John 14:1, 27, both already on these pages). Every use names the same thing: a real, named disturbance in Jesus himself, never papered over by the narrator on his behalf.

‘The disciple whom Jesus loved’ (v23) is this book’s own first use of a title that will recur three more times, always unnamed by the narrator (19:26, 20:2 and 21:7, 20, all already on these pages) — a identity the text withholds exactly where ancient and modern readers alike have most wanted it named.

⚠ John’s own staging of the betrayer’s identification is markedly more private than either Synoptic parallel it can be set beside. Matthew and Mark both have Jesus name the SIGN to the whole table as a riddle nobody is told how to read — ‘the one who has dipped his hand with me in the dish’ (Matthew 26:23, already on these pages), ‘one of the twelve, the one dipping with me into the dish’ (Mark 14:20, already on these pages) — a shared, ordinary act every man at the table was doing, naming no one in particular. This chapter instead has Jesus answer only the beloved disciple’s whispered question, then perform an act no one else is described as doing: dipping the bread HIMSELF and HANDING it directly to Judas. The Synoptic sign leaves a riddle for the whole table; John’s leaves a private signal only one disciple is shown to have understood even afterward.

Luke’s own Gospel places ‘Satan entered into Judas’ earlier and differently — before this supper even begins, at the chief priests’ own plotting session (Luke 22:3–4, already on these pages) — where this chapter times the identical event to the moment Judas takes the morsel from Jesus’s own hand (v27). One Gospel locates the entry of Satan in a backroom deal with the authorities; this one locates it at the table, in the instant after Jesus has just shown him a last, pointed kindness.

‘And it was night’ (v30) closes the scene on the identical opposition this book has been building since its own opening chapter (‘the light shines in the darkness,’ John 1:5, already on these pages) and restated as this ministry’s own closing public image one chapter back (‘walk while you have the light,’ John 12:35–36, already on these pages). Judas does not merely leave a room; on this book’s own terms, he walks out of the light and the sentence says so in four words.

Verses 31–35 · one verb, repeated more times in two verses than almost anywhere else in this book, a title this Gospel keeps calling its disciples that it will use nowhere else, and a commandment already called both new and not new

⚠ ‘Glorified’ / ‘glorify,’ from the single root doxazō, is used FOUR times across these two verses on the shorter reading this translation follows (v31, twice; v32, twice) — a fifth occurrence stands inside a bracketed clause at the start of v32 that a real textual question may remove, since some manuscripts appear to repeat the clause rather than the earliest text carrying it once. However the count resolves, no other two-verse span in this book clusters the word this densely, at the exact moment this Gospel has delayed the word’s own fulfillment since its first sign (‘the hour has not yet come,’ John 2:4, already on these pages; ‘now the hour has come,’ John 12:23, already on these pages).

‘Little children’ (teknia, v33) is the only time in this Gospel’s own text that Jesus addresses his disciples this way — a term of endearment this book’s author will go on to use of his own readers seven times in his later letter, but never again here.

‘A new commandment’ (v34) is the half of a paradox this project’s own dictionary entry on entolē has already stated from the other side: a letter bearing this book’s own name insists it is writing ‘not… a new commandment, but the one we have had FROM THE BEGINNING’ (2 John 1:5, already on these pages), word for word the same command Jesus here calls NEW. Both claims hold in their own frame — new in the manner of its giving, old in that love of neighbor predates this very night — and the letter is plainly written with this verse’s own wording in view.

Verses 36–38 · a prediction every Gospel keeps, placed here before anyone has even left the table

Peter’s boast and its answer, placed here, stay inside this chapter’s own room — nobody has yet stood up to leave. This Gospel’s own account of the Farewell Discourse that follows will not have the group actually get up until ‘rise, let us go from here’ three chapters later (John 14:31, already on these pages, where this project’s own closing note on that chapter has already flagged the oddity of three further chapters of speech following a stated departure) — so Peter is told, at this same table, exactly what he will do before the group has gone anywhere at all.

All four Gospels keep some version of this prediction, and this book’s placement of it — at supper, well before any walk to a garden — is its own, distinct from the Synoptic timing of the identical exchange closer to the arrest itself (compare Matthew 26:33–35; Mark 14:29–31; Luke 22:31–34, all already on these pages). ‘I will lay down my life for you’ (v37) anticipates, in Peter’s own confident words, the very verb this chapter has not yet used of Jesus himself but soon will (John 15:13, already on these pages, ‘greater love has no one than this’) — Peter claims for himself, hours early and by his own will, the thing Jesus is actually about to do for him.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Himatia (vv4, 12) is kept as the plain ‘garments’ both times, preserving the bracket the Greek itself builds around the whole scene. Hypodeigma (v15) is kept as ‘example’ rather than the vaguer ‘pattern,’ to keep its force as something meant for literal repetition rather than loose inspiration. Didaskalos (vv13–14) is kept as ‘Teacher,’ matching ASV and NWT against KJV’s ‘Master,’ to keep it distinct from kyrios, ‘Lord,’ in the same two verses.

Echoes paid. ‘All things…into his hands’ (v3) repeats John 3:35 (already on these pages). ‘A slave is not greater than his master’ (v16) uses the identical doulos this project’s own dictionary entry already flagged as the word Jesus retires at John 15:15 (already on these pages, out of this book’s own sequence). Trōgō (v18) returns the crude ‘eats/gnaws’ verb from John 6:54–58 (already on these pages). The absolute ‘I am’ (v19) continues the construction built at John 8:58 (already on these pages). Tarassō (v21) reaches its third use in this book’s own sequence, after John 11:33 and John 12:27 (both already on these pages), with two more already up out of order at John 14:1 and 27. ‘And it was night’ (v30) answers the light/darkness opposition last stated at John 12:35–36 (already on these pages). And ‘a new commandment’ (v34) is answered, almost word for word, by 2 John 1:5’s ‘not a new commandment but the one we have had from the beginning’ (already on these pages).

Echoes planted. ‘The disciple whom Jesus loved’ (v23) is this book’s first use of a title that recurs twice more, not yet on these pages (20:2; 21:7, 20), and once already, at 19:26. Peter’s boast to lay down his life (v37) anticipates ‘greater love has no one than this’, already up out of sequence at John 15:13. And the prediction of Peter’s denial (v38) is answered at John 18:15–18, 25–27, already on these pages.

Left open on purpose. Exactly how verse 10’s textual question resolves — whether the earliest text named an exception for the feet at all, or stated unqualified cleanness. Whether verse 32’s fifth doxazō belongs to the earliest text or duplicates a clause already stated. And where, precisely, this supper’s own room gives way to a walk toward Gethsemane — a question this book’s own text raises at John 14:31 and this translation will not resolve ahead of reaching that chapter’s own, already-shipped note on it.

Already up ahead of sequence: John 14 and John 15, the Farewell Discourse this chapter’s own closing verses open into. This book’s own sequence now runs unbroken from chapter 1 through John 16.
