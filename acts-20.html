After the uproar at Ephesus Paul sends for the disciples, greets them and goes into Macedonia and then Greece, where he spends three months and, after a plot by the Jews as he was about to sail for Syria, decides to return through Macedonia; seven companions go with him. The narration says “we” again from Philippi, where they sail after the days of Unleavened Bread, and at Troas, on the first day of the week, Paul reasons until midnight and the young man Eutychus falls from the third floor and is picked up dead; Paul goes down, falls on him, and they bring the boy alive. He goes on foot to Assos and sails past Ephesus by way of Mitylene, Chios, Samos and Miletus, hurrying to be at Jerusalem for Pentecost, and from Miletus calls the elders of the Ephesian congregation, to whom he speaks of his service, of the bonds that await him, of overseers and wolves, and of his own hands; he kneels, prays, and they escort him weeping to the ship.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are ‘three months’ in Greece (v3), ‘the days of Unleavened Bread’ at Philippi (v6), ‘five days’ at sea and seven at Troas (v6), the ‘first day of the week’ (v7), and Pentecost as the day Paul is hurrying toward (v16); at v31 he speaks of ‘three years.’ Luke gives no total for the voyage, and this library dates the events no more closely than the book does. It follows Acts 19 at Ephesus; Acts 21 follows.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–6 · τὸν θόρυβον … μεταπεμψάμενος … παρακαλέσας ἀσπασάμενος … λόγῳ πολλῷ … Ἑλλάδα … ἐπιβουλῆς … γνώμης … συνείπετο … Ἀσιανοὶ … προσελθόντες … ἡμᾶς … μετὰ τὰς ἡμέρας τῶν ἀζύμων … ἄχρι ἡμερῶν πέντε ton thorybon … metapempsamenos … parakalesas aspasamenos … logō pollō … Hellada … epiboulēs … gnōmēs … synepeto … Asianoi … proselthontes … hēmas … meta tas hēmeras tōn azymōn … achri hēmerōn pente — Acts 20:1–6: what was the uproar Paul left Ephesus after, why did he change his route because of a plot, who were the seven men with him, and where does the “we” of Acts come back?

The uproar (Acts 20:1). Meta de to pausasthai ton thorybon, “and after the uproar had ceased,” has the article, “the” uproar, and names no cause. The last disturbance Luke has told is the one at Ephesus, which the town clerk ended when he “dismissed the assembly” (19:41). Acts 19 does not use this noun. There the trouble is a tarachos, “disturbance” (19:23), sygchysis, “the confusion” (19:29), and an assembly that is sygkechymenē, “in confusion” (19:32), so thorybos is a new word for the riot at the point where it ends. The noun stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Matthew 26:5, 27:24, Mark 5:38, 14:2, here, and Acts 21:34 and 24:18. Its verb, thorybeō, is what the Jews do to Thessalonica at 17:5, “throwing the city into an uproar,” and it is the verb of Paul’s words at v10 below, “Do not be thrown into an uproar” (see the note at v7), so the same root comes back inside the chapter. KJV, ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 “uproar” keep the noise; Geneva and Douay “tumult” are the older English word for it; TLB “When it was all over” leaves the noun out.

Sent for, encouraged, embraced (Acts 20:1). Metapempsamenos is the middle voice of a verb of summoning, “having sent for (to himself).” It stands in eight verses of the New Testament and all eight are in Acts: Cornelius’s summoning of Peter (10:5, 10:22, 10:29, 11:13), here, 24:24, 24:26 and 25:3. The apparatus gives metapempsamenos to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and proskalesamenos, “having called to himself,” to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “called unto him,” Geneva “called the disciples unto him” and Douay “calling to him” read the second; ASV, NIV, NWT 1984 and TLB “sent for” read the first. The next participle, parakalesas, “having encouraged,” is absent from Robinson–Pierpont and present in the other three. KJV “called unto him the disciples, and embraced them” and Geneva “embraced them” have nothing at this point for it; ASV “exhorted them,” Douay “exhorting them,” NIV “encouraging them,” NWT 1984 “encouraged them” and TLB “preached a farewell message” have it in different words. The same verb comes back at v2 (parakalesas again, “encouraged”) and, in the passive, at v12 (pareklēthēsan, “were comforted,” see the note at v7); this translation prints “encouraged” for it at 11:23, 14:22, 15:32 and 16:40 too. Then aspasamenos, from aspazomai, “to greet.” Its participle stands at 18:22, “having gone up and greeted the congregation, he went down to Antioch,” in the same shape as here: a greeting, then a verb of leaving (exēlthen, “went out”). It stands also at 21:7, 21:19 and 25:13. The English renderings divide on what the greeting is: KJV and Geneva “embraced them” (as this translation does) take it as the parting embrace; ASV “took leave of them,” Douay “took his leave,” NIV “said goodbye” and NWT 1984 “bidden them farewell” take it as the words. The Greek says the first word, “greeted,” and the context supplies the parting. The “embraced” here and the “embracing” at v10 are two different Greek words (aspasamenos here, symperilabōn there). TLB “left for Greece” prints Greece where this verse has Makedonian, Macedonia, and its “was in Greece three months” at v3 uses the same word for the Hellas of v2, so one English name covers two regions of the Greek.

“Much speech,” and Greece (Acts 20:2). Dielthōn de ta merē ekeina, “having gone through those parts,” names no city. Luke does not say that Paul visited Philippi, Thessalonica or Beroea on this journey, though the men of v4 come from the last two. Parakalesas autous logō pollō is “having encouraged them with much speech”: one singular logos and the participle of v1, where several versions merge the two into one noun. KJV and ASV “had given them much exhortation” turn the verb and the speech into a gift of exhortation; Geneva and Douay “exhorted them with many words” keep the verb and make the speech plural; NIV “speaking many words of encouragement” and NWT 1984 “many a word” do the same with the plural; TLB “preaching to the believers along the way in all the cities he passed through” adds the cities, which the Greek does not name. At 15:32 Luke has the same two words, dia logou pollou, of Judas and Silas, and this translation prints them there as “many words.” Hellada, “Greece,” is Hellas, which stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. Luke’s other name for a region of Greece is Achaia, which he has written three times, at 18:12, 18:27 and 19:21, where Paul resolved to pass through “Macedonia and Achaia” on the way to Jerusalem; Luke does not say why the name changes here, or that the two names cover the same ground. The verse gives no city.

Three months, a plot, and a decision (Acts 20:3). Poiēsas te mēnas treis, literally “having made three months,” is the verb Luke also uses of time at 15:33 and 18:23 (poiēsantes chronon, poiēsas chronon tina). Luke says only “Greece” for the place. Many readers connect these three months with Romans, and Romans 16:21 and 16:23 (not yet on these pages) name Timothy, Sosipater and a Gaius who is Paul’s host as sending greetings; that Romans was written in these months, and that they were spent in any one city, is reported here, not checked on this page. Epiboulē, “plot,” stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: the plot of the Jews of Damascus against Saul that “became known to Saul” (9:24), here, Paul’s own “the plots of the Jews” in his speech to the elders (20:19), and 23:30. Luke says here neither where the plot was nor how Paul learned of it. ASV “a plot was laid,” NIV “had plotted” and NWT 1984 “a plot was hatched” keep the noun’s sense of a scheme; KJV, Geneva and Douay “the Jews laid wait for him” picture an ambush, which the word does not name; TLB “discovered a plot by the Jews against his life” adds both the discovery and the target. The ship is mellonti anagesthai eis tēn Syrian, “as he was about to set sail for Syria,” the same destination as the voyage of 18:18 (exeplei eis tēn Syrian); at 21:3 Luke has Syria again. What the Greek gives is a sequence: a plot while he is about to sail, then egeneto gnōmēs tou hypostrephein dia Makedonias, “he came to a decision to return through Macedonia.” It does not say that the plot was aboard the ship or in the port; in v16 Luke says only that Paul was hurrying to be in Jerusalem for Pentecost if he could (20:16). The apparatus gives the genitive gnōmēs to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the nominative gnōmē to Robinson–Pierpont. With the genitive Paul is the one who “became of a decision”; with the nominative the decision is what “came about.” Seven English verbs give Paul the deciding: KJV and Geneva “he purposed to return through Macedonia,” Douay “he took a resolution,” ASV “he determined,” NIV “he decided,” NWT 1984 “he made up his mind” and TLB “he decided to go north to Macedonia first,” which adds a direction and “first” to the Greek hypostrephein, “to return.” The itinerary announced at 19:21, Macedonia and Achaia and then Jerusalem, is the one Luke is following; the plot changes the last leg.

The seven who accompanied him (Acts 20:4). Synepeto, “were accompanying,” is an imperfect, from a verb that stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and Luke puts it in the singular with the first name of the list. The apparatus has a longer reading: autō achri tēs Asias, “him as far as Asia,” is given to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont, and Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 have autō alone. KJV and Geneva “accompanied him into Asia” and ASV “accompanied him as far as Asia” print the longer reading, and TLB “going as far as Turkey” prints it with a modern country for Asia; Douay “there accompanied him Sopater,” NIV “accompanied by Sopater” and NWT 1984 “accompanying him” do not have it. The long reading says where the escort stops; the short one gives no stopping point. A second apparatus note: Robinson–Pierpont has no Pyrrou, “of Pyrrhus.” KJV and Geneva print “Sopater of Berea” without a father’s name. The others have it: ASV “the son of Pyrrhus,” Douay “the son of Pyrrhus,” NIV “son of Pyrrhus” and TLB “the son of Pyrrhus” have it, and so does NWT 1984 “the son of” with the father’s name in its syllabled spelling. As a person, Pyrrhus is named in this verse and nowhere else in the New Testament. The seven are named in three groups by place: Sopater of Beroea; Aristarchus and Secundus of the Thessalonians, the two cities where Paul had preached at 17:10 and 17:1; Gaius of Derbe and Timothy, given no city of his own here; and Asianoi, “of Asia,” Tychicus and Trophimus. Asianoi stands in this verse alone. KJV, ASV and Douay “of Asia, Tychicus and Trophimus” keep it as a place. NIV “from the province of Asia” and NWT 1984 “from the [district of] Asia” make it a province, and TLB “returning to their homes in Turkey” adds that they were going home. Luke does not say what the seven were for. That they were the delegates of the congregations carrying the collection to Jerusalem is an inference some readers draw, from Paul’s letters (1 Corinthians 16:3–4, not yet on these pages, where he says he will send men the Corinthians approve to carry their gift to Jerusalem); this page reports it and does not decide it.

Where the seven stand elsewhere (Acts 20:4). Sōpatros is not found with this spelling anywhere else; Romans 16:21 (not yet on these pages) has Sōsipatros among Paul’s kinsmen, and whether the two names are one man is not said. Aristarchus was one of the two Macedonians the crowd seized at the theater (19:29), is “a Macedonian of Thessalonica” in the ship’s company of Acts 27:2, and is named in Colossians 4:10 (not yet on these pages) and in Philemon 24. Sekoundos stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. Five verses name a Gaius: 19:29, where he is a Macedonian, this verse, where he is of Derbe, Romans 16:23 (not yet on these pages), 1 Corinthians 1:14, and 3 John 1; the name is common, and Luke does not say whether the Gaius of 19:29 and the Gaius of Derbe are one man. Derbe is where Paul and Barnabas came at 14:20 and Paul again at 16:1, where the disciple Timothy is, and the brothers who vouched for him are those “in Lystra and Iconium” (16:2). Timothy’s earlier steps in Acts are 16:1–3, where Paul has him circumcised, and 19:22, where Paul sends him with Erastus to Macedonia. Tychicus is named in four other verses, all of them in letters (Ephesians 6:21, Colossians 4:7, 2 Timothy 4:12 and Titus 3:12, none yet on these pages); in the first two he is sent to bring news and, in the verb of vv1–2, parakalesē tas kardias hymōn, “to encourage your hearts.” Trophimus is named again at 21:29, “the Ephesian,” which is how an Asianos would be placed, and in 2 Timothy 4:20 (not yet on these pages), left sick at Miletus, a city that appears at v15 of this chapter.

“Come over,” or “gone ahead”; and the first “us” (Acts 20:5). The apparatus is a one-letter difference. Proselthontes, from proserchomai, “having come (to),” is given to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont; proelthontes, from proerchomai, “having gone ahead,” to Nestle–Aland 28. This translation prints the first. At v13 the two swap sides: proelthontes is given to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and proselthontes to Robinson–Pierpont, and the English of 20:13 there is “having gone ahead.” The sense of “ahead” is what the seven shelf versions print, each in its own words: KJV and Douay “going before,” Geneva “went before,” ASV “had gone before,” NIV “went on ahead,” NWT 1984 “went on” and TLB “had gone on ahead.” The other word in the verse is hēmas, “us.” Luke’s narration last says “we” at 16:17, “following behind Paul and us,” and it began at 16:10, with the vision that follows Luke’s arrival at Troas (16:8): “we sought to go out to Macedonia”; in the chapters between, the first person plural (at 16:20–21, 16:28, 16:37, the Athenians’ and Paul’s words in ch. 17, 19:2, 19:25–27, 19:37 and 19:40) is in what other speakers say, not in Luke’s own telling. So this “us” is the first since 16:17, in a place Luke says nothing about meanwhile. The “we” of 16:12–17 is set down at Philippi and the next verse picks it up as the ship leaves Philippi, and Luke does not say where the narrator was in the chapters between, or that he stayed in Philippi. From here it runs through vv6, 7, 8 and 13–15, and on into chapter 21.

After the days of Unleavened Bread, five days, and Troas (Acts 20:6). Meta tas hēmeras tōn azymōn, “after the days of the Unleavened Bread,” dates the sailing by the festival. The phrase hēmerai tōn azymōn stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and 12:3, “now those were the days of the Unleavened Bread,” the days of Peter’s arrest. Luke says no more of the feast than the date, and gives no year. KJV, ASV and Geneva “after the days of unleavened bread” keep the phrase; Douay “after the days of the Azymes” keeps the Greek word azyma in English letters; NIV “after the Festival of Unleavened Bread” adds “Festival”; NWT 1984 “after the days of the unfermented cakes” chooses a different English noun, which NWT 2013 replaces with “after the days of the Unleavened Bread”; TLB “As soon as the Passover ceremonies ended” names a different feast, though Luke himself says at Luke 22:1 that the feast of Unleavened Bread is “called the Passover.” Achri hēmerōn pente, “up to five days,” is the length of the crossing: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “in five days” and NWT 1984 “within five days” keep achri as a limit; NIV and TLB “five days later” make it an interval. In the other direction, from Troas toward Philippi, Luke gave the crossing as two stages: 16:11, “we made a straight run to Samothrace, and on the next day to Neapolis,” and then 16:12, “from there to Philippi.” Here Luke names no port and no stop, and says nothing about why the passage was longer. The apparatus gives hou to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont and hopou to Nestle–Aland 28 for “where we stayed seven days”; both mean “where,” and the English does not change. Troas stands in six verses of the New Testament: 16:8, 16:11, vv5 and 6 here, 2 Corinthians 2:12 and 2 Timothy 4:13 (neither yet on these pages). In 2 Corinthians 2:12–13 Paul comes to Troas for the gospel, with “a door” open to him, finds no rest because he does not find Titus, and takes leave of them and goes out to Macedonia; Luke names no Troas stop in the journey of vv1–2, and whether the letter’s visit is a stop inside that journey is not said. The seven days lead to the first day of the week, which is the subject of the note at v7.

Verses 7–12 · τῇ μιᾷ τῶν σαββάτων … κλάσαι ἄρτον … διελέγετο … παρέτεινεν … λαμπάδες ἱκαναὶ ἐν τῷ ὑπερῴῳ … νεανίας ὀνόματι Εὔτυχος … θυρίδος … καταφερόμενος … κατενεχθεὶς … τριστέγου … ἤρθη νεκρός … ἐπέπεσεν … συμπεριλαβών … Μὴ θορυβεῖσθε … ἡ ψυχὴ αὐτοῦ ἐν αὐτῷ ἐστιν … γευσάμενος … ὁμιλήσας ἄχρι αὐγῆς … τὸν παῖδα ζῶντα … οὐ μετρίως tē mia tōn sabbatōn … klasai arton … dielegeto … pareteinen … lampades hikanai en tō hyperōō … neanias onomati Eutychos … thyridos … katapheromenos … katenechtheis … tristegou … ērthē nekros … epepesen … symperilabōn … Mē thorybeisthe … hē psychē autou en autō estin … geusamenos … homilēsas achri augēs … ton paida zōnta … ou metriōs — Eutychus at Troas: the first day of the week, “break bread,” a young man picked up dead from the third floor, and what “his soul is in him” does and does not settle

“The first day of the week” (Acts 20:7). Tē mia tōn sabbatōn is, word for word, “the one of the sabbaths”: the cardinal “one” where Greek would ordinarily say “first,” with sabbaton standing for the whole seven-day round. The phrase or its near kin stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Matthew 28:1 (eis mian sabbatōn), Mark 16:2, Luke 24:1, John 20:1, John 20:19 (tē mia sabbatōn, without tōn), this verse, and the singular kata mian sabbatou at 1 Corinthians 16:2 (not yet on these pages). The bracketed ending of Mark has the ordinal instead, prōtē sabbatou (16:9). This translation prints “first day of the week” at Luke 24:1 and John 20:1 and here, so a reader meets the same English at the tomb and at Troas. KJV and ASV “upon the first day of the week,” NIV, NWT 1984 and Douay “on the first day of the week” and Geneva “the first day of the week” give Luke’s phrase; TLB “On Sunday” names a day that the Greek does not. Nor does Luke write kyriakē hēmera: kyriakos, “of the Lord,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, 1 Corinthians 11:20 (not yet on these pages) and Revelation 1:10, and neither is in Acts 20:7. The verse does not say whether Luke counts the day from sunset or from sunrise, and this page does not decide it. It tells of one meeting, held in the seven days Paul spent at Troas (v6), with Paul “about to leave,” tē epaurion, “the next day.”

“To break bread,” and “we” or “the disciples” (Acts 20:7 and 11). Klasai arton is a purpose clause, a gathering “to break bread,” and at v11 Paul does it, klasas ton arton, “breaking the bread.” Apart from the feeding of the five thousand (Luke 9:16) and the Last Supper (Luke 22:19), Luke uses the verb klaō or the noun klasis of bread in seven verses: Luke 24:30, where Jesus at table “took the bread and blessed it, and broke it”; 24:35, where he “was made known to them in the breaking of the bread”; Acts 2:42, where “the breaking of the bread” stands with the teaching, the fellowship and the prayers; 2:46, “breaking bread from house to house,” where Luke adds that they “were sharing food”; these two verses of Acts 20; and 27:35, where Paul on the ship takes bread, gives thanks to God before all and, klasas, “began to eat.” Paul uses the verb himself at 1 Corinthians 10:16 and 11:24 (not yet on these pages). Deipnon, “supper,” does not stand anywhere in Acts (a search of Acts found no verse), and the Greek of vv7–11 does not say whether this was a meal, a rite or both; this page does not decide it. The shelf decides in two places. NWT 1984 “to have a meal” at v7 and “began the meal and took food” at v11 makes the clause a meal; TLB “a Communion service” makes it a rite; ASV, KJV, Geneva, Douay and NIV print “break bread,” the Greek idiom, and leave the question where Luke left it. The apparatus has a variant in the same clause: hēmōn, “we,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and tōn mathētōn, “the disciples,” to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “the disciples came together” and Geneva “the disciples being come together” print the second; ASV “we were gathered together,” Douay “we were assembled,” NWT 1984 “we were gathered together,” NIV “we came together” and TLB “we gathered” print the first, which keeps the “we” that appears again at v5 and returns in ēmen synēgmenoi, “we were gathered,” at v8. There KJV “where they were gathered together” and Geneva “where they were gathered together” print “they” where the Greek printed here has “we”; the apparatus marks no variant at v8, and what Greek stands behind that “they” has not been checked on this page. The article before arton in v11 is variable too: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print ton and Robinson–Pierpont leave it out, so that the bread of v11 is “the bread” or just “bread.” ASV “had broken the bread” has the article; KJV “had broken bread” and Geneva “had broken bread” do not.

“Reasoning,” “prolonged” and midnight (Acts 20:7). Dielegeto, “was reasoning,” is dialegomai, which this translation prints “reasoned” or “reasoning” at 17:2, 17:17, 18:4, 18:19, 19:8 and 19:9, and prints again at v9 of this passage. The verb stands in thirteen verses of the New Testament, ten of them in Acts: 17:2, 17:17, 18:4, 18:19, 19:8, 19:9, here, v9, 24:12 and 24:25; the other three are Mark 9:34, Hebrews 12:5 and Jude 9. It is a word of give-and-take, and the verse does not say whether anyone besides Paul spoke. The shelf varies. ASV “discoursed,” Douay “discoursed” and NWT 1984 “began discoursing” keep a talk; KJV “preached,” Geneva “preached” and TLB “preaching” use the English word that usually stands for kēryssein, a different Greek verb, which does not stand in this chapter until kēryssōn at v25; NIV “spoke” is the plainest. Pareteinen ton logon, “he prolonged the word,” has a verb, parateinein, that stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. ASV “prolonged his speech” and NWT 1984 “prolonged his speech” keep the verb; KJV “continued his speech,” Douay “continued his speech,” Geneva “continued the preaching” and NIV “kept on talking” give the sense. Mesonyktion, “midnight,” stands in four verses of the New Testament: Acts 16:25, where Paul and Silas, “about midnight,” were singing hymns to God in the prison; this verse; Mark 13:35, where midnight is one of the hours at which the master of the house may come; and Luke 11:5, where a man goes to a friend “at midnight” to ask for three loaves.

The lamps and the upper room (Acts 20:8). Lampades stands in nine verses of the New Testament: five in Matthew 25 (vv1, 3, 4, 7 and 8, the ten girls), John 18:3 (“torches and lamps” in the party that arrests Jesus), this verse, Revelation 4:5 (“lamps of fire” before the throne) and Revelation 8:10, where the same word is a burning star, printed “torch” in this translation, so the word covers a torch as well as a lamp. KJV, ASV and Geneva print “many lights,” while Douay “lamps,” NIV “lamps,” NWT 1984 “lamps” and TLB “flickering lamps” print the word itself, and the TLB adds a flicker that the Greek does not say. Hikanai, here “many,” is a word of sufficient or considerable amount, and it stands again in this chapter at v11, eph’ hikanon, “for a considerable time,” and at v37, hikanos klauthmos, “considerable weeping.” NWT 1984 “quite a few lamps” here and “quite a while” at v11 keeps one English phrase for both, and Douay “a great number of lamps” puts the amount as a number. The hyperōon, the upper room, stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 1:13, where the eleven go up to it; 9:37, where Tabitha’s body is laid in it; 9:39, where Peter is taken up to it; and here. ASV, KJV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 “upper chamber” and NIV and TLB “upstairs room” give it the same sense. The verses do not say in so many words that the upper room is the third floor of v9; Paul’s “going down” (v10) and “going up” (v11) are what connect them.

A young man named Eutychus on the window (Acts 20:9). Neanias, “young man,” is a word that stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 7:58, where the witnesses lay their garments at the feet of “a young man called Saul”; here; and 23:17 and 23:18, where it is Paul’s sister’s son, named as such at 23:16. Its diminutive neaniskos stands in Acts at 2:17 and 5:10, and in the Greek printed at 23:22. The word fixes no age, as the note on Acts 7 also says. ASV, KJV, Geneva, Douay, NIV, NWT 1984 and TLB print “young man” here, and this translation does too. Eutychos stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. It is an ordinary Greek name, from eu, “well,” and tychē, “fortune,” so “Fortunate,” and Luke leaves it without comment: a man named Fortunate is the one picked up dead. The apparatus gives kathezomenos, “sitting,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and kathēmenos to Robinson–Pierpont, two forms of one meaning. The seat is epi tēs thyridos, “on the window.” Thyris, a small opening (from thyra, “door”), stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 2 Corinthians 11:33 (not yet on these pages), where Paul says he was let down “through a window” in a basket; Luke does not say whether the opening at Troas had a shutter or a sill. Douay “on the window” keeps the preposition; ASV “in the window,” KJV “in a window” and NIV “in a window” put him inside the opening, NWT 1984 “at the window” beside it, and TLB “on the windowsill” supplies a sill.

“Borne down” twice, the third floor and “picked up dead” (Acts 20:9). Luke uses one verb twice, with a different tense and a different preposition: katapheromenos hypnōi bathei, a present participle, “being borne down by deep sleep,” while Paul reasons, and then katenechtheis apo tou hypnou, an aorist participle, “borne down from the sleep,” the moment it overcame him. Katapherō stands in three verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: this verse, which has it twice, and 25:7, and 26:10. Epi pleion, “at greater length,” stands in five verses: Acts 4:17 and this verse. The other three are Acts 24:4, 2 Timothy 2:16 and 3:9 (the last two not yet on these pages). Luke has the figure of sleep as weight once more in his Gospel, bebarēmenoi hypnōi, “weighed down with sleep,” of Peter and those with him at Luke 9:32. The shelf mostly changes the English between the two clauses. ASV “borne down with deep sleep” and, a clause later, “being borne down by his sleep” is the one version on the shelf that prints the same English verb twice, and its “with” and “by” follow the Greek’s dative and apo. KJV “being fallen into a deep sleep” and then “sunk down with sleep” uses two verbs, and its first, a state already reached, is not the progressive of the Greek. Geneva “fallen into a dead sleep” and “overcome with sleep” also changes the verb, and adds “dead” for bathys, “deep.” Douay “oppressed with a deep sleep” and then “by occasion of his sleep” turns the second into a cause. NIV “sinking into a deep sleep” and then “sound asleep” and NWT 1984 “fell into a deep sleep” and then “collapsing in sleep” also use two verbs, and TLB “went fast asleep” folds both into one. This translation prints “borne down” both times, as the ASV does, so that the English reader sees the repeated verb. The fall is apo tou tristegou kato, “from the third floor down.” Tristegon stands in this verse alone in the New Testament and is built on tri-, “three,” and stegē, “roof,” the noun of Mark 2:4, where they “took the roof off”; it is, literally, “three-roofed,” and names a level without giving a height. KJV, Geneva and Douay “third loft” and ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 “third story” say it two ways, and TLB “fell three stories” counts the floors. The last clause is kai ērthē nekros, “and was picked up dead”: the verb is airō, “lift, take up,” in the passive, and the Greek has nekros bare, with no “as” or “like” before it. It is not the verb of the raising in Acts 9:41, where Peter, giving Tabitha a hand, anestēsen autēn, “raised her up,” and then parestēsen autēn zōsan, “presented her alive.” The same form ērthē stands elsewhere only at Luke 9:17, of the broken pieces that “were picked up,” and Acts 8:33, “his judgment was taken away.” ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “taken up dead” and NIV and NWT 1984 “picked up dead” keep the verb; TLB “to his death below” names the death and drops the lifting.

“Fell on him” and “embracing him” (Acts 20:10). The young man epesen, “fell,” in v9; Paul, katabas, “going down,” epepesen autō, “fell on him,” the same verb with epi- in front. Epipiptein stands in five verses of Acts: 10:44 and 11:15, where the Holy Spirit falls on the hearers, 19:17, where fear falls on those who lived in Ephesus, here, and 20:37, where the elders, “falling on Paul’s neck,” kiss him. Symperilabōn, “embracing,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. ASV and KJV “fell on him” keep the Greek verb, NWT 1984 “threw himself upon him” and NIV “threw himself on the young man” make it an act, Geneva and Douay “laid himself upon him” make it a laying, and TLB “took him into his arms” has the embrace and not the falling. For the embrace, ASV, KJV and Douay “embracing him,” Geneva and NWT 1984 “embraced him” and NIV “put his arms around him” give the same sense. The posture recalls two prophet stories in the Hebrew. In Elijah’s, at the house of the widow whose son has no breath left, he carries the boy up to the upper chamber (1 Kings 17:19, not yet on these pages) and prays that the boy’s soul return, וַיִּתְמֹדֵד עַל־הַיֶּלֶד שָׁלֹשׁ פְּעָמִים, wayyitmoded al-hayyeled shalosh pe’amim, “he stretched himself out on the child three times,” and תָּשָׁב נָא נֶפֶשׁ־הַיֶּלֶד הַזֶּה עַל־קִרְבּוֹ, “let the soul of this child return into him” (1 Kings 17:21, not yet on these pages); then וַתָּשָׁב נֶפֶשׁ־הַיֶּלֶד עַל־קִרְבּוֹ וַיֶּחִי, “and the soul of the child returned into him, and he lived” (17:22), and Elijah brings him down from the upper chamber and gives him to his mother (17:23). In Elisha’s the prophet shuts the door and prays to Jehovah (2 Kings 4:33, not yet on these pages), then וַיַּעַל וַיִּשְׁכַּב עַל־הַיֶּלֶד, “he went up and lay on the child,” mouth to mouth, eyes to eyes, hands to hands, and וַיִּגְהַר עָלָיו, “bent over him” (4:34); and when he does it again, וַיִּפְקַח הַנַּעַר אֶת־עֵינָיו, “the boy opened his eyes” (2 Kings 4:35, not yet on these pages). Luke names both prophets in one speech of Jesus, at Luke 4:26 and 4:27, and at Luke 7:15 he writes, of the widow’s son at Nain, that Jesus “gave him to his mother,” a clause that stands in the Hebrew of Elijah’s story as וַיִּתְּנֵהוּ לְאִמּוֹ (1 Kings 17:23, not yet on these pages). Acts 20 does not name Elijah or Elisha, Paul prays nothing and stretches nothing three times in the Greek, and the Septuagint wording of these two stories is not checked on this page; whether Luke meant a reader to hear the prophets is reported as open, not decided.

“Do not be thrown into an uproar” (Acts 20:10). Mē thorybeisthe is the verb of the noun thorybos that closes the Ephesus scene at v1, “after the uproar had ceased,” so the chapter opens with a noun for the riot at Ephesus and, at Troas, turns the verb of the same stem on a room. The verb thorybeō stands in four verses of the New Testament: Matthew 9:23, Mark 5:39, Acts 17:5, where the Jews “were throwing the city into an uproar,” and here. The noun stands in seven: Matthew 26:5 and 27:24, Mark 5:38 and 14:2, Acts 20:1, and 21:34 and 24:18. At Mark 5:39 the form is the same, thorybeisthe, as a question at Jairus’s house: this translation prints “Why are you making a commotion and weeping?” and there follows “The child is not dead, but sleeping,” with Jairus’s daughter raised in the verses after. Here the form is a command with mē, and the reason given is hē gar psychē autou en autō estin. The English of v10 and Mark 5:39 differs, “uproar” against “commotion,” for one Greek stem, and a reader of the English alone will not see it. NWT 1984 “STOP raising a clamor” makes the crowd the cause of the noise, which the passive does not say; ASV “Make ye no ado,” KJV “Trouble not yourselves,” Geneva “Trouble not yourselves,” Douay “Be not troubled,” NIV “Don’t be alarmed” and TLB “Don’t worry” render it as a state of mind.

“His soul is in him” (Acts 20:10). Hē gar psychē autou en autō estin, with a present estin, “for his soul is in him.” Psychē is “soul” and just as often “life”; this translation prints “soul” at 2:27, 2:41 and 2:43 and “lives” at 15:26, and here “soul,” the English that keeps the Greek noun visible. The Hebrew behind the old stories is nefesh, which Elijah asks to return (1 Kings 17:21, above). NWT 1984 “his soul is in him” and Douay “his soul is in him” print the noun; ASV, KJV and Geneva “his life is in him” print the sense; NIV “He’s alive!” and TLB “he’s all right!” give a verdict instead of a sentence about the soul. The NWT 2013, a later edition than the 1984, reads “he is alive” at this place. Two readings of the sentence are possible, and the Greek does not choose between them. On the first, Paul says that the young man is not dead, as Jesus says at Mark 5:39 and, in the Greek of Luke 8:52, ouk apethanen alla katheudei, “she has not died, but is sleeping.” On the second, Paul says that life has come back through the falling-on and the embrace, and the narrator’s nekros in v9 means what it says; in Elijah’s story the soul’s return follows a stretching-out and a prayer (1 Kings 17:21–22, above). Luke’s own account of Jairus’s daughter says her spirit returned, epestrepsen to pneuma autēs (Luke 8:55), with pneuma, where Acts 20 says psychē and has no word of returning. Whether Eutychus was dead and restored, or was thought dead and was not, is not said in v10, and the next verse says only that he was brought “alive.”

“Tasting,” “talked” and “until daybreak” (Acts 20:11). Geusamenos, “tasting,” is a verb that can mean to taste or to eat: in Acts it stands in three verses, 10:10, where Peter “wanted to eat,” here, and 23:14. The order of the verse is bread broken, then tasted, then a long talk. Douay “breaking bread and tasting” keeps the word; ASV “eaten,” KJV “eaten” and Geneva “eaten” give the sense; NIV “broke bread and ate” does too; NWT 1984 “took food” keeps the meal of v7, and TLB “ate the Lord’s Supper” chooses the rite. Homilēsas, “having talked,” is the verb homileō, which stands in four verses of the New Testament: Luke 24:14 and 24:15, where the two on the road to Emmaus are “talking” about what had happened when Jesus joins them, and who is then made known to them “in the breaking of the bread” (24:35); here; and Acts 24:26, where Felix, who “kept sending for him more often,” is “conversing with him.” The noun homilia, a gathering or conversation, is the source of the English “homily”; it stands once in the New Testament, in 1 Corinthians 15:33 (not yet on these pages). ASV “talked with them a long while,” KJV “talked a long while,” Douay “talked a long time to them,” Geneva “spoken a long while,” NIV “talking until daylight” and NWT 1984 “conversing for quite a while” keep it as talk, while TLB “preached another long sermon” makes it a sermon, in the way that “homily” later became one. Achri augēs, “until daybreak,” has a noun, augē, that stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. ASV and KJV “even till break of day,” Geneva “till the dawning of the day,” Douay “until daylight,” NWT 1984 “until daybreak” and TLB “dawn” give the same hour. So the meeting that began on the first day of the week ends, by the verse’s own words, at the daybreak after midnight, and Paul “thus went out,” houtōs exēlthen, as he had been about to do (v7).

“The boy alive” and “comforted not a little” (Acts 20:12). Ēgagon de ton paida zōnta: pais, “boy” here, is the word of pais, “child, boy, servant.” It stands in Acts in six verses: 3:13 and 3:26, of Jesus, “his servant,” 4:25, of David, “your servant,” 4:27 and 4:30, “your holy servant Jesus,” and here. This translation prints “boy” where the context is a young person, as at Luke 2:43, “the boy Jesus.” Luke called him neanias at v9 and calls him pais now, and does not say whether the two words describe one age or two sides of his place in the household. At v9 the seven versions on the shelf print “young man” (above); at v12 Geneva “the boy” and NWT 1984 “the boy” keep a separate word, ASV “the lad” and Douay “the youth” vary it, and KJV “the young man” and NIV “the young man” return to the word of v9. The Greek has “they brought,” ēgagon, with no “home” and no “away”; NWT 1984 “took the boy away alive” and NIV “took the young man home alive” supply a direction, and the subject of the verb, who “they” are, is not named. Zōnta, “alive,” is the participle of Luke in two other places where someone is shown alive after death: Acts 1:3, parestēsen heauton zonta, where Jesus “presented himself alive,” and 9:41, zōsan, of Tabitha; there Luke has parestēsen, “presented,” and here ēgagon, “brought.” The infinitive of the same verb stands at 25:19, where Festus reports that Paul asserted of a dead Jesus that he was zēn, “alive.” Pareklēthēsan, “were comforted,” is parakaleō, the verb that this translation prints “encouraged” at v1 and v2, where Paul encourages the disciples and the places he passes through; the English splits where the Greek keeps one verb, and the dictionary entry for paraklēsis sets out the range, “comfort, encouragement, consolation.” Ou metriōs, “not moderately,” is a litotes: the adverb metriōs stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and Luke’s other negative of amount, ouk oligos, “no small,” stands in eight verses of Acts, among them the “no small disturbance” of 12:18 and 19:23 and the “no small profit” of 19:24. ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “not a little comforted” keep the litotes, NIV “greatly comforted” and NWT 1984 “comforted beyond measure” say outright what the double negative implies. In the Greek the comfort is the last event of the scene: a death and a meal and a sermon that ran to daybreak, and then “not a little.”

Verses 13–16 · Ἡμεῖς … προελθόντες … ἀνήχθημεν ἐπὶ τὴν Ἆσσον … ἀναλαμβάνειν … πεζεύειν … συνέβαλλεν … ἄντικρυς Χίου … παρεβάλομεν … παραπλεῦσαι τὴν Ἔφεσον … χρονοτριβῆσαι … πεντηκοστῆς Hēmeis … proelthontes … anēchthēmen epi tēn Asson … analambanein … pezeuein … synebalen … antikrys Chiou … parebalomen … parapleusai tēn Epheson … chronotribēsai … pentēkostēs — Troas to Miletus: why did Paul walk to Assos, and why did he sail past Ephesus?

“We” set sail for Assos (Acts 20:13). Hēmeis de, “and we,” is the pronoun put in for contrast: the ship goes one way and Paul another. The “we” is the one that came back at 20:5, “were waiting for us in Troas,” and at 20:6, hēmeis de exepleusamen, “but we sailed away from Philippi.” Luke’s narrated “we” last stood at 16:16–17; the first-person plurals that turn up in 17:1–19:41 sit inside what speakers say. He does not say who “we” are here, and the same “we” carries through vv13–15 in anēchthēmen, ēlthomen, katēntēsamen and parebalomen. The apparatus gives proelthontes, “having gone ahead,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 at v13 and proselthontes, “having come to,” to Robinson–Pierpont. It is the same pair as at v5, but the editions do not simply swap: at v5 Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont print proselthontes and Nestle–Aland 28 proelthontes, so Westcott–Hort and Tregelles print proselthontes at v5 and proelthontes at v13, Robinson–Pierpont proselthontes at both, and Nestle–Aland 28 proelthontes at both. This translation prints the first at v13 (“having gone ahead”) and the second at v5 (“having come over”). Only the first is a verb of going ahead of someone: proerchomai stands in two verses of Acts, 12:10 and here; the same verb is at Mark 6:33, where the crowds “went ahead” of Jesus and his men. The second apparatus note is epi to ploion, “to the ship,” for Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and eis to ploion, “into the ship,” for Robinson–Pierpont. ASV “going before to the ship,” NWT 1984 “went ahead to the boat” and NIV “went on ahead to the ship” keep the article; KJV “went before to ship” and Geneva “went before to ship” drop it. The verb anēchthēmen, “we set sail,” is the sailors’ word for putting out to sea, literally “were led up”; Luke uses it of ships at 13:13, 16:11 (from Troas, and heading the other way), 18:21 (“he sailed from Ephesus”) and at 20:3, and at 21:1–2 and 27:2. ASV “set sail for Assos” and NWT 1984 “set sail to” keep it; KJV “sailed unto Assos” and NIV “sailed for Assos” say only “sailed.” Assos is, in the Greek, nothing but the name with the article (tēn Asson); its position on the coast is reported from outside the text and is not checked on this page.

“To take on board” and “on foot” (Acts 20:13–14). Analambanein, “to take on board,” is analambanō, “take up,” in its passenger sense; at v14 analabontes auton repeats it, “having taken him on board.” The verb is passive and a different sense at 1:2, anelēmphthē, “he was taken up,” and at 1:11, 1:22 and 10:16; in the active it stands again at 23:31, where the soldiers, analabontes ton Paulon, take Paul up and bring him by night to Antipatris, kata to diatetagmenon autois, “as it had been ordered to them.” That is the same participle diatetagmenon that v13 has as diatetagmenos ēn, “he had arranged”: a perfect participle of diatassō, “to order, to arrange,” with a periphrastic pluperfect, so Paul had given the instruction earlier. The apparatus gives diatetagmenos ēn to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and ēn diatetagmenos to Robinson–Pierpont: word order only. NWT 1984 “after giving instructions to this effect” makes it an order Paul gave; KJV “for so had he appointed” and ASV “for so had he appointed” keep the Greek’s gar, “for,” without saying what was ordered; NIV “He had made this arrangement” turns the “for” into a new sentence. Pezeuein, “to go on foot,” is the verb from the adjective for “on foot”: pezeuein stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Its adverb pezē, “on foot,” is at Matthew 14:13 and Mark 6:33, and each time the people go on foot while Jesus goes en tō ploiō, “in the boat” (Mark 6:32), which is the same contrast, ship and foot, that this verse draws. NWT 1984 “go on foot,” KJV “go afoot” and Geneva “go afoot” keep the word for feet; ASV “go by land,” Douay “travel by land” and TLB “going by land” say what the route was in place of how he went. Why Paul chose to walk the stretch to Assos the text does not say, and this page does not guess. At v14 synebalen hēmin, “he met us,” carries the apparatus synebalen with the imperfect (synebal-len, two ls) for Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the aorist, one l, for Robinson–Pierpont, a difference of tense (“was meeting” against “met”) that English hardly shows. Symballō has a range in Luke–Acts: Luke 2:19 Mary “pondering” things in her heart, Luke 14:31 a king going “to engage” another in war, Acts 4:15 the council “conferring” with one another, 17:18 philosophers “conversing” with Paul, and 18:27 Apollos “helping” the believers; symballō in these forms stands in six verses of the New Testament, and here it is the plain “meet.” NWT 1984 “caught up with us” and TLB “joined us” add a chase or a joining; KJV “met with us,” ASV “met us” and NIV “met us” do not, and Geneva “was come unto us” has no verb of meeting.

Three words for “next day” (Acts 20:14–15). Mitylēnēn is, again, a name only, and the same is true of Chiou and Samon (their positions are not described in the verses; the five stops are named in the order Assos, Mitylene, Chios, Samos, Miletus). Antikrys Chiou, “off Chios” (literally “opposite”), is a word with a single appearance: antikrys stands in one verse of the New Testament, and the apparatus gives antikrys to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the shorter spelling antikry to Robinson–Pierpont. NWT 1984 “opposite Chiʹos,” NIV “off Chios” and TLB “passed Chios” are three readings (a position, a position, and a passing, the last of which says more than katēntēsamen, “we arrived”); KJV “over against Chios” and ASV “over against Chios” keep the sense of “facing.” Luke marks the days with three different phrases: tē epiousē (“the next day,” v15), tē heterā (“the other day”) and tē echomenē (“the next”). Tē epiousē stands in 7:26, 16:11 and here, and again at 21:18 and 23:11, which is five verses of Acts; tē echomenē is also at 21:26 and at Luke 13:33, where Jesus speaks of “today and tomorrow and the next”; and tē heterā as a day-word is here and, as tē te heterā, at 27:3. NWT 1984 prints three English phrases: “the succeeding day,” “the next day” and “on the following day”; KJV prints “the next day” three times over, and Geneva “the next day” also three times. Luke puts no day between Assos and Mitylene: v14 says only that they came to it. The verb of v15, parebalomen, rendered here “we crossed over,” is paraballō, and paraballō in these forms stands in one verse of the New Testament; a nautical sense, “to put in at, to touch at,” is reported for it and is not checked on this page. ASV “touched at Samos” and NWT 1984 “touched at” read it as a stop; NIV “crossed over to Samos” reads it as a crossing; KJV “arrived at Samos” and Douay “arrived at Samos” take the verb to be like katēntēsamen before it. The apparatus has Robinson–Pierpont add, after Samos, kai meinantes en Trogyllio, “and having stayed at Trogyllium”; Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 do not. On the shelf, KJV “tarried at Trogyllium” and Geneva “tarried at Trogyllium” have it (two of the seven versions, not one version alone); the other five (ASV, Douay, NIV, TLB and NWT 1984) go straight from Samos to Miletus, as does this translation. Whether the words belong in Luke is a question of the manuscripts, not of the sea.

Sail past Ephesus, hurrying for Pentecost (Acts 20:16). Kekrikei gar ho Paulos, “for Paul had decided,” is a pluperfect, a decision made before the ship reached the coast; the apparatus gives kekrikei to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the aorist Ekrinen, “decided,” to Robinson–Pierpont. NIV “had decided to sail past” keeps the verb plain. The pluperfect shows in English too in NWT 1984 “had decided to sail past”. KJV “had determined to sail by Ephesus” and ASV “had determined to sail past Ephesus”. Parapleusai (“to sail past”) stands in one verse of the New Testament, and TLB “decided against stopping at Ephesus this time” says what the sailing past meant and adds “this time,” which is not in the Greek. The city had its place in Paul’s story: he left Priscilla and Aquila at Ephesus and reasoned in the synagogue, and when asked to stay longer he refused and said Palin anakampsō pros hymas, “I will come back to you again, God willing” (18:19–21); and after the uproar of Acts 19 he “stayed a time in Asia” (19:22, epechen chronon eis tēn Asian). Now chronotribēsai, “to spend time,” a verb that means to wear time away, stands in one verse of the New Testament and he wants none of it “in Asia” (Asia). NWT 1984 prints “the [district of] Asia” and NIV “the province of Asia,” both of which add a word; KJV “spend the time in Asia” adds the article that the Greek does not have; Douay “lest he should be stayed any time in Asia” follows the Latin’s idea of being kept. Espeuden, “he was hurrying,” is imperfect, a state and not an act; NWT 1984 “he was hastening” and KJV “he hasted” keep it. The goal is tēn hēmeran tēs pentēkostēs, “the day of Pentecost”: pentēkostē stands in three verses of the New Testament, Acts 2:1, here and 1 Corinthians 16:8 (not yet on these pages), where Paul writes epimenō de en Ephesō heōs tēs pentēkostēs, “I will remain in Ephesus until Pentecost”: a plan stated in a letter, which the text of Acts does not connect to this voyage. For the feast itself see pentēkostē and Shavuot. NWT 1984 prints “the [festival of] Pentecost,” with the bracket marking the added word; Douay “to keep the day of Pentecost at Jerusalem” has “keep,” where the Greek has genesthai eis Hierosolyma, “to be in Jerusalem.” What the verses give toward the calendar is this: they left Philippi after the days of Unleavened Bread (20:6), took five days to reach Troas, stayed seven there, and then made the five named stops, with day-words at v15 for three of them. The sum of those days is not stated, and this page does not total them or name a date for Pentecost. Whether Paul reached Jerusalem by it is for Luke’s narrative after this chapter (not yet on these pages).

Verses 17–27 · μετεκαλέσατο τοὺς πρεσβυτέρους τῆς ἐκκλησίας … Ὑμεῖς ἐπίστασθε … δουλεύων τῷ κυρίῳ μετὰ πάσης ταπεινοφροσύνης καὶ δακρύων … οὐδὲν ὑπεστειλάμην … διαμαρτυρόμενος … δεδεμένος ἐγὼ τῷ πνεύματι … τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον κατὰ πόλιν διαμαρτύρεται … τελειῶσαι τὸν δρόμον … οὐκέτι ὄψεσθε τὸ πρόσωπόν μου … καθαρός εἰμι ἀπὸ τοῦ αἵματος πάντων … πᾶσαν τὴν βουλὴν τοῦ θεοῦ metekalesato tous presbyterous tēs ekklēsias … Hymeis epistasthe … douleuōn tō kyriō meta pasēs tapeinophrosynēs kai dakryōn … ouden hypestilamēn … diamartyromenos … dedemenos egō tō pneumati … to pneuma to hagion kata polin diamartyretai … teleiōsai ton dromon … ouketi opsesthe to prosōpon mou … katharos eimi apo tou haimatos pantōn … pasan tēn boulēn tou theou — Paul’s farewell to the Ephesian elders at Miletus (Acts 20:17–27): what “bound in the spirit” means, why he is “clean from the blood of all,” and what “the whole counsel of God” was

“Called over” the elders of the congregation (Acts 20:17). Apo de tēs Milētou pempsas eis Epheson metekalesato tous presbyterous tēs ekklēsias: Paul sends from Miletus to Ephesus and metekalesato, “called over,” the elders. Metakaleō stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 7:14, 10:32, here, and 24:25. In the first two, as here, a sending comes before the calling: Joseph “sent and summoned” Jacob (7:14), and Cornelius is told, “Send, then, to Joppa and call for Simon” (10:32). Verse 1 of this chapter has the other meta- compound of sending, metapempsamenos, “having sent for,” of the disciples he sent for after the uproar of chapter 19 (20:1). The noun is presbyteros. Forms built on presbyter- stand in nineteen verses of Acts, and in ten of them the elders belong to the believers: 11:30, 14:23, 15:2, 15:4, 15:6, 15:22, 15:23, 16:4 and this verse make nine; the tenth is 21:18. The other nine are Joel’s “old men” at 2:17 and the elders of the people or of the Jews, at 4:5, 4:8, 4:23 and 6:12, and at 22:5, 23:14, 24:1 and 25:15. At 14:23 Paul and Barnabas appointed elders “in each congregation,” and the Greek left open whether that meant one in each city; here the elders are those tēs ekklēsias, “of the congregation,” in the singular, and the apparatus has no variant at this verse. This translation prints “congregation” for the believers of a place (“the congregation and the apostles and the elders” at 15:4), and printed “assembly” for the same Greek word where it meant the civil assembly of the city (19:32, 19:39 and 19:41); the word that was the city’s civil gathering in chapter 19 is, in this verse, the name of its believers (dictionary). Six of the seven versions print “church”: KJV “the elders of the church,” ASV “the elders of the church,” Geneva “the Elders of the Church,” NIV “the elders of the church” and Douay “the ancients of the church” (which also says “ancients” for the elders) and TLB “the elders of the church at Ephesus,” and the TLB adds a place the Greek does not name here: “asking them to come down to the boat to meet him.” The seventh, NWT 1984 “the older men of the congregation,” prints “congregation” and renders the elders as “older men.” The same men are named by another word at v28, which belongs to the next note (20:28).

“You yourselves know,” and “set foot” in Asia (Acts 20:18). Hymeis epistasthe is how the speech opens, with an appeal to what the hearers saw. The same two words stand together at 10:28, where this translation prints “You yourselves understand,” and 15:7, both of them Peter’s. Epistasthe, the second person plural of epistamai, stands in five verses of the New Testament: those two, 19:25, where Demetrius says it to his workmen at Ephesus, here, and James 4:14 (not yet on these pages). Paul does not stop with one appeal: oida, “I know,” opens his word about the future at v25 below and again at v29, and ginōskete, “you know,” returns at v34, in the next note (20:28). The verb of epebēn, “I set foot,” is epibainō, which in the sense of stepping onto a place or a ship stands in five verses of Acts: here, 21:2, 21:4, 25:1 and 27:2. At 21:4, in the printed text, it is the verb of the disciples who tell Paul “through the Spirit” not to epibainein into Jerusalem; here it is what he did on his first day in Asia. Ton panta chronon, “the whole time,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. ASV “Ye yourselves know” and “set foot in Asia” keep both the pronoun and the verb, and so does the TLB “I set foot in Turkey,” which names a modern country in place of Asia; NWT 1984 “YOU well know” and “stepped into” are close to the Greek; KJV “Ye know” with “I came into Asia,” Geneva “Ye know” and NIV “You know how I lived” carry neither the emphasis of hymeis nor the stepping. For “the whole time,” NWT 1984 “the whole time” and NIV “the whole time I was with you” keep it, ASV “all the time” and Douay “for all the time” are near it, and KJV “at all seasons” and Geneva “at all seasons” say it as seasons. Asia here is the Roman province, as at 19:10, “all those living in Asia heard the word of the Lord.”

“Being a slave to the Lord” with humility, tears, trials and plots (Acts 20:19). Douleuōn tō kyriō. Douleuō stands in twenty-three verses of the New Testament and in two of Acts, here and 7:7, “the nation to which they will be slaves” (the Greek there is douleusousin). With the dative “the Lord” it is also Paul’s verb: tō kyriō douleuontes at Romans 12:11 and tō kyriō Christō douleuete at Colossians 3:24 (neither yet on these pages). The noun doulos is how he names himself at Romans 1:1, “a slave of Christ Jesus,” and Philippians 1:1, “slaves of Christ Jesus.” Luke does not say, in this verse, which Lord, and this translation keeps “Lord” in this verse and in v21. NWT 1984 “slaving for the Lord” keeps the slave; KJV “Serving the Lord,” Geneva “Serving the Lord,” Douay “Serving the Lord,” ASV “serving the Lord” and NIV “I served the Lord” soften it; TLB “I have done the Lord’s work” drops the verb. Tapeinophrosynē stands in seven verses of the New Testament and in this one alone in Acts: Ephesians 4:2, Philippians 2:3, Colossians 2:18, 2:23, 3:12 and 1 Peter 5:5 (none yet on these pages), and at Colossians 2:18 and 2:23 it stands beside worship words (thrēskeia, ethelothrēskia), a neighborhood quite unlike this one. Reference works report that it is built from “low” and “mind,” a report this page does not verify, and the shelf shows the second half: ASV “lowliness of mind,” NWT 1984 “the greatest lowliness of mind” and KJV “humility of mind” keep “mind”; Douay “humility,” NIV “great humility” and TLB “humbly” do not; Geneva “modesty” is a different word. The tears: dakryōn stands in Acts only here and at v31, where Paul says it again of his three years; elsewhere Paul writes “through many tears” at 2 Corinthians 2:4 (not yet on these pages) and remembers Timothy’s at 2 Timothy 1:4. The apparatus gives kai dakryōn to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and kai pollōn dakryōn, “and many tears,” to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “with many tears” and Geneva “with many tears” print the longer reading. Peirasmos, “trial,” is Luke’s word in “a time of testing” (Luke 8:13) and in the Lord’s “you are the ones who have remained with me in my trials” (Luke 22:28); in Acts the noun stands here alone. ASV “trials,” NWT 1984 “trials” and NIV “severe testing” print the testing sense; KJV “temptations,” Geneva “temptations” and Douay “temptations” print the other; TLB “grave danger” names neither. The epiboulai, “plots,” are in the plural only here: the noun stands in four verses of the New Testament, 9:24, 20:3, here and 23:30, and the other three are singular. ASV “the plots of the Jews,” NWT 1984 “the plots of the Jews,” NIV “the plots of my Jewish opponents” and TLB “the plots of the Jews against my life” print “plots”; Douay “the conspiracies of the Jews,” KJV “the lying in wait of the Jews” and Geneva “the layings await of the Jews” do not. Luke’s own account of Ephesus tells of some “hardening themselves and disobeying” (19:9) and of the Jews pushing Alexander to the front (19:33), and does not use the word “plot”; the plot that Luke does tell in this chapter is the one at v3, in Greece.

“Shrank back,” “profitable,” “publicly and from house to house” (Acts 20:20). Ouden hypestilamēn tōn symphorontōn tou mē anangeilai hymin kai didaxai hymas dēmosia kai kat’ oikous. Hypostellō stands in four verses of the New Testament: this one, v27 below, and Galatians 2:12 and Hebrews 10:38 (the last two not yet on these pages). At Galatians 2:12 Cephas hypestellen and “separated himself” from the table of the nations; at Hebrews 10:38 it is “if he shrinks back” (ean hypostelētai); in this chapter it is Paul’s word for what he did not do, to draw back and keep something from the hearers. The verb is the frame of the first half of the speech: ouden hypestilamēn … tou mē anangeilai here, ou gar hypestilamēn tou mē anangeilai at v27, with the same three words after the verb. The shelf puts the verb in different English at the two places: ASV “I shrank not” (v20 and v27) is the one closest to the Greek, and the TLB “I never shrank” and “I didn’t shrink” keep it; NWT 1984 “did not hold back” and “have not held back” put it as holding back; KJV “kept back nothing” at v20 and “not shunned” at v27 use two different verbs; Geneva “kept back nothing” at v20 and “kept nothing back” at v27; Douay “kept back nothing” at v20 and “not spared” at v27; NIV “not hesitated” in both. Symphero stands in this verse alone in Acts: the “profitable” things are ta symphoronta, a participle used as a noun. “Publicly” is dēmosia, in four verses of Acts: 5:18 (“public custody”), 16:37 (“They beat us publicly”), 18:28 (Apollos refuting the Jews) and here; in the first two it is what was done to the apostles and to Paul and Silas, and in the last two it is what Apollos and Paul did. “From house to house” is kat’ oikous, the plural of kat’ oikon at 2:46 (breaking bread) and 5:42 (teaching and bringing the good news); 8:3 has kata tous oikous, with the article, of Saul “going into house after house.” What the Greek says Paul did “publicly” and “from house to house” is two verbs, anangeilai, “announce,” and didaxai, “teach.” KJV “taught you publickly, and from house to house,” ASV “teaching you publicly, and from house to house,” Douay “taught you publicly, and from house to house,” NIV “taught you publicly and from house to house” and NWT 1984 “teaching YOU publicly and from house to house” keep it; Geneva “openly and throughout every house” and TLB “either publicly or in your homes” do not.

“Testifying solemnly” repentance and faith (Acts 20:21). Diamartyromai stands in nine verses of Acts when the spellings diamart- and diemart- are both searched: 2:40 (printed “bore witness”), 8:25, 10:42, 18:5 (“solemnly testifying”), three times in this passage, diamartyromenos here, diamartyretai at v23 and diamartyrasthai at v24, and at 23:11 and 28:23. Paul is the subject at vv21 and 24, the Holy Spirit at v23, and at v24 the thing testified to is the gospel; at v26 the verb is the simple martyromai, “I testify.” The shelf spreads the one verb across several English words: KJV “Testifying,” ASV “testifying” and Douay “Testifying” print a plain “testifying”; Geneva “Witnessing,” NWT 1984 “thoroughly bore witness” and NIV “I have declared” take other words. The content is tēn eis theon metanoian kai pistin eis ton kyrion hēmōn Iēsoun, with one article before both nouns, so that repentance toward God and faith toward the Lord are named as a pair; metanoia and pistis have their entries. “Both Jews and Greeks” is Ioudaiois te kai Hellēsin, the words of 19:10, in Asia, and 19:17, in Ephesus. The apparatus has three places in this verse. It gives eis theon to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and eis ton theon, with the article, to Robinson–Pierpont; it gives pistin to those three and tēn pistin, with a second article, to Robinson–Pierpont; and at the end it gives Iēsoun, “Jesus,” to Westcott–Hort, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and Iēsoun Christon, “Jesus Christ,” to Tregelles alone. Of the four editions the footnote lists, then, only Tregelles prints “Jesus Christ.” KJV “our Lord Jesus Christ,” ASV “our Lord Jesus Christ,” Geneva “our Lord Jesus Christ,” Douay “our Lord Jesus Christ” and TLB “our Lord Jesus Christ” print the longer wording, which among those four editions is Tregelles’s; NWT 1984 “our Lord Jesus” and NIV “our Lord Jesus” print the short one, as this translation does. The Greek has eis both times; KJV “repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord,” ASV “repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord” and Geneva “repentance toward God, and faith toward our Lord” keep “toward” twice, while NWT 1984 “repentance toward God and faith in our Lord” changes it to “in” the second time, as this translation does. Douay “penance towards God” prints the word that the dictionary entry traces to the Vulgate’s rendering of metanoia.

“Bound in the spirit” (Acts 20:22). Kai nyn idou dedemenos egō tō pneumati poreuomai eis Ierousalēm. Kai nyn idou, “And now, look —,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: Paul’s sentence on Elymas, “And now, look — the hand of Jehovah is upon you” (13:11), and the two hinges of this speech, here and at v25. The word is the one this translation prints “look —” at 13:25, “But look — one is coming after me.” The participle dedemenos, “having been bound,” is a perfect passive, and the dative tō pneumati is the question. It can be “by the spirit,” “in the spirit” or “with respect to the spirit,” and the Greek has the article but not hagion, “holy.” Two readings are open, and this page does not choose between them. On the first, the spirit is Paul’s own, an inner compulsion, as at 19:21, where this translation prints “resolved in the spirit” and the note there does not decide whose spirit it is. On the second, it is the Holy Spirit, who is named in the next verse as to pneuma to hagion, with both articles, and who is the speaker of the “except that” (plēn hoti) clause. The shelf divides the same way and not by the Greek: KJV “I go bound in the spirit,” ASV “I go bound in the spirit,” Douay “being bound in the spirit” and NWT 1984 “bound in the spirit” leave it open, Geneva “bound in the Spirit” capitalizes it, and NIV “compelled by the Spirit” and TLB “drawn there irresistibly by the Holy Spirit” choose the Spirit of God and also turn “bound” into compulsion. NWT 1984 prints “holy spirit” in lower case at v23, a doctrinal rendering that treats the phrase as God’s active force, and neither the article nor the lack of one at v22 decides that. The apparatus has two small points here. It gives dedemenos egō to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the order egō dedemenos to Robinson–Pierpont, and it gives moi, “to me,” in “the things that will meet me” to Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont and the stressed form emoi to Westcott–Hort; the meaning is the same. The verb of those things is synantaō, “to meet,” in the future participle synantēsonta, which stands in this verse alone. The verb stands in six verses of the New Testament: Luke 9:37 (a crowd meets him), 22:10 (“a man will meet you”), Acts 10:25 (Cornelius meeting Peter) and this verse, and Hebrews 7:1 and 7:10 (not yet on these pages); in the other five it is a person or a crowd that meets, and only here are “things” the ones that meet. The shelf uses four different verbs for it, and “meet” is not one of them: KJV “shall befall me there,” ASV “shall befall me there” and Douay “shall befall me there” say “befall”; NWT 1984 “will happen to me” and NIV “will happen to me there” say “happen”; Geneva “shall come unto me there” says “come”; TLB “what awaits me” says “awaits.”

“City by city”: bonds and afflictions (Acts 20:23). Plēn hoti to pneuma to hagion kata polin diamartyretai moi legon hoti desma kai thlipseis me menousin. Kata polin stands in two verses of Acts in this form, here and 15:21, where Moses has “in every city” those who proclaim him; 15:36 has kata pasan polin, “in every city.” The shelf mostly says “every city”: KJV “in every city,” ASV “in every city,” Geneva “in every city,” Douay “in every city” and NIV “in every city”; NWT 1984 “from city to city” and TLB “in city after city” are nearer the distributive sense of kata that this translation prints as “city by city.” The apparatus gives diamartyretai moi to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and omits moi, “to me,” in Robinson–Pierpont; KJV “witnesseth in every city” and Geneva “witnesseth in every city” have no “to me,” and ASV “testifieth unto me,” Douay “witnesseth to me,” NWT 1984 “bears witness to me” and NIV “warns me” have it. It gives kai thlipseis me to the three older editions and me kai thlipseis to Robinson–Pierpont, the same words in another order. The Douay ends “wait for me at Jerusalem,” words for which the footnote to this verse records no variant. The two nouns: desma, “bonds,” in this form stands in two verses of Acts, here and 16:26, where “everyone’s bonds were loosed” in the Philippian jail (16:23–26); and thlipsis stands in five verses of Acts, 7:10, 7:11, 11:19, 14:22 and this one. At 14:22 the disciples at Lystra, Iconium and Antioch are told “through many tribulations we must enter into the kingdom of God,” and the word is printed “tribulations” there and “afflictions” here. At 21:11 the prophet Agabus binds his own feet and hands with Paul’s belt and says, “Thus says the Holy Spirit,” that the Jews in Jerusalem will bind the owner of the belt and hand him over to the nations; at 21:13 Paul answers that he is ready “not only to be bound” (dethēnai) there, and at 21:33 the commander orders him “to be bound” with two chains. KJV “bonds and afflictions” and ASV “bonds and afflictions” and Geneva “bonds and afflictions” keep both words; NWT 1984 “bonds and tribulations” changes the second; NIV “prison and hardships” and TLB “jail and suffering” change both, and name a place in place of bonds.

“I hold my life of no account”: the thickest apparatus in the chapter (Acts 20:24). All’ oudenos logou poioumai tēn psychēn timian emautō hōs teleiōsai ton dromon mou kai tēn diakonian hēn elabon para tou kyriou Iēsou. The verse has five footnotes, and they show what each edition prints. At logou, “of account,” the genitive is Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28; Robinson–Pierpont has logon, the accusative. After poioumai Robinson–Pierpont adds oude echō, “nor do I hold.” After psychēn it adds mou, “my.” It gives teleiōsai, the infinitive “to finish,” to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and teleiōsō, a first person singular (a future, “I will finish,” or a subjunctive; the spelling is the same), to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles. After dromon mou, “my course,” Robinson–Pierpont adds meta charas, “with joy.” The printed text of this translation follows Nestle–Aland 28 at every one of the five, and that does not add “with joy.” On the shelf, the KJV has “But none of these things move me, neither count I my life dear unto myself, so that I might finish my course with joy”: it breaks the sentence into two clauses joined by “neither,” which is the shape of the Robinson–Pierpont reading with oude echō, and it ends “with joy,” which only Robinson–Pierpont has of the four. Geneva “neither is my life dear unto myself, so that I may fulfill my course with joy” has “with joy” too and also breaks the sentence in two. ASV “I hold not my life of any account as dear unto myself, so that I may accomplish my course” and NWT 1984 “I do not make my soul of any account as dear to me, if only I may finish my course” keep the one clause and no joy; NWT 1984 “my soul” also keeps “soul” for psychē, where this translation prints “life.” Douay “I fear none of these things, neither do I count my life more precious than myself” has its own shape and “the ministry of the word,” for which the footnotes record no variant; NIV “I consider my life worth nothing to me” and TLB “life is worth nothing” say it as worth.

“Course,” “ministry” and “the gospel of the grace of God” (Acts 20:24). Dromos, “course,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: John’s “fulfilling his course” (13:25), this verse, and 2 Timothy 4:7, ton dromon teteleka, “I have finished the course” (not yet on these pages). John “was fulfilling” it, and Paul here hopes “to finish” it. KJV “finish my course,” NWT 1984 “finish my course,” ASV “accomplish my course,” Geneva “fulfill my course” and Douay “consummate my course” keep “course,” and NIV “finish the race” says “race.” Diakonia, “ministry,” stands in eight verses of Acts, 1:17, 1:25, 6:1, 6:4, 11:29, 12:25, here and 21:19; this translation prints “ministry” at 1:17 and 1:25, where it is Judas’s share, “service” at 6:1, 6:4, 11:29 and 12:25, and “ministry” here, where the one who gave it is named: para tou kyriou Iēsou, “from the Lord Jesus.” KJV “the ministry,” ASV “the ministry” and NWT 1984 “the ministry” keep it, Geneva “the ministration” puts it a little differently, NIV “the task” and TLB “the work assigned me” say “task” and “work.” The object of the verb is to euangelion tēs charitos tou theou. The noun euangelion stands in two verses of Acts, 15:7 and here, though the verb “to bring good news” is common (5:42). The genitive phrase tēs charitos tou theou, “of the grace of God,” stands in four verses of the New Testament, here and Ephesians 3:2, Ephesians 3:7 and Hebrews 12:15 (the last three not yet on these pages), and in Acts here alone; among Luke’s other phrases are the dative tē chariti tou theou (14:26, “commended to the grace of God”) and “the word of his grace” at 14:3 and again at v32 (the next note, 20:28). The apparatus for this verse has nothing at these words. KJV “the gospel of the grace of God,” ASV “the gospel of the grace of God,” Douay “the gospel of the grace of God” and Geneva “the Gospel of the grace of God” are word for word; NWT 1984 “the good news of the undeserved kindness of God,” NIV “good news of God’s grace” and TLB “the Good News about God’s mighty kindness and love” change “gospel,” and NWT 1984 “undeserved kindness” and TLB “mighty kindness and love” change charis as well.

“You will see my face no more” (Acts 20:25). Kai nyn idou egō oida hoti ouketi opsesthe to prosōpon mou hymeis pantes en hois diēlthon kēryssōn tēn basileian. The sentence is a claim about what Paul knows, and this note reports only what the words say. Ouketi opsesthe to prosōpon mou stands in this verse alone in the New Testament; v38 gives it back as ouketi mellousin to prosōpon autou theōrein, “they were no longer going to see his face,” with another verb for seeing (20:38). The kingdom is what he went through them “proclaiming”: in Acts the kingdom is the object of kēryssō here and at 28:31, and at Ephesus Luke has told it as “reasoning and persuading about the kingdom of God” (19:8). The apparatus gives tēn basileian to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and tēn basileian tou theou, “the kingdom of God,” to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “preaching the kingdom of God,” Geneva “preaching the kingdom of God” and Douay “preaching the kingdom of God” print the longer reading, and ASV “preaching the kingdom,” NWT 1984 “preaching the kingdom” and NIV “preaching the kingdom” the shorter. On the “face,” ASV “shall see my face no more,” KJV “shall see my face no more,” Douay “shall see my face no more” and NWT 1984 “will see my face no more” keep it; NIV “will ever see me again” and TLB “will ever see me again” drop it. Later letters name the same places, and this page sets them down without reading one into the other. 1 Timothy 1:3 has Paul say, “stay on at Ephesus” (1 Timothy 1:3); 2 Timothy 1:18 speaks of “how much he served at Ephesus” (2 Timothy 1:18); and 2 Timothy 4:12 and 4:20 (not yet on these pages) name Ephesus as where Tychicus is sent and Miletus as where Trophimus is left ill, the two men that v4 of this chapter calls Asians. “Clean from the blood of all” (Acts 20:26). Dioti martyromai hymin en tē sēmeron hēmera hoti katharos eimi apo tou haimatos pantōn. Katharos stands in two verses of Acts, here and 18:6, where Paul in Corinth shakes out his garments and says “Your blood be on your own heads. I am clean” (katharos egō); the note there sets the Hebrew idiom behind it, and this verse is the second time he says it. The apparatus gives katharos eimi to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and katharos egō to Robinson–Pierpont, which are the very two words of 18:6. It gives dioti to Westcott–Hort, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont and dio to Tregelles; this translation prints “Therefore.” The setting is the one of the watchman in Ezekiel: the Hebrew damo miyadkha avaqqesh, “his blood I will require from your hand,” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in Ezekiel, 3:18, 3:20 and 33:8 (none yet on these pages), each said to the watchman who does not warn. The counterpart for the watchman who does warn is nafshekha hitztzalta, “you have delivered your soul,” which also stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, Ezekiel 3:19, 3:21 and 33:9 (not yet on these pages). The Greek of v26 has no quotation and no name, and Luke does not say that Paul has the watchman in mind; the Hebrew is put beside the verse because it is the place where “blood” and “warn” stand together. Set beside it, Pilate’s “I am innocent of this blood” (Matthew 27:24) is athōos eimi apo tou haimatos, with the same preposition and another adjective; athōos stands in two verses of the New Testament, Matthew 27:4 and 27:24. Martyromai, “I testify,” is the simple verb after the three diamartyr- forms of vv21–24. On the shelf, KJV “I take you to record this day,” Geneva “I take you to record this day” and Douay “I take you to witness this day” and NWT 1984 “I call YOU to witness this very day” make it a calling to witness, while ASV “I testify unto you this day,” NIV “I declare to you today” and TLB “Let me say plainly” do not. For “clean,” NWT 1984 “clean from the blood of all men” has the Greek word; KJV “pure from the blood of all men,” ASV “pure from the blood of all men” and Geneva “pure from the blood of all men” say “pure,” Douay “clear from the blood of all men” says “clear,” NIV “innocent of the blood of any of you” says “innocent” and narrows “all” to the hearers, and TLB “no man’s blood can be laid at my door” turns it into an idiom. The Greek has pantōn, “of all,” with no noun; the “men” that five of these seven print is an addition.

“The whole counsel of God” (Acts 20:27). Ou gar hypestilamēn tou mē anangeilai pasan tēn boulēn tou theou hymin. Boulē stands in seven verses of Acts: 2:23, 4:28, 5:38, 13:36, here, and 27:12 and 27:42. In four of them it is God’s: the “determined plan” of 2:23, “your counsel” in the prayer of 4:28, David serving “the counsel of God” in 13:36, and this verse; at 5:38 Gamaliel says it of “this plan or this work” and leaves open whether it is from people or from God; and at 27:12 and 27:42 it is a decision made by most of a ship’s company and by soldiers. This translation prints “counsel” at 4:28, 13:36 and here, and “plan” at 2:23 and 5:38. The apparatus has only the order here: pasan tēn boulēn tou theou hymin in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and hymin pasan tēn boulēn tou theou in Robinson–Pierpont. ASV “the whole counsel of God” keeps the Greek “whole”; KJV “all the counsel of God,” Geneva “all the counsel of God,” Douay “all the counsel of God” and NWT 1984 “all the counsel of God” say “all”; NIV “the whole will of God” prints “will,” a word the Greek of this verse does not have, and TLB “all God’s message” prints “message,” which it does not either. The verse repeats the verb and the clause of v20, and what Paul says he did not hold back is “announcing” (anangeilai) all of it; the speech turns at v28, where the hearers are told what to do with it (20:28).

Verses 28–32 · προσέχετε ἑαυτοῖς … ἐπισκόπους, ποιμαίνειν τὴν ἐκκλησίαν τοῦ θεοῦ … περιεποιήσατο διὰ τοῦ αἵματος τοῦ ἰδίου … λύκοι βαρεῖς … διεστραμμένα … γρηγορεῖτε … τριετίαν … παρατίθεμαι … τῷ δυναμένῳ οἰκοδομῆσαι prosechete heautois … episkopous, poimainein tēn ekklēsian tou theou … periepoiēsato dia tou haimatos tou idiou … lykoi bareis … diestrammena … grēgoreite … trietian … paratithemai … tō dynamenō oikodomēsai — Acts 20:28 overseers, elders and shepherds: “the blood of his own,” “congregation of God” or “of the Lord,” the wolves, and who is “able to build up”

Overseers, elders and shepherds: one group under three names (Acts 20:28). At v17 Luke has Paul send to Ephesus and summon tous presbyterous tēs ekklēsias, “the elders of the congregation”; here, eleven verses on, he tells the same men that the Holy Spirit placed them episkopous, “as overseers,” and that their work is poimainein, “to shepherd.” The Greek shows no change of audience between the two verses: three words, presbyteros (dictionary), episkopos (dictionary) and the verb of the shepherd, are used of one body of men. The same pairing stands in Titus: Titus is to appoint presbyterous in every town (Titus 1:5), and two verses on the sentence turns to ton episkopon, “the overseer” (Titus 1:7); and Peter’s Presbyterous are told poimanate to en hymin poimnion tou theou, episkopountes, “shepherd the flock of God among you, overseeing” (1 Peter 5:1–2, not yet on these pages). The noun episkopos stands in five verses of the New Testament: here, Philippians 1:1 (“with overseers and deacons”), Titus 1:7, 1 Timothy 3:2 (not yet on these pages) and 1 Peter 2:25 (not yet on these pages), where the word follows poimena, “shepherd,” in the phrase ton poimena kai episkopon tōn psychōn hymōn, “the shepherd and overseer of your souls.” (A compound, allotriepiskopos, stands at 1 Peter 4:15, and is a different word.) What the shelf prints is split: ASV “hath made you bishops” and Douay “hath placed you bishops” print “bishops”; KJV “hath made you overseers,” Geneva “hath made you Overseers,” NIV “has made you overseers,” NWT 1984 “appointed” YOU “overseers” and TLB “as overseers” print “overseers.” Both are renderings of the one Greek word. This page reports what the Greek shows in these passages, that one group carries all three names, and does not decide how church offices developed afterwards, or when one man came to be called by one of these names and another by another.

“Take heed to yourselves” and “all the flock” (Acts 20:28). Prosechete heautois is an imperative with a reflexive: the overseers are told to watch themselves before the flock. The same two words together stand in four verses of the New Testament: Jesus to the disciples about the leaven of the Pharisees, Prosechete heautois in Luke 12:1; the same words in Luke 17:3 before the rule for a brother who sins; Gamaliel’s “take heed to yourselves” about the men in the Council, 5:35; and here. (Luke 21:34 has the verb and the reflexive with de between them, so a search for the two words side by side does not return it.) KJV “Take heed therefore unto yourselves” and Geneva “Take heed therefore unto yourselves” print “therefore,” which is the oun that the apparatus gives to Robinson–Pierpont alone; ASV “Take heed unto yourselves” and Douay “Take heed to yourselves” do not. NIV “Keep watch over yourselves” and NWT 1984 “Pay attention to yourselves” choose other verbs, and TLB “beware” says something nearer to a warning. Poimnion, “flock,” is the little word Jesus used for his disciples, to mikron poimnion, “the little flock,” in Luke 12:32; in the New Testament the noun poimnion stands in five verses, Luke 12:32, here and v29 and 1 Peter 5:2 and 5:3 (not yet on these pages), while John 10, the chapter of the shepherd (poimēn) and the sheep (probata), does not use this noun (dictionary). Where the English shelf prints the work of the overseers it is split a third way: KJV “to feed the church of God,” ASV “to feed the church of the Lord” and Geneva “to feed the Church of God” print “feed”; Douay “to rule the church of God” prints “rule”; NIV “Be shepherds of the church of God” and NWT 1984 “to shepherd the congregation of God” print the verb itself, which is poimainein.

“The congregation of God” or “of the Lord” (Acts 20:28). Tēn ekklēsian tou theou. This is where the editions differ. The apparatus gives theou, “of God,” to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28, kyriou, “of the Lord,” to Tregelles, and kyriou kai theou, “of the Lord and God,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The shelf follows two of the three: KJV “church of God,” Geneva “Church of God,” Douay “church of God,” NIV “church of God” and NWT 1984 “congregation of God” print “of God”; ASV “church of the Lord” prints the reading Tregelles has; and TLB “his church” names neither. This translation prints “God,” the reading of two of the editions, and does not claim that the other readings are errors or that a reader must follow it: which word stands here changes what the sentence says of the one who “acquired” the congregation, and the page leaves that to the pedigrees above. Ekklēsia is “congregation” throughout (dictionary); the same body Paul summoned in v17 is the one he calls tou theou here.

“Acquired,” and “through the blood of his own” (Acts 20:28). Periepoiēsato is a middle verb, “acquired for himself.” Its family stands in eight verses of the New Testament: the verb here, peripoiēsasthai in Luke 17:33, where the thing sought is tēn psychēn autou, “his soul” or “his life”; peripoiountai in 1 Timothy 3:13 (not yet on these pages), where those who serve well basmon heautois kalon peripoiountai, “acquire for themselves a good standing”; and the noun peripoiēsis at Ephesians 1:14. It also stands in four other verses: 1 Thessalonians 5:9, 2 Thessalonians 2:14, Hebrews 10:39 and 1 Peter 2:9 (these four not yet on these pages). The verb says the congregation became his possession; the “through the blood” says by what means; neither says to whom a price was paid. KJV “purchased,” ASV “purchased,” Douay “purchased,” NWT 1984 “purchased” and TLB “purchased” add a buying that the verb does not itself state, and NIV “bought” does the same. The end of the verse is dia tou haimatos tou idiou, literally “through the blood of the own.” Here the Greek can be read two ways. The form tou idiou is the same in the masculine and the neuter genitive singular: if it agrees with haimatos, “blood,” the phrase is “his own blood”; if it stands alone as a masculine, it is “the blood of his own [one].” Robinson–Pierpont prints idiou haimatos, the order that can only mean “his own blood”; the other three editions print haimatos tou idiou. The shelf: KJV “his own blood,” ASV “his own blood,” Douay “his own blood,” NIV “his own blood,” Geneva “that his own blood” and TLB “his blood” read it as a possession of the blood; NWT 1984 “the blood of his own [Son]” supplies a noun, and the square bracket marks that the word “Son” is not in the Greek. (NWT 2013 prints “his own Son” without the bracket.) Elsewhere the Greek writes the possessive with its noun: tou idiou huiou, “his own Son,” in Romans 8:32, and tou idiou haimatos, “his own blood,” at Hebrews 9:12 and 13:12 (not yet on these pages); whether Luke here left a noun to be understood or put an adjective after its noun is what the reading above turns on, and the page does not choose. It is reported here, and not checked on this page, that the verse was used in later discussions of how to speak of God and of Christ; the words as printed in the shipped editions are all the page can show.

“After my departure” and the “grievous wolves” (Acts 20:29). Aphixis, “departure,” stands in this verse only: a search of the whole New Testament for aphixis returns one verse, and so the page can say that it is a word Luke uses once and cannot say more about it. KJV “my departing,” ASV “my departing,” Geneva “my departing” and Douay “my departure” keep a noun; NIV “after I leave,” TLB “after I leave you” and NWT 1984 “going away” use a verb or a verbal noun. Lykoi, “wolves,” are the threat to sheep in Matthew 7:15 (lykoi harpages, with the same verb prosechete as in v28 above), in Matthew 10:16 and Luke 10:3 (the sent ones as sheep or lambs “in the midst of wolves”) and in John 10:12, where the one who is not a shepherd sees the wolf coming and leaves the sheep; lykos stands in five verses with a wolf, these four and here. Bareis, the adjective “heavy” in the plural, stands with lykoi only here; elsewhere it is said of the “weightier” things of the Law (baryteros, Matthew 23:23) and of Paul’s letters, bareiai, “weighty,” said by his critics (2 Corinthians 10:10, not yet on these pages). KJV “grievous wolves,” ASV “grievous wolves” and Geneva “grievous wolves” keep “heavy” in the sense of hard to bear; Douay “ravening wolves” uses the word with which English renders harpages in the verse from Matthew; NIV “savage wolves,” TLB “vicious wolves” and NWT 1984 “oppressive wolves” each choose a different meaning for the weight. Mē pheidomenoi, “not sparing,” uses the verb of Romans 8:32, where God “did not spare his own Son,” and of 2 Corinthians 1:23 and 1 Corinthians 7:28 (not yet on these pages), where Paul “spares” the Corinthians; NWT 1984 “will not treat the flock with tenderness” is the one on the shelf that does not use “spare.”

“From among yourselves men will arise” (Acts 20:30). Ex hymōn autōn anastēsontai andres: the verb is the one Gamaliel uses for Theudas, anestē Theudas, “Theudas rose up” (5:36), and for Judas the Galilean, anestē Ioudas (5:37); of Judas Luke adds apestēse laon opisō autou, “drew away people after him,” and here the men apospan tous mathētas opisō heautōn, “to draw the disciples away after themselves.” The word for “after” is the same; the verb is a different one, and the page makes no claim beyond that the two scenes use the same shape of sentence. Diestrammena, “twisted things,” is the plural of the participle that describes the “unbelieving and twisted generation” in Matthew 17:17 and Luke 9:41 (the participle diestrammenē stands in four verses, these two, here and Philippians 2:15, “twisted generation”); its verb is diastrephō, which is what Paul tells Elymas he does to “the straight ways of Jehovah” (13:10), and what Elymas was trying to do to the proconsul at 13:8. KJV “perverse things,” ASV “perverse things,” Geneva “perverse things” and Douay “perverse things” keep a plain plural; NWT 1984 “twisted things” is the literal; NIV “distort the truth” and TLB “distort the truth” supply a noun, “the truth,” that the Greek does not have at this point. Apospan, “to draw away,” is the verb of a sword “drawn” in Matthew 26:51 (apespasen) and of Jesus being “withdrawn” from the disciples “about a stone’s throw” in Luke 22:41; the apparatus gives the reflexive heautōn, “themselves,” to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles and the simple autōn, “them,” to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. This translation prints “themselves.” NWT 1984 “after themselves” has the reflexive; KJV “after them,” ASV “after them,” Geneva “after them,” Douay “after them” and NIV “after them” print the plain pronoun. The same verb stands once more in Acts, as apospasthentas, “having been parted from,” at 21:1, where the travelers leave the elders.

“Stay awake,” “three years” and “admonishing” (Acts 20:31). Grēgoreite is the imperative of a verb whose root stands in twenty-three verses of the New Testament (grēgor-): literally staying awake, as in Gethsemane (Mark 14:34, 14:38; Matthew 26:40–41); watchfulness for the master who will come at an hour no one knows (Mark 13:35 and 13:37); and in the letters the same call put to a congregation, in Revelation 3:2, with 1 Corinthians 16:13, Colossians 4:2, 1 Thessalonians 5:6, 1 Peter 5:8 and Revelation 16:15 still to come (not yet on these pages). KJV “watch,” ASV “watch ye,” Geneva “watch” and Douay “watch” give the sense of keeping guard; NWT 1984 “keep awake” keeps the literal; NIV “be on your guard” and TLB “Watch out” use the sense of danger. Trietian, “three years,” is a single word, and a search of the whole New Testament for trietia returns this verse only. Luke’s own account of Ephesus gives “three months” in the synagogue (19:8) and then “two years” in the school of Tyrannus (19:10); those two figures add to two years and three months, not three years, and Luke does not say how the figures are meant to fit, or whether the three years counts the days before and after the school. Paul in v18 says “the whole time” (ton panta chronon), and the page gives the arithmetic and does not push the round number toward either reading. Nouthetōn, “admonishing,” is the verb Paul uses of himself in 1 Corinthians 4:14, “as my beloved children,” and of what the congregations do for each other (Romans 15:14, Colossians 3:16, 1 Thessalonians 5:12 and 5:14, 2 Thessalonians 3:15, all not yet on these pages), and of his work in Colossians 1:28: nouthet- stands in eight verses, and this is the only one where it is joined to tears. ASV “admonish,” Douay “admonish” and NWT 1984 “admonishing” keep the word; KJV “warn” and Geneva “warn” and NIV “warning” narrow it to the sense of danger; TLB “constant watchcare” turns the verb into something Paul has over them.

“I entrust you to God and to the word of his grace” (Acts 20:32). Paratithemai is the word of the Lord’s last cry, Patēr, eis cheiras sou paratithemai to pneuma mou, “Father, into your hands I entrust my spirit” (Luke 23:46), and of the elders appointed on the first journey, whom Paul and Barnabas “entrusted to the Lord” (parethento autous tō kyriō, 14:23; its note); at the end of his life it is said of Timothy, “this charge I entrust to you” (1 Timothy 1:18; 2 Timothy 2:2, not yet on these pages, has the imperative). KJV “commend,” ASV “commend,” Geneva “commend” and Douay “commend” print “commend”; TLB “entrust” matches this translation; NIV “commit” and NWT 1984 “commit” print “commit.” Tō logō tēs charitos autou, “the word of his grace,” is the phrase of 14:3, where it is the Lord who is “bearing witness to the word of his grace”; the whole phrase stands in these two verses and nowhere else in the New Testament (tō logō tēs charitos). The apparatus decides whom autou, “his,” belongs to: Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont print tō theō, “to God,” and Westcott–Hort tō kyriō, “to the Lord,” so the word of “his” grace is God’s in the first reading and the Lord’s in the second. Robinson–Pierpont also has adelphoi, “brothers,” after hymas, which is what KJV “now, brethren” and Geneva “now brethren” print. NWT 1984 “undeserved kindness” is its constant rendering of charis (dictionary).

“The one able to build up” and “the inheritance among all those who have been sanctified” (Acts 20:32). Tō dynamenō, “the one able,” is a dative masculine singular, the same case and gender as tō theō (or tō kyriō), “to God,” and as tō logō, “to the word”; so the Greek can be read as God (or the Lord) being able to build up and to give, or as the word of his grace being able to. The shelf divides on it: Douay “who is able to build up” has the person; KJV “which is able to build you up,” ASV “which is able to build you up,” NIV “which can build you up” and TLB “words that are able to build” have the word; and NWT 1984 “which [word] can build” supplies the word in square brackets, the mark that “word” is not in the Greek at that place. (Geneva prints “which is able to build further,” which is the sense of epoikodomēsai, “build upon,” the reading the apparatus gives to Robinson–Pierpont; the three other editions have oikodomēsai.) The phrase tō de dynamenō, with de, opens the sentences of Jude 24, “to him who is able,” of the doxology at the end of Romans, 16:25 in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 (14:24 in Robinson–Pierpont), and of Ephesians 3:20 (Romans 16 and Ephesians 3 not yet on these pages), all of God; here there is no de, and the page does not say that the same reading follows. Oikodomēsai, “build up,” is the verb of the “being built up” of the congregation in 9:31 and of the “rebuilding” of David’s fallen tent at 15:16, where the compound is anoikodomēsō. Tēn klēronomian, “the inheritance,” sits beside 7:5, where God gave Abraham klēronomian en autē, “an inheritance in it,” and “not even a foot’s length”; the apparatus gives tēn to three editions and hymin, “to you,” to Robinson–Pierpont, so that “give you an inheritance” of KJV “give you an inheritance” has the second and ASV “give you the inheritance” the first with “you” added. Tois hēgiasmenois pasin, “all those who have been sanctified,” is a perfect participle of hagiazō (dictionary; the adjective hagios, “holy,” is its root): the state of having been set apart that has already taken place. The same words, with a different noun, klēron en tois hēgiasmenois, “a lot among those who have been sanctified,” stand at 26:18, and in the opening of 1 Corinthians (1 Corinthians 1:2) the congregation of God is addressed as hēgiasmenois. ASV “among all them that are sanctified,” NWT 1984 “among all the sanctified ones” and TLB “set apart for himself” give the three meanings: the plain participle, the “ones” of persons, and the sense of belonging to the one who sets apart.

Verses 33–38 · Ἀργυρίου ἢ χρυσίου ἢ ἱματισμοῦ οὐδενὸς ἐπεθύμησα … αἱ χεῖρες αὗται … Μακάριόν ἐστιν μᾶλλον διδόναι ἢ λαμβάνειν … θεὶς τὰ γόνατα … ἱκανὸς κλαυθμός … ἐπιπεσόντες ἐπὶ τὸν τράχηλον … προέπεμπον Argyriou ē chrysiou ē himatismou oudenos epethymēsa … hai cheires autai … Makarion estin mallon didonai ē lambanein … theis ta gonata … hikanos klauthmos … epipesontes epi ton trachēlon … proepempon — “It is more blessed to give than to receive”: whose words are they, and why do Paul’s hands, his knees and the elders’ tears end the speech at Miletus?

“Silver or gold or clothing of no one” (Acts 20:33). Argyriou ē chrysiou ē himatismou oudenos epethymēsa puts the three objects first, each in the genitive, then oudenos, “of no one,” and the verb last; the English versions turn the order around. KJV “I have coveted no man’s silver, or gold, or apparel,” Geneva “I have coveted no man’s silver, nor gold, nor apparel” and NWT 1984 “I have coveted no man’s silver or gold” put “have coveted” where the Greek has the simple past epethymēsa; ASV “I coveted no man’s silver, or gold, or apparel” keeps the simple past. NIV “not coveted anyone’s silver or gold or clothing” and Douay “I have not coveted any man's silver, gold, or apparel” keep the verb, while TLB “never been hungry for money or fine clothing” turns the verb into a hunger, the pair “silver or gold” into “money,” and adds “fine.” NWT 2013, a different witness from the 1984 text quoted here, prints “desired” for the same verb. Epithymeō stands in Acts at this verse alone. The commandment against coveting in the Hebrew is built on chamad, and lo tachmod, “you shall not covet,” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Exodus 20:13, which English Bibles number 20:17, Deuteronomy 5:17 and Deuteronomy 7:25. The third has the very objects of this verse: “You shall not covet the silver and the gold” on the carved images of Canaan’s gods, in this translation’s English. Paul writes the commandment in Greek as Ouk epithymēseis, “You shall not covet,” at Romans 7:7 and at Romans 13:9 (not yet on these pages). Luke does not tie v33 to the commandment, and this page does not. Peter’s sentence at 3:6, Argyrion kai chrysion ouch hyparchei moi, “Silver and gold I do not have,” has the same two metals, with “and” where Paul has “or,” and says what the speaker lacks where Paul says what he did not want. Himatismos, “clothing,” is a noun of fine or conspicuous dress where Luke elsewhere uses it: “splendid clothing” in the palaces at Luke 7:25, and the dazzling white of Jesus’s clothing at Luke 9:29; John uses it of the clothing for which the soldiers cast lots (John 19:24). The noun stands in five verses of the New Testament, these three, here, and again at 1 Timothy 2:9 (not yet on these pages).

“Whose ox have I taken?” A leader’s farewell (Acts 20:33–34). In Samuel’s farewell to Israel the Hebrew reads et-shor mi lakachti vachamor mi lakachti, “whose ox have I taken, and whose donkey have I taken?” (1 Samuel 12:3, not yet on these pages). Samuel goes on to ask whom he has defrauded and whom he has oppressed, and asks the people to witness against him, and the people answer at 12:4 that he has taken nothing from any man’s hand. The comparison is of content and not of words: Samuel names his own record to the people and asks them to testify to it, and Paul names his and then says autoi ginōskete, “you yourselves know,” a call to witnesses that his speech has already made at v18 (the note beginning at v17). Luke records no answer from the elders before v36. Moses’s last address to Israel begins with his age and his coming death, “I am a hundred and twenty years old today” (Deuteronomy 31:2), and Paul has already said at v25 that his hearers will see his face no more. This page sets Samuel’s and Moses’s speeches beside Paul’s only as farewells and notes the pattern of a leader’s parting words; it does not claim that Luke modeled Paul’s speech on either.

“These hands” (Acts 20:34). Autoi ginōskete hoti tais chreiais mou kai tois ousin met’ emou hypēretēsan hai cheires autai. The verb comes before its subject, and the subject closes the sentence with its demonstrative: hai cheires autai, “the hands, these.” The phrase stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. The “these” is the Greek’s own demonstrative; that Paul held up his hands is the usual inference, and Luke writes no gesture. The Greek puts the beneficiaries first, in the dative, tais chreiais mou kai tois ousin met’ emou, “to my needs and to those with me,” and Douay “such things as were needful for me and them that are with me, these hands have furnished” keeps that order. ASV “these hands ministered unto my necessities,” KJV “these hands have ministered unto my necessities” and Geneva “these hands have ministered unto my necessities” keep the verb of service; NWT 1984 “needs of me and of those with me” keeps both groups; NIV “supplied my own needs” adds “own” and calls the others “my companions”; TLB “worked to pay my own way” turns the service into paying his way. Hypēretēō, “to serve,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: David “having served in his own generation the counsel of God” at 13:36, here, and again at 24:23, where the governor orders that none of Paul’s own people be hindered from serving him. Chreia, “need,” stands in Acts at five verses: 2:45 and 4:35, where need sets the share given out from the common fund, 6:3, “this need,” here, and again at 28:10. The need here is met from Paul’s own hands and not from a fund. Luke has already shown the hands at work: Aquila and Priscilla were skēnopoioi, “tentmakers,” and Paul “worked” with them (18:3); at Miletus the trade is not named, only the hands. In his letters Paul says it in his own words: “with our own hands” at 1 Corinthians 4:12 (not yet on these pages); “night and day working, so as not to burden any of you” at 1 Thessalonians 2:9 (not yet on these pages) and at 2 Thessalonians 3:8–9 (not yet on these pages); and the refusal of a right he had, at 1 Corinthians 9:12–18 (not yet on these pages). The companions who accompanied him are named at 20:4.

“I showed you … toiling like this … to help the weak” (Acts 20:35). Panta hypedeixa hymin hoti houtōs kopiōntas dei antilambanesthai tōn asthenountōn. Hypedeixa, “I showed,” is hypodeiknymi, which stands in six verses of the New Testament: Matthew 3:7 and Luke 3:7 (“Who warned you to flee”), Luke 6:47 and 12:5 (“I will show you”), Acts 9:16 and this verse. Two of the six carry dei, “it is necessary”: the Lord’s word of Saul at 9:16, “I will show him how much he must suffer,” and here. ASV “I gave you an example” and TLB “a constant example” put an “example” where the Greek says “showed”; KJV “I have shewed you all things,” NWT 1984 “I have exhibited to YOU in all things” and NIV “I showed you” keep the verb. Kopiaō, “to toil,” stands in 21 verses of the New Testament and in Acts only here: Simon’s night of fishing at Luke 5:5, the lilies that “do not toil” at 12:27, Paul’s own “we toil, working with our own hands” at 1 Corinthians 4:12 (not yet on these pages), and the one who is to “toil, working with his own hands” at Ephesians 4:28 (not yet on these pages), so as to have something to share with whoever has need, chreia again. The Greek writes no “you” or “we” as subject of the infinitives, only the accusative plural participle kopiōntas: ASV “so laboring ye ought” and NWT 1984 “by thus laboring YOU must” read it as “you,” NIV “by this kind of hard work we must” reads it as “we,” with Paul inside it, and this page does not choose. Antilambanesthai is the middle of antilambanō, “to take hold of, take someone’s part”; the simple verb stands in three verses: God “has taken hold of Israel his servant” at Luke 1:54, here, and again at 1 Timothy 6:2 (not yet on these pages). Asthenountōn is the participle of astheneō, and in Acts the family of this word is used of the sick: the sick carried into the streets at 5:15, a woman who “fell sick” at 9:37, and the sick to whom cloths were carried at 19:12, where the form is the very participle of this verse. Whether Paul means the sick, the poor or the weak in a wider sense the Greek does not say, and this page does not decide. Six of the seven shelf versions say “weak”: ASV “help the weak,” NIV “help the weak,” KJV “support the weak,” Geneva “support the weak,” Douay “support the weak” and NWT 1984 “assist those who are weak”; TLB “helping the poor” names a category the Greek does not.

“It is more blessed to give than to receive” (Acts 20:35). Makarion estin mallon didonai ē lambanein. This is a saying of the Lord Jesus that none of the four Gospels gives in these words. The Greek words mallon didonai stand in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and so do the words didonai ē lambanein. The infinitive lambanein does not stand in Matthew, Mark or Luke at all, and in John it stands twice, at 3:27 and 7:39, in neither as a pair with giving. The infinitive didonai stands in Matthew once (7:11) and in Luke three times (11:13, 12:42 and 23:2), and none of the four sets it against receiving; at Matthew 7:11 and Luke 11:13 it is the Father who gives good things, a different saying. In the New Testament this saying stands only here, in Luke’s report of Paul’s speech. Later Christian writings are reported to have similar sayings; this page has not checked them. ASV, KJV, NIV and TLB print “It is more blessed to give than to receive”; Douay “It is a more blessed thing to give, rather than to receive” keeps both “more” and “rather”; Geneva “It is a blessed thing to give, rather than to receive” has “rather” and no “more” for mallon; and NWT 1984 “more happiness in giving than there is in receiving” makes the adjective a noun and the two infinitives gerunds. Makarion is the neuter of makarios, the adjective of the Beatitudes, and the choice between “blessed” and “happy” is a lexical one, not a doctrinal one: the note at Matthew 5:3 gives the reasons this translation prints “Happy” there, and NWT 1984 “happiness” here follows the same reading of the word. Six of the seven shelf versions use “blessed,” and this translation prints “blessed” here, as they do.

“To remember the words of the Lord Jesus” (Acts 20:35). Mnēmoneuein te tōn logōn tou kyriou Iēsou hoti autos eipen: “and to remember the words of the Lord Jesus, that he himself said.” Autos, “he himself,” is emphatic. Mnēmoneuō is the verb of the charge at 20:31, mnēmoneuontes hoti, “remembering that for three years…,” where what is to be remembered is Paul’s own night-and-day warning; at v35 it is the Lord’s. The phrase tōn logōn tou kyriou, “the words of the Lord,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament; 1 Timothy 6:3 has “healthy words, those of our Lord Jesus Christ” in another order (not yet on these pages). Two other places have someone remember the Lord’s word: “I remembered the word of the Lord, how he used to say” at Acts 11:16, where the saying recalled is the one Acts gives at 1:5, and Peter’s remembering “the word of the Lord” at Luke 22:61. In his own letters Paul says what he received and handed on, “I received from the Lord what I also handed over to you,” before the words over the bread and the cup at 1 Corinthians 11:23–25 (not yet on these pages), and writes that “the Lord ordered those who announce the gospel to live from the gospel” at 1 Corinthians 9:14 (not yet on these pages), in the chapter where he explains the right he did not use. Whether Paul’s words at v35 rest on a tradition passed on by word of mouth, on a written source, or on something else, Luke does not say, and this page does not decide.

Kneeling to pray (Acts 20:36). Kai tauta eipōn theis ta gonata autou syn pasin autois prosēuxato has the idiom theis ta gonata, “putting down the knees,” which this translation prints “kneeling down” at Luke 22:41, Acts 7:60 and 9:40. In Luke–Acts the phrase ta gonata stands in five verses, these three, this one and 21:5, always with a form of tithēmi; Mark 15:19 has it too, of the soldiers’ mock homage. Only here does Luke add autou, “his,” to the knees. The verb that follows is not always prayer: Jesus prosēucheto, “was praying,” at Luke 22:41, Stephen ekraxen, “cried out,” at Acts 7:60, and Peter and Paul prosēuxato, “prayed,” at 9:40 and here; at 21:5 a participle of the same verb follows. The words syn pasin autois, “with them all,” stand between the kneeling and the praying and can be read with either: NWT 1984 “he kneeled down with all of them and prayed” and NIV “knelt down with all of them and prayed” put them with the kneeling, and KJV “he kneeled down, and prayed with them all,” Geneva “he kneeled down, and prayed with them all” and Douay “kneeling down, he prayed with them all” put them with the praying; this translation’s “kneeling down, he prayed with them all” takes the second. TLB “he knelt and prayed with them” has no “all.”

Weeping, the neck and the kisses (Acts 20:37). Hikanos de klauthmos egeneto pantōn, kai epipesontes epi ton trachēlon tou Paulou katephiloun auton. Klauthmos, “weeping,” stands in nine verses of the New Testament. In one of them Rachel weeps for her children (Matthew 2:18); in seven it is joined to the gnashing of teeth, brygmos, in six verses of Matthew (8:12, 13:42, 13:50, 22:13, 24:51, 25:30) and at Luke 13:28; the ninth is this one, where it is joined to neither. The adjective hikanos, “considerable,” comes back from v8, the “many” lamps, and v11, the “considerable time” of Paul’s talk (the note beginning at v7). Paul had said in this speech that he served the Lord “with all humility and tears” (20:19) and warned each one “with tears” (20:31); now it is the elders’ weeping. The apparatus gives klauthmos egeneto to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and egeneto klauthmos to Robinson–Pierpont, a change of word order alone. ASV “they all wept sore” and KJV “they all wept sore” turn the noun into a verb, as do Geneva “they wept all abundantly” and TLB “they wept aloud”; Douay “much weeping among them all” and NWT 1984 “quite a bit of weeping broke out” keep the noun. The gesture, epipesontes epi ton trachēlon, “falling on the neck,” has an exact match in the Gospel of Luke: epepesen epi ton trachēlon autou kai katephilēsen auton, “fell on his neck and kissed him,” of the father who runs to the returning son (Luke 15:20), with the same verb, epipiptō, the same phrase and the same compound verb of kissing. Of the seven verses of the New Testament with trachēlos, “neck,” these two are the only ones in which someone falls on it. This translation prints “threw his arms around his neck” and “kissed him tenderly” at Luke 15:20 and “falling on Paul’s neck” and “kept kissing” here. In the Hebrew Bible vayipol al tsavvar-, “fell on his neck,” stands in three verses, all in Genesis, and each of the three also has weeping: Esau and Jacob (33:4, with a kiss as well), Joseph and Benjamin (45:14) and Joseph and Jacob (46:29). Those are meetings and Acts 20 is a parting, and the comparison is of the gesture; this page does not claim that Luke has the Genesis verses in mind. Katephiloun is an imperfect, “kept kissing”; the compound kataphileō stands in six verses of the New Testament: Judas’s kiss at Matthew 26:49 and Mark 14:45, the woman at Jesus’s feet at Luke 7:38 and 45, the father at Luke 15:20, and this verse. This translation prints “kissed him warmly” at Matthew 26:49 and Mark 14:45 and “kissing my feet warmly” at Luke 7:45; here it carries the imperfect, “kept kissing,” and not the prefix. NWT 1984 “tenderly kissed” carries the prefix; NIV “embraced him and kissed him” drops the neck; TLB “embraced him in farewell” drops the kiss and adds “in farewell”; KJV “fell on Paul’s neck, and kissed him” and ASV “fell on Paul’s neck and kissed him” keep the neck and the kiss.

“They were about to see his face no more” (Acts 20:38). Odynōmenoi malista epi tō logō hō eirēkei hoti ouketi mellousin to prosōpon autou theōrein. Proepempon de auton eis to ploion. Odynaomai stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Luke–Acts: Mary and Joseph searching for the boy, “in distress” in this translation (Luke 2:48), the rich man in the flame, “in agony” at 16:24, Abraham’s “you are in agony” at 16:25, and this verse. The word they grieve over is logos in the singular: ASV “sorrowing most of all for the word which he had spoken” and Douay “grieved most of all for the word which he had said” keep the singular, as does NWT 1984 “pained at the word he had spoken”; KJV “for the words which he spake” and Geneva “for the words which he spake” make it plural, and NIV “his statement” and TLB “because he said” recast it. The word is Paul’s own, at v25: ouketi opsesthe to prosōpon mou, “you will see my face no more” (the note beginning at v17, v25). Here Luke puts it as ouketi mellousin to prosōpon autou theōrein, with a different verb of seeing and mellousin, “they are about to.” In Acts ouketi and prosōpon stand together in these two verses only; the third verse of Acts with ouketi, 8:39, has no prosōpon. Luke does not say whether they saw him again, and this page does not. Proepempon, “they escorted,” is an imperfect of propempō, which stands in nine verses of the New Testament: this one, Acts 15:3, where those crossing Phoenicia and Samaria are “sent on their way by the congregation,” 2 Corinthians 1:16 and 3 John 6. The other five are Acts 21:5, Romans 15:24, 1 Corinthians 16:6 and 16:11 and Titus 3:13 (the last four not yet on these pages). Reference works are reported to say that the verb can include supplying a traveller for the next stage; this page has not checked that, and the Greek here says only that they escorted him. ASV “they brought him on his way unto the ship” and Douay “they brought him on his way to the ship” print the “on his way” of the dictionary sense; KJV “accompanied him unto the ship,” NIV “accompanied him to the ship” and TLB “accompanied him down to the ship” say “accompanied”; NWT 1984 “proceeded to conduct him to the boat” prints “boat” for to ploion, the word that stands at v13 as the ship the others went ahead to; the Greek has the article both times and does not say whether it is the same vessel.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “uproar” for thorybos at v1, with “Do not be thrown into an uproar” for its verb at v10, where Acts 19 had printed tarachos “disturbance” and sygchysis “confusion”; “encouraged” for parakaleō at vv1 and 2, with “comforted” at v12; “plot” for epiboulē at vv3 and 19, as at 9:24; “we” kept as the narrator’s own pronoun at vv5, 6 and 13; “first day of the week” for mia tōn sabbatōn at v7, as at Luke 24:1 and John 20:1; “break bread” at vv7 and 11; “reasoning” for dialegomai at v7, as at 17:2 and 18:4; “upper room” for hyperōon at v8, as at 1:13; “borne down” twice at v9, the present participle and then the aorist; “not a little” at v12, in the line of Luke’s “no small” at 12:18 and 19:23–24; “elders” for presbyteroi at v17, as at 14:23; “solemnly testifying” for diamartyromai at vv21, 23 and 24, as at 10:42 and 18:5; “look —” for idou at vv22 and 25, as at 13:11; “clean” for katharos at v26, as at 18:6; “overseers” for episkopoi at v28, with “congregation” kept for ekklēsia at vv17 and 28; “entrust” for paratithemai at v32, as at 14:23; “sanctified” at v32; “kneeling down” for theis ta gonata at v36, as at Luke 22:41 and Acts 7:60 and 9:40; and “blessed” for makarios at v35, as at Revelation 1:3, where Matthew 5 has “Happy.”
