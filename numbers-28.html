🕰
Jehovah gives Moses the fixed offering calendar for the tabernacle: the twice-daily tamid lamb, doubled on the sabbath, tripled at the new moon with its own goat 'to Jehovah', and the corporate week-long offerings for Passover/Unleavened Bread and the Feast of Weeks — the Passover day itself named but given no offering of its own.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, and legislative rather than narrative — still on the plains of Moab, continuous with the census and Joshua's commissioning just given. Hebrew and English versification agree: 31 verses on both sides. ⭐ Five Masoretic paragraph breaks, one after each offering-list: the daily (v8), the sabbath (v10), the new moon (v15), Unleavened Bread (v25), and the chapter's own close (v31).

Numbers 28:1-8 — ‘My food, my bread’: the daily offering, and a drink no priest may swallow

⭐ ‘My offering, my food’ (v2) states the sacrifice as something God eats — qorbani lachmi, ‘my offering, my bread’ — and the whole shelf keeps the possessive literal: KJV and Douay both ‘my offering, and my bread’, Geneva the same, ASV ‘My oblation, my food’, NWT 1984 and its 2013 revision both ‘my offering, my bread’, TLB ‘my food’, and both Reina-Valeras «mi ofrenda, mi pan». ⚠ Only NIV lets the doubled noun go, compressing it into one tidy category, ‘my food offerings’ — no bread, no first-person possessive left on the noun itself; the NVI keeps its own half, «es mi comida».

The idiom is not explained away here, but Psalm 50 does it elsewhere (not yet on these pages): ‘Do I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?’ (50:13) — the flattest denial of literal divine hunger in the Hebrew Bible, aimed at exactly this vocabulary. Numbers 28 legislates the meal; the psalm tells Israel not to mistake the menu for an appetite.

⭐⭐ And this time, unlike almost everywhere else this book repeats an earlier law, the numbers agree exactly. Exodus 29:38-42 (already on these pages) instituted this identical offering at Sinai — two lambs, one in the morning and one between the two evenings, the same tenth of an ephah of flour, the same quarter-hin of oil, the same quarter-hin of wine — and thirty-eight years and a whole wilderness generation later, not one figure has moved. V6 says as much itself: this is the offering ‘instituted at Mount Sinai’, still running.

⭐⚠ And v7 names that drink offering not wine but shekhar — strong drink, the very word Leviticus 10:9 (already on these pages) forbids Aaron and his sons ever to drink before they enter the tent of meeting, on pain of death, spoken to Aaron directly in the same chapter his two eldest sons had just died in it. Every other libation named later in this chapter is wine (v14); only the daily offering's is shekhar. Here, decades later, is that word's one other appearance in the Torah's ritual law: not swallowed, but poured out, daily, inside the very holy place the earlier ban exists to protect.

Numbers 28:9-10 — Beside, not instead: the chapter's whole grammar in one preposition

Every occasion from here to the end of the chapter is stacked ON the daily offering, never substituted for it. V10 doubles the sabbath's own lambs ‘besides [al] the continual burnt-offering’, and the same cumulative sense recurs four more times before the chapter ends (vv15, 23, 24, 31), carried by two different prepositions — al, ‘upon’, and milevad, ‘apart from’ — that both mean the same thing here: in addition to, never instead of.

⚠ The sabbath's own addition is exactly proportional: the same two male lambs a year old that make up the whole daily offering, doubled for one day only — no new offering type, just twice the existing one. ⭐ And this ordinary doubling, not any argument from silence, is what stands behind Jesus’s own citation at Matthew 12:5 (already on these pages): ‘the priests in the temple profane the sabbath, and are guiltless’. The sabbath law was never absolute even inside the Torah that gives it — this very doubling of priestly labor, commanded here, is the Law's own built-in exception to itself.

Numbers 28:11-15 — A goat ‘to Jehovah’, and the sages' answer for why

The monthly offering triples the sabbath's already-doubled rate — two young bulls, one ram, seven lambs — and adds, uniquely in this chapter, a male goat ‘for a sin-offering to Jehovah’ (v15). ⚠ Checked against this chapter's other two sin-offering goats — v22's, for the first day of Unleavened Bread, and v30's, for the Feast of Weeks — neither carries that phrase; both simply atone ‘for you’. Only the new moon's goat is aimed, in the text's own words, at Jehovah's own account rather than Israel's.

⭐⭐ The rabbis read exactly this oddity and supplied an answer. Tractate Shevuot (9a) has Reish Lakish name the reason the phrase is unique here: sa’ir zeh yehe kappara al she-mi’atti et ha-yareach — ‘this goat shall be an atonement for the fact that I diminished the moon’. The fuller story is told at Chullin (60b): Genesis 1:16 (already on these pages) calls the sun and moon ‘the two great lights’ and then, in the same verse, makes one of them lesser — and the sages fill the gap with the moon's own complaint that ‘two kings cannot share one crown’, God's command that it shrink itself, and, when no other compensation satisfies it, God's own admission: bring an atonement, for I am the one who made it small. This translation reports the reading as what it is — a rabbinic tradition, centuries later, answering the phrase's own genuine oddity — not as Numbers 28's own stated intent.

⚠ And the disagreement runs further than one tradition's explanation. Ezekiel's own new-moon law (46:6, not yet on these pages) prescribes a different animal count entirely — one bull, six lambs, one ram, no goat named at that verse — a real divergence the sages knew and worked at directly: Shabbat (13b) credits Hananiah ben Hezekiah with burning through three hundred jars of midnight oil to reconcile Ezekiel against the Torah, and says the book would have been withdrawn from the canon without him. This translation states the disagreement and leaves it stated.

Numbers 28:16-25 — The Passover with no offering of its own

V16 names ‘the Passover of Jehovah’ and gives it no sacrifice at all — every animal in this section belongs to the seven days that follow it, not to the day itself. ⚠ That is not an oversight. Exodus 12 (already on these pages) already legislated the paschal lamb as a household rite, killed and eaten at home by families — as this chapter finds it, a meal eaten at each family's own door has nothing on it for the tabernacle's own public list to add. ⚠ That is where THIS chapter leaves it, not where the story ends: Deuteronomy 16:5–7 (now on these pages) later forbids sacrificing the Passover ‘within any of thy gates’ at all and requires it at the one place Jehovah chooses — a genuine centralizing shift the Torah itself makes, not one this translation is reading in from outside. Numbers 28 is a snapshot of the practice as this book knows it, not the whole history of it.

Numbers 9 (already on these pages) makes the identical point from the opposite side: the whole reason a SECOND Passover exists a month later is for the man too unclean or too far from camp to keep the first one at home — which only makes sense if Passover was never a public sacrifice this chapter's own calendar could simply reschedule.

The seven-day offering (vv19-24) repeats the new moon's own headcount exactly — two young bulls, one ram, seven lambs, one goat — but drops the phrase ‘to Jehovah’ from the goat: v22 reads only ‘to make atonement for you’, matching v30's goat at the Feast of Weeks. The new moon's own address to God, noted above, is not repeated here.

Numbers 28:26-31 — The one festival this chapter never dates

Every other occasion in this chapter has a fixed place on the calendar — the fourteenth day (v16), the fifteenth (v17), the new moon itself (v11), the sabbath. ⚠ The Feast of Weeks alone is named by relation, not date: ‘the day of the firstfruits… in your feast of weeks’ (v26) — and neither this verse nor the whole shelf checked here supplies a number; NIV, KJV, Douay, Geneva and ASV all describe it the same relational way, and both RV60 and NVI do the same in Spanish. Leviticus 23 (already on these pages) is where the actual mechanism lives — count fifty days from the wave-sheaf — a count that chapter's own note already ties to the Greek word for ‘fiftieth’ and to the crowd Acts 2 (already on these pages) reports gathered in Jerusalem for exactly this festival's own pilgrimage requirement.

⚠ And the offering itself genuinely disagrees with Leviticus 23's own list for the identical day. This chapter's vv27-30: two young bulls, one ram, seven lambs, one goat. Leviticus 23:18-19 (already on these pages): one young bull, TWO rams, seven lambs, plus a separate goat AND two more lambs offered as a peace-offering — a different headcount for the same festival, in the same Torah. This translation states both counts and does not harmonize them.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English agree on 31 verses. ⚠ Three renderings are kept deliberately literal against a smoother option. ‘My food, my bread’ at v2, against NIV's single compressed ‘food offerings’. ‘Strong drink’ (shekhar) at v7, kept distinct from the ‘wine’ (yayin) of v14, rather than flattened to one word for both, so the echo with Leviticus 10:9's prohibition stays visible. ‘The beginnings of your months’ at v11, keeping the literal ‘heads’ (rashei) that gives rosh chodesh its own name, rather than jumping straight to ‘new moons’. ⭐ Five Masoretic paragraph breaks, and each falls exactly where one offering-list ends and the next begins — after v8 (the daily offering), v10 (the sabbath), v15 (the new moon), v25 (Unleavened Bread) and v31 (the close of the chapter).

Library. Two new dictionary entries in both languages: tamid, the continual offering this whole chapter's grammar is built to protect; and chodesh, the new moon that gives the Hebrew month its name. No new encyclopedia entries — this chapter names no new person or place.

Echoes paid — five. Exodus 29:38-42's daily offering is reaffirmed here, unchanged, thirty-eight years later. Leviticus 10:9's ban on shekhar before entering the tent finds its one counterpart: poured, not drunk. Genesis 1:16's lesser light is what the new moon's own goat is read, by tradition, as atoning for. Exodus 12 and Numbers 9 together explain why this chapter's Passover carries no offering of its own. And Leviticus 23 supplies the actual date this chapter withholds for the Feast of Weeks — and a genuinely different headcount for its offering. ⭐ A sixth, in the other direction: Matthew 12:5 (already on these pages) cited this chapter's own vv9-10 by number before they existed here; the citation is now a live link both ways.

Patterns worth carrying forward. First: this chapter's whole grammar is additive, never substitutive — five paragraph breaks, and every occasion after the first is layered ON the daily offering, never replacing it. Second: a genuine asymmetry inside one chapter (only the new moon's goat is ‘to Jehovah’) is worth checking BEFORE reaching for outside tradition — the rabbis noticed something real, not invented. Third, a method note: this repeated-law corpus agrees with itself far more often than it disagrees (Exodus 29's tamid, unchanged), which makes the disagreements (Leviticus 23's headcount, Ezekiel's new-moon law) worth stating plainly rather than smoothing over — agreement is the norm here, and that is exactly what makes a real discrepancy worth a reader's attention.

Echoes planted. Numbers 29 (now on these pages), which continues this same calendar with Trumpets, the Day of Atonement, and Tabernacles; Ezekiel 46 (not yet), whose new-moon and sabbath offerings genuinely disagree with this chapter's own; Deuteronomy 16:5–7 (now on these pages), which later forbids the household Passover this chapter takes for granted and centralizes it at the one place Jehovah chooses; and Psalm 50 (not yet), which answers this chapter's ‘my food’ with a flat denial that God eats at all.
