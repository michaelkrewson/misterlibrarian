🕰
A poor widow's two small coins at the temple treasury outweigh, in Jesus' own verdict, every large gift dropped in beside them. Admirers of the temple's stonework are told not one stone will be left on another, which prompts a question about timing and a sign — a question Luke's own narrator frames narrowly, about the temple alone, never about the end of the world. Wars, earthquakes and persecution are foretold, with an endurance that gains the soul; Jerusalem 'surrounded by encamped armies' and trampled by the nations until their own times are fulfilled; cosmic signs and the Son of Man coming in a single cloud; a fig tree's lesson widened to every tree; and a closing warning against hearts weighed down with dissipation, drunkenness and life's anxieties, set inside a habitual routine of teaching by day and withdrawing to the Mount of Olives by night.
c. AD 30, the same temple week as chapter 20's four hostile questions, delivered on the Mount of Olives 'opposite the temple' (compare Mark 13:3); the discourse runs in close parallel with Matthew 24-25 and Mark 13, though Luke's own version describes Jerusalem's coming fall in plain military language rather than quoting Daniel's 'abomination that desolates' as the other two do, and its own closing routine (vv37-38) is written as a repeated habit across the several days this teaching week actually spanned, not a single afternoon.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · πενιχρὰν … ἡ πτωχὴ αὕτη … τὰ δῶρα penichran … hē ptōchē hautē … ta dōra — two different Greek words for “poor” in back-to-back verses, and a Byzantine addition to the gift the widow's coins are set beside

⭐⭐ Luke reaches for two different words for “poor” within two verses of each other, and tightens rather than repeats. The narrator first calls her penichran (v2), a New Testament hapax from the word-family for a laborer's want — getting by on little, not destitute. Jesus himself, naming what she has just done, reaches instead for ptōchē (v3), the New Testament's standard word for utter destitution, the word the Beatitudes use of ‘the poor in spirit’ (Matthew 5:3, already on these pages) and Jesus uses of himself at Nazareth (Luke 4:18, already on these pages). The description of her poverty gets STARKER exactly where the sentence needs the reader to feel the weight of two lepta more, not less.

⭐⭐⭐ This is, almost verse for verse, the identical scene Mark already told (Mark 12:41–44, already on these pages, with the lepton and chēra dictionary entries already written from that telling). Mark's own version has Jesus deliberately sit down ‘opposite the treasury’ to watch the crowd give, a staging detail Luke's shorter account drops — Luke moves straight from ‘looking up’ to what he sees, with no scene-setting at all. Both accounts agree, nearly word for word in the Greek, on the sentence that matters: she put in more than all of them, because she gave out of her lack and not her abundance.

⚠ A later manuscript tradition adds a phrase to v4 that this translation's source text does not have. Where the earlier witnesses simply read ‘the gifts’ (ta dōra), the Byzantine text underlying the KJV adds ‘of God’ — ‘the offerings of God’ — a reading also followed by Douay-Rheims (‘the offerings of God’) and Geneva (‘the offerings of God’), and, in Spanish, by the RV 1909 and RV60, both «las ofrendas de Dios». ASV, NIV, NVI and the NWT all print the shorter reading this translation follows. The addition changes nothing about what is being described — the temple treasury's gifts were, on any reading, gifts to God — but it is a real and checkable difference in what the earliest manuscripts actually say.

Verses 5–11 · τί τὸ σημεῖον … ὁ καιρὸς ἤγγικεν … ἀκαταστασίας ti to sēmeion … ho kairos ēngiken … akatastasias — a question Luke's own disciples never ask, an extra line Luke alone gives the false messiahs, and a plainer word than “rumors of wars”

⭐⭐⭐ Luke's version of the disciples' question never asks about Jesus' own presence or the end of the age at all — only about the temple. Matthew's parallel has the disciples ask about three things at once: when the temple will fall, the sign of Jesus' own parousia (see the dictionary entry, already written from that verse), and the synteleia tou aiōnos, the completion of the age (Matthew 24:3, already on these pages) — and the Greek there shares a single article across the last two, which may mean Matthew's disciples are asking about one combined event. Luke's own question stays narrower: ‘when will THESE THINGS be, and what will be the sign’ — these things meaning the temple's fall foretold in the verse just before, nothing more. Whatever this Gospel's own discourse goes on to describe, it is never framed by its own narrator as an answer to a question about the world's end.

⚠ Luke's false messiahs add a second claim the other two Synoptics never give them. Mark's parallel impostors say only ‘I am he’, Matthew's ‘I am the Christ’ (Mark 13:6; Matthew 24:5, both already on these pages); Luke's own version has them add a second slogan — ‘the time has drawn near’ (ho kairos ēngiken) — a claim about timing that belongs, elsewhere in the Gospels, to Jesus' own preaching (compare Mark 1:15, already on these pages, where Jesus himself opens his ministry with the identical verb of the kingdom, after John's own arrest). The impostors are not just claiming an identity; in Luke's telling, they are claiming a schedule.

⚠ “Wars and upheavals” is a plainer, less specific pairing than the other two Gospels use. Mark and Matthew both write of ‘wars and RUMORS of wars’ (polemous kai akoas polemōn, Mark 13:7; Matthew 24:6, both already on these pages) — hearsay about distant fighting. Luke's hearers likewise only HEAR of the wars, but Luke drops the ‘rumors’ and pairs the wars with akatastasias, civil disorders and instabilities generally — a broader category than fighting reported from far off.

Shelf comparison, v11: this translation's “dreadful sights,” for the New Testament hapax phobēthra, matches ASV's ‘terrors’ and KJV's ‘fearful sights’ in substance; NWT reads ‘fearful sights,’ its Spanish twin TNM 1987 «escenas espantosas»; NIV and NVI fold the phrase directly into the surrounding list rather than naming it as its own item.

Verses 12–19 · οὐ μὴ ἀπόληται … κτήσασθε ou mē apolētai … ktēsasthe — a promise that not a hair will perish, spoken two verses after a promise that some of them will be put to death, and an old dispute over mood that the KJV's own archaic grammar already answers one way

⚠ Verse 18's promise and verse 16's warning sit two verses apart and say opposite things about the same body, and this translation does not smooth the tension away. ‘They will put some of you to death’ (v16) is a plain, physical statement; ‘not a hair of your head will perish’ (v18) cannot mean bodily safety without contradicting the sentence just before it. The most consistent reading takes v18 the way v19 reads it — a promise about what ultimately survives (the psychē, the self or life, gained through endurance), not a promise that the body will go unharmed. Mark's own version of this same persecution section has no equivalent to v18 at all, and closes instead on ‘the one who endures to the end will be saved’ (Mark 13:13, already on these pages) — the same reassurance, without the hair image's own apparent tension to resolve.

⭐⭐⭐ The verb this translation reads as a command — ‘gain your souls’ — is disputed between the editions, but not the way it first looks: the modern critical text and the Byzantine majority text actually AGREE against an older generation of criticism. The apparatus splits between ktēsasthe, an AORIST IMPERATIVE (‘gain!’, an active task laid on the hearer) — read by both this chapter's own critical text and the Byzantine tradition — and ktēsesthe, a FUTURE INDICATIVE (‘you will gain,’ a plain prediction), read only by the older Westcott-Hort and Tregelles editions. This translation follows the imperative its own source text prints, and so, on this one word, does the text underlying the KJV and the Geneva Bible — both of which already print the verse in imperative shape four centuries ago, ‘possess ye your souls’ and ‘possess your souls,’ with no ‘shall’ or ‘will’ anywhere in the English. ASV — translated from the newer Westcott-Hort text this same generation of criticism produced — reads the future instead, ‘ye shall win your souls,’ and Douay-Rheims likewise, ‘you shall possess your souls.’ NWT (‘YOU will acquire YOUR souls’), translated from the Westcott-Hort text, reads the future it found there, and NIV (‘you will win life’) a future as well. So the shelf is not united against an imperative reading, as it first appears — it is split exactly along the same manuscript line the Greek itself is split on, four hundred years apart.

Verses 20–24 · στρατοπέδων … ἐκδικήσεως … καιροὶ ἐθνῶν stratopedōn … ekdikēseōs … kairoi ethnōn — plain soldiers where Mark and Matthew both reach for Daniel, and a phrase whose later theological weight has run far past what the verse itself specifies

⭐⭐⭐ Where Mark and Matthew both quote Daniel's own coined phrase, Luke describes the same coming catastrophe in language any resident of a besieged Roman-era city would recognize on sight, without borrowing Daniel's phrase. Mark's and Matthew's versions of this same warning both reach for ‘the abomination that desolates, standing where it ought not’ (Mark 13:14; Matthew 24:15, both already on these pages, their notes tracing the phrase to Daniel 9, 11 and 12). Luke's own image, by contrast, is the plainest possible military language — see the stratopedon dictionary entry: Jerusalem ‘surrounded by encamped armies.’ No cultic desecration, no sacred-space violation, no scripture quoted — just soldiers, camped, around the walls (two verses later Luke calls these days the fulfilment of ‘all that is written’, v22, but names no text). Whether this reflects one evangelist writing after the actual Roman siege of Jerusalem in AD 70 and describing what by then had already happened in plain terms, or simply a difference in how three writers chose to report one saying, the library does not decide; but the difference in vocabulary between the three accounts is real and checkable, not a matter of emphasis.

⚠ “Days of vengeance” is a phrase this translation's own dictionary has already flagged apart from private revenge. See the ekdikēsis dictionary entry: the word names justice carried out, a wrong set right, not spite. Neither Mark nor Matthew's own version of this discourse uses this word at all; it is Luke's own addition to the scene, framing the coming catastrophe in the vocabulary of overdue justice rather than mere disaster.

⚠ “The times of the nations” (v24) names a limit and a duration — a beginning and an end — and specifies neither. The verse says plainly that Jerusalem's trampling by the nations will continue only ‘until the times of the nations are fulfilled,’ which means the sentence is not open-ended even though it leaves the terminus unnamed. Later interpretive traditions have built entire chronological schemes on this half-verse; the text itself commits to no calendar, only to an end that is coming. TLB's paraphrase — ‘until the period of Gentile triumph ends in God's good time’ — makes explicit what the plainer shelf versions (KJV, ASV, NWT) leave as bare description; this translation follows the plainer versions and reports the paraphrase as commentary, not as a fourth reading of the Greek.

Verses 25–28 · ἐν νεφέλῃ en nephelē — a cloud kept singular where Mark writes it plural, in the one Gospel whose own sequel needs it singular again

⭐⭐⭐ Luke's own wording of the Son of Man's coming keeps “a cloud” singular, where Mark's parallel sentence makes it plural. Mark writes ‘coming en nephelais’, IN CLOUDS — plural — for this same moment (Mark 13:26, already on these pages); Matthew's own version goes further still, ‘upon the clouds of heaven’ (Matthew 24:30, already on these pages). Luke alone writes en nephelē, a single cloud. The same author, writing the same detail again in his own sequel, keeps it singular a second time: the cloud that ‘took him up from their eyes’ at the ascension is one cloud, not clouds (Acts 1:9, already on these pages) — a small, checkable echo across Luke's own two-volume work that neither of the other Synoptics can produce, because neither of their own Gospels has a sequel to echo it in.

Redemption, drawing near (v28), reuses the same noun this project's own dictionary has already glossed as a slave's or a captive's ransomed freedom — see the apolytrōsis entry, first written from 1 Corinthians 1:30. The command that follows it, ‘stand up straight and lift up your heads,’ is the posture of a released prisoner, not a cowering one; the discourse's darkest images (vv25–26: the sea roaring, the nations in anguish, the powers of the heavens shaken) are answered, in the very next sentence, with an instruction to look UP rather than to brace.

Verses 29–33 · ἡ γενεὰ αὕτη hē genea hautē — the same hard sentence, all but word for word, in all three Synoptic tellings

⭐⭐ “This generation will not pass away until all these things happen” is not softened, hedged or paraphrased in any of the three Gospels that carry it. Mark's own version reads all but word for word the same (Mark 13:30, already on these pages); Matthew's does too, differing only in its small connecting words (Matthew 24:34, already on these pages, whose own note there sets out the four standard readings of ‘this generation’ and what each one costs). This translation adds nothing to that discussion here rather than repeat it — the reader is pointed to the fuller treatment already written where the same sentence first appears in this project's own sequence.

⚠ The fig tree image (v29) is generalized here to ‘the fig tree, AND ALL THE TREES’ — a small addition neither Mark nor Matthew makes (compare Mark 13:28; Matthew 24:32, both already on these pages, both naming only the fig tree). Luke's own readership, drawn more broadly across the Greek-speaking world than a Judean audience for whom the fig tree's seasonal budding was a locally obvious sign, gets the lesson widened to any tree at all.

Verses 34–38 · κραιπάλῃ … ἐπεισελεύσεται kraipalē … epeiseleusetai — a hangover named nowhere else in the New Testament, a trap-verb this translation follows against the majority text, and a closing routine repeated, in Greek, as an ongoing habit rather than a single day

⭐⭐⭐ “Dissipation” translates a genuine New Testament hapax, and it is paired with the ordinary word for drunkenness rather than standing in for it. See the kraipalē dictionary entry: it names the SICK AFTEREFFECT of heavy drinking, not the drinking itself, which the very same clause already names separately (methē). Only Luke's version of this warning names this specific bodily condition; Mark's and Matthew's own closing exhortations to watchfulness carry no vocabulary of drink at all (Mark 13:33–37; Matthew 24:42–44, both already on these pages) — Matthew comes nearest only in the parable that follows, where the wicked servant eats and drinks with drunkards (24:49).

⚠ The verb describing how the day arrives is itself disputed, and the rarer reading has the trap's mechanism built into the word itself. This translation's own source text reads epeiseleusetai, a true New Testament hapax on this reading — a rare double compound meaning to come IN so as to close ON something. A more widely copied later tradition simplifies this to the ordinary epeleusetai, ‘will come upon’ — the same event, described with an everyday verb rather than one built to sound like a trap springing shut.

The chapter's own closing routine (vv37–38) is written in Greek's imperfect tense throughout — not a single day's schedule but a repeated HABIT across however many days this teaching actually spanned: he WAS TEACHING by day, he WOULD GO OUT by night, the people WOULD RISE EARLY to hear him. The whole discourse just given, on this reading, was not necessarily delivered in one sitting on one afternoon; it is the summary of a pattern, with this one particular exchange folded into it as an instance.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: two different Greek words for “poor” in vv2–3 (needy for penichros, a hapax; poor for the ordinary ptōchos), kept apart rather than flattened to one English word; a cloud, singular, at v27, kept apart from Mark's own plural “clouds” for the identical sentence, and echoed forward to this same author's own singular cloud at Acts 1:9; and the aorist imperative gain at v19, the same reading the text behind the KJV and Geneva Bible already prints in their own archaic imperative grammar, against ASV's and Douay-Rheims's future tense for the same clause.

Echoes paid. The scribes who ‘devour widows’ houses’ at the close of Luke 20 (20:47, already on these pages) are answered, two verses into this chapter, by one poor widow giving everything she has at the very same treasury — what the scribal class takes and what she gives, placed back to back exactly as Luke 20's own closing note promised. And the temple, praised by admiring onlookers for its stonework at the opening of this chapter, is told in the same breath that it will not have one stone left on another — the destruction Luke 20's own closing note had already flagged as coming.

Echoes planted. The singular cloud of v27 is promised its payoff already, in this same author's own sequel: the identical single cloud that will receive Jesus ‘out of their sight’ at the ascension (Acts 1:9, already on these pages) — a small detail this translation now has both ends of. And the plotting this whole discourse has interrupted resumes without a word of transition: the very next chapter opens with the chief priests and scribes still looking for a way to kill him, ‘for they feared the people’ (22:1–2, already on these pages) — the same calculation already named twice in Luke 20 (the leaders' fear of the crowd's own reaction, 20:6; the identical phrase ‘they feared the people’, 20:19, both already on these pages), now a third time, unresolved.

Next in sequence: Luke 22 — the plot against Jesus finds its instrument in one of the Twelve, Satan entering Judas by name; a final Passover meal, and a supper the Christian world has never stopped repeating; a betrayal sealed with a kiss, a servant's ear cut off and healed, and Peter's own three-fold denial, foretold and then fulfilled before the rooster crows.
