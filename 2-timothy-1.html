From a second Roman imprisonment, cold and mostly abandoned, with execution in view, Paul writes what tradition holds to be his LAST letter — to Timothy, his 'beloved child': rekindle the gift, do not be ashamed, guard the deposit and hand it on. The chapter names the faith of Timothy's grandmother and mother, and one man, Onesiphorus, who was not ashamed of the chain.
Traditionally c. AD 66–67, from a second imprisonment after the events of Acts and shortly before Paul's execution under Nero (4:6, 'the time of my departure has come'). ⚠ Like its two companions it is a Pastoral, its authorship disputed — but 2 Timothy is the most personal of the three, and even scholars who reject the Pastorals as a group often allow that it preserves genuine Pauline notes. Both positions are reported; the library casts no vote.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · a last letter, and a warmer word for the same son

⚠ This is, on the traditional reading, the LAST thing Paul wrote. Later in the letter he says “I am already being poured out… the time of my departure has come” (4:6); he is in prison a second time, cold, mostly abandoned, and asking Timothy to hurry “before winter.” It reads like a man’s last will, and it is the most personal of the three Pastorals — which is why even some who doubt Paul wrote the letters think 2 Timothy preserves genuine notes from him. The authorship question (set out in the book intro and at 1 Timothy 1:1) still stands over it; the notes read the Greek as it is.

“According to the promise of LIFE.” Where 1 Timothy opened “by the command of God our Saviour,” this one opens toward life — a fitting keynote for a letter written under a death sentence. And where 1 Timothy called Timothy his “true child” (gnēsios, legitimate), here he is the “beloved child” (agapētos). The first word was about standing; this one is about affection. The three-part greeting — “grace, mercy, peace” — is again the Pastorals’ form, with mercy added.

Verses 3–5 · tears, and the faith that came down a female line

“God, whom I SERVE, as my forefathers did.” Latreuō is the word for worship-service, priestly service; and “from my forefathers” sets Paul’s faith in continuity with his Jewish ancestry, not as a break from it — the God he serves in Christ is the God of his fathers. He remembers Timothy’s tears (at their last parting, most likely), and the memory is a wound the letter keeps pressing: he wants to see him “that I may be filled with joy.”

⚠ Lois and Eunice — the one place the New Testament traces a faith through three named generations, all of them women but the last. The “unfeigned faith” dwelt first in the grandmother Lois, then the mother Eunice (Acts 16:1 tells us she was a believing Jewess married to a Greek), then Timothy. His father was a Gentile and is passed over in silence; the faith he inherited came down the maternal side of a mixed marriage, which is quietly remarkable. ⚠ KJV “thy grandmother Lois” — the Greek mammē is the affectionate word, “granny.”

Verses 6–8 · fan the coals, and the spirit God did give

“REKINDLE the gift of God.” Anazōpyrein — to fan a fire back into flame, to blow on coals going grey — and the charisma, the gift, is something Timothy already has and has let bank down. The image assumes the fire is still there; it needs air, not relighting. “Through the laying on of my hands” points back to Timothy’s commissioning (the “prophecies” of 1 Timothy 1:18).

⚠ “Not a spirit of COWARDICE, but of power and love and self-control.” One of the best-loved verses in the letters, and worth getting exact. Deilia is cowardice, timidity that shrinks back — a stronger and more shaming word than KJV’s “fear” — and it is set against three: dynamis (power), agapē (love), and sōphronismos, a hard word to render — not quite “a sound mind” (KJV) and not quite “discipline” (NIV), but the making-sensible, the self-command that keeps a level head. ⚠ The verse is not a general reassurance about anxiety; it is aimed at a young leader tempted to keep quiet under pressure, and the next verse draws the conclusion: “so do not be ashamed.”

“Do not be ASHAMED… but suffer along with me.” Shame is the nerve of this chapter — it recurs at verse 12 (Paul is not ashamed) and verse 16 (Onesiphorus was not ashamed of the chain). Synkakopathēson is a single compound: “suffer-together-with,” take your share of the hardship. Paul writes it as a man in chains asking a free man to accept the same risk.

Verses 9–12 · grace before time, the appearing, and “I know whom I have believed”

⚠ “Not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace.” For all that the Pastorals press good works, the ground of salvation here is exactly Paul’s elsewhere: not earned but given, rooted in God’s prothesis, his set purpose. And the grace was “given to us in Christ Jesus before times eternal” (pro chronōn aiōniōn) — the same striking phrase as Titus 1:2: a gift settled before time began and only now unwrapped. This is the kind of overlap that ties the three letters into one hand.

⚠ “Through the APPEARING of our Saviour.” Epiphaneia — a shining-out, the word Greek used for a god’s manifestation and the court used for a royal visitation, and the Pastorals’ chosen term for Christ’s coming (they use it of both his first coming, here, and his return). English “epiphany” is the same word. What the appearing accomplished is put in two hammer-blows: he “abolished death” (katargeō, put it out of work, cancelled it) and “brought life and immortality to light” (phōtizō) — not created them, but turned the lamp on them, so that what was always true became visible. TLB “broke the power of death”; NWT “has abolished death.”

“I know whom I have BELIEVED.” Not what but whom — the trust is in a person, not a proposition. And the confidence is a banker’s: he is sure God “is able to guard my DEPOSIT (parathēkē) until that day.” ⚠ The word is a technical one for property left with someone for safe-keeping, which the holder is bound in honour to return intact. Whose deposit, and left with whom, is deliberately open: it can be what Paul has entrusted to God (his life, his life’s work) or what God has entrusted to Paul (the gospel) — and verse 14 will turn it the other way, telling Timothy to guard his deposit. We keep the single word “deposit” so the two directions stay visible.

Verses 13–14 · the pattern to hold, and the deposit to guard

“Hold the PATTERN of sound words.” Hypotypōsis — an outline, a model sketch, the same word Paul used of himself as a “pattern” at 1 Timothy 1:16 — and “sound words” is the Pastorals’ health-metaphor again. What Timothy holds is not a rulebook but a shape, a template “that you heard from me,” to be kept “in faith and love,” not merely repeated.

“Guard the good DEPOSIT… through the Holy Spirit who dwells in us.” The parathēkē returns from verse 12, now handed on: the gospel is a trust passed from Paul to Timothy to the next keeper, and the guarding is not done by willpower but “through the Holy Spirit.” ⚠ It is one of the earliest pictures of tradition as something received and transmitted intact — the concern of a generation beginning to think about how the faith will outlast the apostles.

Verses 15–18 · deserted by a province, refreshed by one man

“All who are in ASIA turned away from me.” A bleak of real pain, and unexplained: whether they abandoned the faith or only abandoned Paul — too dangerous to be associated with a condemned prisoner — the Greek does not say, and the second is likelier. Two are named, Phygelus and Hermogenes, otherwise unknown; naming them is itself the rebuke.

⚠ Onesiphorus, who “was not ashamed of my CHAIN.” Against the desertion stands one man who, coming to Rome, “sought me out diligently and found me” — no small thing when finding a prisoner meant being seen to look. Aneps­yxen, “he refreshed me,” is the word for a cool breath of air in a stifling place. ⚠ Two things are quietly notable. Paul blesses “the household of Onesiphorus” and prays that he may “find mercy… on that day” — speaking of the man himself only in the past tense — which has led many to think Onesiphorus had died, and made verse 18 one of the very few prayers in the New Testament that may be offered for a dead man; the point is debated and the library reports it. And the double “Lord” — “may the Lord grant him to find mercy from the Lord” — the sentence reads oddly in Greek too, and some have heard in it a distinction between the Lord who grants and the Lord before whom one stands.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “beloved child” for agapētos, the warmer word where 1 Timothy had “true”; “cowardice” for deilia, harder than the KJV’s “fear,” and “self-control” for the near-untranslatable sōphronismos; “the appearing” for epiphaneia, the Pastorals’ word for the coming; “abolished death” and “brought… to light” kept as two distinct verbs; “deposit” for parathēkē, the single word kept in both directions (vv12, 14); “the pattern of sound words” for hypotypōsis and hygiainō; and “rekindle” for anazōpyreō, the fanning of coals.

Echoes paid. Grace given “before times eternal,” the exact phrase of Titus 1:2; “our Saviour,” the Pastorals’ title, as at 1 Timothy 1:1; the “sound words” of Titus 1:9 and 1 Timothy 1:10; the “pattern” Paul made of himself at 1 Timothy 1:16; the “gift… through the laying on of hands,” recalling the commissioning of 1 Timothy 1:18; God “whom I serve… unceasingly” in prayer, as at Romans 1:9; and Eunice, the believing mother of Acts 16:1.

Echoes planted. The “deposit” to be guarded and handed on, the letter’s governing image and the seed of 2:2, “what you heard from me… entrust to faithful people who will be able to teach others”; “suffer along with me,” opened here and pressed through the whole letter (2:3, “a good soldier”; 4:5, “endure suffering”); and the note of Paul’s ending, which 4:6-8 will make explicit: “I have fought the good fight… there is laid up for me the crown.”

2 Timothy is new to the library — the last of the three Pastorals, and, on the traditional reading, the last letter Paul wrote, from a second imprisonment with execution in view. Next in this book: chapter 2 — “a good soldier of Christ Jesus,” the athlete and the hardworking farmer; “if we died with him, we will also live with him”; and “the Lord knows those who are his.”
