The Spirit that hovered at creation now fills a craftsman, and the whole building project is read back in eleven verses

Bezalel is filled with ‘the spirit of God’ (v3, ruach Elohim) — the identical phrase for the identical Spirit that hovered over the waters at the very first creation (Genesis 1:2, already on these pages). The same power that shaped the world out of formlessness now indwells one man so he can shape gold, silver, bronze, stone, and wood into a dwelling for the God who made them. Bezalel is also named by full genealogy — ‘the son of Uri, the son of Hur’ (v2) — and Hur is not a stranger to these pages: he is the very man who, with Aaron, held up Moses’ failing hands at Rephidim so Israel could win the battle against Amalek (Exodus 17:12, already on these pages). The grandson of the man who once held up a leader’s arms now builds the house where that leader’s God will dwell.

⚠ ‘Wise of heart’ (v6, chakham-lev) is the exact phrase Exodus 28:3 (already on these pages) already used for the anonymous craftsmen who make Aaron’s garments — ‘all who are wise of heart, whom I have filled with a spirit of wisdom.’ That earlier promise widens here into every skilled hand building any part of the tabernacle, not garments alone. And vv7–11 are the chapter reading its own preceding five chapters back to itself in miniature: tent, ark, cover, table, lampstand, altar of incense, altar of burnt offering, laver, garments, oil, incense — every object Exodus 25–30 specified, named once more in one breath. The order is not chronological (the incense altar of Exodus 30 is named before the burnt-offering altar of Exodus 27) but spatial — innermost furniture first, working outward to the courtyard, then garments, then the two recipes.

⚠ The ‘finely worked garments’ (v10, bigdei ha-serad) are a genuinely uncertain phrase, and the shelf shows it. KJV guesses ‘the cloths of service’; ASV and ESV both read ‘the finely wrought/worked garments,’ the reading this translation follows; NIV and NASB both simplify to ‘the woven garments.’ Strong’s own note calls the root ‘perhaps plaited or braided work’ — an honest ‘perhaps’ for a word that occurs only here and twice more in Exodus 39, all describing the same unspecified vestments.

A third covenant sign, and the strangest verb in the whole chapter

The Sabbath is called ‘a sign’ (v13, ot) between God and Israel — the THIRD covenant-sign this translation has met, after the rainbow (Genesis 9:12–13) and circumcision (Genesis 17:11, both already on these pages). Each covenant gets its own visible mark: a bow in the sky for every living thing, a cut in the flesh for Abraham’s line, a kept day for the nation redeemed from Egypt. ⚠ And the penalty for violating this sign is the identical formula already fixed for violating the circumcision-sign: ‘that soul shall be cut off from among his people’ (v14) — the same clause, almost word for word, that closed Genesis 17:14. Two different signs, one shared consequence for breaking either.

Verses 15 and 17 restate the Fourth Commandment almost verbatim — ‘six days shall work be done’ and ‘in six days Jehovah made heaven and earth’ both already fixed at Exodus 20:9–10 (already on these pages). ⚠ But v17’s closing verb is new and startling: God rested ‘and was REFRESHED’ (vayinafash) — the identical rare verb already used at Exodus 23:12 (already on these pages) for the exhausted ox, donkey, slave, and foreigner who are refreshed by the very same weekly rest. The word appears nowhere else in the Torah. God is not merely described as resting on the seventh day; God is described, in the one verb this book otherwise reserves for a tired laborer or animal, as catching his breath.

A promise from seven chapters back, finally kept

‘I will give you the tablets of stone… that I have written’ — Jehovah’s own words to Moses at Exodus 24:12 (already on these pages), spoken before a single verse of tabernacle instruction had been given. Everything since — the ark, the table, the lampstand, the tent, the altar, the courtyard, the priesthood’s garments, the ordination, the incense altar, the census, the laver, the anointing oil, the incense, and now the appointment of the craftsmen who will build it all — has unfolded inside a single suspended sentence, Moses still standing on the mountain waiting for what was promised him seven chapters ago. This verse is where that sentence finally closes: he gives him the tablets.

⚠ They are written ‘with the finger of God’ — the identical idiom Pharaoh’s own magicians were forced to use of the plague of gnats they could not reproduce (Exodus 8:15, already on these pages). The same small phrase for God acting directly, without instrument or effort, first wrung as an unwilling confession out of Egypt’s own sorcerers, now names the writing of Israel’s own covenant law. And the chapter ends there, with no scribal paragraph break at all — unlike every other section of this instruction, the story does not pause to let the reader catch up. It runs straight on.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. ‘Finely worked garments’ for bigdei ha-serad (v10) — a genuinely uncertain phrase; see the note above for the full shelf split. ‘Sign’ kept for ot (vv13, 17), matching the rainbow and circumcision entries already on these pages, rather than varying the English across three covenants that share one Hebrew word. ‘Refreshed’ kept literal for vayinafash (v17) rather than folded into ‘rested’ — see the note above for why the distinction matters. The chapter’s own two paragraph breaks ({פ} at v11, {ס} at v17) mark exactly the seam between the craftsmen and the Sabbath command; v18 carries none at all, running straight into the next chapter without pause. Verse numbering follows the Hebrew exactly; Hebrew and English Exodus 31 both run 1–18.

A note on order: Exodus stands on these pages at chapters 1–36 — the whole tabernacle instruction, begun at Exodus 25, is now complete, its craftsmen named, its Sabbath commanded, and its promised tablets delivered. Exodus 32, the golden calf and Moses’ intercession, and Exodus 33, Moses’ plea to see Jehovah’s glory and his glimpse of God’s back from the cleft of the rock, and Exodus 34, the covenant remade and Moses’ own face left shining, and Exodus 35, the people’s own gifts for the tabernacle at last brought forward, and Exodus 36, the craftsmen at work and the people’s giving finally restrained, are now on these pages too. Exodus 37, the ark and the table and the lampstand actually built, remains open, along with the rest of the book.
