The book's own appendix, closing on the price list rather than the law itself: fixed valuations for a person vowed to Jehovah (a flat tariff by age and sex, with a built-in exception for the poor), for animals, houses, and fields dedicated to him, and for tithes of land and livestock. A dedicated ancestral field prices out against the jubilee cycle exactly as a sold one already did in Leviticus 25; a firstborn animal cannot also be vowed, since it already belongs to Jehovah by right. The chapter's hardest material comes late: cherem, the devoted thing that can never be sold or redeemed at any price — including, starkly, a person placed under the ban, who cannot be ransomed at all. It closes on the tithe of the herd, fixed by an actual count rather than a priest's appraisal, and then on the book's own final verse: 'these are the commandments which Jehovah commanded Moses for the children of Israel at Mount Sinai.'
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus. V34's own colophon, identical in form to Leviticus 26:46's closing formula, marks the end of the entire book — the Masoretic text itself sets this verse apart with a distinct scribal mark, not the ordinary paragraph breaks used everywhere else in Leviticus. Still inside the same Sinai window as the rest of this block (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages) — Numbers picks up one month later, at the same mountain.

Y si de cinco a veinte años, tu tasación por un varón será de veinte siclos, y por una mujer diez siclos.

9
וְאִם-בְּהֵמָה--אֲשֶׁר יַקְרִיבוּ מִמֶּנָּה קָרְבָּן, לַיהוָה: כֹּל אֲשֶׁר יִתֵּן מִמֶּנּוּ לַיהוָה, יִהְיֶה-קֹּדֶשׁ.

Y si es un animal de los que se puede ofrecer ofrenda a Jehová, todo lo que de él se dé a Jehová será santo.

No lo cambiará, ni lo sustituirá, bueno por malo o malo por bueno; y si en efecto sustituye un animal por otro, entonces tanto él como su sustituto serán santos.

Y si es cualquier animal impuro, de los que no se ofrece ofrenda a Jehová, entonces presentará el animal ante el sacerdote;
12
וְהֶעֱרִיךְ הַכֹּהֵן אֹתָהּ, בֵּין טוֹב וּבֵין רָע: כְּעֶרְכְּךָ הַכֹּהֵן, כֵּן יִהְיֶה.

Y si alguien consagra a Jehová parte del campo de su posesión, tu tasación será conforme a su siembra: un homer de semilla de cebada tasado en cincuenta siclos de plata.

28

But no devoted thing that a man devotes to Jehovah, of all that he has, whether of man or animal or of the field of his own possession, shall be sold or redeemed; every devoted thing is most holy to Jehovah.note
Pero ninguna cosa consagrada por anatema que alguien consagre a Jehová, de todo lo que tenga, sea de persona, de animal o del campo de su posesión, se venderá ni se rescatará; toda cosa consagrada por anatema es santísima a Jehová.
29

Nadie consagrado por anatema, apartado de entre los hombres para destrucción, podrá ser rescatado; morirá sin falta.
30

Y todo diezmo de la tierra, sea de la semilla de la tierra o del fruto del árbol, es de Jehová; santo es a Jehová.
31

Y si alguien quiere rescatar parte de su diezmo, añadirá una quinta parte.
32

Leviticus 27 meaning — valuing a person, and the price that already happened

This closing chapter is a fixed price list: not what a person is worth, but what a VOW of a person costs to fulfill. ⚠ Erekh, ‘valuation,’ is grammatically addressed to Moses himself — ‘YOUR valuation’ — a flat tariff by age and sex, not a market appraisal, with an explicit provision (v8) for the poor to have the priest set a figure they can actually pay. KJV renders it ‘thy estimation’ throughout, keeping the second-person address visible; NIV smooths it to the impersonal ‘the following amounts,’ losing the grammatical oddity entirely; NWT keeps a version of the personal address, ‘your valuation.’ This project's own note on Genesis 37:28 (already on these pages) already put this exact number to use: Joseph, seventeen years old, is sold for twenty pieces of silver — precisely this chapter's own fixed valuation for a male between five and twenty. The price was not an insult or an underpayment; it was, horribly, the going institutional rate.

Animals already fit for the altar — no swap allowed

An animal already qualified for sacrifice cannot be substituted once vowed, not even trading a worse one in for a better one — and if someone tries anyway, the law's penalty is generous rather than punitive: both animals become holy, doubling the gift rather than nullifying the swap. An unclean animal (one that could never be offered) gets an actual appraisal instead, ‘between good and bad’ (v12) — the only place in this chapter something is genuinely assessed for its own individual worth rather than slotted into a fixed tariff.

A house given to God, and the fifth that buys it back

A dedicated house gets the same individual appraisal as an unclean animal (v14) — and the same 20% redemption premium recurs here for the first of five times in this chapter (vv13, 15, 19, 27, 31): a person may always buy back what they dedicated, but never at par. The premium is not a fine; it is the price of changing your mind after the gift was already, legally, Jehovah's.

Fields, and the jubilee doing the same work it did two chapters ago

Dedicating land runs the identical logic Leviticus 25 (already on these pages) already established for selling it: an ancestral field's value is priced by how many jubilee-years remain, because the land was never fully alienable past the reset in the first place. ⚠ Vv22-24 draw a sharp distinction this chapter never explains outright: a field the man BOUGHT (not his own ancestral holding) reverts to its original family at the jubilee regardless of what he does with it — he never had the standing to dedicate more than his own temporary lease. But an ancestral field dedicated and never redeemed becomes the priest's permanent property at the jubilee, ‘like a field devoted’ (v21) — the chapter's first use of cherem, before the term gets its own full, harder treatment at vv28-29.

You cannot dedicate what was never yours to give

A firstborn animal cannot be vowed to Jehovah for the same reason a field already sold back at the jubilee cannot be permanently dedicated: it already belongs to him. This project's own note on Exodus 13:1-2 (already on these pages) already fixed the firstborn's consecrated status before Israel had even left Egypt; this verse simply closes the loophole someone might try — you cannot vow, as a gift, something you never owned to begin with.

Cherem — the one gift that cannot be taken back

⚠ Everything else in this chapter can be redeemed, for a price. Cherem, ‘the devoted thing,’ cannot — not property, not an animal, and, starkest of all, not a person. V29 is one of the hardest verses in this book, and this translation does not soften it: a human being placed under the ban is not ransomable, full stop. This project's own cherem entry (already on these pages) already named the moral weight of this word in the conquest narratives to come; here, for the first time, is the actual law those narratives will carry out.

The tithe, and a verb this project has already met in a psalm

Land's tithe can be redeemed at valuation plus a fifth, same as everything else; livestock's tithe cannot — every tenth animal that ‘passes under the staff’ (v32, the actual counting ritual: animals filed past one at a time, every tenth one tapped and set aside) is fixed by the count itself, not by appraisal. ⚠ V33's ‘inspect’ (levaqer) is the identical verb this project's own note on Psalm 27:4 (already on these pages) already flagged: the psalmist asking to be gone over by God the way a priest goes over a tithe animal, item by item, or a shepherd goes over a scattered flock. No favoritism, no cherry-picking the best for yourself and passing off the worst — the count itself, not a judgment call, decides which one is holy.

The last verse of Leviticus

⚠ V34 closes not just this chapter but the entire book — the identical colophon formula Leviticus 26:46 (already on these pages) already used to close the Sinai law code proper, here repeated to close this appendix as well. Mechon-Mamre's own source text marks this exact verse with a symbol used nowhere else in this book — by their own stated convention, ‘an empty line at the end of some books,’ distinct from the ordinary open/closed paragraph breaks (‘{פ}’/‘{ס}’) used throughout Leviticus. It is a page-layout note about their source printing, not a claim this project can verify as ancient Masoretic practice — but its placement, exactly here and nowhere else in the book, is itself suggestive. Leviticus opened at the tent of meeting with the mechanics of animal sacrifice (1:1-2); it closes on tithes, vows, and valuations — the whole book, start to finish, is instruction in what it costs to approach a holy God, and what that God is willing to accept in return. Numbers picks up immediately where this leaves off: the same wilderness camp, one month later (Numbers 1:1, already on these pages), still camped at the same mountain.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 27 both run 1–34 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. One Masoretic paragraph break divides the chapter: closed after v8; the final verse, v34, carries a distinct layout mark in Mechon-Mamre's own source text (rendered here with its own tooltip) that their site glosses as ‘an empty line at the end of some books’ — used nowhere else in Leviticus, and appearing exactly at the book's own last verse. One new dictionary entry: erekh, the fixed, non-negotiable valuation tariff that gives this chapter its structure. Cherem and ma'aser are both extended, not forked, with this chapter's own legal content.

Echoes paid. Genesis 37:28's own price for Joseph (already on these pages), now revealed as this chapter's exact fixed valuation for a boy his age; Leviticus 25's jubilee-tied land pricing (already on these pages), reused here for dedicated fields; Exodus 13:1-2's consecration of the firstborn (already on these pages), the reason a firstborn animal cannot also be vowed; this project's own cherem dictionary entry (already on these pages), given its actual source law for the first time; and Psalm 27:4's own note on levaqer (already on these pages), which already named this chapter's tithe-inspection verb.

Echoes planted. Numbers 18:21-24 (not yet on these pages), which will assign this chapter's own land-tithe to the Levites by name; Joshua 6 (already on these pages), where Jericho itself is placed under the ban, and Joshua 7 (not yet on these pages), where Achan's theft from it turns this chapter's abstract law into a narrative; and 1 Samuel 15 (not yet on these pages), where a king's own failure to carry out cherem against Amalek costs him the throne.
