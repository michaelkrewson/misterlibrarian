🕰
Jesus is led before Pilate, then to Herod for a hearing found in no other Gospel, then back to Pilate, who declares him innocent three times before yielding to the crowd's demand for Barabbas. Led out with two evildoers, he asks forgiveness for his executioners, is mocked by rulers and soldiers alike, and promises Paradise to a repentant criminal crucified beside him. Darkness covers the land, the temple curtain tears, and Jesus dies commending his spirit to the Father; a centurion calls him righteous. Joseph of Arimathea, a council member who had not consented to Jesus' condemnation, asks Pilate for the body and buries it in a new tomb before the Sabbath begins.
c. AD 30, the day of the crucifixion, the Friday following chapter 22's Passover night; the trial before Pilate, the crucifixion, and the burial all run in close parallel with Matthew 27 and Mark 15, though Luke's own account alone adds the Herod hearing, the penitent criminal's exchange with Jesus, and Joseph's explicit non-consent to the council's verdict.

Verses 1–5 · Σὺ λέγεις Sy legeis — a three-part political charge that appears in no other Gospel’s account of this hearing, and an answer as guarded as the one Jesus gave the Sanhedrin at daybreak

⭐⭐ Only Luke’s own account gives the Roman court a specific, three-part political charge — subversion, tax refusal, a rival kingship — where Matthew and Mark both report only a general “many things” and a single question about kingship. Matthew 27:12–13 and Mark 15:3–4 (both already on these pages) have the chief priests accuse Jesus of polla, “many things,” without specifying what; only Luke’s own narrator supplies the actual wording a Roman governor would need to hear — a would-be king refusing Rome its taxes. ⚠ The second charge is a flat reversal of what this Gospel’s own narrative has Jesus say about taxes: asked directly whether it is lawful to pay tribute to Caesar, Jesus answers “give back to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s” (20:25, already on these pages) — the opposite of forbidding it. The accusation Luke’s own account preserves is, on this Gospel’s own earlier evidence, false.

“You say so” (v3) answers Pilate’s question about kingship with the same Greek construction — sy legeis, literally “you say” — that Jesus used at daybreak to answer the Sanhedrin’s own question about being the Christ: “you say that I am” (22:70, already on these pages). Both answers hand the wording back to the asker rather than supplying a flat yes, and both courts treat the reply as sufficient grounds to proceed.

Verses 6–12 · ἐγένοντο … φίλοι egenonto … philoi — a scene found in no other Gospel, and a friendship struck between two men Jesus’s own Gospel has already called enemies

⭐⭐⭐ The whole Herod hearing — vv6–12 — has no parallel anywhere in Matthew, Mark, or John. Only Luke’s own account sends Jesus across the city to a second Roman-recognized authority; the other three Gospels move directly from Pilate’s first hearing to the crowd’s demand for Barabbas. ⚠ Herod gets, in this scene, the meeting this same Gospel has said he wanted since 9:9, and nothing more: earlier Jesus calls him “that fox” and is warned Herod wants to kill him (13:31–32, already on these pages); here Herod’s only recorded desire is to see him perform a sign (v8) — and gets to see him say nothing at all.

⭐⭐ “Herod and Pilate became friends with each other that very day” is a detail only Luke’s own narrator reports, and a detail Luke’s own second volume returns to name as a fulfillment. Acts 4:27 (not yet on these pages) quotes Psalm 2’s “the kings of the earth took their stand… against the Lord and against his Anointed” and names, as that fulfillment, “both Herod and Pontius Pilate, with the nations and the peoples of Israel” gathered together against Jesus — the identical two names, and the alliance this reconciliation sealed, read back as prophecy coming true rather than an incidental political footnote.

Verses 13–17 · τρίτον triton — a verdict of innocence stated three times before the crowd, and a verse absent from the critical text used here

⭐⭐⭐ Pilate declares Jesus free of any capital charge three separate times in this chapter — v4, v14–15, and v22, explicitly counted “a third time” — and only Luke’s own narrator keeps the count visible. Each of the three repeats the same verdict — no aition, no ground for a charge, found — and the third spells it out in full, aition thanatou, no “ground for death” (v22). No other Gospel numbers Pilate’s declarations this explicitly.

⚠⚠ Verse 17 is missing from this translation on purpose. The Byzantine text and the Textus Receptus add, after v16, a sentence explaining that Pilate was obligated to release one prisoner to the crowd at the feast — printed by the KJV in parentheses, by the Douay and Geneva without brackets at all, and by both Reina-Valera editions (RV 1909, RV60) as an ordinary verse. The earliest witnesses — the papyrus P75, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Alexandrinus — do not have it (Codex Sinaiticus does), and the sentence reads as a scribal note explaining the custom, imported from Matthew 27:15 and Mark 15:6 (both already on these pages, both of which DO state the Passover-amnesty custom directly). The NIV and NVI both omit it outright, matching the critical Greek text printed here; the NWT and TNM both print the verse number with no text under it, a silent flag rather than a footnote. This translation follows the earliest manuscripts and leaves the number out of the sequence rather than filling it with borrowed material.

Verses 18–25 · Βαραββᾶν Barabban — the crowd asks for a man whose own name means “son of the father,” in place of the one who has just called God his Father

See the Barabbas encyclopedia entry for the name itself. ⚠ Luke’s own account, like Mark’s, connects Barabbas to an actual stasis, an insurrection or riot, and a killing (v19, v25) — a specific, violent crime named twice in five verses, set directly beside Pilate’s own threefold finding that Jesus has committed none.

Verses 26–31 · ἐν τῷ ὑγρῷ ξύλῳ … ἐν τῷ ξηρῷ en tō hygrō xylō … en tō xērō — a green-wood riddle found in no other Gospel, spoken to mourners rather than to any of his accusers

⭐⭐ Verses 27–31 — the daughters of Jerusalem, and Jesus’s own last recorded words to a crowd before the cross — have no parallel in Matthew, Mark, or John. All three Synoptics report Simon of Cyrene pressed into carrying the cross (John has Jesus carry it himself, 19:17) (see the Simon of Cyrene entry), but only Luke’s own narrative has Jesus stop to address the women weeping for him, with a saying that predicts a coming reversal so severe that barrenness — elsewhere in this Gospel treated only as a “disgrace” lifted from Elizabeth (1:25, already on these pages) — will be called “happy.”

The closing riddle — if this is done to green wood, what will happen to dry? — is a proverb about kindling: green wood resists burning; dry wood catches easily. Applied to Jesus’s own execution and a coming siege, the logic runs: if Rome will do THIS to an innocent man at the peak of his popularity, what will it do to a whole city already dried out for burning? The image extends, without repeating any of its vocabulary, the same forecast of a besieged and fallen Jerusalem already given twice in this Gospel (19:43–44; 21:20–24, both already on these pages).

Verses 32–34 · κακούργους … Κρανίον kakourgous … Kranion — the word this Gospel already promised for the two men crucified beside Jesus, paid off exactly, and a place named by translation alone

⭐⭐⭐ The “evildoers” of v32–33 are the literal fulfillment of a promise this same Gospel made one chapter ago. At the Last Supper, Jesus quotes Isaiah — “with transgressors he was numbered” (Isaiah 53:12, already on these pages) — and says of himself, “this which is written must be fulfilled in me: ‘And he was numbered with the lawless.’ For indeed, what concerns me is reaching its fulfillment” (22:37, already on these pages), using the word anomoi, “lawless ones.” See the new kakourgos dictionary entry: this chapter does not repeat Isaiah’s own word but supplies its narrative equivalent — two kakourgoi, evildoers, crucified one on each side — the plainest possible staging of a quotation spoken one chapter, and one night, earlier.

⭐ “The place called The Skull” — Luke’s own account is the only Gospel that never writes the Aramaic name at all. See the Golgotha encyclopedia entry: Matthew and Mark both give the Aramaic Golgotha and then translate it; John does the same. Luke’s own Greek narrator skips the transliteration entirely and writes only the Greek translation, Kranion, “Skull” — consistent with a Gospel addressed, on its own opening claim, to a single named reader, “most excellent Theophilus” (1:3, already on these pages), who would have needed the Greek and not the Aramaic. “Calvary” is neither word: it is the Vulgate’s Latin translation of the same Greek noun, calvaria, which is where the KJV, Douay and Geneva all get ‘Calvary’ at this exact verse — a third language layered onto a name that was already, in Luke’s own telling, a translation rather than a place-name proper.

⚠⚠⚠ “Father, forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing” is absent from some of the very oldest surviving manuscripts of this Gospel. Codex Vaticanus (B) and the early papyrus P75 both lack the sentence; Codex Sinaiticus’ first scribe copied it, an early corrector deleted it, and a later one restored it. Bruce Metzger’s Textual Commentary calls the absence of the words from such early and diverse witnesses “most impressive”, and reports the committee’s judgment that the saying, though probably not part of Luke’s original Gospel, bears the marks of going back to Jesus himself — which is exactly why the UBS/NA critical text keeps it printed, but inside double square brackets, the notation for a reading the editors consider probably not original yet too significant to omit outright. Of the thirteen versions on both shelves, eleven print the sentence as plain running text with no bracket or footnote at all; only the NWT 1984 and its Spanish twin, the TNM 1987, keep the double-bracket notation visible to the reader (both later revised away from it, in the NWT 2013 and TNM 2019) — the one place on this shelf an older Watchtower edition is textually more cautious than its own revision. This translation follows the same critical convention and marks the sentence, in the Greek line above, with the same double-bracket status the editors themselves assign it.

Verses 35–38 · ὁ χριστὸς τοῦ θεοῦ, ὁ ἐκλεκτός ho christos tou theou, ho eklektos — a title of glory, spoken back as mockery

⭐⭐⭐ The rulers’ taunt echoes the title a voice from a cloud gave Jesus at the Transfiguration. “This is my Son, the Chosen One” (9:35, already on these pages) is echoed here as “the Christ OF GOD, the Chosen One” — the same Greek root, ho eklelegmenos there and ho eklektos here, turned from the Father’s own naming on a mountain into a crowd’s sneer at an execution. Peter’s confession, the Father’s voice, and this jeer are the formula’s three uses in this Gospel, the last one weaponized.

⚠ Shelf comparison, v36’s drink offered by the soldiers: KJV and Douay both read ‘vinegar’, and NIV reads the fuller ‘wine vinegar’; NWT alone reads ‘sour wine’, this translation’s own choice — oxos is the cheap, sour ration wine Roman soldiers themselves drank, posca, not a condiment. Offering it reads less as cruelty added to the mockery and more as the soldiers sharing their own drink while they jeer — which does not soften the scene so much as relocate its casualness.

The inscription’s exact wording is the one detail all four Gospels report differently: Matthew has “This is Jesus, the king of the Jews” (27:37, already on these pages), Mark has only “The King of the Jews” (15:26, already on these pages), John has the longest form and adds that it was written in Hebrew, Latin, and Greek (19:19–20, not yet on these pages), and Luke’s own version here reads “This is the King of the Jews.” No two of the four match word for word, which is itself the ordinary shape of four independent summaries of a single placard rather than four copies of one source.

Verses 39–43 · σήμερον μετʼ ἐμοῦ ἔσῃ ἐν τῷ παραδείσῳ sēmeron met’ emou esē en tō paradeisō — a scene found in no other Gospel, and a single comma that has divided the shelf over when, exactly, the promise takes effect

⭐⭐⭐ The exchange between Jesus and the two criminals — the first man’s taunt, the second man’s rebuke, and Jesus’s own promise of Paradise — is unique to Luke’s own Gospel. Matthew 27:44 and Mark 15:32 (both already on these pages) report only that both criminals reviled Jesus, with no exchange between the men and no dialogue at all; only Luke’s own account gives one of the two a change of heart, a rebuke of his own partner, a confession of Jesus’s innocence, and an answered request.

⭐⭐⭐ “This one calls him Jesus, without a title” — and one Greek manuscript tradition adds ‘Lord’. The critical text printed here reads simply Iēsou, “Jesus, remember me” (v42); the Byzantine text and Textus Receptus instead read kyrie, “Lord, remember me,” which is what the KJV and Douay both print, against the ASV, NIV, and NWT, all of which keep the bare name with no title. A dying man who has just rebuked his own partner for not fearing God addresses Jesus, on the earliest evidence, by name alone — a plea made without yet using any title for him at all, the request itself carrying all the confession.

⭐⭐⭐ “Today” pays off a promise this Gospel planted one chapter ago — see the sēmeron dictionary entry, now extended with this verse. The last of Luke’s own eleven uses of the word is spoken here, to a dying man, on the same day sēmeron closed out a countdown to failure in Peter’s own denial (22:61, already on these pages). Most of the word’s uses in this Gospel mark an arrival; this last one marks the fullest arrival of all — a promise of Paradise made to a man who, by any ordinary reckoning, has only hours left to receive it.

⚠⚠ Where the comma falls changes what the sentence promises, and every Watchtower-family edition on this shelf punctuates it the same way, against every other version checked. The Greek has no punctuation at all: amēn soi legō sēmeron met’ emou esē en tō paradeisō can be split either “Truly I tell you TODAY, you will be with me in Paradise” (making “today” describe the moment of the promise, leaving its fulfillment open) or “Truly I tell you, TODAY you will be with me in Paradise” (making “today” the day Paradise itself arrives). KJV, ASV, NIV, Douay, Geneva, and both Reina-Valera editions and the NVI all punctuate the second way; NWT 1984 AND NWT 2013, together with both the TNM 1987 and TNM 2019, print the first in both languages and across both revisions — “Truly I tell you today, you will be with me in Paradise” — a reading that lines up with the Jehovah’s Witnesses’ own doctrinal position that the dead are unconscious until a future resurrection rather than present in paradise immediately. This is a punctuation choice, not a textual one; the Greek supports either reading on grammar alone, and the doctrinal stake riding on the comma is exactly why this note names it rather than letting the choice pass silently.

Verses 44–46 · τοῦ ἡλίου ἐκλιπόντος … εἰς χεῖράς σου παρατίθεμαι τὸ πνεῦμά μου tou hēliou eklipontos … eis cheiras sou paratithemai to pneuma mou — a torn curtain moved before the death it marks in the other Synoptics, and the only Synoptic Gospel to give Jesus articulate last words at all

⭐⭐ Luke’s own sequence places the torn temple curtain BEFORE Jesus dies, not after. Matthew 27:50–51 and Mark 15:37–38 (both already on these pages) both report the loud cry, THEN the death, THEN the curtain tearing; Luke’s own narrative order in vv45–46 puts the curtain first and the death second — the same two events, told in reverse sequence. Whether this reflects two different underlying traditions about timing or simply a narrator willing to group a natural-world sign with the darkness rather than the death itself, the library does not decide; the order is a genuine difference, not a discrepancy this translation is built to paper over.

⚠⚠ “Because the sun’s light failed” uses a verb, ekleipō, that is the ordinary Greek word for a solar ECLIPSE — and a real eclipse is astronomically impossible at Passover. Passover falls at the full moon, when the moon is on the far side of the earth from the sun; a solar eclipse requires the moon directly between the two. Some later manuscripts soften the verb to the vaguer “the sun was darkened” (the reading behind the KJV’s ‘the sun was darkened’), avoiding the technical word altogether; the earliest witnesses keep ekleipontos regardless of the astronomical problem it raises. This translation keeps the harder, earlier reading and states the problem rather than quietly choosing the easier one.

⭐⭐⭐ Luke alone, of the three Synoptics, gives Jesus articulate last words at the moment of death. Mark’s Jesus dies “letting out a loud cry”, with no words recorded in it at all (Mark 15:37, already on these pages); Matthew’s parallel matches Mark’s wordless cry. John’s own account gives a different final word, “it is finished” (19:30, not yet on these pages). Only Luke’s Jesus dies quoting Scripture — “into your hands I entrust my spirit” takes over whole the words of Psalm 31:5 (not yet on these pages), with one word added: “Father.” The same verb for “breathed his last,” exepneusen, is shared word for word with Mark’s own account of the death.

Verses 47–49 · δίκαιος dikaios — a verdict the other Synoptics give as a title of divine sonship, and this Gospel gives as a judgment on a life

⭐⭐⭐ Where Mark and Matthew both have the centurion declare Jesus ‘God’s son’, Luke’s own centurion says only that he was righteous. See the new dikaios dictionary entry: Mark 15:39 and Matthew 27:54 (both already on these pages) both have the centurion say, in nearly the same words, “truly this man was God’s son”; Luke’s own version replaces a confession of divine sonship with a verdict on how Jesus lived and died, the identical word this Gospel will use of Joseph of Arimathea three verses later (v50) — the same adjective, spoken of two different men, three verses apart.

The crowds “returning home beating their breasts” (v48) is a gesture this Gospel has already used once, of the tax collector in the temple (18:13, already on these pages) — a posture of grief bordering on guilt, here given to an anonymous crowd rather than to any named disciple or accuser.

Verses 50–56 · βουλευτὴς … οὐκ ἦν συγκατατεθειμένος bouleutēs … ouk ēn synkatatetheimenos — the one detail Mark’s own account leaves open, and Luke closes

⭐⭐ Only Luke’s own account states directly that Joseph did NOT vote for Jesus’s condemnation. See the bouleutēs dictionary entry: Mark calls Joseph a member of the council but never says whether he was present, or how he voted, at the night session that condemned Jesus (Mark 15:43, already on these pages). Luke’s own extra clause — “this man had not consented to their plan and action” — answers the question Mark’s account leaves open: whatever Joseph’s presence that night, his vote was not with the majority.

The day of Preparation (v54) is paraskeuē, the ordinary Jewish term for the day before a Sabbath, spent readying meals and affairs for the day when no work could be done — which is also why the women who watched the burial (v55) could not anoint the body until the Sabbath had ended, the errand this Gospel’s own next chapter opens with.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps a harder, earlier reading against the shelf’s own near-unanimous smoothing: v17’s whole sentence about the Passover-amnesty custom, omitted here as an import from Matthew and Mark rather than Luke’s own text; the double-bracketed “Father, forgive them” (v34), kept but flagged rather than printed as plain running text; and v45’s technical “the sun’s light FAILED” (ekleipontos), kept over the later manuscripts’ vaguer “the sun was darkened.”

Echoes paid. The kakourgoi promised at the Last Supper — “he was numbered with the lawless” (22:37, already on these pages) — are staged here exactly, one on each side (v32–33). The “today” that closed a countdown to failure in Peter’s denial (22:61, already on these pages) returns here marking the opposite: an arrival, promised to a dying stranger (v43). And “the Christ of God, the Chosen One” — first spoken by a voice from a cloud (9:35, already on these pages) — comes back here as a crowd’s taunt (v35), the same title once glory and now mockery.

Echoes planted. Dikaios, “righteous,” spoken twice in this chapter of two different men — the centurion’s verdict on Jesus (v47) and the narrator’s own description of Joseph (v50) — is the word this Gospel used already of Zechariah and Elizabeth, “both righteous before God” (1:6), and of Simeon, “righteous and devout” (2:25), and which Luke's second volume will turn from a verdict into a title, ‘the Righteous One’ (Acts 3:14, already on these pages; 7:52; 22:14, not yet on these pages).
