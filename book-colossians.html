📖 Colossians

Πρὸς Κολοσσαεῖς (Pros Kolossaeis) — 'To the Colossians' — the congregation in a small, declining wool town of the Lycus valley in Phrygia, overshadowed by its richer neighbours Laodicea and Hierapolis. It is the least significant place any New Testament letter is addressed to, and it received one of the highest statements about Christ ever written.

A short letter to a town Paul had never visited, answering a religion of angel-ranks, food rules and secret knowledge with a hymn — probably older than the letter itself — that says all the fullness settled in one man, and that in him all things hold together.

Chapter by chapter

Colossians 1The least important town any New Testament letter is addressed to receives one of the highest statements about Christ ever written. Colossae was a small, declining wool town in the Lycus valley, overshadowed by its richer neighbours; Paul had never been there — the congregation was founded by a local man, Epaphras — and an earthquake would flatten the valley about the time the letter was carried. Its people were being offered access to God through ranks of angels, food rules, festivals, ascetic discipline and special knowledge for insiders. Paul’s answer is not an argument but a HYMN, almost certainly one the churches were already singing before he wrote it down: “He is the image of the invisible God, firstborn of all creation… all things were created through him and for him… and in him all things hold together.” ⚠ Two of its words have carried enormous weight: “firstborn” (prōtotokos), which the fourth century went to war over — does it place Christ inside creation, or over it? — and “fullness” (plērōma), which was probably the opponents’ own term for the graded ranks of beings between God and the world, and which Paul says settled entirely in one man. Both are laid out with their pedigrees and no vote cast. ⚠ And the height of the hymn is deliberately anchored: the cosmic paragraph — thrones and lordships, all things visible and invisible — comes to rest on an execution, “having made peace through the blood of his cross.” The chapter’s centre is four Greek words: “Christ in you, the hope of glory.”

About the book

Where it sits
The TWELFTH book of the New Testament and one of the four 'prison letters' (with Ephesians, Philippians and Philemon). Colossians and Philemon are addressed to the same household circle: Onesimus, the runaway slave of Philemon's letter, is named at 4:9 as one of the two men carrying this one.
Genre
A LETTER, and a polemical one, though its opponents are handled by exposition rather than attack: Paul quotes a HYMN (1:15-20) and then spends chapter 2 turning it into an argument. It is the twin of EPHESIANS — roughly a third of the two run closely parallel, sometimes phrase for phrase — and the two were carried by the same messenger on the same journey.
Author
PAUL, with TIMOTHY as co-sender, and he signs it in his own hand (4:18, 'remember my chains'). ⚠ Authorship is DISPUTED, though less sharply than Ephesians: the objections are the letter's style (long chained sentences, heaped synonyms) and a Christology and eschatology some judge more developed than the undisputed letters. Those who defend it point to the situation — a hymn being quoted, an opponent's vocabulary being borrowed — as sufficient to explain both. The library reports both positions.
Date written
c. AD 60–62 if Paul wrote it, from the Roman imprisonment, carried with Philemon and Ephesians by Tychicus and Onesimus (4:7-9). ⚠ An earthquake devastated the Lycus valley around AD 60-61 and Colossae never fully recovered; if the town was already ruined the letter must predate it, which some read as favouring the earlier end — or an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus.
Place
Rome, on the traditional view; Ephesus and Caesarea have also been argued.
Audience
A congregation Paul had NEVER MET (2:1), founded by EPAPHRAS, a Colossian. It was being pressed by some teaching the letter never names outright — chapter 2 lets us reconstruct it: circumcision, food and drink rules, festivals and new moons and sabbaths, 'self-abasement and worship of angels', visionary experience, and 'do not handle, do not taste, do not touch'. It looks like a blend of Jewish observance, ascetic discipline and local Phrygian religious enthusiasm, offering access to God through ranks of intermediaries. Scholars call it 'the Colossian heresy' and argue endlessly about its exact shape, which is a caution: we know it only from the answer.

How it's laid out

1:1–14Thanksgiving for a congregation he has only heard of, and a prayer for knowledge, endurance and thankfulness.
1:15–23The HYMN — image of the invisible God, firstborn, all things created in him and held together in him, and reconciled through the blood of his cross.
1:24–2:5Paul's own labour: the mystery now made plain, 'Christ in you, the hope of glory', and every person presented mature.
2:6–23The argument the hymn was assembled for — against philosophy and empty deceit, the record of debt nailed to the cross, the powers disarmed, and rules that are 'a shadow of the things to come'.
3:1–4:6The life that follows: seek what is above, put off and put on, and the household code.
4:7–18Greetings, an exchange of letters with LAODICEA, and 'remember my chains' in Paul's own hand.

What it's about

  • The SUPREMACY of Christ — over creation, over the powers, over every claimed intermediary; the hymn is the letter's whole answer to the false teaching.
  • FULLNESS (plērōma) — a word the opponents used for graded ranks of divine beings, taken over and applied to Christ: 'in him all the fullness of deity dwells bodily' (2:9).
  • 'CHRIST IN YOU' — against a religion of access through intermediaries, the claim that what they are seeking is already inside them.
  • The POWERS — thrones, lordships, rulers, authorities: not denied, but created by him (1:16) and led in a triumphal procession (2:15).
  • SHADOW and SUBSTANCE — food rules, festivals and new moons as 'a shadow of the things to come, but the substance belongs to Christ' (2:17).
  • MATURITY for EVERY PERSON — teleios, a word the mystery-cults reserved for initiates, applied three times in one verse to anybody at all (1:28).

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. The text is secure; chapter 1's notable points are small. The greeting at 1:2 lacks 'and the Lord Jesus Christ' in the earliest witnesses (later copyists supplied it, and the KJV prints it), and 1:20 carries a bracketed 'through him' whose repetition some manuscripts omit as accidental. The chapter's real interpretive weight falls on two words rather than two readings: prōtotokos at 1:15 and plērōma at 1:19.

Looking forward

Colossians holds the highest Christology in the New Testament alongside John's prologue, and states it as a quoted HYMN rather than an argument: image of the invisible God, firstborn of all creation, the one in whom and through whom and for whom all things were made, who is before all things and in whom all things hold together; head of the body; firstborn from the dead; in whom all the fullness was pleased to dwell; through whom all things are reconciled — 'having made peace through the blood of his cross'. Chapter 2 sharpens it to the letter's decisive sentence: 'in him all the fullness of DEITY dwells bodily' (2:9). ⚠ And the hymn's height is deliberately anchored: the cosmic paragraph ends at an execution.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP, and the literary relationship with Ephesians. What the 'Colossian heresy' actually was, given that we know it only from the rebuttal. Whether prōtotokos at 1:15 places Christ inside creation or over it — the fourth century's central dispute, and the reason for the NWT's inserted 'other' at 1:16-17, which no manuscript supports. How far the 'reconciliation of all things' (1:20) reaches. And in what sense anything could be 'lacking of the afflictions of Christ' (1:24), on which the readings are several and old. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
