Paul, in Macedonia and newly reconciled with the Corinthians after a painful visit and a letter of tears, opens the most personal letter he wrote — a blessing of the God of all comfort, and the near-death 'in Asia' that taught him to trust the God who raises the dead.
c. AD 55–56, a year or so after 1 Corinthians, written from MACEDONIA (2:13; 7:5) rather than Ephesus — Paul had left Asia after the crisis he refuses to name, crossed to Macedonia, and there met Titus with the news that the breach was healed. The relief is the reason the letter exists.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · the same city, a different mood, and a new co-sender

The most personal letter Paul wrote. Where 1 Corinthians answered a congregation’s questions point by point, this one is a wound with the bandage half off — a defense of his own ministry against people who had turned the Corinthians against him, written in relief after a reconciliation that had nearly failed. It swings from despair to triumph and back inside a paragraph, and the swings are the content. It opens, therefore, not with the thanksgiving that opens almost every Pauline letter (“I thank my God…”) but with a blessing of God — the Jewish berakah form — which is the first sign that something has changed since the last letter.

⚠ The co-sender is different. First Corinthians was signed with Sosthenes; this one with Timothy the brother — Paul’s closest and youngest colleague, the man he had in fact sent to Corinth to represent him (1 Corinthians 4:17) and whose mission appears to have gone badly, which is part of why the relationship needed repairing. That the letter of reconciliation is co-signed by the very envoy the Corinthians had mistreated is a quiet, deliberate touch.

“Together with all the holy ones in the whole of Achaia.” The address widens past Corinth to the whole Roman province — southern Greece, with Corinth as its capital. It is broader than 1 Corinthians’ address, and the widening is not accidental: a letter this raw about one congregation’s quarrel is being handed, deliberately, to the neighbours as well. Paul renders the same words as before — congregation, and holy ones rather than “saints” — on the same grounds as in Romans and 1 Corinthians.

Verses 3–7 · ten times in five verses

⚠ One word carries the paragraph, and English cannot keep up with it. The noun-and-verb family of paraklēsis — comfort, encouragement, consolation, the drawing-alongside of someone who calls you to your feet — appears ten times in these five verses. Greek can ring one root like a bell; English has to alternate “comfort,” “console,” “encourage,” which breaks the chime. We hold to comfort throughout, at some cost in elegance, because the drum-beat is the argument: the God of all comfort comforts us in affliction so that we can comfort the afflicted with the comfort we were comforted with. It is deliberately claustrophobic. KJV keeps “comfort” the whole way and is the better for it; NIV and NWT both switch to “comfort… encouragement” and lose the repetition. ⚠ The related noun paraklētos is the word John’s Gospel uses for the Spirit — the Advocate, the Comforter — so this is not sentiment; it is the word for one called to your side in a court.

“The Father of compassions.” Oiktirmōn is plural in the Greek — not an abstract quality but a heap of concrete mercies — and it renders a standard Hebrew title of God (av ha-rachamim), the “womb-compassions,” which the Zechariah 1 note met from the other side. KJV “the Father of mercies”; ASV the same; NWT “the Father of tender mercies”; NIV “the Father of compassion.”

⚠ “The sufferings of Christ overflow into us.” A startling claim, and the middle of the paragraph: Paul does not say his own sufferings resemble Christ’s, he says Christ’s sufferings flow over into him. Ta pathēmata tou Christou — the same sufferings, spilling from the head into the members — and the comfort flows by the same channel. The point of the chapter’s opening is that Paul’s near-death in Asia was not an interruption of his apostleship but the substance of it.

Verses 8–11 · a disaster he never names

⚠ “We despaired even of living.” Then the concrete case — and Paul, who could not stop describing his hardships when it mattered less (11:23–28), here refuses to name the one that mattered most. Something happened “in Asia” (the Roman province around Ephesus) that crushed him “beyond our strength” so that he “despaired even of living.” He says no more. KJV “we despaired even of life”; ASV the same; NWT “we were very uncertain even of our lives.” The guesses are ancient and none is provable: the Ephesus riot of Acts 19; a mortal illness; an imprisonment we have no record of; “fighting wild beasts at Ephesus” (1 Corinthians 15:32), whether literal or figurative. The reticence is itself remarkable in a man usually so specific — and it is the reticence of someone who has decided the point is not the calamity but what it taught.

“We had received within ourselves the sentence of death.” Apokrima is a technical legal word — an official verdict, a ruling handed down — found only here in the New Testament. The image is of a man who has heard the death sentence pronounced over him and stopped expecting a reprieve. KJV “the sentence of death”; Douay “the answer of death” (following the Vulgate’s responsum); NIV “the sentence of death.” And the purpose, he says, was to burn away self-reliance: “so that we would not trust in ourselves but in God, who raises the dead.” The resurrection is not brought in here as a doctrine to be defended but as the only ground a man at the end of himself has left to stand on.

“Rescued… will rescue… will rescue again.” Three tenses of one verb (rhyomai, to drag out of danger, the verb of the Lord’s Prayer’s “deliver us from evil”) — past, future, and future-again — which is the grammar of a hope reaching in both directions from a rescue already tasted. And verse 11 quietly makes the Corinthians part of it: the deliverance came “as you also join in helping us by your prayer.”

Verses 12–14 · a clear conscience, and a pun on reading

“The testimony of our conscience.” Syneidēsis — literally “knowing-with-oneself,” the inner witness — is very nearly Paul’s own contribution to moral vocabulary; the word barely exists in this sense before him. His single boast, against opponents who boast of eloquence and credentials, is a clear one: that he behaved with haplotēs (some manuscripts) or hagiotēs, holiness — the texts split by one letter — and with eilikrineia, “sincerity,” a lovely word that may mean “judged in sunlight” (heilē, sun-warmth), the honesty of a thing that has nothing to hide when held up to the light.

⚠ “We write you nothing other than what you read.” A pun that only half-survives translation. Anaginōskete means “you read,” and epiginōskete “you understand” or “recognise” — the same verb, ginōskō (know), twice, plainly and then more deeply. Paul, accused of writing letters that meant something other than they said (his enemies apparently called them weighty on paper and himself feeble in person, 10:10), insists there is no hidden layer: what is on the page is what is in the man. KJV “none other things… than what ye read or acknowledge.”

Verses 15–17 · the changed travel plan, and the charge of double-talk

⚠ This is the wound. The whole warm meditation on comfort has been leading here: Paul had promised to visit Corinth, then changed the plan, and his opponents had used the change as proof that his “yes” meant nothing — that a man who reverses a travel itinerary will reverse a gospel. The itinerary he describes (Corinth → Macedonia → back to Corinth → Judea) is the “second favor”: a double visit he had offered and then dropped. Chapter 2 will explain why — a visit had gone so badly (“a painful visit,” 2:1) that a third would have wounded everyone — but here he only defends the honesty of the change.

“Yes, yes and No, no at once.” To Nai nai kai to Ou ou — the charge is that Paul says yes and no in the same breath, promises and un-promises, plans “according to the flesh” (by mere convenience and self-interest). ⚠ There is an irony a first reader would catch: Jesus had said “let your yes be yes and your no be no” (Matthew 5:37), and the accusation turns that very saying into a weapon — you, of all people, cannot even keep an appointment. Paul is about to take the weapon back. KJV “that with me there should be yea yea, and nay nay”; NWT “so that it is Yes, Yes and No, No.”

Verses 18–20 · the counter-attack, and where “Amen” comes from

⚠ He turns a travel dispute into theology, which is the most Pauline move there is. Accused of being a “yes-and-no” man, he answers by pointing not at his own reliability but at God’s: “God is faithful.” And then the pivot that has outlived the quarrel entirely: the Son of God preached among you “did not become Yes and No, but in him it has become Yes. For however many are the promises of God, in him they are Yes.” Every ancient promise — to Abraham, to David, through every prophet — is a question God answers with one word, and the word is Jesus. It is one of the great sentences in Paul, and it grew out of an argument about whether he could keep to a schedule.

⚠ “Therefore also through him is the Amen.” The single Hebrew word this translation, like every other, leaves untranslated. Amen is from the root aman, to be firm, reliable, trustworthy — the same root as emunah, the “faith” of Habakkuk 2:4. To say Amen is to say “firm, it stands, so be it” — and Paul’s point is exact: if God’s promises are all “Yes” in Christ, then the congregation’s “Amen” in worship is the human echo of the divine yes, given back “to God, for glory.” The word every congregation says without thinking is here traced to its root, in a chapter about whether a man’s word can be trusted.

Verses 21–22 · four commercial and legal metaphors in two verses

Four images, stacked. God confirms (bebaioō, a legal word for guaranteeing a contract), anointed (chrisas — the verb behind Christos, so “the one who Christs us”), sealed, and gave a down payment. The metaphors are all from commerce and law, which is fitting in a chapter defending Paul’s trustworthiness: he answers a charge of bad faith with the vocabulary of guarantees.

“Sealed us.” Sphragis is the impression a signet ring leaves in wax — a mark of ownership and of authenticity, stamped on a document, a jar of goods, or a letter to prove it had not been opened. To be sealed is to be marked as genuinely God’s and not tampered with.

⚠ “The down payment of the Spirit.” Arrabōn is one of the most vivid words in the New Testament: a commercial term, borrowed by Greek from Semitic traders (Hebrew eravon, the pledge Judah left with Tamar in Genesis 38), meaning the first instalment that guarantees the rest — earnest-money, a deposit that is itself part of the full payment and legally binds the buyer to complete it. The Spirit is not a substitute for what is coming; it is a down payment on it, the same currency, a genuine first taste that makes the remainder certain. ⚠ In modern Greek arravónas is still the word for an engagement ring, which is nearly the perfect gloss. KJV “the earnest of the Spirit” — “earnest” being the old English commercial word for exactly this; NWT “a token of what is to come”; NIV “a deposit, guaranteeing what is to come.”

Verses 23–24 · an oath, and a hasty correction

“I call on God as a witness against my own soul.” An oath, and a heavy one: he stakes his life on the true reason he stayed away — not fickleness, but mercy. Pheidomenos, “sparing,” is a word for holding back a blow. He did not come back to Corinth because a third confrontation would have hurt them, and he would rather be accused of unreliability than inflict it.

⚠ Then he hears how that sounds, and catches himself. “It was to spare you” risks sounding like a man who thinks the congregation exists at his mercy — so he corrects it on the spot: “not that we lord it over your faith, but we are fellow workers for your joy.” Synergoi, co-workers, not kyrioi, lords — the same self-correction reflex we saw in 1 Corinthians 1 (“I baptized… well, and Stephanas… beyond that I don’t recall”), and one of the most attractive things about him. An apostle writing to defend his authority stops mid-sentence to disclaim it. KJV “not for that we have dominion over your faith, but are helpers of your joy”; ASV “not that we have lordship over your faith, but are helpers of your joy”; NWT “not that we are the masters over your faith, but we are fellow workers.” And the last four words are the whole of his pastoral theology: “for by faith you stand” — not by their loyalty to Paul, which is what the factions of the first letter had made it about.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “comfort” held through all ten occurrences of the paraklēsis family in verses 3–7, keeping the claustrophobic drum-beat the versions break by alternating synonyms; “the Father of compassions” for the plural oiktirmōn; “the sentence of death” for the legal apokrima; “you read… you understand” keeping the ginōskō pun; “sealed” for sphragis, the signet in wax; and above all “down payment” for arrabōn, the first instalment that guarantees the rest and is itself part of the sum — not a mere “token,” which loses that the deposit is the same currency as the balance.

Echoes paid. The Jewish berakah replacing the usual thanksgiving, and “the Father of compassions” carrying the Hebrew av ha-rachamim and its womb-root, met from the other side in Zechariah 1; arrabōn reaching back to the eravon, the pledge Judah left with Tamar in Genesis 38:18; “let your yes be yes” (Matthew 5:37) turned into an accusation and taken back; and Amen traced to the same root, aman, as the “faith” of Habakkuk 2:4.

Echoes planted. The affliction in Asia, named but never described, standing behind the whole letter’s theology of strength-in-weakness that peaks at 12:9 (“my power is made perfect in weakness”); the changed travel plan, whose real reason — the “painful visit” and the “letter of tears” — chapter 2 will finally give; the self-correction reflex (“not that we lord it over your faith”), which becomes the argument of the whole defense — authority exercised as service; and the Yes / Amen, one of the seeds of the letter’s recurring insistence that everything true about Paul’s ministry is really something true about God.

Next in this book: 2 Corinthians 2 — why he really stayed away (“I made up my mind not to make you another painful visit”); the “letter written with many tears”; the call to forgive and restore the man who had caused the pain, “so that he may not be overwhelmed by too much sorrow”; and the image of the gospel as a triumphal procession trailing the smell of incense — “to some the fragrance of death, to others the fragrance of life.”
