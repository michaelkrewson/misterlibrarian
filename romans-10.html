The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. Chapter 9 ended on a stone of stumbling and a believer who will not be put to shame; chapter 10 begins with Paul’s wish and petition for his people, names their lack (a zeal without accurate knowledge), and then walks a chain: Christ the end of law, the word that is near (Deuteronomy), a confession and a belief, one Lord rich toward all who call on him (Joel), and the questions that run backwards from calling to being sent. It closes with Israel and three quotations from Moses, the Psalms and Isaiah.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 9; this library dates the letter no more closely than those chapters do. The only time-words the chapter uses are the ones it quotes: “all day long” (verse 21), and the “first” of “First Moses says” (verse 19).

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · Ἀδελφοί, ἡ μὲν εὐδοκία τῆς ἐμῆς καρδίας … ζῆλον θεοῦ ἔχουσιν· ἀλλʼ οὐ κατʼ ἐπίγνωσιν … τῇ δικαιοσύνῃ τοῦ θεοῦ οὐχ ὑπετάγησαν Adelphoi, hē men eudokia tēs emēs kardias … zēlon theou echousin; all’ ou kat’ epignōsin … tē dikaiosynē tou theou ouch hypetagēsan — Romans 10:1–3: “a zeal for God, but not according to accurate knowledge.” A wish and a petition, a zeal that lacks one thing, and two righteousnesses, one of them “their own”

“Brothers, the goodwill of my heart and my petition to God on their behalf” (Adelphoi, hē men eudokia tēs emēs kardias kai hē deēsis pros ton theon hyper autōn, Romans 10:1). The sentence has no verb, and the English supplies “are.” Chapter 9 closed on a stone of stumbling and on the believer who “will not be put to shame” (9:33); chapter 10 begins with a plain address, “Brothers,” the word the letter used of the congregation (7:1) and, at 9:3, of Paul’s own people “according to the flesh.” Eudokia (dictionary) stands in nine verses of the New Testament (eudokia, searched), and in five of them it is God’s own good pleasure: Matthew 11:26, Luke 10:21, Ephesians 1:5, 1:9 and Philippians 2:13 (the last not yet on these pages). Here it is Paul’s, as at Philippians 1:15, where some proclaim Christ di’ eudokian, “out of goodwill”; Luke 2:14 has a textual variant on its last letter (the dictionary entry sets out the two readings), and in 2 Thessalonians 1:11 the Greek does not say whose it is. The second noun, deēsis (dictionary), is a petition made to someone, as in Luke 1:13 (“your petition has been heard”); the two nouns are a wish and a request, and the verse says that both are “for salvation.”

The apparatus has a variant at the pronoun. The SBLGNT has hyper autōn, “on their behalf,” with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition; the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont has hyper tou Israēl estin, “is on behalf of Israel,” and puts one more article after deēsis. KJV “for Israel” and Geneva “for Israel” carry the name; ASV “for them”, Douay “for them” and NWT 1984 “for them” keep the pronoun, which reaches back to the “Israel” of 9:31; and NIV “for the Israelites” and the TLB “the Jewish people” name the people in their own words. The closing phrase, eis sōtērian, “for salvation,” is the phrase of 1:16 (“God’s power for salvation to everyone who believes, to the Jew first and also to the Greek”), and this chapter uses the letter’s thesis words on purpose: eis sōtērian again at verse 10, panti tō pisteuonti, “to everyone who believes,” at verse 4, and “Jew and Greek” at verse 12.

“A zeal for God, but not according to accurate knowledge” (zēlon theou echousin; all’ ou kat’ epignōsin, Romans 10:2). The verb is martyrō, “I bear witness”: Paul called his own conscience as a witness in 9:1, and here he is the witness, and what he testifies is in their favor. He can say it from experience. Of himself he says that he was “a zealot of God, just as all of you are today” (Acts 22:3) and “far more zealous for the traditions of my fathers” (Galatians 1:14). The genitive in zēlon theou can point toward God (a zeal directed at him) or describe the zeal (a zeal of the kind that concerns God), and the shelf divides: KJV “a zeal of God”, Geneva “the zeal of God” and Douay “a zeal of God” keep the old “of”; ASV “a zeal for God”, NWT 1984 “a zeal for God” and NIV “zealous for God” point it toward him; and the TLB “the honor of God” makes it a concern for his reputation. The knowledge they lack is epignōsis (dictionary), a noun that stands in three verses of Romans (epignōsis, searched): 1:28, where the nations “did not approve of holding God in accurate knowledge”; 3:20, where through law comes “the recognition of sin”; and here. This page prints “accurate knowledge” here as at 1:28, so that the indictment of the first chapter and the diagnosis of the tenth can be heard in the same words. KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva have “according to knowledge”; NIV “not based on knowledge”; only NWT 1984 “accurate knowledge” marks the prefix epi-, which reference works read as sharpening the word (reported, not checked in a lexicon); and the TLB “misdirected zeal” says what it concludes and leaves the noun out.

“Not knowing God’s righteousness, and seeking to establish their own” (agnoountes tēn tou theou dikaiosynēn, kai tēn idian zētountes stēsai, Romans 10:3). Agnoeō, “not know,” is the verb Paul used to the judge at 2:4 (“not knowing that the kindness of God is leading you to repentance”), and God’s righteousness is the letter’s own theme-phrase (1:17, 3:5, 3:21–22). The Greek genitive can mean the righteousness that is God’s own or the righteousness he gives, and the verse does not say which. The word idian, “their own,” has no noun after it in the SBLGNT, with Westcott–Hort and Tregelles; Nestle–Aland and Robinson–Pierpont add dikaiosynēn. KJV “their own righteousness” and Geneva “their own righteousness” print the noun; ASV “their own”, Douay “their own”, NIV “their own” and NWT 1984 “their own” do not. The verb stēsai, “to make stand,” is of the same family as the one at 3:31, where the letter says of law, “No, we establish law” (histanomen): two things a person can set up in this letter, and the verses name both. The last verb, hypetagēsan, is passive in form (“were not subjected”) and reflexive in sense, and it is the verb of 8:7 (“it is not subjected to God’s law, nor can it be”) and of 8:20 (“the creation was subjected to futility”). ASV “did not subject themselves” and NWT 1984 “did not subject themselves” keep the verb; KJV “have not submitted themselves”, Geneva “have not submitted themselves” and Douay “have not submitted themselves” use the older word; NIV “they did not submit”; and the TLB “that is not God’s way of salvation” has replaced it with a verdict.

Verse 4 · τέλος γὰρ νόμου Χριστὸς εἰς δικαιοσύνην παντὶ τῷ πιστεύοντι telos gar nomou Christos eis dikaiosynēn panti tō pisteuonti — Romans 10:4: “Christ is the end of law, for righteousness to everyone who believes.” “The end of the law”: a noun the Greek puts first, that means where something stops, what it aims at, or where it is completed

“For Christ is the end of law” (telos gar nomou Christos, Romans 10:4). The Greek puts telos, “end,” first in the clause, and gives nomou no article, as 9:31 gave nomon none (“a law of righteousness”). All six versions on the shelf that translate the clause supply “the”: KJV “the end of the law”, ASV “the end of the law”, Douay “the end of the law”, Geneva “the end of the Law”, NIV “the culmination of the law” and NWT 1984 “the end of the Law.” The page prints “law” bare, as the Greek does, and leaves the reader to hear the article or not.

Telos (dictionary) is the word for the place where something stops and also for what it is for. In Romans it stands in four verses (telos, searched): 6:21 (“the end of those things is death”), 6:22 (“and the end, eternal life”), this verse, and 13:7, where it is a toll that is owed, which is another sense altogether (now on these pages). The two in chapter 6 are outcomes, the place a road comes to. Outside Romans the same noun is printed “goal” at 1 Timothy 1:5 (“the goal of the charge is love”) and, in an idiom with the verb “have,” “reaching its fulfillment” at Luke 22:37 (“what concerns me is reaching its fulfillment”). So the noun has three senses in these pages’ own English: a stopping place, an aim, a completion. The English word “end” carries all three together (“the end of the road,” “to what end,” “the end of the matter”), which is why this page prints it and does not choose: the three readings are that Christ ends law as a way to righteousness, that Christ is what law was aiming at, and that Christ is where law is completed. The shelf: five say “end” as a noun (KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva and NWT 1984); the NIV “culmination” takes the third sense; and the TLB “He ends all of that” takes the first, and says what “that” is: “everything they are trying to get by keeping his laws.”

Whatever the noun means, it sits in a letter that asked at 3:31, “Do we then abolish law through the faith? May it never be! No, we establish law,” and in a chapter whose very next sentence cites the law (“Moses writes,” verse 5). The rest of the clause is built from the letter’s own phrases. Eis dikaiosynēn, “for righteousness,” stands in seven verses of Romans (eis dikaiosynēn, searched): 4:3, 4:5, 4:9 and 4:22 (Abraham’s faith, reckoned for righteousness), 6:16 (“obedience to righteousness”), this verse and verse 10; and panti tō pisteuonti, “to everyone who believes,” is the phrase of 1:16.

Verses 5–8 · Μωϋσῆς γὰρ γράφει … ἡ δὲ ἐκ πίστεως δικαιοσύνη οὕτως λέγει· Μὴ εἴπῃς ἐν τῇ καρδίᾳ σου … Ἐγγύς σου τὸ ῥῆμά ἐστιν Mōysēs gar graphei … hē de ek pisteōs dikaiosynē houtōs legei: Mē eipēs en tē kardia sou … Engys sou to rhēma estin — Romans 10:5–8: “Moses writes … but the righteousness out of faith speaks.” Leviticus 18:5, Deuteronomy 30:12–14, an abyss where Moses had a sea, and a quotation that stops one phrase early

“Moses writes that the man who has done the righteousness that is out of the law will live by it” (Mōysēs gar graphei hoti tēn dikaiosynēn tēn ek tou nomou ho poiēsas anthrōpos zēsetai en autē, Romans 10:5). The present-tense “writes” (graphei) stands in Romans only here (searched); the letter’s usual introduction is “it is written,” as at verse 15. The quotation is Leviticus 18:5, which this site prints “You shall keep my statutes and my ordinances — the one who does them will live by them.” The Hebrew is asher yaaseh otam ha-adam vachai bahem, and the Greek Old Testament (in Brenton’s printed text, checked) has ha poiēsas auta anthrōpos zēsetai en autois. The sentence is a formula: the Hebrew words stand in four verses of the Hebrew Bible (searched in both spellings), Leviticus 18:5 and Ezekiel 20:11, 13 and 21 (Ezekiel 20 is not yet on these pages), where they are said three times of statutes given in the wilderness, twice of a people that rebelled against them. Paul quotes the Greek again at Galatians 3:12 (ho poiēsas auta zēsetai en autois; not yet on these pages; Greek checked).

Romans 10:5 has a textual problem on exactly this point. The SBLGNT, with Westcott–Hort, reads the sentence with “the righteousness that is out of the law” as the thing the man has done and lives by: hoti tēn dikaiosynēn tēn ek tou nomou ho poiēsas anthrōpos zēsetai en autē, singular “in it.” Tregelles, the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont move the first hoti to after the phrase, add auta after poiēsas and read en autois (four apparatus notes at this verse, one of them the article before nomou, which does not show in English), so that the sentence says “Moses writes the righteousness that is out of the law: that the man who has done them will live by them” — word for word the Greek of Leviticus and of Galatians 3:12. The shelf splits along that line. ASV “that the man that doeth the righteousness which is of the law shall live thereby”, Douay “that the justice which is of the law, the man that shall do it, shall live by it” and NWT 1984 “that the man that has done the righteousness of the Law will live by it” take the SBLGNT’s shape; KJV “the righteousness which is of the law, That the man which doeth those things shall live by them”, Geneva “the righteousness which is of the Law, That the man which doeth these things, shall live thereby” and NIV “The person who does these things will live by them”, after “this about the righteousness that is by the law”, take the other; the NWT’s 2013 revision moved from the first group to the second; and the TLB turns the sentence into an argument of its own: “if a person could be perfectly good and hold out against temptation all his life and never sin once, only then could he be pardoned and saved.” Between this sentence and the next the Greek has only de, “but,” and does not say more about how they stand to each other.

“The righteousness out of faith speaks thus: Do not say in your heart” (hē de ek pisteōs dikaiosynē houtōs legei: Mē eipēs en tē kardia sou, Romans 10:6). Righteousness is made a speaker, and it speaks in the words of Moses. “Out of faith” is the phrase of 9:30, where the nations laid hold of “the righteousness that is out of faith,” and this verse sets it against the righteousness “out of the law” of verse 5 and the “their own” of verse 3. The speaker’s first six Greek words, Mē eipēs en tē kardia sou, are also the first six words of the Greek of Deuteronomy 9:4 (checked), the verse that tells Israel not to say “It is for my righteousness that Jehovah brought me in”; the Hebrew al-tomar bilvavekha stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Deuteronomy 30:12, which Paul goes on to quote, has only “that you should say,” and no command, so the imperative with “in your heart” has come from somewhere. Whether Paul has Deuteronomy 9:4 in mind the text does not say; its subject, “my righteousness,” is the subject of his sentence.

“Who will go up into heaven? … Who will go down into the abyss?” (Tis anabēsetai eis ton ouranon … Tis katabēsetai eis tēn abysson, Romans 10:6–7). Deuteronomy 30:12 has the first question nearly as Paul quotes it (“Who will go up for us to the heavens”; the Greek has tis anabēsetai hēmin eis ton ouranon, and Paul drops hēmin, “for us”). The second question is not Moses’s. Hebrew Deuteronomy 30:13 is mi yaavor-lanu el-ever ha-yam, “who will cross over for us to the other side of the sea,” and the Greek Old Testament has peran tēs thalassēs, “beyond the sea.” Paul has neither “sea” nor “cross”: he has “go down into the abyss.” Abyssos (dictionary) stands in nine verses of the New Testament (abyssos, searched): Luke 8:31 (the demons beg not to be ordered into it), this verse, and seven in Revelation (9:1, 2 and 11; 11:7; and 17:8, 20:1 and 20:3, these last three not yet on these pages). The nearest sentence in the Psalms pairs the two directions Paul pairs. Yaalu shamayim yeredu tehomot, “they go up to heaven, they go down to the depths” (Hebrew Psalm 107:26), is in the Greek Psalter anabainousin heōs tōn ouranōn, kai katabainousin heōs tōn abyssōn (Psalm 106:26 in the Greek numbering; checked), the same two verbs and the same noun, and umitthomot ha-arets tashuv taaleni, “and from the depths of the earth you will bring me up again” (Hebrew Psalm 71:20), is ek tōn abyssōn tēs gēs palin anēgages me (Psalm 70:20 in the Greek numbering; checked), whose verb “bring up” is Paul’s own at the end of verse 7. (Neither psalm is yet on these pages.) The shelf: KJV, Geneva, Douay and NIV “the deep”; ASV and NWT 1984 “the abyss.”

“That is, to bring Christ down … that is, to bring Christ up from the dead” (tout’ estin Christon katagagein … tout’ estin Christon ek nekrōn anagagein). The explaining formula tout’ estin, “that is,” stands in five verses of Romans (tout’ estin, searched): 7:18 (“that is, in my flesh”), 9:8 (“That is, it is not the children of the flesh …”) and three times in a row here, verses 6, 7 and 8. Where Deuteronomy has a man who might say a thing, Paul’s gloss says what the saying would amount to: going up to heaven would be to bring Christ down, and going down to the abyss would be to bring Christ up from the dead. The Greek does not say whether the gloss gives the sense Moses had or the use Paul is putting his words to, and six of the seven versions on the shelf print it as an aside (the TLB turns the whole thing into advice: “You don’t need to search the heavens to find Christ and bring him down to help you”). The second half sets the verb of an act that cannot be performed (“to bring Christ up from the dead”) beside the verb of an act God has performed, in verse 9: “God raised him from the dead.”

“The word is near you, in your mouth and in your heart” (Engys sou to rhēma estin, en tō stomati sou kai en tē kardia sou, Romans 10:8). The quotation drops the adverb sphodra, “very” (Hebrew meod), and the closing words, which differ in the Hebrew and the Greek: the Hebrew ends “in your mouth and in your heart, to do it” (laasoto), and the Greek Old Testament adds a third place, kai en tais chersi sou poiein auto, “and in your hands, to do it.” Paul stops at the heart. Where Moses’s sentence ends on doing, Paul’s gloss ends on proclaiming: “the word of faith that we proclaim” (to rhēma tēs pisteōs ho kēryssomen; dictionary). The noun is rhēma (dictionary), a thing said, and in Romans it stands only in this chapter, here and in verses 17 and 18 (rhēma, searched). For the end of the verse the shelf has KJV “the word of faith, which we preach”, ASV “the word of faith, which we preach”, Geneva “This is the word of faith which we preach”, Douay “This is the word of faith, which we preach” and NWT 1984 “the “word” of faith, which we are preaching”; the NIV “the message concerning faith that we proclaim” changes “word of faith” into a message about it; and the TLB “salvation that comes from trusting Christ” says what the word of faith is taken to be.

Verses 9–10 · ὅτι ἐὰν ὁμολογήσῃς ἐν τῷ στόματί σου κύριον Ἰησοῦν … ὁ θεὸς αὐτὸν ἤγειρεν ἐκ νεκρῶν, σωθήσῃ· καρδίᾳ γὰρ πιστεύεται εἰς δικαιοσύνην, στόματι δὲ ὁμολογεῖται εἰς σωτηρίαν hoti ean homologēsēs en tō stomati sou kyrion Iēsoun … ho theos auton ēgeiren ek nekrōn, sōthēsē; kardia gar pisteuetai eis dikaiosynēn, stomati de homologeitai eis sōtērian — Romans 10:9–10: “if you confess in your mouth Jesus as Lord … you will be saved.” “Jesus is Lord” or “the Lord Jesus”, and a heart and a mouth in reverse order

“That if you confess in your mouth Jesus as Lord” (hoti ean homologēsēs en tō stomati sou kyrion Iēsoun, Romans 10:9). The opening hoti can be “that,” telling what the word of faith of verse 8 says, or “because,” giving a reason for it. KJV “That if thou shalt confess” takes the first; ASV “because if thou shalt confess” takes the second; Geneva “For if thou shalt confess”, Douay “For if thou confess”, NWT 1984 “For if you publicly declare” and the TLB “For if you tell others” read it as “for,” and the NIV “If you declare” leaves it out. Homologeō (dictionary), “confess,” stands in Romans only here and in the next verse (homologeō, searched; the compound exomologeō is separate, at 14:11 and 15:9, both on these pages). This page prints “confess,” as at John 1:20 (“He confessed — he did not deny, but confessed”). The two words after it, kyrion Iēsoun, are accusatives with nothing to say which is the object and which the complement, so the Greek can be “confess the Lord Jesus” or “confess Jesus as Lord.” The same two words stand in seven verses of the New Testament (kyrion Iēsoun, searched): Acts 11:17, 11:20, 16:31, this verse, Philemon 5, and Romans 13:14 (now on these pages) and Philippians 3:20 (not yet on these pages). At Acts 16:31 they stand beside this verse’s own verb: Pisteuson epi ton kyrion Iēsoun kai sōthēsē, “Believe on the Lord Jesus, and you will be saved.” The shelf: KJV, Geneva and Douay “the Lord Jesus”; ASV “Jesus as Lord”; and NIV “Jesus is Lord”, NWT 1984 “that Jesus is Lord” and the TLB “that Jesus Christ is your Lord” turn it into the words of a confession.

The apparatus has two additions in Westcott–Hort that the SBLGNT does not: “the word” (to rhēma) after “confess,” and hoti, “that,” after “your mouth.” The NWT 1984 carries both: “if you publicly declare that ‘word in your own mouth,’ that Jesus is Lord”; the 2013 revision drops the first and prints “if you publicly declare with your mouth that Jesus is Lord.” Verse 8 and verse 9 use the same preposition for the mouth and the heart: “in your mouth and in your heart” (en tō stomati sou kai en tē kardia sou), and “in your mouth … in your heart” again here. Six of the seven versions on the shelf keep “in your heart” and change the mouth to “with”: KJV “with thy mouth”, ASV “with thy mouth”, Geneva “with thy mouth”, Douay “with thy mouth”, NIV “with your mouth” and the TLB “with your own mouth”; only the NWT 1984 “in your own mouth” keeps the preposition (its 2013 revision has “with your mouth”). This page keeps “in” for both, so the English hears verse 8 in verse 9.

“Believe in your heart that God raised him from the dead” (pisteusēs en tē kardia sou hoti ho theos auton ēgeiren ek nekrōn). The half-sentence is already in the letter: at 4:24 the ones to whom righteousness will be reckoned are those who believe “on the one who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead” (epi ton egeiranta Iēsoun ton kyrion hēmōn ek nekrōn), the two halves of this verse in one phrase. The verb at the end, sōthēsē, “you will be saved,” is the verb of verse 13 and the noun sōtēria of verses 1 and 10.

“With the heart it is believed, for righteousness … with the mouth it is confessed, for salvation” (kardia gar pisteuetai eis dikaiosynēn, stomati de homologeitai eis sōtērian, Romans 10:10). Verse 10 reverses the order of verse 9. Verse 8 and verse 9 run mouth, heart; verse 10 runs heart, mouth, and it is a pair of impersonal passives with no subject and no preposition on the two organs, each followed by eis, “for.” The result words are the letter’s own: eis dikaiosynēn, as in chapter 4 (4:3, 4:5), and eis sōtērian, as in 1:16. The Greek puts two clauses side by side with the same word between each clause and its result, and no “then.” The shelf reads it four ways. KJV “with the heart man believeth unto righteousness; and with the mouth confession is made unto salvation” keeps the shape; NWT 1984 “with the heart one exercises faith for righteousness, but with the mouth one makes public declaration for salvation” keeps it too; the NIV “it is with your heart that you believe and are justified, and it is with your mouth that you profess your faith and are saved” turns the two nouns into two verbs; and the TLB “it is by believing in his heart that a man becomes right with God; and with his mouth he tells others of his faith, confirming his salvation” makes the mouth the confirmation of what the heart has done. There is an older sentence about mouths in this letter. At 3:19, at the end of the indictment, the point of the law is “so that every mouth may be stopped”; here a mouth is opened for a salvation.

Verses 11–13 · λέγει γὰρ ἡ γραφή· Πᾶς ὁ πιστεύων ἐπʼ αὐτῷ οὐ καταισχυνθήσεται … οὐ γάρ ἐστιν διαστολὴ Ἰουδαίου τε καὶ Ἕλληνος … Πᾶς γὰρ ὃς ἂν ἐπικαλέσηται τὸ ὄνομα κυρίου σωθήσεται legei gar hē graphē: Pas ho pisteuōn ep’ autō ou kataischynthēsetai … ou gar estin diastolē Ioudaiou te kai Hellēnos … Pas gar hos an epikalesētai to onoma kyriou sōthēsetai — Romans 10:11–13: “everyone who calls on the name of Jehovah will be saved.” Isaiah’s stone again with one word added, a sentence from chapter 3 repeated, and Joel’s Jehovah in a chapter whose Lord is Jesus

“For the scripture says” (legei gar hē graphē, Romans 10:11). The scripture is made a speaker, as at 4:3 (“What does the scripture say?”), 9:17 (“the Scripture says to Pharaoh”) and 11:2: the formula stands in four verses of Romans (legei hē graphē, searched; 4:3 and 11:2 as questions). What it says is the last clause of the stone-sentence of 9:33 (Isaiah 28:16), with one word added at the front. That word, pas, “everyone,” is in the Byzantine text of 9:33 and the SBLGNT has it here. The Hebrew of the end of Isaiah 28:16 is hamaamin lo yachish, “the one who believes will not hasten,” and the Greek Isaiah has ho pisteuōn ou mē kataischynthē, “the one who believes will by no means be put to shame” (checked; Isaiah 28 is not yet on these pages). Paul writes ou kataischynthēsetai where the Greek has ou mē kataischynthē; the verb is the Greek one, and 1 Peter 2:6 has it again (ou mē kataischynthē; not yet on these pages; Greek checked). Kataischynō stands in ten verses of the New Testament (kataischynō, searched), and three of them are this sentence: Romans 9:33, this verse and 1 Peter 2:6. The pronoun in ep’ autō is the same Greek as at 9:33, where this page printed “on it” because the nearest masculine noun was “stone.” Here the stone is ten verses behind, and the last person named is Jesus (verse 9), so the page prints “on him.” The English “Everyone … will not” can be heard as “not everyone”; the Greek pas … ou puts the “not” on the verb and so denies it of the whole class, and the next verse, “no distinction,” takes the doubt away. The shelf: KJV “Whosoever believeth on him shall not be ashamed”; ASV “shall not be put to shame”; Geneva “Whosoever believeth in him, shall not be ashamed”; Douay “shall not be confounded”; NIV “Anyone who believes in him will never be put to shame”; and NWT 1984 “None that rests his faith on him will be disappointed” and the TLB “who believes in Christ will ever be disappointed” both change “shame” to “disappointed,” and the TLB names Christ.

“There is no distinction between Jew and Greek” (ou gar estin diastolē Ioudaiou te kai Hellēnos, Romans 10:12). Four Greek words of this clause, ou gar estin diastolē, are the last four words of Romans 3:22 (“for all who believe; for there is no distinction”). Diastolē (dictionary) stands in three verses of the New Testament (diastolē, searched): those two, and 1 Corinthians 14:7, where the pipe and the lyre give “no distinction” to their notes (not yet on these pages; Greek checked). The pair “Jew and Greek” has been in the letter from the start, first in the order of “first” (1:16, 2:9, 2:10) and then in the charge of 3:9 that Jews and Greeks alike are all under sin; here it is the pair for which there is no distinction. The shelf: ASV “no distinction between Jew and Greek”, NWT 1984 “no distinction between Jew and Greek” and Douay “no distinction of the Jew and the Greek”; KJV “no difference between the Jew and the Greek”; Geneva “no difference between the Jew and the Grecian”; NIV “no difference between Jew and Gentile” changes the second term; and the TLB “Jew and Gentile are the same in this respect.”

“The same one is Lord of all, being rich toward all who call on him” (ho gar autos kyrios pantōn, ploutōn eis pantas tous epikaloumenous auton). Who is the Lord? The Greek does not say, and the letter gives two candidates. The last person named is Jesus, Lord by confession in verse 9, and Luke’s Peter says of Jesus Christ in the same words, in the other order, houtos estin pantōn kyrios, “this one is Lord of all” (Acts 10:36). But the argument of 3:29–30 had God as its subject: “Is God the God of Jews only? Is he not also of the nations? … God is one,” and the sentence about distinction here is that sentence’s twin. This page prints “Lord,” the word that stays where the referent is Jesus or genuinely open, and all seven versions on the shelf do the same (KJV “the same Lord over all”, ASV “the same Lord is Lord of all”, NWT 1984 “the same Lord over all”). The verb plouteō, “be rich,” completes a family: riches are in the letter at 2:4 (“the riches of his kindness”), 9:23 (“the riches of his glory”), here, and at 11:12 and 11:33 (plout-, searched). The shelf: KJV “is rich unto all that call upon him”, NWT 1984 “who is rich to all those calling upon him”, NIV “richly blesses all who call on him”, and the TLB “generously gives his riches to all those who ask him for them.”

“Everyone who calls on the name of Jehovah will be saved” (Pas gar hos an epikalesētai to onoma kyriou sōthēsetai, Romans 10:13). The quotation is Joel 3:5 in the Hebrew numbering (2:32 in English Bibles; Joel is on these pages only for chapter 1): vehayah kol asher yiqra beshem YHVH yimmalet, “and it shall be that everyone who calls on the name of Jehovah shall escape.” The Greek Old Testament has pas hos an epikalesētai to onoma Kyriou sōthēsetai (checked), the words Paul writes, and the words Peter quotes at Acts 2:21. The Hebrew verb is malat, “escape,” where the Greek has “be saved.” “Calling on the name of Jehovah” is a phrase of the Hebrew Bible, and the letters stand in seven verses (searched): Genesis 4:26 (in the days of Enosh), 12:8 and 26:25 (Abraham and Isaac at their altars), Exodus 34:5, 1 Kings 18:24 (Elijah’s challenge to the prophets of Baal), 2 Kings 5:11 (Naaman, who expected Elisha to call on the name) and Joel 3:5, the only one of the seven with an “everyone.” This page prints “Jehovah” because the Greek here quotes a Hebrew sentence whose word is YHVH, as it did at Acts 2:21 and in the Isaiah quotations of chapter 9 (9:28, 29); the Greek reads kyrios. Paul does not say, and this page does not decide, whether the “Lord” of Joel’s sentence is for him the same person as the “Lord” of verse 9 or the God of Israel in whose name the calling is done. What the New Testament shows is that the verb is said of both. Stephen is “calling out and saying, ‘Lord Jesus, receive my spirit’” (Acts 7:59); Ananias tells the Lord who appeared to Saul that Saul has authority to bind “all who call on your name” (Acts 9:14), and the Lord is identified as Jesus at 9:17; and believers are “all those who in every place call on the name of our Lord Jesus Christ” (1 Corinthians 1:2). The shelf: NWT 1984 “everyone who calls on the name of Jehovah will be saved” follows the Hebrew; KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and the TLB “the Lord.”

Verses 14–15 · Πῶς οὖν ἐπικαλέσωνται εἰς ὃν οὐκ ἐπίστευσαν; … πῶς δὲ κηρύξωσιν ἐὰν μὴ ἀποσταλῶσιν; … Ὡς ὡραῖοι οἱ πόδες τῶν εὐαγγελιζομένων τὰ ἀγαθά Pōs oun epikalesōntai eis hon ouk episteusan? … pōs de kēryxōsin ean mē apostalōsin? … Hōs hōraioi hoi podes tōn euangelizomenōn ta agatha — Romans 10:14–15: “How are they to hear without someone proclaiming?” A chain of four questions asked backwards, a verb that means “hear” and a verb that means “send,” and Isaiah’s feet

“How then are they to call … to believe … to hear … to proclaim?” (Pōs oun epikalesōntai … pisteusōsin … akousōsin … kēryxōsin, Romans 10:14–15). Four questions, each resting on the one before and each asked backwards from the call of verse 13: calling needs believing, believing needs hearing, hearing needs someone proclaiming, proclaiming needs sending. The verbs are in the subjunctive in the SBLGNT, with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition, the “how are they to” of a question about what is possible; the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont has futures in all four (apparatus notes at verses 14 and 15). English shows either with “shall”: KJV “How then shall they call”, ASV “How then shall they call”, Geneva “how shall they call” and Douay “How then shall they call”; NIV “How, then, can they call”; NWT 1984 “how will they call.” The last link, apostalōsin, “they are sent,” is the verb under the noun apostolos: the letter’s opening says “we received grace and apostleship for the obedience of faith among all the nations” (1:5), and the “obedience” of that sentence returns in the next verse.

“In the one whom they have not heard” (pisteusōsin hou ouk ēkousan, Romans 10:14). The relative pronoun is in the genitive, and the genitive is the case this verb takes for a person who is heard. The verse can therefore mean “believe in one whom they have not heard” (heard speaking) or “one of whom they have not heard” (heard about), and the Greek does not choose. The shelf is split: ASV “in him whom they have not heard” takes the first; KJV “in him of whom they have not heard”, Geneva “in him, of whom they have not heard”, Douay “believe him, of whom they have not heard”, NIV “in the one of whom they have not heard” and NWT 1984 “in him of whom they have not heard” take the second; and the TLB “if they have never heard about him” commits to it. Luke has the first sense with the same verb and the same case: Luke 10:16, “The one who listens to you listens to me” (ho akouōn hymōn emou akouei), goes on “and the one who rejects me rejects the one who sent me” (ton aposteilanta me, from the verb of verse 15); and John 17:20 speaks of “those who will believe in me through their word.” The page prints “whom they have not heard,” and the second reading is in this note.

“How beautiful are the feet of those who announce the good news of the good things” (Hōs hōraioi hoi podes tōn euangelizomenōn ta agatha, Romans 10:15). The quotation is shorter than Isaiah 52:7. The Hebrew is mah-navu al-heharim raglei mevasser mashmia shalom mevasser tov mashmia yeshuah omer le-Tsiyyon malakh Eloheikh, “how beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of the herald, who announces peace, the herald of good, who announces salvation, who says to Zion, Your God reigns”; in the SBLGNT’s text Paul keeps the feet and the announcing and none of the mountains, the salvation or the reign, and the peace is in the Byzantine reading below. The Greek Isaiah has no word for “beautiful” (its text is hōs podes euangelizomenou akoēn eirēnēs, hōs euangelizomenos agatha, checked), though the clause before it in the Greek has hōs hōra epi tōn oreōn, and Paul’s hōraioi is built on hōra (reported, not checked in a lexicon). Hōraios stands in four verses of the New Testament (hōraios, searched): Matthew 23:27 (the tombs that look beautiful), Acts 3:2 and 3:10 (the Beautiful Gate) and here. The verb is the one this letter used at 1:15 (“announce the good news to you also who are in Rome”), and its next use is 15:20 (euangeliz-, searched). The introduction, kathōs gegraptai, “just as it is written,” is kathaper in Westcott–Hort alone, with the same sense. The SBLGNT, with Nestle–Aland and Robinson–Pierpont, has the article, ta agatha, “the good things,” and Westcott–Hort and Tregelles do not. The Byzantine text is longer, with two clauses like the Hebrew’s: tōn euangelizomenōn eirēnēn, tōn euangelizomenōn ta agatha, “who announce peace, who announce the good things.” The long text is the shelf’s majority: KJV “that preach the gospel of peace, and bring glad tidings of good things”, Douay “that preach the gospel of peace, of them that bring glad tidings of good things”, Geneva “which bring glad tidings of peace, and bring glad tidings of good things” and the TLB “who preach the Gospel of peace with God and bring glad tidings of good things”; the short text is ASV “that bring glad tidings of good things”, NIV “who bring good news” and NWT 1984 “who declare good news of good things.” On “beautiful” the shelf agrees, except NWT 1984 “comely” (the 2013 revision has “beautiful”).

Verses 16–17 · ἀλλʼ οὐ πάντες ὑπήκουσαν τῷ εὐαγγελίῳ· Ἠσαΐας γὰρ λέγει· Κύριε, τίς ἐπίστευσεν τῇ ἀκοῇ ἡμῶν; ἄρα ἡ πίστις ἐξ ἀκοῆς, ἡ δὲ ἀκοὴ διὰ ῥήματος Χριστοῦ all’ ou pantes hypēkousan tō euangeliō; Ēsaias gar legei: Kyrie, tis episteusen tē akoē hēmōn? ara hē pistis ex akoēs, hē de akoē dia rhēmatos Christou — Romans 10:16–17: “faith is from the thing heard, and the thing heard through a word of Christ.” “Obeyed” as “heard under,” one noun doing two jobs, and a genitive with three readings

“But not all obeyed the good news” (all’ ou pantes hypēkousan tō euangeliō, Romans 10:16). The verb is hypakouō (dictionary), built, reference works say, from akouō, “hear,” with a prefix meaning “under” (reported, not checked in a lexicon), and it is the verb of the letter’s frame: Paul received an apostleship “for the obedience of faith among all the nations” (1:5), and the chapter-6 slaves “obeyed from the heart the pattern of teaching” (6:17). “Obey the gospel” is said again of those who do not at 2 Thessalonians 1:8 (“those who do not obey the gospel of our Lord Jesus”), and of the other side at Acts 6:7 (“a great crowd of the priests were obeying the faith”). The word “all” is the chapter’s own: pas stands in verses 4, 11, 12, 13, 16 and 18, and verse 16 is the one place where “all” itself is put under a “not.” The shelf: KJV “have not all obeyed the gospel”, Geneva “have not all obeyed the Gospel”, Douay “all do not obey the gospel” and NWT 1984 “did not all obey the good news” keep the verb; ASV “did not all hearken to the glad tidings” takes the half of it that is “hear”; the NIV “not all the Israelites accepted the good news” changes the verb to “accepted” and adds who; the TLB “not everyone who hears the Good News has welcomed it.”

“Lord, who has believed the thing heard from us?” (Kyrie, tis episteusen tē akoē hēmōn?, Romans 10:16). The quotation is Isaiah 53:1, which this site prints “Who has believed what we heard? And the arm of Jehovah — upon whom has it been revealed?” The Hebrew has no “Lord” at the front: it begins mi heemin lishmuatenu. The Greek Isaiah adds the vocative Kyrie (checked), Paul and John both carry it, and this page prints “Lord” for the Greek vocative as it does at John 12:38; those two verses are the only two in the New Testament that quote this line (tis episteusen, searched). John goes on to the second line, “the arm of Jehovah,” and Paul stops after the first. The noun akoē (dictionary) does two jobs, the act of hearing and the thing heard, and the Hebrew shemuah is built on shama, “hear.” In the New Testament it stands in twenty-two verses (akoē, searched at the start of a word), and it means a report at Matthew 4:24 (“the report of him went out into all Syria,” hē akoē autou), ears at Acts 17:20 (“strange things to our ears,” eis tas akoas hēmōn), and hearing at Acts 28:26 (Akoē akousete, printed “You will indeed hear”). In Romans it stands only in this verse and the next. The shelf: KJV “who hath believed our report”, ASV “who hath believed our report”, Geneva “who hath believed our report” and Douay “who hath believed our report”; NIV “who has believed our message”; NWT 1984 “who put faith in the thing heard from us” (with “Jehovah” for the vocative); and the TLB “who has believed me when I told them.”

“So faith is from the thing heard, and the thing heard is through a word of Christ” (ara hē pistis ex akoēs, hē de akoē dia rhēmatos Christou, Romans 10:17). The root akou-, “hear,” stands six times in verses 14 to 18, and the “obeyed” of verse 16 is the same root with a prefix. This verse says it twice in one line, and the English follows the chain of verse 14 back: faith is from what is heard, and what is heard is through a word. The apparatus has a variant at the last word. The SBLGNT, with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition, has Christou, “of Christ”; the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont has theou, “of God.” KJV “the word of God” and Geneva “the word of God” have it; ASV “the word of Christ” and Douay “the word of Christ” keep the genitive; NIV “the word about Christ” and NWT 1984 “the word about Christ” decide it as an objective genitive; and the TLB “the Good News about Christ” does too. The Greek has no article before rhēmatos and no preposition, so “of Christ” can be a word that Christ speaks, a word about him, or a word that is his to give; the page prints the genitive and leaves the three readings here. The two nearest sentences in the Gospels are about the second and the first: John 17:20’s “through their word,” and the sender of Luke 10:16. The noun rhēma is the one of verse 8, “the word of faith,” and of verse 18, “their words”; the page prints “word” for it and for logos (9:6), and the note at verse 8 says why.

Verse 18 · Ἀλλὰ λέγω, μὴ οὐκ ἤκουσαν; μενοῦνγε· Εἰς πᾶσαν τὴν γῆν ἐξῆλθεν ὁ φθόγγος αὐτῶν, καὶ εἰς τὰ πέρατα τῆς οἰκουμένης τὰ ῥήματα αὐτῶν Alla legō, mē ouk ēkousan? menounge: Eis pasan tēn gēn exēlthen ho phthongos autōn, kai eis ta perata tēs oikoumenēs ta rhēmata autōn — Romans 10:18: “Into all the earth their sound went out.” An objector’s question and a psalm about the heavens, a “line” that became a “sound,” and a pronoun with no one in front of it

“But I say, they did not fail to hear, did they? On the contrary” (alla legō, mē ouk ēkousan? menounge, Romans 10:18). The question begins with mē, which expects the answer “no,” and carries ouk inside, so that the objector’s wording is “surely they did not not hear?” The English “they did not fail to hear, did they?” keeps both. The same turn of speech opens four questions in this letter: legō with mē at 10:18 and 10:19, and at 11:1 and 11:11, Legō oun, mē apōsato ho theos ton laon autou? and Legō oun, mē eptaisan hina pesōsin? (legō mē, searched; Greek checked). The answer word menounge stands in three verses of the New Testament (menounge, searched): Romans 9:20, this verse and Philippians 3:8 (not yet on these pages); the shorter menoun is Luke 11:28. It sets aside the question and puts something else in its place, and this page prints “on the contrary,” as at 9:20. The shelf: KJV “Have they not heard? Yes verily”, ASV “Did they not hear? Yea, verily”, Douay “Have they not heard? Yes, verily” and NIV “Did they not hear? Of course they did” answer “yes”; Geneva “Have they not heard? No doubt”; NWT 1984 “They did not fail to hear, did they? Why, in fact”; and the TLB “But what about the Jews? Have they heard God’s Word?” names the people in the question.

“Into all the earth their sound went out, and to the ends of the inhabited world their words” (Eis pasan tēn gēn exēlthen ho phthongos autōn, kai eis ta perata tēs oikoumenēs ta rhēmata autōn). The quotation is Psalm 19:5 in the Hebrew numbering (19:4 in English Bibles; 18:5 in the Greek Psalter; Psalm 19 is not yet on these pages). The Hebrew is bekhol ha-arets yatsa qavvam, uvikhtseh tevel millehem, “in all the earth their line went out, and to the end of the world their words.” Where the Hebrew has qav, “line, cord,” the Greek Psalter has phthongos, “sound” (checked), and Paul follows the Greek. Phthongos (dictionary) stands in two verses of the New Testament (phthongos, searched): this one and 1 Corinthians 14:7 (not yet on these pages), the notes of a pipe or a lyre, in the very verse that holds the diastolē of verse 12. Why the Greek translators wrote “sound” where the Masoretic text has “line” is not something the text says; that a stretched string gives a note is a suggestion commonly made (reported, not verified). Oikoumenē (dictionary), the inhabited world, is the word of Luke 2:1 (“the whole inhabited world”) and stands in Romans only here; the Hebrew has tevel. The pronoun “their” has no antecedent in Paul’s sentence. In the psalm it is the heavens: the Hebrew begins “The heavens declare the glory of God” (19:2) and the verse before the one Paul quotes says, in the Greek, ouk eisin lalioi oude logoi, hōn ouchi akouontai hai phōnai autōn, “there are no speeches nor words whose voices are not heard” (checked), with the verb of this chapter. Paul does not say whose line it is, and the pronoun can be heard as the preachers of verse 15 or, as in the psalm, as the creation. The shelf: KJV “their sound went into all the earth, and their words unto the ends of the world”; ASV “Their sound went out into all the earth, And their words unto the ends of the world”; Geneva “their sound went out through all the earth, and their words into the ends of the world”; Douay “their sound hath gone forth into all the earth, and their words unto the ends of the whole world”; NIV “Their voice has gone out into all the earth, their words to the ends of the world”; NWT 1984 “into all the earth their sound went out, and to the extremities of the inhabited earth their utterances”; and the TLB “the Good News has been told to the ends of the earth” gives the pronoun its antecedent and drops the sound.

Verses 19–21 · μὴ Ἰσραὴλ οὐκ ἔγνω; πρῶτος Μωϋσῆς λέγει· Ἐγὼ παραζηλώσω ὑμᾶς ἐπʼ οὐκ ἔθνει … Ἠσαΐας δὲ ἀποτολμᾷ … πρὸς δὲ τὸν Ἰσραὴλ λέγει· Ὅλην τὴν ἡμέραν ἐξεπέτασα τὰς χεῖράς μου πρὸς λαὸν ἀπειθοῦντα καὶ ἀντιλέγοντα mē Israēl ouk egnō? prōtos Mōysēs legei: Egō parazēlōsō hymas ep’ ouk ethnei … Ēsaias de apotolma … pros de ton Israēl legei: Holēn tēn hēmeran exepetasa tas cheiras mou pros laon apeithounta kai antilegonta — Romans 10:19–21: “All day long I spread out my hands to a disobedient and contradicting people.” Two witnesses and a closing sentence, a “no-nation,” a prophet who “dares,” and two participles for one Hebrew adjective

“Israel did not fail to know, did they?” (mē Israēl ouk egnō?, Romans 10:19). The second question of verse 18’s kind, and it goes back to the chapter’s opening: in verse 2 they lack epignōsis, in verse 3 they are agnoountes, “not knowing,” and here the question is whether Israel egnō, “knew.” The apparatus has a small variant on the order of the words: the SBLGNT has Israēl ouk egnō, with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition; the Byzantine text has ouk egnō Israēl. The shelf: KJV “Did not Israel know?”, ASV “Did Israel not know?”, Douay “Hath not Israel known?”, NWT 1984 “Israel did not fail to know, did they?”, NIV “Did Israel not understand?” and Geneva “Did not Israel know God?”, which adds the object. “First Moses says” (prōtos Mōysēs legei) makes Moses the first witness of two.

“I will make you jealous over what is not a nation” (egō parazēlōsō hymas ep’ ouk ethnei, Romans 10:19). The quotation is Deuteronomy 32:21, the Song of Moses, which this site prints “They roused me to jealousy with a no-god; they provoked me to anger with their vapors. So I will rouse them to jealousy with a no-people; with a foolish nation I will provoke them to anger.” Three things differ. In the Greek Old Testament the line reads kagō parazēlōsō autous ep’ ouk ethnei, epi ethnei asynetō parorgiō autous (checked), and Paul has egō for kagō and hymas, “you,” for autous, “them”: the Song’s third person has become the second, and the readers are addressed. The Hebrew of the first clause is belo-am, “with a no-people,” and the Greek has ouk ethnei, “a not-nation”: the Greek says “nation” in both clauses where the Hebrew says “people” in the first and “nation” in the second. And the Hebrew naval, “foolish,” is the Greek asynetos (dictionary), “uncomprehending,” an adjective that stands in five verses of the New Testament (asynet-, searched): Matthew 15:16 and Mark 7:18 (disciples), Romans 1:21 (“their uncomprehending heart was darkened”), 1:31 (“without understanding”) and here. The verb parazēloō (dictionary), “make jealous,” stands in four verses of the New Testament (parazēl-, searched): here, Romans 11:11 (eis to parazēlōsai autous, “to make them jealous”), 11:14 and 1 Corinthians 10:22 (the last not yet on these pages). The shelf: KJV “by them that are no people, and by a foolish nation” follows the Hebrew and not Paul’s Greek; ASV “with that which is no nation”, Douay “by that which is not a nation”, NIV “by those who are not a nation” and NWT 1984 “through that which is not a nation” follow the Greek; Geneva “by a nation that is not my nation” adds “my”; the TLB “the foolish heathen nations” has one noun for both clauses; and for the second adjective NWT 1984 “a stupid nation” (the 2013 revision, “a foolish nation”), KJV “a foolish nation”, ASV “a nation void of understanding” and NIV “a nation that has no understanding.”

“Isaiah dares to say: I was found by those who were not seeking me” (Ēsaias de apotolma kai legei: Heurethēn en tois eme mē zētousin, Romans 10:20). Apotolmaō (dictionary), “dare, be bold to the point of daring,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (apotolm-, searched). The quotation is Isaiah 65:1, and Paul reverses its two lines. The Hebrew is nidreshti lelo shaalu, nimtseti lelo viqshuni, “I was inquired of by those who did not ask, I was found by those who did not seek me” (Isaiah 65 is not yet on these pages); the Greek Old Testament has emphanēs egenēthēn tois eme mē eperōtōsin, heurethēn tois eme mē zētousin, “I became manifest to those not inquiring after me, I was found by those not seeking me” (checked). Paul writes the “found” line first and the “manifest” line second. Two small apparatus notes are in this verse: Tregelles, the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and the SBLGNT have en before tois eme mē zētousin, and Westcott–Hort and Robinson–Pierpont do not; and Tregelles alone has a second en after egenomēn. The line that follows in Isaiah 65:1 is the one about calling: the Greek says the nation hoi ouk ekalesan mou to onoma, “who did not call my name” (checked), and the Hebrew has the passive lo-qora vishmi, “not called by my name”; Paul does not quote it. The shelf: KJV “Esaias is very bold”, ASV “Isaiah is very bold”, NWT 1984 “Isaiah becomes very bold”, Geneva “Isaiah is bold”, Douay “Isaias is bold” and NIV “Isaiah boldly says.”

“To a disobedient and contradicting people” (pros laon apeithounta kai antilegonta, Romans 10:21). The quotation is Isaiah 65:2, and it closes the chapter on a posture and not on a verdict. The Hebrew is paras’ti yadai kol-hayom el-am sorer, “I have spread out my hands all the day to a rebellious people”: one adjective, sorer. The Greek has two participles for it, apeithounta kai antilegonta, and puts “all day long” (holēn tēn hēmeran) at the front, where the Greek Isaiah has it at the end of the clause (checked). Antilegō (dictionary), “speak against, contradict,” stands in nine verses of the New Testament (antileg-, searched): Luke 2:34, Luke 20:27, John 19:12, Acts 13:45, 28:19, 28:22, this verse and Titus 1:9 and 2:9. Three of those are Acts, and two are in its last chapter, spoken of the same people: “when the Jews spoke against it” (28:19) and “everywhere it is spoken against” (28:22); at 13:45 they are “contradicting the things being spoken by Paul” just before “we are turning to the nations” (13:46). Chapter 9 closed on Israel stumbling over a stone made from two prophets; chapter 10 closes on Israel in a sentence from Isaiah. The next chapter opens with a question the Greek asks in the same shape as verses 18 and 19, Legō oun, mē apōsato ho theos ton laon autou?, “I say, then, God has not pushed away his people, has he?” (Romans 11:1; Greek checked), and the answer is the letter’s own: mē genoito, “May it never be!” The shelf for the last words of this verse: KJV “a disobedient and gainsaying people”, ASV “a disobedient and gainsaying people” and Geneva “a disobedient, and gainsaying people” keep both words; Douay “a people that believeth not, and contradicteth me” changes the first; NWT 1984 “a people that is disobedient and talks back” changes the second; NIV “a disobedient and obstinate people” (as does the NWT 2013, “a disobedient and obstinate people”) turns the second into a temper; and the TLB “they keep arguing and refusing to come.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “God’s righteousness” for dikaiosynē theou (v. 3), as at 1:17 and 3:21–22; “accurate knowledge” for epignōsis (v. 2), as at 1:28; “subject themselves” for hypotassō (v. 3), as at 8:7 and 8:20; “petition” for deēsis (v. 1), as at Luke 1:13; “end” for telos (v. 4), as at 6:21–22 (1 Timothy 1:5 prints the same noun “goal”); “confess” for homologeō (vv. 9–10), as at John 1:20; “distinction” for diastolē (v. 12), as at 3:22; “call on” for epikaleō (vv. 12–14), as at Acts 2:21 and 9:14; “Jehovah” for kyrios in the quotation from Joel (v. 13), as at Acts 2:21 and in the Isaiah quotations of 9:28–29; “Lord” for the vocative the Greek Old Testament added to Isaiah 53:1 (v. 16), as at John 12:38; “the thing heard” for akoē (vv. 16–17), as at John 12:38; “put to shame” for kataischynō (v. 11), as at 5:5 and 9:33; “proclaim” for kēryssō (vv. 8, 14, 15), as at Mark 1:4 and Acts 28:31; “announce the good news” for euangelizomai (v. 15), as at 1:15; “on the contrary” for menounge (v. 18), as at 9:20; “inhabited world” for oikoumenē (v. 18), as at Luke 2:1; “uncomprehending” for asynetos (v. 19), as at 1:21; and “contradicting” for antilegō (v. 21), as at Acts 13:45. Four pairs are split on purpose: pisteuō is “believe on” where the Greek has epi (v. 11, as at 4:5, 4:24 and 9:33) and “believe in” where it has eis (v. 14); akouō is “hear” and hypakouō “obey” (v. 16); kēryssō is “proclaim” and euangelizomai “announce the good news” (v. 15); and rhēma and logos are both “word,” with the Greek in the note (v. 8, 9:6).

Echoes paid. God’s righteousness (1:17, 3:21–22) — v. 3. “Not knowing” (2:4) and accurate knowledge (1:28) — vv. 2–3 and 19. “We establish law” (3:31) — vv. 3–4. The mind that is not subjected (8:7) — v. 3. The end, as outcome (6:21–22) — v. 4. “Reckoned for righteousness” (4:3, 5) — vv. 4 and 10. The thesis words “for salvation,” “to everyone who believes,” “Jew first and also Greek” (1:16) — vv. 1, 4, 10 and 12. “The righteousness out of faith” (9:30) — v. 6. The stone-sentence (9:33), and “hope does not put to shame” (5:5) — v. 11. Believing on the one who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead (4:24) — v. 9. “Every mouth may be stopped” (3:19) — vv. 9–10. “There is no distinction” (3:22) and “Is God the God of Jews only?” (3:29–30) — v. 12. Riches (2:4, 9:23) — v. 12. Apostleship and the obedience of faith among all the nations (1:5) — vv. 15–16. “Obeyed from the heart” (6:17) — v. 16. Calling on the name (Acts 2:21, 9:14, 1 Corinthians 1:2) — vv. 12–14. “Uncomprehending” (1:21, 31) — v. 19. Israel’s stumbling over a stone and the nations that laid hold of righteousness (9:30–33) — vv. 19–21. The scripture that “says” (4:3, 9:17) — v. 11.

Echoes planted. “I say … they did not fail …?” (vv. 18–19) is asked twice more in Romans 11:1 and 11:11, and the first asks whether God has pushed away his people, with the letter’s answer mē genoito, “May it never be!” (Greek checked). “Make jealous” (v. 19) returns at Romans 11:11 and 11:14 and in 1 Corinthians 10:22 (not yet on these pages), and riches (v. 12) at Romans 11:12 and 11:33. The stone-sentence (v. 11) is put together again at 1 Peter 2:6. “The man who has done them will live by them” (v. 5) is Galatians 3:12 and, in the Hebrew, Ezekiel 20:11, 13 and 21; the line and the sound (v. 18) meet again in 1 Corinthians 14:7, with the diastolē of verse 12; and the answer word menounge is also Philippians 3:8 (these not yet on these pages). The Isaiah of verses 16, 20 and 21 is the Isaiah the last chapter of Acts quotes, from another passage, to the Jews of Rome (Acts 28:25–27).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether telos in verse 4 is an end, an aim or a completion; whether Moses’s sentence in verse 5 is about doing the righteousness or describing it, and so which of two Greek texts the page prints; whether Mē eipēs en tē kardia sou in verse 6 has Deuteronomy 9:4 behind it, and whether the glosses of verses 6–8 give the sense Moses had or the use Paul makes of his words; whether kyrion Iēsoun in verse 9 is “the Lord Jesus” or “Jesus as Lord”; whether ep’ autō in verse 11 is “on him” or “on it”; who the Lord is in verse 12, and how the Lord of Joel’s sentence stands to the Lord of verse 9; whether hou ouk ēkousan in verse 14 is hearing him or hearing of him; whether rhēmatos Christou in verse 17 is a word Christ speaks, a word about him, or a word that is his to give; who “their” is in verse 18, and why the Greek Psalter has a “sound” where the Hebrew has a “line”; and whether the glosses and the shortened quotations of the chapter are meant to give Moses’s sense or Paul’s use. The SBLGNT apparatus has twenty notes in this chapter, at verses 1 (two), 3, 5 (four), 9 (two), 14 (three), 15 (four), 17, 19 and 20 (two), and each is treated in the note for its verse.
