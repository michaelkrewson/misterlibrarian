“God is for us a refuge and strength”: a song of the sons of Korah in which the earth changes and the mountains totter, a river gladdens the city of God, Jehovah of armies is “with us”, wars are made to cease, and the nations are told “Let go, and know that I am God”.
Undated: the heading names the singers, not the occasion. The Geneva Bible's heading ties it to Jerusalem's deliverance from Sennacherib (701 BC); the psalm itself names no event.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–2 · “God is our refuge and strength” — why the verse numbers run one ahead, the sons of Korah, and Alamoth

One verse ahead. In the Hebrew the heading is verse 1, and the psalm proper begins at verse 2; English Bibles leave the heading unnumbered, so every verse here is one ahead of the English: “God is our refuge” is 46:1 in most English Bibles and 46:2 in the Hebrew, and “Be still, and know” is 46:10 there and 46:11 here. Psalm 51 (now on these pages) runs two ahead for the same reason. The Douay, following the Latin, numbers the heading as verse 1 too, and counts the psalm as Psalm 45: “Unto the end, for the sons of Core, for the hidden”.

The sons of Korah, on Alamoth. “For the director. Of the sons of Korah. On Alamoth. A song”: the sons of Korah were a family of temple singers, descended from the Korah who rebelled against Moses and was swallowed by the earth (Numbers 16:32); “the sons of Korah did not die” (Numbers 26:11, both now on these pages), and eleven psalms bear their name. Al alamot is a musical direction of unknown meaning, found again in 1 Chronicles 15:20 for harps; alamot is the plural of almah, a young woman (Isaiah 7:14, now on these pages), and many take it to mean high voices. The Greek read the same letters as “hidden things”, as in “the secrets of the heart” (Psalm 44:22), and so the Douay has “for the hidden”. The ASV has “For the Chief Musician. A Psalm of the sons of Korah; set to Alamoth. A Song”; the NWT 1984 “Of the sons of Korah upon The Maidens”; the KJV “A Song upon Alamoth”.

A refuge and strength. “God is for us a refuge and strength, a help in troubles, readily found”: machseh, the place one runs into, the word of “my refuge and my stronghold” (Psalm 91:2, now on these pages), and oz, strength. Nimtsa me’od, “found very much”, is a help that is there when looked for; the phrase stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Martin Luther built his hymn Ein feste Burg ist unser Gott, “A mighty fortress is our God”, on this psalm in the late 1520s, and the page shown here, from a Magdeburg hymnbook of 1545, prints it under the heading “The 46th Psalm, Deus noster refugium et virtus”. The ASV has “God is our refuge and strength, A very present help in trouble”; the NWT 1984 “A help that is readily to be found during distresses”; the NIV “an ever-present help in trouble”.

Verses 3–4 · “Therefore we will not fear” — the mountains totter, the waters roar, and Selah

Though the mountains totter. “Therefore we will not fear, though the earth changes, and though the mountains totter into the heart of the seas”: mot, to totter, is the verb the psalm keeps turning over. Here the mountains totter; in verse 6 the city of God “will not totter”; in verse 7 the kingdoms tottered. The translation keeps one word for it all three times. “The heart of the seas” is the place of the Song at the Sea, “the deeps congealed in the heart of the sea” (Exodus 15:8, now on these pages). The NWT 1984 has “though the earth undergo change And though the mountains totter into the heart of the vast sea”; the ASV “though the earth do change”; the KJV “though the earth be removed”.

The waters roar. “Though its waters roar and foam, though the mountains quake at its swelling”: yehemu, roar, is the verb of verse 7 too, “nations roared”: the sea and the nations make the same noise. Ga’avah, “swelling”, is also the word for pride. The ASV has “Though the waters thereof roar and be troubled, Though the mountains tremble with the swelling thereof”; the NWT 1984 “Though its waters be boisterous, foam over”; the NIV “though its waters roar and foam”.

Selah. The verse ends with selah, a word whose meaning is not known. It stands in seventy-four verses of the Hebrew Bible, seventy-one in the Psalms and three in the prayer of Habakkuk; the Greek renders it diapsalma, an interlude, and it is usually taken as a direction to the musicians, a pause or a lifting of the voice. It comes three times in this psalm, after verses 4, 8 and 12. The refrain of verses 8 and 12 does not stand after verse 4, where the first Selah does, and many readers have suspected it once did; the Hebrew does not have it there. The NWT 1984 prints “Selah”; the Douay, from the Latin, leaves it out.

Verses 5–6 · “There is a river” — the city of God, the dwelling of the Most High, and help toward morning

A river. “A river — its streams make glad the city of God”: Jerusalem has no river, only the spring of Gihon. The river is the one that “flows out of Eden to water the garden” (Genesis 2:10), seen again in Revelation as “a river of the water of life … coming out from the throne of God” (Revelation 22:1, both now on these pages). Pelagav, “its streams”, are channels cut for water; the form stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. “The holiest of the dwellings of the Most High”: the Greek read a verb, “the Most High has sanctified his dwelling”, and the Douay follows: “the most High hath sanctified his own tabernacle”. The ASV has “There is a river, the streams whereof make glad the city of God”; the NWT 1984 “The holiest grand tabernacle of the Most High”.

Toward morning. “God is in her midst; she will not totter. God will help her toward morning”: lifnot boqer, “at the turning of the morning”, and the phrase stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at the sea, when “toward morning the sea returned to its steady flow” (Exodus 14:27, now on these pages). The Geneva Bible’s heading reads the psalm as “A song of triumph or thanksgiving for the deliverance of Jerusalem, after Sennacherib with his army was driven away”, whose army was found dead in the morning (2 Kings 19:35); the psalm itself names no occasion. The ASV has “God will help her, and that right early”; the NWT 1984 “God will help it at the appearance of morning”; the KJV “God shall help her, and that right early”.

Verses 7–8 · “Jehovah of armies is with us” — the nations roar, the earth melts, and the God of Jacob a secure height

He gave out his voice. “Nations roared, kingdoms tottered; he gave out his voice, the earth melts”: the roaring of verse 4 and the tottering of verse 3 now belong to the nations, and a single sound from God undoes them. Tamug, “melts”, is from mug, the verb of hearts melting in fear; “the earth melts” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The ASV has “The nations raged, the kingdoms were moved: He uttered his voice, the earth melted”; the NWT 1984 “The nations became boisterous, the kingdoms tottered”; the Douay “the earth trembled”.

With us. “Jehovah of armies is with us; a secure height for us is the God of Jacob”: immanu, “with us”, the word inside the name Immanuel, “God with us” (Isaiah 7:14; Matthew 1:23, both now on these pages). The refrain stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, verses 8 and 12 of this psalm. Misgav is a high place out of reach, a refuge by height rather than by walls. The NWT 1984 has “Jehovah of armies is with us; The God of Jacob is a secure height for us”; the ASV “Jehovah of hosts is with us; The God of Jacob is our refuge”; the Douay “the God of Jacob is our protector”.

Verses 9–10 · “He makes wars cease” — desolations on the earth, the bow broken, and the wagons burned

Desolations. “Come, see the works of Jehovah, who has set desolations on the earth”: shammot, ruins that leave onlookers appalled. The Greek and the Latin read a word for astonishment, “wonders”, and the Douay has “what wonders he hath done upon earth”. The desolation is of war itself, as the next verse shows. The ASV has “Come, behold the works of Jehovah, What desolations he hath made in the earth”; the NWT 1984 “How he has set astonishing events on the earth”; the NIV “the desolations he has brought on the earth”.

The bow, the spear, the wagons. “Making wars cease to the end of the earth; he breaks the bow and cuts the spear apart; he burns the wagons with fire”: agalot are carts and wagons, the word of the wagons Joseph sent for his father (Genesis 45:19) and of the new cart that carried the ark home (1 Samuel 6:7, both now on these pages). The Greek read a word one vowel different, “round shields”, and so the NIV has “he burns the shields with fire” and the Douay “the shield he shall burn in the fire”. The ASV has “He burneth the chariots in the fire”; the NWT 1984 “The wagons he burns in the fire”.

Verses 11–12 · “Be still, and know that I am God” — let go, exalted among the nations, and the refrain again

Let go. “Let go, and know that I am God”: harpu, from raphah, to go slack; in this form it is to loosen one’s grip on something. It is the verb of Jehovah’s “Let go of me, that I may destroy them” to Moses (Deuteronomy 9:14) and of Samuel’s “Stop” to Saul (1 Samuel 15:16, both now on these pages). Spoken here after the bow is broken and the wagons burned, it is addressed first to the warring nations: drop your weapons, and know who is God. The older shelf’s “Be still” has become one of the best-loved lines of devotion; it is true to the verb, which is about stopping, though the Hebrew is not about quiet. The Greek has scholasate, “be at leisure”. The ASV has “Be still, and know that I am God”; the NWT 1984 “Give in, YOU people, and know that I am God”; the TLB “Stand silent! Know that I am God!”.

Exalted among the nations. “I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted in the earth”: the nations that roared in verse 7 and the earth that melted end by honoring the one who stopped their wars. The psalm closes on its refrain, “Jehovah of armies is with us”, and on the third Selah. The ASV has “I will be exalted among the nations, I will be exalted in the earth”; the KJV “I will be exalted among the heathen”; the NWT 1984 “I will be exalted among the nations”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: the Hebrew verse numbering, with the heading as verse 1, as in Psalm 51; “refuge” for machseh, as in Psalm 91:2; “totter” for mot in verses 3, 6 and 7 alike; “roar” for hamah in verses 4 and 7; “toward morning”, as in Exodus 14:27; “with us” for immanu; “a secure height” for misgav; “wagons” for agalot; and “let go” for harpu, as in Deuteronomy 9:14.

Echoes paid. The refuge of Psalm 91; the heart of the sea of Exodus 15 and the morning of Exodus 14; the river of Eden; Immanuel of Isaiah 7; the sons of Korah who did not die (Numbers 26); and the “let go” of Deuteronomy 9.

Echoes planted. The city of God and its towers (Psalm 48); the other psalms of the sons of Korah (Psalms 42, 44–49, 84–85 and 87–88); and the river from the temple (Ezekiel 47). None is translated here yet.
