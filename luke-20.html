🕰
Still teaching daily in the temple, Jesus is challenged by the chief priests, scribes and elders to name the authority behind his actions, and answers their hostile question with a counter-question about John the Baptist's own baptism that leaves them unable to answer either way. He tells the crowd the parable of the vineyard's tenant farmers, who beat and kill a series of servants and finally the owner's own son — a story the leaders recognize is aimed at them, though they cannot yet act on their anger. Hired spies try to trap him over paying tribute to Caesar; Sadducees pose a riddle about a widow married in turn to seven brothers to argue against the resurrection; Jesus then asks his own unanswered riddle about the Christ as both David's son and David's Lord, before warning the crowd against scribes who love status and 'devour widows' houses.'
c. AD 30, the same temple week as chapter 19's clearing of the temple and chapter 21's Olivet discourse; all four confrontations here — authority, the vineyard, Caesar's tribute, and the resurrection — run in close parallel with Matthew 21:23-22:33 and Mark 11:27-12:27, though Luke omits the 'greatest commandment' exchange found in both (having already told that exchange, differently framed, at 10:25-28), and the vineyard parable itself is the one Synoptic pericope shared by all three Gospels in this sequence.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–8 · ἐν ποίᾳ ἐξουσίᾳ … τὸ βάπτισμα Ἰωάννου en poia exousia … to baptisma Iōannou — a question about authority answered with a question no one in the temple courts can safely answer

⭐⭐ The trap Jesus sets is built from the same two horns his questioners cannot escape either way. “From heaven” forces them to explain why they never believed John; “from men” risks a stoning from a crowd ‘convinced that John was a prophet.’ The narrator lets the reader watch the calculation happen in real time (v5–6) before the leaders ever speak, so their final answer — ‘we do not know’ — is heard for what it is: not ignorance, but a refusal to be pinned down in public. Matthew’s and Mark’s versions of this same exchange run the identical logic (Matthew 21:23–27; Mark 11:27–33, both already on these pages); all three Synoptics let the crowd’s own opinion of John do the trapping.

⚠ Jesus never actually refuses to answer — he makes the refusal to answer BE the answer. “Neither will I tell you” is itself a claim: that the authority behind John’s baptism and the authority behind what Jesus is doing in the temple stand or fall together, which is exactly the connection the leaders cannot afford to make out loud.

Verses 9–19 · ἀμπελῶνα … πρὸς τὸν λαόν … κεφαλὴν γωνίας ampelōna … pros ton laon … kephalēn gōnias — a parable spoken to the crowd about the leaders standing right there, and a rejected stone Luke leaves undisputed where Matthew hedges

⭐⭐⭐ Luke names a different audience for this parable than the leaders who are, on the story's own terms, the ones it is aimed at. “He began to tell the people this parable” (v9, pros ton laon) — not the chief priests and scribes who have just finished questioning him. Mark’s telling of the identical parable instead has Jesus speaking ‘to them’ (Mark 12:1, already on these pages), the same leaders named two verses earlier in Mark’s own sequence; Matthew’s introduction is likewise addressed to the same group as the authority question just before it (Matthew 21:33, already on these pages). Only Luke puts the crowd between Jesus and his targets, which is exactly why v19 has to spell out what the other two Gospels leave implicit: the leaders ‘knew that he had spoken this parable against them’ — a parable they overheard rather than one told to their faces.

⚠ The order of the son’s death and his expulsion from the vineyard is not the same in all three Synoptic tellings, and this translation does not smooth the disagreement away. Here, as in Matthew, the tenants ‘threw him out of the vineyard and killed him’ (v15) — expelled while still alive, killed outside (compare Matthew 21:39, already on these pages). Mark’s own version reverses the two actions: ‘they killed him, and threw him out of the vineyard’ (Mark 12:8, already on these pages) — death first, the corpse denied burial after. Luke and Matthew agree against Mark here; none of the three manuscript traditions try to make the other two match.

⭐⭐ The quoted stone-saying is undisputed here, where Matthew's own copy of the same image is not. Psalm 118:22–23's rejected stone becoming ‘the head of the corner’ (v17) stands in every witness to this chapter without question. Matthew's own parallel adds a second stone-verse immediately after — falling on the stone or having it fall on you (Matthew 21:44, already on these pages) — and that ADDITIONAL verse is exactly the one a small group of early witnesses omit, with some editors judging it an import into Matthew from this very passage (v18). Luke's own copy of that second stone-image, here, carries no such doubt in the apparatus at all — whichever Gospel is the image's original home, Luke's text of it is the more secure of the two.

⚠ Verse 19's own middle clause answers a question the story doesn't ask out loud: after a parable and a scripture that both indict them by name, why don't the leaders arrest him on the spot? Not repentance, but a practical fear — ‘they feared the people’ — the same calculation that stalled them at the start of this very exchange (v6).

⚠ The shelf varies on v18's closing verb without disagreeing about its violence: the KJV reads the stone will ‘grind him to powder,’ the ASV ‘scatter him as dust,’ and the NWT 1984 ‘pulverize him’ — three renderings of one verb, each reaching for a different English image of the same total destruction.

Verses 20–26 · ἐγκαθέτους … φόρον … τὰ Καίσαρος Καίσαρι egkathetous … phoron … ta Kaisaros Kaisari — hired men found nowhere else in the New Testament, a plainer word for tax than Matthew's own, and an answer that gives away nothing about how much belongs to Caesar

⭐⭐⭐ “Spies” translates a word that occurs exactly once in the whole New Testament. See the egkathetos dictionary entry: a hapax legomenon built from a verb meaning to plant or set down in ambush, describing men deliberately ‘seated in’ the crowd to look like honest questioners while working for the prosecution. Matthew and Mark's parallel accounts of this same trap skip straight to the disciples of the Pharisees and the Herodians (Matthew 22:16; Mark 12:13, already on these pages) without ever naming the deception itself; only Luke's narrator tells the reader up front that the questioners' own piety is theater — ‘pretending to be righteous’ — before a word of their flattering opening line is spoken.

⭐⭐ Luke's own word for the tax at stake is plainer, and less Roman, than Matthew's. Luke's Greek asks about phoros, the ordinary word for tribute paid by a subject people to a ruling power; Matthew's parallel instead asks about the kēnsos (Matthew 22:17, already on these pages), a direct loanword from the Latin census, naming the specific, hated Roman poll-tax registration by name. Mark keeps Matthew's own loanword (Mark 12:14, already on these pages). Luke, writing for a wider Greek-speaking readership less fluent in Roman bureaucratic vocabulary, reaches for the plain word instead of the technical one — the same tax, three Gospels, two different registers of Greek for naming it.

⚠ The answer specifies nothing about how much belongs to each side, and this translation leaves that silence exactly where the Greek leaves it. See the apodidomi dictionary entry on the prefix's own logic: the coin already carries Caesar's image, so handing it back returns his own property to him. What is NOT settled is the size of Caesar's own share against God's — read as two separate spheres, or as the claim that since everything ultimately bears God's image Caesar's own portion is the small change, the saying supports either reading and this translation reports both rather than choosing.

⚠ A later tradition of manuscripts imports Matthew's own words for the same moment into Luke's account, and this translation's source text does not have the import. After ‘he perceived their craftiness’ (v23), a widely-copied later text adds ‘Why are you testing me?’ — the identical question, minus ‘hypocrites,’ that Matthew's Jesus asks at this same moment natively, in Matthew's own words (Matthew 22:18, already on these pages). The earlier text this translation follows has Jesus answer the trick with the coin alone, no verbal rebuke first — a harder, more compressed reply than the Matthew-flavored one later copyists supplied.

Verses 27–40 · ἀντιλέγοντες ἀνάστασιν μὴ εἶναι … ἰσάγγελοι … κύριον τὸν θεὸν Ἀβραάμ antilegontes anastasin mē einai … isangeloi … kyrion ton theon Abraam — a word for the Sadducees’ own unbelief that the received text and the newest critical edition share against the older critical editions, a New Testament hapax for what the resurrected are LIKE, and a proof from the burning bush Luke tells differently from Matthew and Mark

⚠ The verb describing the Sadducees' own position on the resurrection is itself disputed among the editions, and this translation follows the harder, more pointed reading. The apparatus splits: this chapter's own critical text and the Byzantine tradition alike read antilegontes, actively ‘speaking against’ or denying the resurrection, while an older-generation critical edition (Westcott-Hort, followed by Tregelles) prints the plainer legontes, simply ‘saying’ there is none. This translation follows the reading its own source text prints. The Sadducees' rejection of the resurrection is set out in the encyclopedia's own Sadducees entry and independently reported by Josephus (Antiquities 18.16; War 2.165), so the stronger verb is not inventing a controversy — it is naming one this group is elsewhere known to have actively argued.

⭐⭐⭐ “Like the angels” translates a genuine New Testament hapax — a word coined, as far as the surviving evidence shows, for exactly this sentence. See the isangelos dictionary entry: isos (equal) fused to angelos (messenger, angel) into a single compound that appears nowhere else in the whole New Testament and in no clearly earlier Greek text at all. Matthew's and Mark's own versions of this same saying use an ordinary comparison instead — ‘like [hōs] the angels of God in heaven’ (Matthew 22:30; Mark 12:25, already on these pages) — two common words doing the same work Luke compresses into one that, on the evidence we have, did not exist as a word before this sentence needed it.

⭐⭐⭐ Luke's own proof from the burning bush never lets God speak in the first person — Matthew's and Mark's both do. Matthew and Mark both quote God's own words to Moses directly: ‘I am the God of Abraham…’ (Matthew 22:32; Mark 12:26, both already on these pages). Luke's Greek instead stays in the narrator's own voice throughout: Moses, at the bush, ‘calls [kyrios] the God of Abraham, and the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob’ — a report about a title used, not a quotation of the divine voice itself. Since the referent here is unmistakably the God Moses met at the bush rather than Jesus, this translation reads the bare kyrios as standing in for the divine name and prints ‘Jehovah,’ the same choice made wherever this translation's own kyrios entry finds the word doing that Old-Testament-formula work rather than naming Jesus. ⚠ The KJV and ASV both keep the generic ‘the Lord’ here rather than a proper name, since neither shelf version prints the divine name inside New Testament narration at all.

⚠ “The sons of this age” keeps a word this translation has already flagged apart from its near-synonym. See the aion dictionary entry: Luke's Greek is tou aiōnos toutou, this AGE, an epoch with a character, not tou kosmou toutou, this physical world (see the kosmos entry) — the same distinction the NWT 1984's own ‘this system of things’ is reaching for at this verse, even where the more familiar ‘this world’ (KJV, ASV) blurs it back into the ordinary physical sense.

Verses 41–44 · Δαυὶδ υἱόν … ὑποπόδιον τῶν ποδῶν σου Dauid huion … hypopodion tōn podōn sou — a riddle Luke leaves unresolved exactly as Mark does, and a footstool that stays a footstool where Matthew and Mark leave it out

⭐⭐ Luke never returns to the ‘greatest commandment’ question that immediately precedes this same riddle in both Matthew and Mark — because Luke already told that exchange, in a different scene, ten chapters earlier. Matthew and Mark both place a lawyer's question about the greatest commandment directly before the Son-of-David riddle in this exact sequence of temple confrontations (Matthew 22:34–40; Mark 12:28–34, both already on these pages). Luke has no such episode here at all — his own version of a lawyer testing Jesus with the very same two commandments, love of God and love of neighbor, already happened on the road, framing the parable of the good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–28, already on these pages). Rather than tell the same exchange twice, Luke moves straight from the resurrection question to the riddle about David's son — four confrontations in this Gospel's version of the cycle where Matthew and Mark both have five.

⭐⭐⭐ Luke keeps the Psalm's own noun where Matthew and Mark both paraphrase it away — and three checked shelf versions agree with Luke, not with them. The Hebrew of Psalm 110:1 and its Greek translation both read hypopodion, a FOOTSTOOL, for what the enemies become; Matthew's and Mark's identical wording of this same quotation instead reads ‘underneath your feet’ (Matthew 22:44; Mark 12:36, both already on these pages) — the same idea, a lost noun. The KJV, ASV and NWT 1984 all keep ‘footstool’ at this verse, so Luke's own copy of the citation, on this one word, sides with those three against this site's own translation of its two Synoptic parallels.

⚠ As at Mark 12:35–37 and Matthew 22:44 (both already on these pages), the one Hebrew line behind this quotation names two different words for ‘lord’ — YHVH and adoni — that Greek collapses into a single repeated kyrios. This translation keeps the choice already made at both of those parallels: ‘Jehovah said to my Lord,’ since the first occurrence quotes the Hebrew Bible's own divine name and the second names David's own master, a distinction the Hebrew keeps and the Greek here cannot show on its own. ⭐ The KJV's own typography agrees without meaning to: it prints ‘The LORD said unto my Lord’, small capitals for the first and ordinary type for the second — the same distinction this translation draws with two different words, drawn there with two different cases of the same one.

⚠ The riddle itself is left exactly as open here as in Mark's own version: how the Christ can be both David's son by descent and David's own Lord by address is asked and never answered on the page, in any of the three Synoptic accounts. The crowd's own reaction — delighted, per Mark; the leaders' own silence, per Matthew and this Gospel alike — is the only recorded response the riddle gets.

Verses 45–47 · κατεσθίουσιν τὰς οἰκίας τῶν χηρῶν katesthiousin tas oikias tōn chērōn — the harshest charge this Gospel brings against the scribal class, spoken to the disciples in the hearing of the very crowd that has just kept Jesus alive

⭐⭐⭐ The charge that closes this chapter is the identical accusation Mark's Jesus levels against the same class of men, word for word. ‘They devour widows' houses, and for a pretense make long prayers’ matches Mark's own version of this warning almost exactly (Mark 12:40, already on these pages) — the harshest concrete charge either Gospel brings against the scribes, worse than the love of robes and greetings and the chief seats named just before it, because it names a victim rather than a vanity.

⚠ Some later manuscripts add a nearly identical sentence to Matthew's own woes against the scribes, and the earliest witnesses do not have it there. The words some editions print as Matthew 23:14, condemning those who ‘devour widows' houses,’ are missing from the earliest Matthean manuscripts and do not sit still in the ones that do carry it — some traditions place it before the surrounding material, some after, the mark of a line imported from elsewhere rather than original to that page. Its most secure home in the Synoptic record is here, and at Mark 12:40 (both already on these pages), where the sentence is undisputed in the manuscripts.

⚠ Luke gives this whole warning a narrower, quieter audience than Matthew does. Matthew's parallel is a public chapter-length denunciation, addressed to ‘the crowds and his disciples’ alike (Matthew 23:1, already on these pages); here it is spoken ‘to the disciples,’ even though ‘all the people’ are said to be listening in (v45) — a private word overheard by the public, rather than a public speech.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: Jehovah for the bare, OT-formula kyrios of v37 (Moses at the burning bush), kept apart from the plain Lord of vv42–44's own riddle, where the Hebrew's two different words for ‘lord’ have already collapsed into one Greek word before the translation ever sees it; tribute for Luke's own plain phoros (v22), kept distinct from Matthew and Mark's Latin loanword kēnsos, the same Roman tax named in two different registers of Greek; and footstool for hypopodion (v43), kept where Matthew and Mark both paraphrase the Psalm's own noun away.

Echoes paid. Asked by what authority he is acting, Jesus answers a hostile question with a counter-question about John's own baptism — exactly the echo Luke 19's own closing note planted (19:47–48, already on these pages). And the parable of the vineyard tenants that follows immediately is the story that chapter's last verse had already announced was coming, against the very leaders that verse named.

Echoes planted. The charge that the scribes ‘devour widows' houses’ (v47) is answered two verses into the next chapter by a single poor widow giving everything she has at the temple treasury (21:1–4, already on these pages) — what the scribal class takes and what she gives, placed back to back exactly as Mark places them. And the temple itself, praised by admiring onlookers for its stonework in the very next scene, is told it will not have one stone left on another (21:5–6, already on these pages) — the same temple these four confrontations have all just played out inside.

Next in sequence: Luke 21 — admirers of the temple's stonework are told not one stone will be left on another; Jesus foretells wars, earthquakes, persecution and the destruction of Jerusalem, and describes his own return in glory; and a poor widow's two small coins outweigh, in his own verdict, every large gift dropped into the treasury beside them.
