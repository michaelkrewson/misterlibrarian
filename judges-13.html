Israel again does evil and is given into the hand of the Philistines for forty years, and this time no one cries out. The angel of Jehovah appears to the barren wife of Manoah of Zorah, a Danite, and promises a son who will be a Nazirite of God from the womb and will begin to save Israel. Manoah prays for the visitor to return; the angel comes again to the woman, will not eat, will not give his name ("It is wonderful"), and goes up in the flame of the offering on the rock. Samson is born, Jehovah blesses him, and the spirit begins to stir him in Mahaneh-dan, between Zorah and Eshtaol.
Within the period of the judges, with no date the chapter gives for itself. The Philistine forty years (13:1) are the longest oppression the book gives, and Samson only begins the deliverance (13:5). The early/late question applies (c. 1200 BC on the early reckoning this project foregrounds, c. 1100 BC on the late).

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verse 1 · Forty years under the Philistines — and, this time, no cry

The old formula, with a piece missing. “The children of Israel again did what was evil in the eyes of Jehovah” is the book’s refrain (Judges 3:12, 4:1, 10:6, all now on these pages), and the Hebrew here is word for word the line that opened the Ammonite oppression at 10:6. Then the pattern breaks. Every earlier oppression brought Israel to cry out to Jehovah (3:9, 3:15, 4:3, 6:6, 10:10, all now on these pages). Here no one cries, and the deliverer is announced anyway, to a woman who did not ask.

Forty years. It is the longest oppression the book gives. The TLB adds a reason the Hebrew does not give, “by worshiping other gods”; the KJV keeps the plain “did evil again”.

Verses 2–3 · Manoah’s barren wife — a certain man of Zorah, and the angel who comes to her

“There was a certain man.” The opening words, vayehi ish echad, stand in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and the start of Samuel’s story, “a certain man from Ramathaim-zophim” (1 Samuel 1:1, now on these pages). Both open on a man and a wife who cannot have children, and both sons will be given to God before birth. Manoah’s name is built on nuach, “rest”; his town, Zorah, is on Dan’s border (Joshua 19:41, now on these pages).

Barren. Akarah is the word for Sarai (Genesis 11:30), Rebekah (Genesis 25:21) and Rachel (Genesis 29:31), all now on these pages. Those three are named; this woman never is. The chapter calls her “the woman” and “his wife” throughout, and she is the one the angel comes to, twice.

“Look — you are barren … but you will conceive.” The angel’s first words state the problem and then reverse it. The NIV has “You are barren and childless”, the ASV “thou art barren, and bearest not”, and the NWT 1984 “you are barren and have borne no child”.

Verses 4–5 · Nazirite of God from the womb — no wine, no razor, and “he will begin to save”

“You will be pregnant and bear a son.” The words harah ve-yoladt ben stand in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: the angel’s word to Hagar (Genesis 16:11), the sign of Isaiah 7:14 (both now on these pages), and verses 5 and 7 here. Harah is an adjective, “pregnant”, and the phrase carries no tense of its own; Hagar is already with child, and this woman, told in verse 3 that she “will conceive”, is not yet. The translation reads it as future here.

A Nazirite before birth. The rules are those of Numbers 6:3–5 (now on these pages): no wine or strong drink, nothing from the grapevine, no razor. But there the vow is made by an adult, for a set time; here the boy is nezir Elohim, “a Nazirite of God”, from the womb, and his mother keeps the food rules for him while she carries him. “No razor shall touch his head” is literally “no razor shall go up on his head”, and the sentence stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and in Hannah’s vow for Samuel (1 Samuel 1:11, now on these pages), which the translation renders the same way. The NWT 1984 reads “on leaving the belly”, the Douay “from his infancy, and from his mother’s womb”.

“He will begin to save.” Only begin. The phrase yachel lehoshia stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the verb comes back at the chapter’s end, when the spirit “began” to stir him (v. 25). The NIV has “He will take the lead in delivering Israel”; the KJV keeps “he shall begin to deliver”.

Verses 6–7 · “A man of God … very awe-inspiring” — and what the mother adds: “to the day of his death”

A man who looks like an angel. She calls him “a man of God” whose appearance was “like the appearance of the angel of God, very awe-inspiring”. The words nora me’od stand in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and the day of Jehovah in Joel 2:11. The KJV has “very terrible” and the NWT 1984 “very fear-inspiring”.

“I did not ask him where he was from.” The A Greek text has the opposite, ērōtōn pothen estin: she asked where he was from. The Douay, from the Latin, has her ask everything: “when I asked him who he was, and whence he came, and by what name he was called, he would not tell me”. The Hebrew has her not ask, and him not tell.

“To the day of his death.” She repeats the angel’s words to her husband with two things left out, the razor and “he will begin to save Israel”, and three Hebrew words added that the angel never said: ad yom moto, “to the day of his death”. The angel spoke of the boy’s beginning; his mother is the first to speak of his end. The ASV has “from the womb to the day of his death”.

Verses 8–14 · Manoah’s prayer, “Please, Lord”, and the question the angel does not answer

“Manoah entreated Jehovah.” Vaye’tar is the verb of Isaac, who “entreated Jehovah on behalf of his wife, because she was barren” (Genesis 25:21, now on these pages). Isaac prayed for a child; Manoah, already promised one, prays for instructions.

“Please, Lord.” Bi Adonai is what Gideon said to the angel, “Please, my lord” (Judges 6:15, now on these pages); here it is addressed to Jehovah, so it is “Lord”. The NWT 1984 prints “Excuse me, Jehovah”, putting the name where the Hebrew has Adonai; the ASV has “Oh, Lord”. The B Greek text keeps the Hebrew word itself, kyrie Adōnaie.

The answer goes to her again. God listens to Manoah, and the angel of God comes back — to the woman, “as she sat in the field; but Manoah her husband was not with her” (v. 9). She runs to fetch him. His question in verse 12 is mah yihyeh mishpat ha-na’ar u-ma’asehu, “what will be the boy’s rule, and his work?” Mishpat is the word of judging and of judges, a strange first question to ask about the next one. The KJV has “How shall we order the child”, the NWT 1984 “the child’s mode of life and his work”. The angel answers only about the mother, repeating the rules he gave her and adding one, nothing that comes from the grapevine (vv. 13–14). The phrase “from the grapevine” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, verse 14 and the Nazirite law at Numbers 6:4 (now on these pages).

Verses 15–18 · “Why is it that you ask my name? It is wonderful” — pil’i, and Jacob at the Jabbok

A meal refused. Manoah offers what Gideon offered, a young goat (Judges 6:19, now on these pages). The angel will not eat, and turns the meal into a burnt offering “to Jehovah”; the narrator explains, “For Manoah did not know that he was the angel of Jehovah” (v. 16).

The name. Manoah asks it so that he can honour him when the words come true. The answer, lamah zeh tish’al lishmi, “Why is it that you ask my name?”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and the night at the Jabbok, when Jacob asked the one who wrestled him (Genesis 32:30, now on these pages; 32:29 in many English Bibles). The translation gives both the same words. Neither questioner gets a name.

“It is wonderful.” Pil’i is from the root of wonder, pala, the word of “such knowledge is too wonderful for me” (Psalm 139:6, now on these pages). The shelf splits three ways: the ASV and the Douay have “wonderful”; the KJV and the Geneva “secret”; the NIV “beyond understanding”. Both Greek texts have thaumaston, “wonderful”. The next verse plays on it.

Verses 19–23 · The flame on the rock — “he did a wonder”, “we will surely die”, and the wife who reasons

“He did a wonder.” U-mafli la’asot is the root of pil’i again, as a verb: the one whose name is wonderful does something wonderful. The Hebrew does not say who. The KJV and ASV supply “the angel did wondrously”, the NIV “did an amazing thing” with God as the subject, the NWT 1984 “He was doing something in a wonderful way”; the Douay makes the wonder-worker the one the offering is for, “who doth wonderful things”, as the A Greek text does. The translation keeps the Hebrew’s “he”.

Everything goes up. The root alah, “go up”, runs through the scene: the burnt offering is the olah, the thing that goes up (v. 16); Manoah offers it up (v. 19); the flame goes up from the altar, and the angel goes up in the flame (v. 20). Gideon’s visitor also brought fire out of a rock and was gone (Judges 6:21, now on these pages).

“We will surely die, for we have seen God.” The doubled verb mot namut stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and 2 Samuel 14:14. Gideon was afraid in the same way: “I have seen the angel of Jehovah face to face” (Judges 6:22, now on these pages). Manoah’s wife answers with three reasons: Jehovah accepted the offering, showed them all this, and let them hear such things. A God who meant to kill them would not have done any of it. The NIV has Manoah say “We are doomed to die!”

Verses 24–25 · Samson — the name from shemesh, “sun”, and the spirit that began to stir him

Samson. Shimshon is built on shemesh, “sun”, with the ending -on that many Hebrew names carry; whether it makes “little sun” or “of the sun” is not settled, and the chapter gives no explanation. His country had the sun in its place names: Zorah and Eshtaol stand beside Ir-shemesh, “city of the sun”, at the head of Dan’s border (Joshua 19:41, now on these pages). Josephus, in the Greek of the Antiquities (5.285, numbered 5.8.4 in Whiston), says the name means “strong”, ischyron; the Hebrew letters do not say that. The name stands in thirty-seven verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in Judges 13–16.

“The spirit of Jehovah began to stir him.” Lefa’amo is from pa’am, a beat, a step, a time, and the verb is the one used of Pharaoh, whose spirit “was disturbed” by his dreams (Genesis 41:8, now on these pages). The KJV hears the “times” in it, “to move him at times”; the NWT 1984 has “to impel him”, the Douay “to be with him”. The place is Machaneh-Dan, “the camp of Dan”, the name of Dan’s camp in the wilderness order (Numbers 2:25, now on these pages).

Josephus’s Manoah. Josephus tells the visit as a story of jealousy: Manoah was “madly in love with his wife and therefore immoderately jealous”, the visitor appeared as “a beautiful, tall young man”, and when she praised his looks Manoah was beside himself with suspicion (Antiquities 5.277–279, numbered 5.8.2–3 in Whiston). None of that is in the chapter, where Manoah is slow to understand and never jealous.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “Look —” for hineh; “barren” for akarah, as for Sarai, Rebekah and Rachel; “a Nazirite of God” for nezir Elohim; “no razor shall touch his head”, the same as Hannah’s vow; “begin” for chalal in verses 5 and 25; “Please, Lord” for bi Adonai addressed to Jehovah; “Why is it that you ask my name?”, the same as Genesis 32:30; “wonderful” and “a wonder” for pil’i and mafli; “offer up” and “went up” for alah; and “stir” for pa’am.

Echoes paid. The refrain of Judges 10:6 opens the chapter word for word, without the cry of 10:10. Sarai, Rebekah and Rachel stand behind the barren wife, and Isaac’s prayer at Genesis 25:21 behind Manoah’s. Hagar’s annunciation at Genesis 16:11 and the sign of Isaiah 7:14 share the angel’s words. The Nazirite law of Numbers 6 becomes a life, as in Hannah’s vow at 1 Samuel 1:11. Gideon’s goat, rock, fire and fear in Judges 6:19–22 are told again, and Jacob’s question at Genesis 32:30 gets the same non-answer.

Echoes planted. The Nazirite secret is told at Judges 16:17, and the same verb of beginning returns at 16:19 and 16:22. Samson is buried between Zorah and Eshtaol at 16:31, in the tomb of Manoah his father. Those are now on these pages. Mahaneh-dan is named again at Judges 18:12, where the Danites set out from Zorah and Eshtaol, also now on these pages.
