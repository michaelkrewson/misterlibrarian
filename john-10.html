Jesus continues straight out of chapter 9's confrontation with a figure of speech about a shepherd who enters the sheepfold by the gate, then declares 'I am the gate' and 'I am the good shepherd, who lays down his life for the sheep.' Months later, at the winter Feast of Dedication, the religious leaders surround him demanding a plain answer on whether he is the Christ; he answers 'I and the Father are one,' and they try to stone him for blasphemy. He withdraws across the Jordan, to where John the Baptist first baptized, and many there believe in him.
The shepherd discourse (vv1-21) continues directly from chapter 9 with no narrative gap, so c. AD 29, still in or just after the Feast of Tabernacles; vv22-39 are explicitly dated 'the Feast of Dedication... it was winter' (v22), i.e. Hanukkah, c. December AD 29 — a jump of roughly two months the chapter itself marks but does not narrate.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–6 · ⚠ no chapter break in the Greek — and a figure of speech this Gospel never calls a parable

This chapter does not begin. There is no ‘Jesus said to them’ here, no new scene, not even a paragraph marker in the earliest manuscripts — the sentence that becomes John 10:1 runs straight out of ‘your sin remains’, the last words of John 9:41 (already on these pages), addressed to the same Pharisees in the same breath. The chapter division is a thirteenth-century convenience (traditionally credited to Stephen Langton), not a seam John himself left. NIV is the one shelf version that makes the continuity explicit in the English itself: ‘Very truly I tell you Pharisees’ — supplying a vocative the Greek never states, because without it an English reader opening at verse 1 would have no idea who ‘you’ is.

‘Figure of speech’ (v6) is a deliberate, exact rendering, not a stylistic choice. The Greek is paroimia, never parabolē — and this Gospel never once uses parabolē, the word the Synoptic Gospels use close to fifty times between them for Jesus’s story-illustrations. Paroimia stands in exactly four New Testament verses: three in John (here; 16:25, twice; 16:29) and one in 2 Peter 2:22 (not yet on these pages), of a proverb about a dog and its vomit. John’s Jesus never tells a parable in the Synoptic sense anywhere in this Gospel; he speaks in paroimiai instead — extended figures that, as this very scene shows, a hearer can fail to decode even when every image in it (a pen, a gate, a voice) is perfectly ordinary.

The picture itself has deep roots this Gospel does not quote but clearly assumes: a leader asking that Israel not be left ‘like sheep that have no shepherd’ (Numbers 27:17, already on these pages) and, later, a whole oracle against Israel’s own failed shepherds (Ezekiel 34, not yet on these pages) that this chapter’s contrast of a thief, a hired hand, and a true shepherd reads like a dramatized answer to.

Verses 7–10 · a second ‘I am’ — this one with a predicate — and a textual fork over who, exactly, came ‘before me’

‘I am the gate’ (vv7, 9) is built from the identical two Greek words — egō eimi — that this Gospel has just spent a whole chapter using WITHOUT a predicate, as an absolute claim to divine being (John 8:24, 28, 58, already on these pages). Here the construction is ordinary predicate-nominative Greek, ‘I am [the gate]’, one of this Gospel’s seven famous ‘I am ___’ sayings rather than the bare, syntactically naked claim of chapter 8. This translation keeps the two kinds distinct rather than reading the weight of one back into the other, exactly as it did for the man born blind’s own ordinary ‘I am he’ one chapter earlier (John 9:9, already on these pages).

⚠ Verse 8 carries a real textual decision with real theological stakes. ‘All who came before me’ (pro emou) is printed by every critical edition and kept by nearly every modern version, including NWT and NIV on this shelf; the Byzantine majority text drops the phrase entirely, as do some of the very earliest witnesses (the papyri P45 and P75 and Sinaiticus' first hand), which would leave the sentence reading simply that all who came are thieves and robbers — almost certainly because a scribe balked at a sentence that, read carelessly, brands every prophet and patriarch before Jesus a thief. Read in context it does no such thing: the whole figure (vv1–5) has been about illegitimate ENTRY — climbing the wall instead of using the gate — not about a timeline of Israel’s history, and the chapter’s own target is the religious leadership Jesus has been arguing with since chapter 9, not Moses or Isaiah. This translation keeps the harder reading the critical editions print and states the problem rather than smoothing it away.

Verses 11–16 · ‘good’, but not that kind of good — and a mistranslation old enough to have started an argument about the church itself

‘Good shepherd’ translates kalos, not agathos — and the difference is worth a reader’s attention even where the familiar English phrase is kept. Kalos, already met in this Gospel’s companion pages at Matthew 12:33, is closer to ‘fine, sound, doing exactly what it is for’ than to the purely moral ‘good’ of its near-synonym agathos. A kalos shepherd is not merely a virtuous one; he is a shepherd functioning as a shepherd actually should — which this chapter immediately defines in one sentence: he ‘lays down his life for the sheep’, the single qualification that separates him from the hired hand (misthōtos), who is paid to be near the sheep but owns nothing and risks nothing when the wolf comes.

⚠ Verse 16 is where the Latin Vulgate (unum ovile), and the King James Version after it, carried one of the most historically consequential mistranslations in the whole New Testament, and the Greek makes the error impossible to miss once the two words are set side by side. ‘Other sheep… not of this fold’ uses aulē, the walled courtyard of vv1–3; ‘one flock, one shepherd’ at the end of the same verse uses the entirely different word poimnē. KJV renders both ‘fold’ — ‘other sheep I have… them also I must bring… and there shall be one FOLD, and one shepherd’ — erasing a distinction the Greek draws on purpose: many folds (plural, local, architectural) can still become one flock (a single people, no building implied). ASV, NWT and NIV all correct it to ‘flock’ at the end of the verse; the Spanish shelf never had the problem to begin with, since RV 1909, RV60, NVI and both editions of the TNM all keep the two words apart on their own — redil for aulē, rebaño for poimnē. The Vulgate’s one-word error, carried into the KJV, was read for centuries as scriptural warrant for the idea of one single visible, institutional Church — ‘one fold’ in the architectural sense the Greek never claims — a reading this translation’s own exactness standard exists specifically to catch.

Verses 17–18 · a death announced with total authority, months before it happens

Every verb of agency in these two verses belongs to Jesus, not to the men who will eventually kill him. ‘No one takes it from me’ is stated flatly before the narrative itself ever shows a single hand raised against him; the laying-down and the taking-up again are both his own initiative, twice over, and the whole arrangement is called an entolē, a charge or commandment, received ‘from my Father’ — the identical word Jesus uses of what his disciples owe HIM, twice, once the Farewell Discourse opens (‘if you love me, you will keep my commandments,’ John 14:15, already on these pages, out of sequence; repeated at 14:21). A reader who has only chapters 18–19 ahead of them already has, here, the chapter that explains in advance why none of it will be a defeat.

Verses 19–21 · the same word splitting the same crowd — and the strongest argument in the whole scene left for the crowd itself to make

Schisma, ‘division’ (v19), is the identical word this Gospel has now used three times for the same recurring effect — a crowd split over Jesus at the Feast of Tabernacles (John 7:43, already on these pages) and the Pharisees split over the Sabbath healing one chapter ago (John 9:16, already on these pages). Each time the split runs along the identical fault line: behavior-to-origin reasoning (‘he has a demon’) against sign-to-origin reasoning (‘can a demon do THAT?’).

‘Can a demon open the eyes of the blind?’ (v21) is not a rhetorical flourish invented for this scene — it is a direct, specific callback to the healing this Gospel has just finished narrating in full. Nobody in this argument is speculating about a hypothetical power; the man born blind (John 9, already on these pages) is the case in evidence, and the question answers itself precisely because the healing just happened in public, with a trial transcript and parents who testified under pressure. This translation lets the question stand as the chapter leaves it — genuinely rhetorical, needing no narrator’s comment to land.

Verses 22–23 · the one time Hanukkah appears in the New Testament

The Feast of Dedication (Greek ta enkainia, ‘the renewals’) is this chapter’s own Greek New Testament echo of the Hebrew chanukkah — the eight-day festival instituted by Judas Maccabeus in 164 BC to commemorate the temple’s re-dedication after its desecration by Antiochus IV Epiphanes, told in 1 and 2 Maccabees (outside this project’s translated canon, which follows the Hebrew and Greek canonical Scriptures). This is the only verse in the whole New Testament where the festival is even mentioned, and the narrator’s own aside — ‘it was winter’ — does double duty: it dates the scene (Hanukkah falls in Kislev, our December) and explains why Jesus is walking in a COVERED colonnade rather than an open court. Solomon’s Colonnade (already on these pages) is the only other New Testament scene set in that exact location, decades later, in Acts 3:11 (already on these pages) and 5:12.

Verses 24–30 · an idiom, a famous textual crux, and a grammatical fact neither side of an old argument can simply claim

‘How long will you keep us in suspense?’ (v24) renders a vivid Greek idiom literally built on ‘lift up our soul’ — to keep someone’s whole self hanging, unresolved. KJV reads ‘make us to doubt’; ASV ‘hold us in suspense’; NWT keeps the body-part image, ‘keep our souls in suspense’.

⚠ Verse 29 is a genuine, well-known textual and grammatical crux, and the shelf itself splits right down the middle of it. The Greek reads, almost unparseably, ‘my Father, that which he has given me, is greater than all’ — a neuter relative pronoun (ho) sitting awkwardly against the masculine ‘my Father’. Some manuscripts instead read the masculine hos, ‘who’, which lets the sentence run straight — the reading KJV follows (‘my Father, which gave them me, is greater than all’) and ASV follows in its own words (‘my Father, who hath given them unto me, is greater than all’). The neuter reading the critical editions print, which NWT follows (‘what my Father has given me is something greater than all other things’), makes the sentence about the SHEEP — the gift the Father has placed in the Son’s hand is worth more than anything that might threaten to take it.

And that reading carries straight into the very next clause of the same verse, where the Greek gives the verb no object at all and this translation supplies ‘it’, the gift: ‘no one is able to snatch it out of the Father’s hand’ — what is held is the sheep, not the Father’s own greatness. This translation follows the earlier, harder, neuter reading and states what each path does to the sentence’s own sense.

‘I and the Father are one’ (v30) uses the neuter hen, ‘one THING’, not the masculine heis, ‘one PERSON’ — a grammatical fact every side of the historic debate over this verse has to reckon with rather than skip past. The neuter form alone does not settle whether the claim is a unity of will and mission (the reading many unitarian-leaning interpreters prefer, and the kind of ‘one’ Jesus later prays his disciples will share with him and each other, John 17:11, 22, already on these pages) or a unity of being (the reading behind the Trinitarian tradition’s historic use of this verse, reinforced by how directly the Jewish leaders react in the very next verse). This translation states the grammar plainly and does not referee the theology built on top of it.

Verses 31–39 · a quotation from the Psalms used as a legal argument, and the single most historically loaded phrase in this scene left exactly where it falls

The charge is stated in the Jewish leaders’ own words with no ambiguity at all: not the healing, not the Sabbath, but ‘because you, though you are a man, make yourself God’ (v33). Jesus does not deny the implication of his own words; he answers it with a quotation and an argument from Scripture, a rabbi’s own kind of defense. ‘Is it not written in your Law, “I said, you are gods”?’ quotes Psalm 82:6 (not yet on these pages) — a psalm in which God himself addresses human judges, Israel’s own appointed authorities, as elohim, ‘gods’, before pronouncing that they will ‘die like mere mortals’ for judging unjustly. Jesus’s argument runs from the lesser case to the greater (a classical rabbinic pattern, qal wahomer): if Scripture itself, which ‘cannot be set aside’ (lythēnai, ‘loosed, annulled’ — NWT ‘nullified’, KJV ‘broken’), can call mere human judges ‘gods’ without blasphemy, how much less is it blasphemy for the one the Father himself ‘set apart and sent into the world’ to call himself God’s Son.

⚠ This translation states what the argument IS and is not: it is an argument from a lesser case to a greater one, built on a specific psalm about human judges, not a claim that ‘Son of God’ and ‘a god’ in Psalm 82’s sense mean the same thing. Readers and traditions have drawn very different conclusions from this passage for centuries; the text itself offers the argument once and moves on — to the works, again, as the thing that should settle the question (v38), exactly as it did for the healed man at the end of chapter 9.

Verses 40–42 · back to the beginning of the book, and a testimony finally vindicated

Jesus withdraws to the exact place this Gospel opened in — ‘Bethany beyond the Jordan, where John was baptizing’ (already on these pages at John 1:28) — and the chapter closes the book’s very first thread. ‘John performed no sign’ is this Gospel’s only explicit statement that the Baptist himself worked no miracle of his own — his entire authority was always testimonial, pointing at someone else (John 1:19–34, already on these pages) — and ‘everything John said about this man was true’ is the crowd itself, at the far end of the book’s central conflict, reaching the verdict John asked them to reach from the very first chapter.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Hoi Ioudaioi kept as ‘the Jewish leaders’, the same choice carried since John 1:19 (already on these pages). Thyra rendered ‘gate’ rather than the more literal ‘door’, matching the sheepfold image rather than a house; aulē and poimnē kept as the distinct ‘fold’ and ‘flock’ the KJV’s own history shows the real cost of conflating (v16). ‘Good shepherd’ kept for kalos poimēn despite kalos’s own nuance of ‘fine, functioning as it should’ rather than purely moral goodness — the familiar phrase is not wrong, only imprecise, and the note states the difference rather than replacing a four-century-old rendering outright.

Echoes paid. The absolute egō eimi of John 8 (already on these pages) gets its deliberate predicate-nominative counterpart at vv7 and 9. Schisma (v19) repeats John 7:43 and John 9:16 (both already on these pages) a third time. The man born blind’s own healing (John 9, already on these pages) becomes, in this chapter, the evidence a divided crowd argues directly from (v21). And Jesus going looking for the man thrown out by the authorities at the end of that same chapter is, in miniature, exactly the shape of this chapter’s own shepherd going after sheep that are not yet gathered (v16) — the echo John 9:35 itself promised.

Echoes planted. ‘I and the Father are one’ (v30) anticipates the unity Jesus will pray his disciples share, with him and each other, in the high-priestly prayer (John 17:11, 21–22, already on these pages). The ‘other sheep… not of this fold’ (v16) looks ahead to a gathering beyond Israel that this Gospel has so far only glimpsed among the Samaritans (John 4:39–42, already on these pages). And ‘this charge I received from my Father’ (v18) is the first appearance of a word, entolē, that will return again and again once the Farewell Discourse begins.

Left open on purpose. Whether ‘I and the Father are one’ (v30) claims a unity of will or a unity of being — the grammar (neuter hen) does not settle it, and this translation states the fact rather than the verdict. Whether the ‘before me’ of v8 was ever meant to include every righteous figure in Israel’s own Scriptures, or only the immediate claimants and revolutionaries contesting Jesus’s own ministry — the text names no one. And the real manuscript fork at v29, neuter against masculine, which this translation resolves one way while stating plainly that it followed the reading the critical editions print, not certainty about which reading is ‘right’.
