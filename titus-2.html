Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · “The sound teaching” — older men, and one word five times

“But you, speak what is fitting for the sound teaching.” “Sound” is hygiainō, the healthy teaching of Titus 1:9 and 1 Timothy 1:10 (dictionary); it returns twice in this chapter, “sound in faith” in verse 2 and “sound speech” in verse 8. “Fitting” is prepō, as at 1 Timothy 2:10, “as is fitting for women who profess reverence for God.” The KJV reads “But speak thou the things which become sound doctrine”; the NWT 1984 reads “keep on speaking what things are fitting for healthful teaching”; the NIV reads “what is appropriate to sound doctrine.”

“Older men are to be temperate, dignified, sensible, sound in faith, in love, in endurance.” Presbytēs, an old man by age, stands in three verses: Zechariah’s “I am an old man” at Luke 1:18, Paul at Philemon 1:9, and here; it is a different word from the presbyteros, “elder,” of Titus 1:5 (dictionary). Nēphalios, “temperate,” stands in three verses, all in the Pastoral letters: the overseer at 1 Timothy 3:2, the women at 1 Timothy 3:11, and these older men (dictionary). “Dignified” is semnos, as at 1 Timothy 3:8 (dictionary). Faith, love and endurance are the triad of 1 Thessalonians 1:3, where endurance is “of hope.” The KJV reads “That the aged men be sober, grave, temperate, sound in faith, in charity, in patience”; the NWT 1984 reads “Let the aged men be moderate in habits, serious, sound in mind, healthy in faith”; the NIV reads “sound in faith, in love and in endurance.”

⚠ “Sensible” — one root, five words in one chapter. Sōphrōn is built from sōs, “safe, sound,” and phrēn, “mind”: a mind kept safe, good sense and self-command. This chapter uses the root five times: sōphrōn of the older men in verse 2 and of the young women in verse 5 (dictionary); sōphronizō, to train in good sense, in verse 4 (dictionary); sōphroneō, to be sensible, of the younger men in verse 6 (dictionary); and the adverb sōphronōs, “sensibly,” in verse 12, where grace itself teaches it (dictionary). The noun is the sōphrosynē of 1 Timothy 2:9 (dictionary). Elsewhere this translation has “of sound mind” (1 Timothy 3:2); here, where “sound” belongs to hygiainō, every member of the family is “sensible,” as at Titus 1:8. The KJV spreads the five words over four English ones, beginning “That the aged men be sober, grave, temperate”; the NWT 1984 reads “sound in mind” at verse 2 and verse 5.

Verses 3–5 · Older women and younger women — “workers at home” and “submitting to their own husbands”

“Older women likewise, reverent in demeanor, not slanderers nor enslaved to much wine, teachers of what is good.” Four words of the verse stand nowhere else in the New Testament: presbytis, “older woman” (dictionary); katastēma, “demeanor”; hieroprepēs, “fitting for what is sacred,” here “reverent” (dictionary); and kalodidaskalos, “teacher of what is good” (dictionary). “Slanderers” is diaboloi, as at 1 Timothy 3:11 and 2 Timothy 3:3 (dictionary). “Enslaved” is douloō, the verb of Galatians 4:3, “enslaved under the elements of the world.” The KJV reads “teachers of good things”; the ASV reads “reverent in demeanor, not slanderers nor enslaved to much wine”; the NIV reads “or addicted to much wine.”

“So that they may train the young women in good sense, to love their husbands, to love their children.” Sōphronizō, “to train in good sense, to bring to one’s senses,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. Philandros, “loving one’s husband,” stands in one verse too (dictionary), and so does philoteknos, “loving one’s children” (dictionary). They are two more of the phil- words, set against the “lovers of self” of 2 Timothy 3:2. The older women teach the younger; Titus himself is not told to. The NWT 1984 reads “recall the young women to their senses to love their husbands”; the ASV reads “train the young women to love their husbands, to love their children”; the Douay reads “teach the young women to be wise.”

⚠ “Sensible, pure, workers at home, good.” Oikourgos, “working at home,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, the reading of Westcott and Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle-Aland (dictionary); the Byzantine text has oikouros, “keeping the house, staying at home,” and so the KJV: “keepers at home.” The younger widows of 1 Timothy 5:14 are to “manage their households.” “Pure” is hagnos, as at 1 Timothy 5:22, “keep yourself pure.” The ASV reads “workers at home, kind”; the NIV reads “to be busy at home.”

⚠ “Submitting to their own husbands, so that the word of God may not be blasphemed.” “Submitting” is hypotassō, the verb of Colossians 3:18, “Wives, submit to your husbands,” and of Ephesians 5:21, “submitting to one another in the fear of Christ.” The purpose clause is the one given for slaves at 1 Timothy 6:1, “so that the name of God and the teaching may not be blasphemed,” and it echoes Romans 2:24, from Isaiah 52:5. Readers take the line three ways: as a standing order of marriage; as instruction shaped by its stated purpose, the good name of the teaching in a society that expected it; or as one side of the mutual submission of Ephesians 5:21. Churches have built on each reading, and this page sets them side by side without choosing. The KJV reads “obedient to their own husbands, that the word of God be not blasphemed”; the NWT 1984 reads “subjecting themselves to their own husbands”; the Douay reads “obedient to their husbands, that the word of God be not blasphemed.”

Verses 6–8 · Younger men, and Titus as the example — “the one on the opposing side”

“Urge the younger men likewise to be sensible, in all things showing yourself an example of good works.” “Younger men” are the “brothers” of 1 Timothy 5:1. “To be sensible” is sōphroneō, the verb of the man “in his right mind” at Mark 5:15 and of “think soundly” at Romans 12:3. “In all things” can close verse 6, “sensible in all things,” or open verse 7, as it is printed here. “Example” is typos, as at 1 Timothy 4:12, “be an example to the believers” (dictionary). “Good works” are the letter’s refrain, from Titus 1:16 to verse 14. The KJV reads “Young men likewise exhort to be sober minded”; the NIV reads “Similarly, encourage the young men to be self-controlled”; the ASV reads “in all things showing thyself an ensample of good works.”

“In your teaching, integrity, dignity, sound speech that cannot be condemned.” Aphthoria, “integrity, incorruption,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); the Byzantine text has adiaphthoria and adds aphtharsia, and so the KJV: “in doctrine shewing uncorruptness, gravity, sincerity.” Semnotēs, “dignity,” stands in three verses: 1 Timothy 2:2, 1 Timothy 3:4 and this one. Akatagnōstos, “that cannot be condemned,” stands in one verse (dictionary). The NWT 1984 reads “showing uncorruptness in your teaching, seriousness”; the ASV reads “sound speech, that cannot be condemned.”

“So that the one on the opposing side may be put to shame, having nothing bad to say about us.” Ho ex enantias, “the one from the opposite side,” is the phrase of the centurion “who stood facing him” at Mark 15:39. “Put to shame” is entrepō, as at 2 Thessalonians 3:14, “so that he may be ashamed.” “Bad” is phaulos (dictionary). The printed text has “about us”; the Textus Receptus has “about you,” and so the KJV: “having no evil thing to say of you.” The ASV reads “having no evil thing to say of us”; the NWT 1984 reads “having nothing vile to say about us.”

Verses 9–10 · ⚠ Slaves and masters — verses once quoted to defend slavery

⚠ The use these verses were put to. In 1841 Thornton Stringfellow published A Brief Examination of Scripture Testimony on the Institution of Slavery, a defense of American slavery from the Bible, and among his texts he quotes these two verses: that “servants be obedient to their own masters, and please them well in all things,” “not answering again, not purloining, but showing all good fidelity” (read in the copy at civilwarcauses.org). He uses 1 Timothy 6:1 the same way. That is a misreading of what the verses do. They give instructions to people who were already enslaved, in a household order the letter did not create, and the reason they give is the good name of “the teaching of God our Saviour”; they do not say that the institution is good or that it ought to exist. Elsewhere Paul tells a slave, “if you can become free, rather make use of it” (1 Corinthians 7:21), and asks Philemon to receive Onesimus “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother” (Philemon 1:16).

“Slaves are to submit to their own masters in everything, to be acceptable, not talking back.” “Masters” is despotēs, the slave-owner, as at 1 Timothy 6:1 (dictionary). “In everything” belongs with “submit” in the printed text and with “acceptable” in the Byzantine punctuation. “Acceptable” is euarestos, the word of Romans 12:1, “acceptable to God.” “Talking back” is antilegō, the verb of “those who contradict it” at Titus 1:9 (dictionary). The KJV reads “Exhort servants to be obedient unto their own masters, and to please them well in all things; not answering again”; the NWT 1984 reads “Let slaves be in subjection to their owners in all things”; the NIV reads “not to talk back to them.”

“Not pilfering, but showing all good faithfulness, so that in everything they may adorn the teaching of God our Saviour.” Nosphizomai, “to keep back for oneself,” stands in three verses: Ananias, who “kept back some of the price,” at Acts 5:2 and 5:3, and here (dictionary). The Greek of Joshua 7:1 uses it of Achan and the devoted things (Greek checked). “Adorn” is kosmeō, the verb of the women who “adorn themselves” at 1 Timothy 2:9. “God our Saviour” is the title of Titus 1:3 and 1 Timothy 1:1. The KJV reads “Not purloining, but shewing all good fidelity”; the NWT 1984 reads “not committing theft”; the ASV reads “that they may adorn the doctrine of God our Saviour in all things.”

Verses 11–15 · ⚠ “The grace of God has appeared” — and “our great God and Saviour, Jesus Christ”

“For the grace of God has appeared, bringing salvation to all people.” Epiphainō, “to appear, to shine forth,” stands in four verses: the dawn that will “shine on those sitting in darkness” at Luke 1:79, the sun and stars not “appearing” at Acts 27:20, this one, and Titus 3:4. Grace “has appeared” here, and in verse 13 the “appearing” is still awaited (dictionary). Sōtērios, “bringing salvation,” the adjective, stands in one verse (dictionary); its neuter is the “salvation” Simeon holds at Luke 2:30 (dictionary). “To all people” is read with “bringing salvation” or with “has appeared.” The ASV reads “For the grace of God hath appeared, bringing salvation to all men”; the KJV reads “For the grace of God that bringeth salvation hath appeared to all men”; the Douay, following the Latin, reads “For the grace of God our Saviour hath appeared to all men.”

“Training us that, denying ungodliness and worldly desires, we should live sensibly and justly and godly in the present age.” “Training” is paideuō, the verb of 1 Timothy 1:20, “so that they may be taught,” and of 2 Timothy 2:25, “instructing”; grace is the teacher. “Denying” is the verb of Titus 1:16, “by their works they deny him.” Kosmikos, “worldly,” stands in two verses: here and Hebrews 9:1. “Godly” is eusebōs, as at 2 Timothy 3:12; “the present age” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 6:17 and 2 Timothy 4:10. The KJV reads “we should live soberly, righteously, and godly, in this present world”; the NWT 1984 reads “instructing us to repudiate ungodliness and worldly desires”; the ASV reads “we should live soberly and righteously and godly in this present world.”

⚠ “The appearing of the glory of our great God and Saviour, Jesus Christ” — one person or two? The Greek is tou megalou theou kai sōtēros hēmōn Iēsou Christou: one article stands before “great God,” and “Saviour” has none of its own. Readers take it three ways. First, as one person: Jesus Christ is “our great God and Saviour,” by the rule named after Granville Sharp, that two singular nouns for persons joined by “and” under one article name one person (the rule is reported here, not checked); so the NIV, “the appearing of the glory of our great God and Savior, Jesus Christ.” Second, as two persons: “Saviour” can stand without its own article as a title, and the great God and the Saviour Jesus Christ are two; so the KJV, “the glorious appearing of the great God and our Saviour Jesus Christ,” and the NWT 1984, “glorious manifestation of the great God,” which goes on to supply a bracketed “the” before “Savior.” Third, Jesus Christ is himself “the glory” of the great God that appears. The same shape stands at 2 Peter 1:1, where this translation prints “our God and Saviour Jesus Christ”; at 2 Thessalonians 1:12, with “Lord” in place of “Saviour,” it prints two persons. This translation prints the first reading here, as at 2 Peter 1:1; the grammar is argued both ways, and this page does not decide between the readings. Westcott and Hort and Tregelles print “Christ Jesus.”

“Who gave himself for us, to redeem us from all lawlessness and to cleanse for himself a people as his treasured possession, zealous for good works.” “Gave himself” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 2:6 and Galatians 1:4. Lytroō, “to redeem, to ransom,” stands in three verses: “the one who was going to redeem Israel” at Luke 24:21, this one, and 1 Peter 1:18 (dictionary). The words are close to the Greek of Psalm 130:8 (129:8 in the Greek numbering), “he will redeem Israel from all its lawless deeds,” and of Ezekiel 37:23, “I will cleanse them, and they will be my people” (yet to come in this translation; Greek checked). Periousios, “one’s own, treasured,” stands in one verse of the New Testament; it is the Greek of “my treasured possession” at Exodus 19:5 and of Deuteronomy 7:6, Deuteronomy 14:2 and Deuteronomy 26:18 (Greek checked; dictionary, dictionary). “Zealous” is zēlōtēs (dictionary). The KJV reads “and purify unto himself a peculiar people, zealous of good works”; the ASV reads “a people for his own possession”; the NWT 1984 reads “a people peculiarly his own.”

“Speak these things, and exhort and reprove with all authority.” “Authority” is epitagē, the “command of God” of Titus 1:3 and 1 Timothy 1:1 (dictionary). “Reprove” is the verb of 2 Timothy 4:2. Periphroneō, “to disregard, to think past,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); it is close to the “look down on” of 1 Timothy 4:12. The KJV reads “These things speak, and exhort, and rebuke with all authority”; the NWT 1984 reads “with full authority to command”; the ASV reads “These things speak and exhort and reprove with all authority.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “sound” for hygiainō three times, as at 1:9; “sensible,” “good sense” and “sensibly” for the five words of one root, as at 1:8; “temperate” and “dignified,” as in 1 Timothy 3; “slanderers” for diaboloi; “to love their husbands, to love their children” for two phil- words; “workers at home,” the printed text’s word; “example,” as at 1 Timothy 4:12; “slaves” and “masters”; “pilfering,” Ananias’s verb; “adorn,” as at 1 Timothy 2:9; “appeared” and “appearing” for one root; “denying,” as at 1:16; “our great God and Saviour,” as at 2 Peter 1:1, with the readings in the note; “redeem,” as at Luke 24:21; “treasured possession,” as at Exodus 19:5; and “good works.”

Echoes paid. The sound teaching of Titus 1:9; temperate and dignified from 1 Timothy 3:8 and 1 Timothy 3:11; the lovers of self of 2 Timothy 3:2, set against lovers of husbands and children; blasphemed from 1 Timothy 6:1; the example of 1 Timothy 4:12; pilfering from Acts 5:2; adorn from 1 Timothy 2:9; God our Saviour of Titus 1:3; denying from Titus 1:16; the present age of 1 Timothy 6:17; the appearing of 2 Timothy 4:8; redeem from Luke 24:21; the treasured possession of Exodus 19:5; and good works from Titus 1:16.

Echoes planted. “Ready for every good work” at 3:1; the kindness of God our Saviour that “appeared,” the verb of verse 11, at 3:4; “the washing of rebirth” at 3:5; and “good works” again at 3:8 and 3:14.

Next in this book: Titus 3, already on these pages. Submission to rulers, “the kindness and the love for humankind of God our Saviour appeared,” “the washing of rebirth,” and the last names: Artemas, Tychicus, Zenas the lawyer and Apollos.
