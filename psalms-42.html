A maskil of the sons of Korah: a soul that pants for God like a deer for streams of water, thirsts for the living God and asks when it will see his face; tears for bread, and the taunt “Where is your God?”; the memory of going to the house of God in the festival throng; a soul addressed and bidden to wait for God; remembering him from the land of Jordan and the Hermons while deep calls to deep and the waves pass over; Jehovah commands his steadfast love by day and his song is with me by night; and the question and the refrain again.
Undated. The heading names the sons of Korah, the temple singers, and no occasion; the speaker is far from the house of God, in the north near Jordan and Hermon, which is as much as the psalm says of where. ⚠ Psalm 43 carries the same refrain a third time and has no heading of its own, so some readers take the two as one poem.

¿Por qué estás abatida, alma mía, y por qué gimes dentro de mí? Espera en Dios, porque todavía le daré gracias — las salvaciones de su rostro.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verse 1 · Psalm 42:1 “a maskil of the sons of Korah” — and the numbers

⭐ The heading counts as a verse. As at Psalm 63, the Hebrew counts the heading as verse 1, so the deer is in verse 2 here and in verse 1 in the English versions on the shelf, which print the heading unnumbered; from verse 2 on they are one number behind. The verse numbers on this page are the Hebrew ones. The heading is “to the director, a maskil of the sons of Korah”, one of the eleven headings of that family. The Douay numbers this psalm 41, because the Greek and Latin Psalter join Psalms 9 and 10, and heads it “Unto the end, understanding for the sons of Core”.

⭐ One psalm, or two. Psalm 43 has no heading of its own in the Hebrew, and its last verse is this psalm’s refrain a third time (“why are you bowed down, my soul” stands in three verses: 42:6, 42:12 and 43:5). Psalm 43 is yet to come in this translation.

Verses 2–3 · Psalm 42:2–3 “As a deer pants for streams of water” — a soul that thirsts for the living God

⭐ The deer pants. Ta’arog is a rare verb, to pant, to cry out with longing, and it stands in this one verse, twice, in the form with this spelling; the book of Joel has it once with a vav, of the cattle at a dry streambed, “even the beasts of the field pant for you, because the streams of water have dried up” (Joel 1:20, now on these pages; dictionary). The stream is afiq, a channel with water in it (dictionary), and “streams of water” stands in four verses: Joel 1:20, Psalm 18:16, this verse and Song of Solomon 5:12 (the last two yet to come in this translation). The animal is ayyal, a male deer. The KJV has “As the hart panteth after the water brooks”, the NIV “As the deer pants for streams of water”, the NWT 1984 “As the hind that longs for the water streams”, the TLB “As the deer pants for water” and the Douay “As the hart panteth after the fountains of water”.

⭐ The living God. “My soul thirsts for God” stands in this one verse, and the soul is the thirsty part, as at Psalm 63:2 (now on these pages; dictionary). God is El chai, “the living God” (dictionary), and the thing wanted is to see him: “and see the face of God” stands in this one verse. The KJV has “when shall I come and appear before God?”, the NIV “When can I go and meet with God?”, the NWT 1984 “When shall I come and appear [before] God?”, the TLB “Where can I find him to come and stand before him?” and the Douay “when shall I come and appear before the face of God?”.

Verses 4–5 · Psalm 42:4–5 “My tears have been my bread” — “Where is your God?” and the memory of the festival

⭐ Tears for bread. “My tears have been my bread” stands in this one verse, and the taunt that goes with it is “Where is your God?”, which stands in three verses: this one, verse 11, and Jeremiah 2:28 (Jeremiah 2:28, now on these pages), where Jehovah asks it of the idols, “where are your gods that you made for yourself?”. The KJV has “My tears have been my meat day and night”, the ASV “My tears have been my food”, the NIV “My tears have been my food day and night”, the NWT 1984 “my tears have become food day and night” and the Douay “My tears have been my bread day and night”.

⭐ The throng, and the festival. “I will pour out my soul upon me”, ve’eshpekhah alai nafshi, is the verb of Hannah at Shiloh, “I have poured out my soul before Jehovah” (1 Samuel 1:15, now on these pages). What the soul remembers is the pilgrimage: “in the throng I used to move slowly” stands in this one verse, with two rare words, sakh, a throng, and eddaddem, to move slowly in a procession, and “a multitude keeping festival” stands in this one verse. The KJV has “I had gone with the multitude, I went with them to the house of God”, the ASV “How I went with the throng, and led them to the house of God”, the NIV “among the festive throng”, the NWT 1984 “I used to walk slowly before them to the house of God” and the TLB “you led a great procession to the Temple on festival days”. The Douay, from the Latin, has “I shall go over into the place of the wonderful tabernacle”.

Verse 6 · Psalm 42:6 “Why are you bowed down, my soul?” — the refrain

⭐ Talking to the soul. The psalmist argues with his own soul: “why are you bowed down?”, tishtochachi, “and why do you moan within me?”, vatehemi (which stands in this one verse), then the command, “wait for God”, hochili, and the reason, “for I will yet thank him”, which stands in three verses, the same three as the refrain. The KJV has “Why art thou cast down, O my soul? and why art thou disquieted in me? hope thou in God”, the NIV “Why, my soul, are you downcast? Why so disturbed within me? Put your hope in God”, the NWT 1984 “Why are you in despair, O my soul, And why are you boisterous within me? Wait for God”, the TLB “Take courage, my soul!” and the Douay “Why art thou sad, O my soul? and why dost thou trouble me? Hope in God”.

⭐ “The salvations of his face”. The first refrain ends “the salvations of his face”, which stands in this one verse; the last ends with “the salvations of my face, and my God” (which stands in two verses, verse 12 and Psalm 43:5), and the first Hebrew refrain does not end with “my God”, which begins the next verse: Elohai alai nafshi tishtochach, “my God, within me my soul is bowed down”. The KJV has “for the help of his countenance”, the NWT 1984 “as the grand salvation of my person” and the Douay “the salvation of my countenance”.

Verses 7–8 · Psalm 42:7–8 “from the land of Jordan, and the Hermons” — deep calls to deep

⭐ Jordan, Hermon, Mizar. The place the soul remembers God from is the far north: “from the land of Jordan” stands in this one verse, “the Hermons” (the plural stands in this one verse, the many peaks of the mountain), and “Mount Mizar” stands in this one verse, mits’ar, “little”. The KJV has “from the land of Jordan, and of the Hermonites, from the hill Mizar”, the NIV “the heights of Hermon — from Mount Mizar”, the NWT 1984 “the peaks of Hermon, From the little mountain” and the TLB “this lovely land where the Jordan River flows and where Mount Hermon and Mount Mizar stand”.

⭐ Deep calls to deep. “Deep calls to deep” stands in this one verse, with tehom, the deep of Genesis 1:2 (now on these pages), and the first deep is answered by a second, with a voice: “your cataracts”, tsinnorekha, stands in this one verse, the water-pipe word (dictionary; the same word as in 2 Samuel 5:8, now on these pages). The cry out of the depths of Psalm 130:1 (now on these pages) uses a different word for deep, ma’amaqqim. The last line, “all your breakers and your waves have passed over me” stands in two verses, this one and Jonah 2:4. The KJV has “Deep calleth unto deep at the noise of thy waterspouts”, the NIV “Deep calls to deep in the roar of your waterfalls”, the NWT 1984 “Watery deep to watery deep is calling”, the Geneva “One deep calleth another deep by the noise of thy waterspouts”, the TLB “floods of sorrow pour upon me like a thundering cataract” and the Douay “Deep calleth on deep, at the noise of thy flood-gates”.

Verses 9–10 · Psalm 42:9–10 “By day Jehovah commands his steadfast love” — “Why have you forgotten me?”

⭐ The name, once. The name Jehovah stands in this psalm once, here, in a psalm where God is otherwise Elohim and El: “Jehovah commands his steadfast love” stands in this one verse, with chesed (dictionary), by day, and “his song is with me” stands in this one verse, by night. The prayer is addressed to “the God of my life”, which stands in this one verse. The KJV has “Yet the Lord will command his lovingkindness in the day time”, the ASV “Yet Jehovah will command his lovingkindness”, the NIV “By day the Lord directs his love”, the NWT 1984 “By day Jehovah will command his loving-kindness” and the TLB “Yet day by day the Lord also pours out his steadfast love upon me”.

⭐ “My rock”, and “forgotten”. “To God my rock” stands in this one verse, and the question follows it, “why have you forgotten me”, which stands in this one verse; “under the oppression of the enemy” stands in two verses, this one and Psalm 43:2. The KJV has “I will say unto God my rock, Why hast thou forgotten me?”, the NIV “I say to God my Rock, Why have you forgotten me?”, the NWT 1984 “I will say to God my crag”, the TLB “why have you forsaken me?” and the Douay “Thou art my support”.

Verses 11–12 · Psalm 42:11–12 “with a crushing in my bones” — the taunt again, and the refrain

⭐ A crushing in the bones. “With a crushing in my bones” stands in this one verse, with retsach, a word that is also the Hebrew for murder; the taunt of verse 4, “Where is your God?”, is said again as a blow. The shelf splits: the KJV has “As with a sword in my bones”, the ASV “As with a sword in my bones”, the NIV “My bones suffer mortal agony”, the NWT 1984 “With murder against my bones”, the Geneva “My bones are cut asunder”, the TLB “Their taunts pierce me like a fatal wound” and the Douay “Whilst my bones are broken”.

⭐ The refrain, the second time. The refrain of verse 6 is said once more, now ending “and my God”. The KJV has “who is the health of my countenance, and my God”, the NIV “my Savior and my God”, the NWT 1984 “as the grand salvation of my person and as my God”, the TLB “He is my help! He is my God!” and the Douay “the salvation of my countenance, and my God”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “pants” for ta’arog, as at Joel 1:20; “streams of water” for afikei mayim; “the living God” for El chai; “soul” for nefesh; “steadfast love” for chesed; “my rock” for sal’i; “God” for Elohim and El, and “Jehovah” for the name, which stands once in the psalm, in verse 9. The Hebrew has no written and read pairs in this psalm.

Echoes paid. The panting of Joel 1:20; the thirst of Psalm 63:2; the question of Jeremiah 2:28; Hannah’s poured-out soul in 1 Samuel 1:15; the deep of Genesis 1:2; the water-pipe of 2 Samuel 5:8; and the depths of Psalm 130:1.

Echoes planted. The other streams of water (Psalm 18:16, Song of Solomon 5:12); the same waves in Jonah 2:4; and the refrain’s third saying, with the rest of this psalm, in Psalm 43 (43:2 and 43:5). None of these is translated here yet.

⚠ A note on order: the Psalms stand on these pages at 1, 8, 19, 22, 23, 24, 27, 34, 37, 42, 46, 51, 56, 63, 90, 91, 100, 103, 118, 119, 121, 130, 139 and 150, chosen by how often they are read rather than in sequence. The sequential thread is unchanged: next in sequence is Psalm 2.
