Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–7 · imitators of God, a fragrant aroma, clever jesting, and co-sharers

“Imitators of God, as beloved children.” Mimētēs, “imitator,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament; elsewhere Paul asks to be imitated as he imitates Christ, “Become imitators of me, just as I also am of Christ” (1 Corinthians 11:1), and only here are the readers told to imitate God himself (dictionary). Children copy a parent. The KJV reads “Be ye therefore followers of God, as dear children”; the ASV reads “imitators of God, as beloved children.”

“Handed himself over for us … for a fragrant aroma.” “Handed himself over” is the verb of Galatians 2:20, “who loved me and handed himself over for me,” and it returns in v25. “A fragrant aroma” stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and Philippians 4:18, where the Philippians’ gift is “a fragrant aroma, a sacrifice acceptable.” It is the Greek Old Testament’s phrase for the burnt offering, as at Exodus 29:18, “a soothing aroma” in the Hebrew. Prosphora, “offering,” is the word of Romans 15:16, “the offering of the nations” (dictionary). The KJV reads “an offering and a sacrifice to God for a sweetsmelling savour”; the NIV reads “as a fragrant offering and sacrifice to God.”

⚠ “Obscenity and foolish talk or clever jesting.” Three words, each of which stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one: aischrotēs, “obscenity”; mōrologia, “foolish talk”; and eutrapelia, literally “turning well,” the quick, nimble wit of a good talker (dictionary). Aristotle is reported to have counted eutrapelia a virtue, the happy mean between the buffoon and the boor; here it is wit that has slid into the coarse. In its place Paul sets eucharistia, “thanksgiving,” a word that sounds like it. “Let it not even be named” is said of the three of Galatians 5:19, sexual immorality, impurity and greed. The KJV reads “nor foolish talking, nor jesting, which are not convenient”; the NIV reads “foolish talk or coarse joking”; the NWT 1984 reads “obscene jesting.”

“A greedy person — that is, an idolater.” Greed is counted as idolatry, the thing wanted taking God’s place. “Has any inheritance in the kingdom” is the warning of 1 Corinthians 6:9 and Galatians 5:21. “The sons of disobedience” are those of Ephesians 2:2. Symmetochoi, “co-sharers,” turns the word of Ephesians 3:6 around: there the nations are co-sharers of the promise; here they are not to be co-sharers with the disobedient (dictionary). The KJV reads “Be not ye therefore partakers with them”; the NIV reads “Therefore do not be partners with them.”

Verses 8–14 · darkness and light, the fruit of the light, exposing, and “Wake up, sleeper”

“You were once darkness, but now you are light in the Lord.” Not in darkness but darkness itself, and now light itself. “Children of light” is close to the “sons of light” of Luke 16:8 and John 12:36, “believe in the light, so that you may become sons of light.” The KJV reads “For ye were sometimes darkness, but now are ye light in the Lord: walk as children of light”; the NWT 1984 reads “light in connection with [the] Lord.”

“The fruit of the light.” The Byzantine text reads “the fruit of the Spirit,” the phrase of Galatians 5:22, and so the KJV: “For the fruit of the Spirit is in all goodness and righteousness and truth”; the ASV reads “for the fruit of the light is in all goodness and righteousness and truth.” Agathōsynē, “goodness,” is one of the names of that fruit (dictionary).

“Testing what is pleasing to the Lord … expose them.” Dokimazō is the “test” of Romans 12:2, “so that you may test what the will of God is.” Elenchō is to bring into the light and show for what it is, the “exposed” of John 3:20, “so that his works may not be exposed.” The KJV reads “but rather reprove them”; the NIV reads “but rather expose them.”

⚠ “Wake up, sleeper, and rise from the dead, and Christ will shine on you.” “It says” brings in a quotation, but no book of the Old Testament has these words. The readings are an early Christian hymn, perhaps sung at baptism; a free joining of Isaiah 60:1, “Arise, shine, for your light has come,” with Isaiah 26:19 (both yet to come in this translation); or a saying otherwise lost. This page does not choose. Epiphauskō, “shine on,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; the waking is that of Romans 13:11, “the hour for you to wake from sleep.” The KJV reads “Christ shall give thee light”; the ASV reads “Christ shall shine upon thee”; the TLB supplies a source the Greek does not name, “That is why God says in the Scriptures.”

Verses 15–20 · buying out the time, wine and the Spirit, psalms, hymns and songs, and thanks

“Buying out the time.” Exagorazō is the “buy out” of Galatians 4:5, “so that he might buy out those under law” (dictionary); here the thing bought back is kairos, the time that is ripe. The participle stands in two verses of the New Testament, both of time: this one and Colossians 4:5. The KJV reads “Redeeming the time, because the days are evil”; the NIV reads “making the most of every opportunity”; the NWT 1984 reads “buying out the opportune time for yourselves.”

⚠ “Do not get drunk with wine … but be filled with the Spirit.” Asōtia, “debauchery,” is the word of Titus 1:6, “not open to a charge of debauchery,” and its adverb is the prodigal “living dissolutely” at Luke 15:13 (dictionary). Drink and the Spirit are set side by side, as at Pentecost, where the scoffers said “They are filled with sweet new wine” (Acts 2:13). The Greek is en pneumati, “in spirit” or “by the Spirit,” and readers differ on whether the Spirit is what fills them or the one who fills them. The verb is a present, ongoing: the NWT 1984 reads “keep getting filled with spirit.” The KJV reads “And be not drunk with wine, wherein is excess; but be filled with the Spirit”; the Douay reads “be ye filled with the holy Spirit,” from the Vulgate’s added Sancto.

“Psalms and hymns and spiritual songs.” The same three nouns stand together once more, at Colossians 3:16. Whether they are three kinds of song or one thing named three ways is not known. Psallō, “making music,” first meant plucking a string. The NWT 1984 reads “to Jehovah” for “to the Lord”; this translation prints “the Lord” where the Greek kyrios may mean Christ, as the next verse’s “our Lord Jesus Christ” suggests. The KJV reads “singing and making melody in your heart to the Lord.”

“Giving thanks always for all things.” The thanksgiving of v4 returns. The participles run on from “be filled”: speaking, singing, making music, giving thanks, and in the next verse submitting. The KJV reads “Giving thanks always for all things unto God and the Father in the name of our Lord Jesus Christ.”

Verses 21–24 · submitting to one another, a verse with no verb, how the passage has been used, and the head

⚠ “Submitting to one another in the fear of Christ.” This is the last of the participles that hang from “be filled with the Spirit” in v18, and it opens the instructions to the household. Hypotassō is to put oneself in order under another (dictionary); allēlois, “to one another,” makes it mutual. The Received Text reads “the fear of God,” and so the KJV: “Submitting yourselves one to another in the fear of God”; the ASV reads “in the fear of Christ”; the NIV reads “out of reverence for Christ.”

⚠ “Wives, to your own husbands” — a verse with no verb. In the critical text v22 has no verb of its own; it borrows “submitting” from v21, and this translation keeps the line as the Greek has it. The Byzantine text supplies “submit yourselves,” and Tregelles “let them submit,” and so the KJV: “Wives, submit yourselves unto your own husbands, as unto the Lord”; the ASV reads “Wives, be in subjection unto your own husbands”; the NWT 1984 reads “Let wives be in subjection to their husbands as to the Lord.”

⚠ How this passage has been used. These verses and their parallels at Colossians 3:18 and 1 Peter 3:1 have been used to tell wives to stay with and obey husbands who abused them, and to argue that a wife owes her husband obedience in everything; that use is part of the record of the churches. The passage itself speaks to wives directly, as people who act, not as property to be managed. It gives no husband an instruction to make his wife submit. Its word to husbands is the longer one, and its measure is Christ, who “handed himself over for her” (v25). Readers still divide over what it asks. One tradition reads a lasting order of headship in marriage, joined to a husband’s self-giving love; another reads v21’s mutual submission as governing all that follows, and the household arrangement as the custom of the day being changed from within. This page sets out both and does not choose.

⚠ “A husband is head of the wife as Christ also is head of the congregation.” Kephalē, “head,” is read by some as authority, by others as source or origin, as a river has a head (dictionary). The letter has used it twice already: Christ given “as head over all things to the congregation” (Ephesians 1:22), and “the head, Christ, from whom the whole body” grows (Ephesians 4:15 and 4:16). At 1 Corinthians 11:3 “the head of a woman is the man.” “Himself the savior of the body” is said of Christ alone. The KJV reads “For the husband is the head of the wife, even as Christ is the head of the church: and he is the saviour of the body”; the TLB reads “a husband is in charge of his wife,” one of the readings, printed as the text.

Verses 25–33 · husbands loving as Christ, the washing of water, one flesh, a great mystery, and fear

“Husbands, love your wives, just as Christ also loved the congregation and handed himself over for her.” The verb is the “loved us and handed himself over” of v2, now made the husband’s pattern; the present tense asks for love that keeps going. The picture of the congregation as a bride is Paul’s at 2 Corinthians 11:2, “to present you as a pure virgin to Christ.” The KJV reads “Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the church, and gave himself for it”; the NIV reads “and gave himself up for her.”

“The washing of water with the word.” Loutron, “washing” or “bath,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Titus 3:5 (dictionary). Many read baptism here; others the bride’s bath before the wedding. Rhēma, “word,” is the spoken word: the gospel, a confession made at baptism, or God’s word, as readers take it. “Holy and unblemished” is the phrase of Ephesians 1:4; rhytis, “wrinkle,” stands in one verse, this one. The KJV reads “with the washing of water by the word”; the TLB reads “washed by baptism and God’s Word”; the Douay reads “by the laver of water in the word of life.”

“He nourishes and cherishes it … members of his body.” Thalpō, “cherish,” to keep warm, stands in two verses: this one and 1 Thessalonians 2:7, of a nursing mother. The Byzantine text adds to v30 “of his flesh and of his bones,” the words of Genesis 2:23, “Bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh”; so the KJV: “For we are members of his body, of his flesh, and of his bones”; the ASV reads “because we are members of his body.”

⚠ “The two will become one flesh” — Genesis 2:24, in Greek. Paul quotes the Greek Old Testament, which has “the two”; the Hebrew, as Genesis 2:24 prints it here, reads “and they become one flesh.” “Into one flesh” stands in four verses of the New Testament: Jesus quoting it about marriage at Matthew 19:5 and Mark 10:8, Paul quoting it about a prostitute at 1 Corinthians 6:16, and this one, where the same words are made to speak of Christ. The KJV reads “and they two shall be one flesh”; the Douay reads “and they shall be two in one flesh.”

⚠ “This mystery is great.” Mystērion is the open secret of the letter, a thing once hidden and now made known (dictionary). The Vulgate prints sacramentum, and so the Douay reads “This is a great sacrament”; that Latin wording has been cited in Catholic teaching that marriage is a sacrament, while other readers note that the Greek word means a revealed secret, not a rite. This page reports both. Whether the mystery is the union of husband and wife, the union of Christ and the congregation, or the Genesis text read of Christ, Paul’s “but I am speaking about Christ and the congregation” leaves open. The KJV reads “This is a great mystery: but I speak concerning Christ and the church”; the NWT 1984 reads “This sacred secret is great.”

⚠ “Let the wife fear her husband.” Phobētai is the verb of v21’s noun, “in the fear of Christ”: reverence, the regard owed to one placed above. It does not ask a wife to be afraid of her husband, and it is read beside the mutual submission of v21 and the husband’s love “as himself” in the same verse. The ASV reads “and let the wife see that she fear her husband”; the KJV reads “and the wife see that she reverence her husband”; the NIV reads “and the wife must respect her husband”; the TLB adds “obeying, praising, and honoring him,” words the Greek does not have.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “handed himself over” at 5:2 and 5:25, as at Galatians 2:20; “a fragrant aroma,” as at Philippians 4:18; “co-sharers,” as at 3:6; “the sons of disobedience,” as at 2:2; “test,” as at Romans 12:2; “expose,” as at John 3:20; “buying out,” as at Galatians 4:5; “debauchery,” as at Titus 1:6; “wives, to your own husbands” with no verb, as the critical text has it; “head,” as at 1:22 and 4:15; “holy and unblemished,” as at 1:4; “one flesh,” as at Mark 10:8; “mystery”; and “fear” at 5:21 and 5:33, noun and verb.

Echoes paid. The walk again at 5:2, 5:8 and 5:15, as 4:1 planted; handed himself over from Galatians 2:20; the co-sharers of Ephesians 3:6; the sons of disobedience of Ephesians 2:2; the body and its head from Ephesians 1:22 and Ephesians 4:15; holy and unblemished from Ephesians 1:4; the mystery from 3:3; one flesh from Genesis 2:24, Mark 10:8 and 1 Corinthians 6:16; and the bride presented to Christ from 2 Corinthians 11:2.

Echoes planted. The household continues at 6:1–9, children and parents, slaves and masters; bring them up, the verb of “nourishes,” at 6:4; the mystery once more at 6:19; and the parallel household instructions of Colossians 3:18–4:1 and 1 Peter 3:1–7.
