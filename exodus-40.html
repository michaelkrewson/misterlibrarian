The finished tabernacle is set up, every object placed and anointed exactly as commanded, and Aaron and his sons clothed and consecrated. The cloud covers the tent and the glory of Jehovah fills the tabernacle so completely that even Moses cannot enter.
The one dated event in this whole stretch: 'the first day of the first month, in the second year' (v17) — roughly nine months after Israel's arrival at Sinai in the third month of the first year (chapter 19), and about a year after leaving Egypt at Passover.

The command repeated, one last time, then obeyed in a single sentence

Verses 2–15 repeat, almost word for word, instructions this book has already given in pieces across five chapters — the ark screened by the veil (Exodus 26:31–33, already on these pages), the anointing oil (Exodus 30:26–30, already on these pages), Aaron and his sons washed, clothed, and anointed at the tent’s door (Exodus 29:4–9, already on these pages) — but now given as one continuous sequence, an assembly order rather than a materials list. And the whole nine-chapter, nine-verse command is answered in a single line: ‘Thus Moses did; according to everything Jehovah commanded him, so he did’ (v16). Not a report of each step — that comes next, in exhausting, satisfying detail — but a verdict rendered before the detail even starts.

⚠ Verse 15’s ‘everlasting priesthood throughout their generations’ is where the shelf quietly splits over one adjective. KJV and ASV agree exactly, ‘an everlasting priesthood’; NASB softens it to ‘a permanent priesthood’; NIV drops the adjective altogether and restructures the clause as ‘a priesthood that will continue’ — converting a claim about eternal STATUS into a claim about ongoing continuity, a real shift in what is being promised.

⚠ A second, smaller shelf split sits in plain sight at v4: this translation calls the object ‘the lampstand,’ and stays with that word every time it appears, rather than the older English name for the same thing. KJV renders the Hebrew menorah as ‘candlestick’ throughout the tabernacle account, including here; ASV, NASB, and NIV all render it ‘lampstand’ instead — the more accurate word, since the object held burning oil lamps, not wax candles, which had no place in the tabernacle at all.

Command becomes report, one more time — and Moses finishes what was only ever finished FOR him before

The assembly itself (vv17–33) answers verses 2–15 point for point, in order, closing seven of its clauses with the identical formula that dominated the last chapter — ‘as Jehovah commanded Moses’ (vv19, 21, 23, 25, 27, 29, 32). Nothing here is new information; the whole passage is a checklist being ticked off, object by object, in the exact sequence the command laid out. ⚠ One small, consistent choice this translation makes and two of the five shelf versions do not: the recurring verb for ‘set up’ (taqim/vayakem, vv2, 18, 33) is rendered the same way every time it appears. KJV shifts from ‘set up’ (v2) to ‘reared up’ (vv18, 33); NASB shifts from ‘set up’ (v2) to ‘erected’ (vv18, 33) — the same Hebrew word, translated two different ways within a single Bible.

‘So Moses finished the work’ (v33, vayechal Moshe) uses the identical root, kalah, that closed the last chapter — but where Exodus 39:32 said the work ‘WAS finished’ (vatechel, a passive, no one named), this verse names the man: Moses himself finishes it. The same completion, said twice, in two grammatical voices — first as a fact about the tabernacle, then as an act of the one person who oversaw its every piece from a mountain’s worth of instructions down to a single gold thread.

The glory Moses could not survive facing now fills the whole room

‘The glory of Jehovah filled the tabernacle’ (v34) — the same word, the same weight, that Moses begged to see outright three chapters ago and was shown only a retreating back, because ‘man shall not see me and live’ (Exodus 33:18–23, already on these pages). That refusal is not undone here; it is confirmed at a larger scale. The very man who spoke with Jehovah ‘face to face, as a man speaks to his friend’ (Exodus 33:11, already on these pages), who came and went from the tent of meeting freely, now cannot enter it at all — ‘Moses was not able to enter’ (v35). The glory that once had to be limited to a hand over his eyes and a hand’s-width of God’s back now fills an entire building, and even Moses stands outside it.

The verb for the cloud ‘settling’ (v35, shakhan) is the exact root behind this whole structure’s own name, mishkan, ‘dwelling-place’ — the promise made in the abstract nine chapters ago, ‘I will DWELL among the children of Israel’ (Exodus 29:45–46, already on these pages), is not merely kept here; it is enacted, the tabernacle’s own name turning into a verb one final time as the thing it names actually happens.

The pillar that led them out now settles where it led them to — and the book ends with the door still shut

The cloud by day and the fire by night (v38) are not a new sign — they are the identical pillar that went before Israel out of Egypt, at the very start of this book’s wilderness road (Exodus 13:21–22, already on these pages). There it MOVED, leading a people with nowhere yet to live. Here it SETTLES, marking a place that now exists for it to settle on. The same sign that once meant ‘follow me’ now also means ‘stay,’ and will keep meaning both for the whole of the next book — when the cloud lifts, the camp moves; when it does not, no one does (Numbers 9:15–23, already on these pages) — the rule this closing image quietly establishes for a journey that has not ended.

Exodus itself ends on that unresolved image: the door shut, Moses outside, the glory inside. The very next words in the Torah answer it — ‘Jehovah called to Moses, and spoke to him FROM THE TENT OF MEETING’ (Leviticus 1:1, already on these pages) — but that answer belongs to a different book. This one closes with the door still closed.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Every technical term (veil, screen, altar, laver, cover, tabernacle, tent of meeting) matches this translation’s own established choices throughout Exodus 25–39, already on these pages — a closing chapter is the wrong place to introduce a new word for something already named a dozen times. ‘Set up’ is kept consistently for the recurring verb taqim/vayakem at vv2, 18, and 33 — see the note above on where two of the shelf’s own five versions do not manage the same consistency. ‘Jehovah’ kept throughout for the divine name. The chapter’s nine ordinary paragraph breaks ({ס} at v16, v19, v21, v23, v25, v27, v29, v32, and {פ} at v33) mark the command’s own close and each finished task in turn. Verse 38 carries a rarer mark instead — {ש}, ‘an empty line at the end of some books,’ Mechon-Mamre’s own name for it — the traditional scribal notation that this is where a book actually ends, used nowhere else on these pages yet. Verse numbering follows the Hebrew exactly; Hebrew and English Exodus 40 both run 1–38.

📖 That is the end of Exodus. Forty chapters — the second complete book in this translation, and the harder one to keep whole: a slave people leave a country with nothing but borrowed silver and a promise, cross a sea, break the covenant within weeks of receiving it at a mountain that is still shaking, and spend the back half of the book building, with their own hands and their own gold, a small tent for the thing that terrified them at the mountain to live in permanently. The book that opens with a people who have no dwelling — not even their own bodies are safe, boys drowned at birth by decree — closes with God dwelling among them, so completely that the man who used to walk in and speak to him face to face can no longer get through the door. Nothing about that ending resolves cleanly; it is not supposed to. The glory that fills the tabernacle in the last verses is the same glory Moses begged, three chapters earlier, to see just once, and was refused. Exodus does not answer that refusal by softening it. It answers it by building a room large enough to hold what could not be looked at directly, and then reporting, honestly, that even Moses cannot go in.

Next in this translation: the story continues directly from where this chapter leaves off — Leviticus 1, already on these pages, opens with Jehovah calling to Moses FROM the tent of meeting he could not enter a chapter ago. Leviticus 1 and 19 are already translated here; the rest of the book, and the rest of the Torah’s own unfinished journey, remains open.
