From Rome — 'Babylon' in the letter's code (5:13) — the apostle Peter writes to scattered, suffering Christians across five provinces of Asia Minor, calling them exiles and steadying them with hope: born again to a living hope, an inheritance kept in heaven, and a faith tried like gold in the fire, ransomed by the blood of the lamb.
c. AD 62–64, on the traditional reading, from Rome, shortly before Peter's martyrdom under Nero (tradition, early and consistent, has him crucified there, by his own request head-downward). Silvanus is named as the one 'through whom' Peter wrote (5:12), which many take to explain the letter's polished Greek. ⚠ A body of scholarship holds the letter pseudonymous and later (c. AD 80-95), citing that Greek and the settled persecution it assumes; the traditional authorship is taken here and the dispute reported.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · a letter to exiles, and a greeting shaped like the Trinity

The address names the readers by their whole situation: they are EXILES. Parepidēmoi — resident aliens, people living somewhere that is not home — scattered across five Roman provinces of northern Asia Minor (Galatia among them). ⚠ The word is the letter’s keynote: to be a Christian, for Peter, is to live as a temporary resident in a world that is not your country, and it comes back at verse 17 (“the time of your sojourning”) and 2:11 (“strangers and exiles”). KJV “strangers scattered”; NIV “exiles scattered”; NWT “temporary residents.”

⚠ The greeting is already the doctrine of the Trinity, in shorthand. The readers are chosen “according to the foreknowledge of God the Father, in the sanctification of the Spirit, for obedience and the sprinkling of the blood of Jesus Christ” — Father, Spirit and Son named in one breath, each with a distinct work. The “sprinkling of blood” reaches back to Exodus 24:8, where Moses sealed the covenant by throwing blood on the people; the new covenant is sealed the same way. “May grace and peace be multiplied” is the Greek of Daniel’s letters (Daniel 4:1) — an old, formal, Jewish way to open.

Verses 3–5 · born again to a living hope, and an inheritance that cannot fade

⚠ “He gave us NEW BIRTH into a living hope.” Anagennaō — to beget again, to bring to birth anew — is Peter’s great word (it frames the chapter, returning at verse 23), and it is the same thing John records Jesus telling Nicodemus: you must be born again. The hope is called “living” because it rests on a living person: it comes “through the resurrection of Jesus Christ.” The whole sentence opens with the Jewish blessing-formula, “Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ” — word for word the opening of 2 Corinthians 1:3.

⚠ Three words, all beginning with the same sound, for an inheritance that will not spoil. The Greek rings: aphtharton, amianton, amaranton — imperishable, undefiled, unfading — three a-words in a row. Each denies a way earthly inheritances fail: they rot, they are polluted, they wither. ⚠ Behind it stands the old inheritance, the land of Canaan, which could be defiled and lost; this one is “kept in the heavens” — on deposit, out of reach of moth and rust and army — and the heirs are themselves “guarded” (a military word, phroureō, set a garrison over) for it. KJV “incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away.”

Verses 6–9 · gold in the fire, and love for a face never seen

“For a little while… grieved by various trials.” The suffering is real — the letter is written to people under social pressure and worse — but it is measured against eternity (“a little while”) and qualified (“if it must be”). ⚠ The image is the assayer’s furnace: the “tested genuineness” (dokimion) of faith is worth more than gold, and the point is pressed home — even gold, the most durable thing they knew, “perishes,” and yet it is proved by fire; how much more a faith that does not perish. It is the same testing James wrote of (James 1:3), the trial that proves and purifies.

⚠ “Whom, not having seen, you love… joy INEXPRESSIBLE and glorified.” A sentence written for people who, unlike Peter, never walked with Jesus: they love a face they have not seen and trust a person they cannot now see. And the joy Peter gives them a word for — chara aneklalētos kai dedoxasmenē, “joy unspeakable and full of glory” in the old phrase — is aneklalētos, literally “un-out-speakable,” a joy that cannot be talked out to the end. KJV “joy unspeakable and full of glory.”

Verses 10–12 · the prophets searched their own words, and angels stoop to look

⚠ The prophets studied their own prophecies, trying to see what they meant. A remarkable picture: the Old Testament prophets “searched and inquired” (two intensive verbs) into their own oracles, asking “what person or what time” the “Spirit of Christ in them” was pointing to. Peter reads the whole prophetic corpus as having been about Christ before Christ came — and as only partly understood by the men who wrote it. What they glimpsed was a pattern: “the sufferings destined for Christ and the glories after them” — suffering, then glory, the shape of the letter and of the life it calls its readers to.

“Things into which angels long to LOOK.” Parakypsai — to stoop down and peer in, the very verb James used of bending to look into the perfect law (James 1:25); here it is the angels, craning to see into the gospel of grace. ⚠ Even the prophets who foretold it and the angels who attend it saw less of this salvation than the ordinary believers who now possess it. The scattered exiles of verse 1 turn out to hold what prophets and angels strained toward.

Verses 13–16 · roll up your sleeves, and be holy

⚠ “GIRDING UP the loins of your mind.” A vivid, physical figure that English has mostly lost: a man in a long robe, about to run or work or travel, pulled the hem up through his belt so his legs were free — “girding up the loins.” ⚠ The picture is straight out of the Passover (Exodus 12:11, eaten “with your loins girded,” ready to march), and Peter applies it to the mind: pull your thoughts together, clear the decks, be ready to move. Modern versions reach for the idiom — NIV “with minds that are alert”; KJV keeps the old body-image, “gird up the loins of your mind.” We keep it too, because the metaphor is doing work the paraphrase drops.

“Be HOLY, for I am holy.” Peter grounds Christian conduct in the oldest holiness formula, quoting Leviticus 11:44 (“you shall be holy, for I am holy”) — the ethics of the exiles are the ethics of the God who called them, and holiness here is not ritual purity but a whole manner of life (anastrophē, “conduct,” a keyword of the letter). The former life was lived “in ignorance”; the new one takes its shape from the character of God.

Verses 17–21 · ransomed, not with silver, but with the blood of the lamb

“The time of your SOJOURNING.” The exile-theme again: paroikia, living abroad as a foreigner (the root of “parish”), and it is to be spent “in fear” — not terror, but the reverence owed to a Father who is also an impartial Judge, “who judges according to each one’s work.”

⚠ “You were RANSOMED… not with silver or gold, but with the precious blood of Christ.” Elytrōthēte is the language of the slave-market and the redemption of captives: a price paid to buy someone out. And Peter names the two coinages and dismisses them — not perishable silver or gold (the very metals that measure all other ransoms), but blood, “as of a lamb without blemish or spot.” ⚠ The lamb is the Passover lamb of Exodus 12, required to be flawless; Christ is read as the true Passover, and the exiles’ whole redemption as a new Exodus out of “the futile way of life handed down from your fathers.” He was “foreknown before the foundation of the world” — the lamb was chosen before there was a world to redeem — “but made manifest at the end of the times.”

Verses 22–25 · born of imperishable seed, and the word that outlasts the grass

“A sincere BROTHERLY LOVE… love one another earnestly.” Philadelphia — love of the brother and sister — is to be anypokritos, “unfeigned,” not a performance, and ektenōs, “stretched out to the full,” the word for a muscle straining or a runner at full extension. Love here is effort, not sentiment.

⚠ “Born again… of imperishable SEED, through the living and abiding word.” The birth-word of verse 3 returns (anagegennēmenoi), and now the seed of the new birth is named: not perishable human seed but “the living and abiding word of God.” The word is what begets, and the word is what lasts. To prove it Peter quotes Isaiah 40:6-8 — “all flesh is grass… the grass withered, the flower fell, but the word of the Lord remains forever” — the same fading flower James set against the rich man (James 1:10-11). ⚠ And Peter lands it exactly: “this is the word that was preached as good news to you.” The everlasting word of Isaiah is not far off in the scroll; it is the gospel they heard, the seed they were born from.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “exiles” and “sojourning” for parepidēmoi and paroikia, the resident-alien theme; “new birth” and “born again” for anagennaō, framing the chapter; the alliterating “imperishable, undefiled, unfading” for the three a-words of verse 4; “joy inexpressible” for aneklalētos; “girding up the loins of your mind” kept as the bodily Passover image; “ransomed” for lytroō, the buying-out of a slave; and “sincere brotherly love” for philadelphia anypokritos, love without the actor’s mask.

Echoes paid. The “Dispersion” that also opens James 1:1, the other letter to scattered believers; the “Blessed be the God and Father” formula shared with 2 Corinthians 1:3; the “tested genuineness of faith” tried like gold, as in James 1:3; the “lamb without blemish” that John 1:29 hails as “the Lamb of God”; the “living and abiding word” that begets, as in James 1:18; and the fading “flower of grass” of Isaiah 40, set against the rich man at James 1:10.

Echoes planted. Suffering, then glory — the prophets’ pattern (1:11) that structures the whole letter, worked out at 2:21 (“Christ suffered for you, leaving you an example”), 4:13 (“share Christ’s sufferings… rejoice”) and 5:1; the exile theme, sharpened at 2:11, “strangers and pilgrims”; holiness of conduct (anastrophē), the word that recurs through chapter 2; and “Babylon” at 5:13, the code-name that has always been read as Rome, from which Peter writes.

1 Peter is new to the library — the New Testament’s great letter of hope in suffering, written to scattered exiles by (on the traditional reading) the fisherman who denied his Lord and was restored. Next in this book: chapter 2 — “living stones” built into a spiritual house, “a chosen race, a royal priesthood, a holy nation”, and the long passage on Christ’s example in suffering, “who committed no sin… who, when he was reviled, did not revile in return.”
