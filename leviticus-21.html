Priestly holiness law in two tiers. Ordinary priests may defile themselves for corpse contact only for their five closest relatives, may not follow ordinary mourning practices, and may not marry a prostitute, a profaned woman, or a divorced woman. The high priest's own rules are stricter still — no torn clothes, no corpse contact even for his own parents, no leaving the sanctuary, and marriage restricted to a virgin of his own people. The chapter closes with a long list of physical blemishes barring a priest from altar service specifically, while explicitly preserving his standing as a priest and his right to eat the holy bread.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus. Its mourning-practice law (v5) restates, word for word, a rule already given to all Israel at Leviticus 19:27-28 (already on these pages). Still inside the same Sinai window as the rest of this block (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

Leviticus 21 meaning — why priests get a stricter mourning law than everyone else

Verses 1–3 grant ordinary priests corpse-contact only for the five closest relatives — parent, child, sibling, and an unmarried sister (a married sister falls to her husband's household, and so drops off the list). Verse 5's ban on shaving a bald spot, trimming beard corners, or cutting the flesh restates, word for word, a law Leviticus 19:27–28 (already on these pages) already gave to all Israel — the priesthood held to the same standard as everyone else, not a harsher one, on this specific point.

⚠ V4's ‘a husband among his people’ is genuinely uncertain. KJV and ASV both read ba'al as ‘a chief man,’ forbidding a priest to defile himself even in a position of prominence; other readings take ba'al literally as ‘husband,’ restricting a priest from defiling himself for in-laws gained through marriage rather than blood. Both readings are defended; this translation does not choose.

Marriage restrictions for priests, and the one case punished by fire

A priest's marriage options exclude a prostitute, a profaned woman, and a divorced woman (v7) — a shorter list than the high priest will face in vv13–14, the first sign of a graduated holiness this chapter builds in stages, the same layered structure this project has already tracked in the tabernacle's own construction (choshev/rokem, already on these pages).

V9's priest's-daughter case — burned for prostituting herself — answers a warning this project's own Leviticus 19:29 (already on these pages) already gave in general form: ‘do not profane your daughter by making her a prostitute.’ Both verses use the identical root, chalal, ‘to profane’ — the general warning there, the specific priestly penalty here.

The high priest's own stricter law, and the gesture it cost Caiaphas nothing to break

The high priest's restrictions (vv10–15) sharpen every rule just given to ordinary priests: no loosened hair, no torn clothes, no corpse contact even for his own parents, no leaving the sanctuary during the days of consecration, and a marriage law that admits only a virgin of his own people — not even the widow an ordinary priest may marry.

⚠ V10's ban on tearing clothes is the exact gesture Caiaphas performs at Jesus's own trial (Mark 14:63, not yet on these pages) — the Law's own high priest, at the Law's own climactic moment, breaking a law written for his office specifically. Later Jewish tradition distinguished a judicial tear on hearing blasphemy from the mourning-tear this verse actually forbids, a real qualification worth stating alongside the fact rather than in place of it.

Why a blemish bars the altar but not the bread — approach, not worth

The blemish list (vv18–20) is long and clinical — blindness, lameness, a mutilated face, broken limbs, a hunchback, dwarfism, eye disease, skin disease, crushed testicles — and it would be easy to misread as a verdict on the men it names. KJV and ASV both keep the plain ‘blemish’ for mum throughout; NIV's ‘defect’ carries a slightly more clinical register but names the same physical standard.

The text itself will not allow that reading: v22 states plainly that a priest disqualified from the altar still eats the holy bread, still belongs fully to the priestly family, still draws provision as a priest. What he cannot do is APPROACH — go behind the veil, come near the altar (v23) — the identical distinction this project has tracked since Leviticus 11 (already on these pages): tame and tahor measure nearness to the sanctuary, not the worth of the person standing at whatever distance the law assigns him. A blemished priest is not a lesser priest morally or personally; he is a priest the text keeps three steps back from one specific doorway.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 21 both run 1–24 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Three Masoretic paragraphs divide it: closed after v9, closed after v15, open after v24. Chalalah, ‘a profaned woman,’ and mum, ‘a blemish,’ are both newly entered, the chapter's own two load-bearing technical terms. V4's ba'al be'amav is flagged as genuinely uncertain rather than resolved by fiat.

Echoes paid. Leviticus 19:27–28's mourning-practice ban (already on these pages), restated here word for word for priests specifically; Leviticus 19:29's warning against profaning a daughter (already on these pages), given its specific priestly penalty; the choshev/rokem graduated-holiness pattern from the tabernacle's own construction (already on these pages), now visible in the gap between an ordinary priest's marriage restrictions and the high priest's stricter ones; and the tame/tahor-as-approach-not-morality reading this project has held since Leviticus 11 (already on these pages), now extended from ritual impurity to physical blemish — a related but distinct case, held to the same principle.

Echoes planted. Mark 14:63 (not yet on these pages), where Caiaphas tears his own clothes at Jesus's trial, breaking the exact law v10 writes for his office.
