🕰
The book's own covenant conclusion: obedience blessed, defiance cursed, laid out in careful proportion — ten verses of blessing (rain, harvest, peace, victory, God's own presence walking among the people) against thirty-two verses of curse that escalate in four distinct waves, each one triggered specifically by Israel's continued refusal to respond to the wave before it, each opened by the identical formula 'seven times more for your sins.' The curses run from disease and military defeat, through crop failure and wild animals, through siege and a famine so total that ten women bake in one oven, to the worst line in the book — parents eating their own children — and finally exile, the land left empty enough to enjoy the sabbaths Israel denied it. It closes on hope rather than despair: confession, a humbled heart, and Jehovah remembering his covenant with Jacob, then Isaac, then Abraham, even at the furthest point of exile.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus. V46's own closing colophon — 'these are the statutes and the ordinances and the laws' — reads like the intended close of the whole Sinai law code beginning at Exodus 19, with Leviticus 27 arriving afterward as its own appendix. Still inside the same Sinai window as the rest of this block (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

Leviticus 26 — The Mister Translation

1
לֹא-תַעֲשׂוּ לָכֶם אֱלִילִם, וּפֶסֶל וּמַצֵּבָה לֹא-תָקִימוּ לָכֶם, וְאֶבֶן מַשְׂכִּית לֹא תִתְּנוּ בְּאַרְצְכֶם, לְהִשְׁתַּחֲוֺת עָלֶיהָ: כִּי אֲנִי יְהוָה, אֱלֹהֵיכֶם.
You shall not make idols for yourselves, nor set up a carved image or a sacred pillar for yourselves, nor place a carved stone in your land, to bow down to it: for I am Jehovah your God.note
No os haréis ídolos, ni os levantaréis imagen tallada ni piedra sagrada, ni pondréis en vuestra tierra piedra grabada para postraros ante ella: porque yo soy Jehová vuestro Dios.
2
אֶת-שַׁבְּתֹתַי תִּשְׁמֹרוּ, וּמִקְדָּשִׁי תִּירָאוּ: אֲנִי, יְהוָה. {פ}
You shall keep my sabbaths, and reverence my sanctuary: I am Jehovah. {פ}note
Guardaréis mis sábados, y reverenciaréis mi santuario: yo soy Jehová.
3
אִם-בְּחֻקֹּתַי, תֵּלֵכוּ; וְאֶת-מִצְוֺתַי תִּשְׁמְרוּ, וַעֲשִׂיתֶם אֹתָם.
If you walk in my statutes, and keep my commandments, and do them,note
Si anduáis en mis estatutos, y guardáis mis mandamientos, y los ponéis por obra,
4
וְנָתַתִּי גִשְׁמֵיכֶם, בְּעִתָּם; וְנָתְנָה הָאָרֶץ יְבוּלָהּ, וְעֵץ הַשָּׂדֶה יִתֵּן פִּרְיוֹ.
then I will give your rains in their season, and the land shall yield its produce, and the trees of the field shall yield their fruit.note
yo daré vuestras lluvias en su tiempo, y la tierra dará su producto, y el árbol del campo dará su fruto.
5

And your threshing shall last until the grape harvest, and the grape harvest shall last until the sowing; and you shall eat your bread to the full, and dwell in your land in safety.note
Y vuestra trilla alcanzará hasta la vendimia, y la vendimia alcanzará hasta la siembra; y comeréis vuestro pan hasta saciaros, y habitaréis en vuestra tierra con seguridad.
6
וְנָתַתִּי שָׁלוֹם בָּאָרֶץ, וּשְׁכַבְתֶּם וְאֵין מַחֲרִיד; וְהִשְׁבַּתִּי חַיָּה רָעָה, מִן-הָאָרֶץ, וְחֶרֶב, לֹא-תַעֲבֹר בְּאַרְצְכֶם.
And I will give peace in the land, and you shall lie down, and none shall make you afraid; and I will remove harmful animals from the land, and the sword shall not pass through your land.note
Y daré paz en la tierra, y os acostaréis sin que nadie os espante; y haré que cesen las bestias fieras de la tierra, y la espada no pasará por vuestra tierra.
7

Y perseguiréis a vuestros enemigos, y caerán a espada delante de vosotros.

And five of you shall chase a hundred, and a hundred of you shall chase ten thousand; and your enemies shall fall before you by the sword.note
Y cinco de vosotros perseguirán a cien, y cien de vosotros perseguirán a diez mil; y vuestros enemigos caerán a espada delante de vosotros.
9

11

And I will set my dwelling among you, and my soul shall not abhor you.note
Y pondré mi morada en medio de vosotros, y mi alma no os abominará.
12

Leviticus 26 meaning — idols, sabbath, sanctuary: the whole code in two verses

Before the blessings and curses begin, two verses close the Holiness Code's own opening priorities in miniature: no idols (echoing this book's own repeated warnings, most recently Leviticus 19:4, already on these pages), the sabbath kept, the sanctuary revered. Everything that follows — forty-four verses of blessing and curse — hangs entirely on these two things and the ten commandments they compress.

The blessings — rain, harvest, peace, and a people who can finally stand up straight

Ten verses of blessing move from field to nation to something closer than either: rain in season, harvest running so long into the next that threshing overlaps the grape crop and the grape crop overlaps sowing (v5), peace secure enough to lie down with nothing to fear, and victory so total that five chase a hundred, and a hundred chase ten thousand — a ratio that jumps by a factor of twenty rather than staying constant, since obedience compounds rather than merely adding up. But the blessing's real center is not weather or war: ‘I will set my dwelling among you… I will walk among you’ (vv11-12) reuses, deliberately, the exact verb for God ‘walking’ in the garden at Genesis 3:8 (already on these pages) — the same intimacy Eden lost, offered back as a covenant reward, and the same language John 1:14 (already on these pages) and Revelation 21:3 (already on these pages) use for God ‘tabernacling’ among humanity again. ⚠ V13 closes on qomemiyut, a rare word this project renders ‘upright’ — freed people who can finally stand with their heads up, not merely freed but restored to dignity.

The curses begin — and the structure is the message

What follows is thirty-two verses of escalating consequence, not a flat list — four separate waves (vv18, 21, 24, 28), each opened by the identical formula, ‘I will punish you seven times more for your sins,’ each triggered specifically by Israel's continued refusal to respond to the wave before it. The first wave (vv16-17) is disease and military defeat: terror, wasting sickness, fever, a harvest sown in vain because an enemy eats it before Israel does, and the particularly bleak detail of v17 — fleeing ‘when no one pursues you,’ the psychology of defeat outrunning any actual army.

Second wave — sky like iron, land like bronze

The first ‘seven times more’ (v18) targets agriculture directly and with a striking image: the sky itself turned to unyielding iron, the ground to bronze — no rain can fall through the one, nothing can grow from the other. Strength is ‘spent in vain’ (v20) echoes the futility already threaded through this book's blessing section in reverse: where obedience made threshing run into the grape harvest, defiance empties the same labor of any result at all.

Third wave — wild animals, and a word this chapter alone uses seven times

⚠ ‘Walk contrary to me’ (halakh imi keri) is one of the Torah's genuine translation cruxes — a rare word that occurs nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible in this sense, seven times, only in this chapter (vv21, 23, 24, 27, 28, 40, 41), itself a structural echo of the fourfold ‘seven times’ punishment formula surrounding it. KJV and ASV both keep ‘walk contrary,’ the older, more literal-sounding option; NIV shifts to ‘remain hostile toward me,’ reading the disputed root as hostility rather than mere unpredictability. See the dictionary entry for the fuller range of readings. Here the third wave sends wild animals to rob Israel of its children and cattle, leaving roads ‘deserted’ — too dangerous to travel, the countryside itself turned hostile.

Fourth wave — siege, and ten women baking in one oven

‘A sword that avenges the vengeance of a covenant’ (v25) doubles the Hebrew root for vengeance on itself — the same intensifying construction this project has already flagged in phrases like Genesis 1's own ‘swarm with swarms’ (already on these pages), here turned to a legal penalty instead of abundance. ⚠ V26's image is domestic and devastating precisely because it is small-scale: ten women, ordinarily each baking a household's own full batch, now sharing a single oven because fuel and flour have both run out, doling out bread by careful weight — and still no one eats enough. This is the verse Haggai 1 (already on these pages) will later apply directly to Judah's own post-exilic poverty: sowing much and gathering little, eating without being satisfied, the covenant curse still operating on a much smaller scale.

Fifth wave — the worst verse in the Torah, and total desolation

⚠ V29 is the bleakest single line in this book: parents eating the flesh of their own children under siege. This is not invented for shock; it is remembered by name in Israel's own later history — at the siege of Samaria (2 Kings 6:28-29, not yet on these pages) and at the fall of Jerusalem in 586 BC (Lamentations 2:20 and 4:10, already on these pages, which name this exact covenant penalty as the thing that actually happened). The rest of the wave is total: shrines destroyed, corpses piled on the ruins of the idols that failed to save anyone, cities razed, the land itself left so empty that even the enemies who move into it are ‘appalled’ (v32) — conquest without triumph, because there is nothing left worth conquering.

The land collects what it was denied — exile as the earth's own sabbath

⚠ Vv34-35 name the theological purpose behind the exile in terms this project has already set up: the land finally ‘enjoys its sabbaths’ it was denied — the direct payoff of Leviticus 25's own sabbatical-year law (already on these pages), now revealed as enforceable on a national scale, not merely a legal ideal Israel could quietly ignore. ‘The sound of a driven leaf’ (v36) putting fugitives to flight is one of the Bible's starkest images of psychological terror — not an army, not even a real threat, just the rustle of a single dead leaf enough to trigger a rout, because guilt has already done an enemy's work for it.

Confession, an uncircumcised heart, and a covenant remembered backward

⚠ V41's ‘uncircumcised heart’ (lev ha-arel) takes the physical sign of the covenant, first given at Genesis 17:11 (already on these pages), and applies it metaphorically to the organ that actually needs cutting — a figure this project expects to recur at Deuteronomy 10:16 and Jeremiah 4:4 (neither yet on these pages), and that Paul will build an entire argument on at Romans 2:29 (already on these pages): real circumcision is a matter of the heart, not the flesh. V42 remembers the patriarchal covenant in reverse chronological order — Jacob, then Isaac, then Abraham — nearest generation first, as if working backward through a family's own memory rather than reciting a fixed formula forward. The chapter's real ending is not despair: even at the furthest point of exile, v44 promises Jehovah will not ‘break my covenant,’ and v46 closes with a colophon — ‘these are the statutes and the ordinances and the laws’ — that reads like the intended close of the whole Sinai law code beginning at Exodus 19, with Leviticus 27 arriving afterward as its own appendix.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 26 both run 1–46 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Only three Masoretic paragraph breaks divide this entire chapter: open after v2, closed after v26, open after v46 — the curses section (vv14-45) is a single unbroken unit in the scroll, matching its own unbroken escalation in content. Two new dictionary entries: keri, the genuinely disputed ‘contrary’ / ‘chance-encounter’ word occurring seven times, only here; and qomemiyut, ‘upright,’ occurring only at v13.

Echoes paid. Genesis 3:8's own verb for God walking in the garden (already on these pages), reused at v12 for God walking among a redeemed people; Genesis 1's own ‘swarm with swarms’ intensifying construction (already on these pages), the same doubling pattern behind v25's ‘vengeance of the covenant’; Genesis 17:11's covenant sign of circumcision (already on these pages), applied metaphorically to the heart at v41; Leviticus 19:4's ban on idols (already on these pages), restated to open this chapter; Leviticus 25's own sabbatical-year law (already on these pages), revealed at vv34-35 as the very thing exile enforces; Lamentations 2 (already on these pages), which will name this exact chapter's covenant penalty as literally fulfilled at Jerusalem's fall; and Haggai 1 (already on these pages), which applies v26's own futility curse to a much smaller, later poverty.

Echoes planted. Deuteronomy 28 (not yet on these pages), the Torah's other great blessings-and-curses chapter, running the same structure at greater length; 2 Kings 6:28-29 (not yet on these pages), where the child-cannibalism curse of v29 is remembered as historical fact at the siege of Samaria; 2 Chronicles 36:21 (not yet on these pages), which will cite this chapter's own land-sabbath language as literally fulfilled by the length of the Babylonian exile; Deuteronomy 10:16 and Jeremiah 4:4 (neither yet on these pages), which will repeat v41's own uncircumcised-heart figure; and Romans 2:29 (already on these pages), where Paul builds directly on it.

⚠ A note on order: Leviticus stands complete on these pages, chapters 1–27.
