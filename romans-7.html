The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. Chapter 6 ended on the wages of sin and the gracious gift of God; chapter 7 takes up what the last two chapters left standing, the law. It answers in three moves: a picture from marriage law, in which a death ends a claim; the objection that the law is itself sin; and a first-person speaker who finds the law holy, himself divided, and a rescue he can only thank God for.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 6; this library dates the letter no more closely than those chapters do. The only time-markers the chapter uses are the speaker’s own: “once” and “when the commandment came” (verses 9–10), and the shift from past to present tense at verse 14.

¿O no saben, hermanos—porque hablo a personas que conocen ley—que la ley se enseñorea del hombre por todo el tiempo que vive?
2
ἡ γὰρ ὕπανδρος γυνὴ τῷ ζῶντι ἀνδρὶ δέδεται νόμῳ· ἐὰν δὲ ἀποθάνῃ ὁ ἀνήρ, κατήργηται ἀπὸ τοῦ νόμου τοῦ ἀνδρός.

Porque la mujer casada está atada por ley al marido que vive; pero si el marido muere, queda desligada de la ley del marido.
3
ἄρα οὖν ζῶντος τοῦ ἀνδρὸς μοιχαλὶς χρηματίσει ἐὰν γένηται ἀνδρὶ ἑτέρῳ· ἐὰν δὲ ἀποθάνῃ ὁ ἀνήρ, ἐλευθέρα ἐστὶν ἀπὸ τοῦ νόμου, τοῦ μὴ εἶναι αὐτὴν μοιχαλίδα γενομένην ἀνδρὶ ἑτέρῳ.
So then, while the husband is living, she will be called an adulteress if she becomes another man’s; but if the husband dies, she is free from the law, so that she is not an adulteress when she has become another man’s.note
Así que, mientras el marido vive, será llamada adúltera si llega a ser de otro hombre; pero si el marido muere, es libre de la ley, de modo que no es adúltera cuando ha llegado a ser de otro hombre.
4
Ὥστε, ἀδελφοί μου, καὶ ὑμεῖς ἐθανατώθητε τῷ νόμῳ διὰ τοῦ σώματος τοῦ Χριστοῦ, εἰς τὸ γενέσθαι ὑμᾶς ἑτέρῳ, τῷ ἐκ νεκρῶν ἐγερθέντι ἵνα καρποφορήσωμεν τῷ θεῷ.
So, my brothers, you also were put to death to the law through the body of Christ, so that you might become another’s—his who was raised from the dead—in order that we might bear fruit for God.note
Así que, hermanos míos, también ustedes fueron puestos a muerte a la ley mediante el cuerpo de Cristo, para que lleguen a ser de otro—del que fue levantado de entre los muertos—a fin de que demos fruto para Dios.
5
ὅτε γὰρ ἦμεν ἐν τῇ σαρκί, τὰ παθήματα τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν τὰ διὰ τοῦ νόμου ἐνηργεῖτο ἐν τοῖς μέλεσιν ἡμῶν εἰς τὸ καρποφορῆσαι τῷ θανάτῳ·
For when we were in the flesh, the passions of sins that were through the law were at work in our members, so as to bear fruit for death.note
Porque cuando estábamos en la carne, las pasiones de los pecados que eran mediante la ley actuaban en nuestros miembros, para dar fruto para la muerte.
6
νυνὶ δὲ κατηργήθημεν ἀπὸ τοῦ νόμου, ἀποθανόντες ἐν ᾧ κατειχόμεθα, ὥστε δουλεύειν ἡμᾶς ἐν καινότητι πνεύματος καὶ οὐ παλαιότητι γράμματος.
But now we have been released from the law, having died to that by which we were held, so that we are slaves in newness of spirit and not in oldness of letter.note
Pero ahora hemos sido desligados de la ley, habiendo muerto a aquello en lo que éramos retenidos, de modo que somos esclavos en novedad de espíritu y no en vejez de letra.
7
Τί οὖν ἐροῦμεν; ὁ νόμος ἁμαρτία; μὴ γένοιτο· ἀλλὰ τὴν ἁμαρτίαν οὐκ ἔγνων εἰ μὴ διὰ νόμου, τήν τε γὰρ ἐπιθυμίαν οὐκ ᾔδειν εἰ μὴ ὁ νόμος ἔλεγεν· Οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις·
What shall we say, then? Is the law sin? May it never be! But I did not know sin except through law; for I would not have known coveting if the law had not said, “You shall not covet.”note
¿Qué diremos, pues? ¿Es pecado la ley? ¡Que nunca suceda! Pero yo no conocí el pecado sino mediante ley; porque tampoco habría conocido la codicia si la ley no hubiera dicho: «No codiciarás».
8
ἀφορμὴν δὲ λαβοῦσα ἡ ἁμαρτία διὰ τῆς ἐντολῆς κατειργάσατο ἐν ἐμοὶ πᾶσαν ἐπιθυμίαν, χωρὶς γὰρ νόμου ἁμαρτία νεκρά.
But sin, taking an opportunity through the commandment, worked out in me all coveting; for apart from law sin is dead.note
Pero el pecado, tomando oportunidad mediante el mandamiento, obró en mí toda codicia; porque aparte de ley el pecado está muerto.
9
ἐγὼ δὲ ἔζων χωρὶς νόμου ποτέ· ἐλθούσης δὲ τῆς ἐντολῆς ἡ ἁμαρτία ἀνέζησεν,
And I was alive apart from law once; but when the commandment came, sin came to life again,note
Y yo vivía aparte de ley en otro tiempo; pero cuando vino el mandamiento, el pecado volvió a vivir,
10
ἐγὼ δὲ ἀπέθανον, καὶ εὑρέθη μοι ἡ ἐντολὴ ἡ εἰς ζωὴν αὕτη εἰς θάνατον·
and I died; and the commandment that was for life—this very one was found by me to be for death.note
y yo morí; y el mandamiento que era para vida—este mismo fue hallado por mí para muerte.
11
ἡ γὰρ ἁμαρτία ἀφορμὴν λαβοῦσα διὰ τῆς ἐντολῆς ἐξηπάτησέν με καὶ διʼ αὐτῆς ἀπέκτεινεν.
For sin, taking an opportunity through the commandment, deceived me and through it killed me.note
Porque el pecado, tomando oportunidad mediante el mandamiento, me engañó y mediante él me mató.
12
ὥστε ὁ μὲν νόμος ἅγιος, καὶ ἡ ἐντολὴ ἁγία καὶ δικαία καὶ ἀγαθή.
So the law, for its part, is holy, and the commandment is holy and righteous and good.note
De modo que la ley, por su parte, es santa, y el mandamiento es santo y justo y bueno.
13
Τὸ οὖν ἀγαθὸν ἐμοὶ ἐγένετο θάνατος; μὴ γένοιτο· ἀλλὰ ἡ ἁμαρτία, ἵνα φανῇ ἁμαρτία διὰ τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ μοι κατεργαζομένη θάνατον· ἵνα γένηται καθʼ ὑπερβολὴν ἁμαρτωλὸς ἡ ἁμαρτία διὰ τῆς ἐντολῆς.
Did what is good, then, become death to me? May it never be! It was sin, so that it might be shown to be sin, working out death in me through what is good—so that through the commandment sin might become sinful beyond all measure.note
¿Lo bueno, entonces, llegó a ser muerte para mí? ¡Que nunca suceda! Fue el pecado, para que se mostrara como pecado, obrando muerte en mí mediante lo bueno—para que mediante el mandamiento el pecado llegara a ser pecaminoso sobremanera.
14
Οἴδαμεν γὰρ ὅτι ὁ νόμος πνευματικός ἐστιν· ἐγὼ δὲ σάρκινός εἰμι, πεπραμένος ὑπὸ τὴν ἁμαρτίαν.
For we know that the law is spiritual; but I am made of flesh, sold under sin.note
Porque sabemos que la ley es espiritual; pero yo soy de carne, vendido bajo el pecado.
15

For what I work out I do not know; for I do not practice what I wish, but what I hate, this I do.note
Porque lo que obro no lo conozco; pues no practico lo que quiero, sino que lo que odio, esto hago.
16
εἰ δὲ ὃ οὐ θέλω τοῦτο ποιῶ, σύμφημι τῷ νόμῳ ὅτι καλός.
But if what I do not wish, this I do, I agree with the law that it is good.note
Pero si lo que no quiero, esto hago, estoy de acuerdo con la ley en que es buena.
17
νυνὶ δὲ οὐκέτι ἐγὼ κατεργάζομαι αὐτὸ ἀλλὰ ἡ οἰκοῦσα ἐν ἐμοὶ ἁμαρτία.
But now it is no longer I who work it out, but the sin that dwells in me.note
Pero ahora ya no soy yo quien lo obra, sino el pecado que habita en mí.
18

For I know that in me—that is, in my flesh—nothing good dwells; for wishing lies beside me, but working out what is good does not.note
Porque sé que en mí—es decir, en mi carne—no habita nada bueno; pues el querer está junto a mí, pero el obrar lo bueno, no.

For I do not do the good that I wish, but the evil that I do not wish, this I practice.note
Porque no hago lo bueno que quiero, sino lo malo que no quiero, esto practico.
20
εἰ δὲ ὃ οὐ θέλω τοῦτο ποιῶ, οὐκέτι ἐγὼ κατεργάζομαι αὐτὸ ἀλλὰ ἡ οἰκοῦσα ἐν ἐμοὶ ἁμαρτία.
But if what I do not wish, this I do, it is no longer I who work it out, but the sin that dwells in me.note
Pero si lo que no quiero, esto hago, ya no soy yo quien lo obra, sino el pecado que habita en mí.
21
Εὑρίσκω ἄρα τὸν νόμον τῷ θέλοντι ἐμοὶ ποιεῖν τὸ καλὸν ὅτι ἐμοὶ τὸ κακὸν παράκειται·
I find, then, the law for me who wish to do what is good, that evil lies beside me;note
Encuentro, pues, la ley para mí, que quiero hacer lo bueno: que lo malo está junto a mí;
22
συνήδομαι γὰρ τῷ νόμῳ τοῦ θεοῦ κατὰ τὸν ἔσω ἄνθρωπον,
for I delight in the law of God according to the inner man,note
porque me deleito en la ley de Dios según el hombre interior,
23
βλέπω δὲ ἕτερον νόμον ἐν τοῖς μέλεσίν μου ἀντιστρατευόμενον τῷ νόμῳ τοῦ νοός μου καὶ αἰχμαλωτίζοντά με ἐν τῷ νόμῳ τῆς ἁμαρτίας τῷ ὄντι ἐν τοῖς μέλεσίν μου.
but I see a different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind and taking me captive in the law of sin that is in my members.note
pero veo en mis miembros una ley diferente que hace guerra contra la ley de mi mente y me lleva cautivo en la ley del pecado que está en mis miembros.

ταλαίπωρος ἐγὼ ἄνθρωπος· τίς με ῥύσεται ἐκ τοῦ σώματος τοῦ θανάτου τούτου;
Wretched man that I am! Who will rescue me from the body of this death?note
¡Hombre desventurado que soy! ¿Quién me librará del cuerpo de esta muerte?
25

Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord! So then, I myself with the mind am a slave to the law of God, but with the flesh to the law of sin.note
¡Gracias a Dios mediante Jesucristo, nuestro Señor! Así que yo mismo, con la mente, soy esclavo de la ley de Dios, pero con la carne, de la ley del pecado.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · Ἢ ἀγνοεῖτε … ὁ νόμος κυριεύει τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἐφʼ ὅσον χρόνον ζῇ … ἡ ὕπανδρος γυνή … μοιχαλὶς χρηματίσει Ē agnoeite … ho nomos kyrieuei tou anthrōpou eph’ hoson chronon zē … hē hypandros gynē … moichalis chrēmatisei — Romans 7:1–3: “the law is lord over a man for as long as he lives.” A marriage law, a limit set by death, and a name that a woman earns by an act

“Or do you not know” (ē agnoeite, Romans 7:1). The opener is the one of Romans 6:3, and those two are the only verses in the New Testament that begin with these words (ē agnoeite, searched). As there, the question counts on an answer the readers already hold, and Paul says what it is: “I am speaking to people who know law.” The Greek has no article (nomon, “law”), and the shelf divides over it. NWT 1984 “(for I am speaking to those who know law,)” keeps the bare noun. KJV “for I speak to them that know the law”, ASV “for I speak to men who know the law” and NIV “those who know the law” add “the,” and then every reader hears the Law of Moses. The bare noun is wider: law as such, which the readers know how to reason about, and the example that follows could come from any system of marriage law (what Jewish and Roman marriage law said is not checked here). The TLB “dear Jewish brothers in Christ” settles who is addressed by adding “Jewish,” which the Greek does not have, and the letter has already placed its readers among “the nations” (Romans 1:13: “among you also, just as among the rest of the nations”).

“Is lord over” (kyrieuei). The verb is the one of 6:9 (“death is no longer lord over him”) and 6:14 (“sin will not be lord over you”), and it stands in four verses of Romans (kyrieuō, searched): those two, this one, and Romans 14:9. So the argument has now named three lords, death, sin and law, and each of them is given a limit. This translation keeps “lord” because the verb is built on kyrios. KJV “hath dominion”, ASV “hath dominion”, Geneva “hath dominion” and Douay “hath dominion” are four of the seven; NIV “has authority over” and NWT 1984 “is master over” are the others that translate the verse, and the TLB turns the whole sentence round into its conclusion, “when a person dies the law no longer holds him in its power.”

“For as long as he lives” (eph’ hoson chronon zē). Nothing in the clause says who is the one living. Douay “as long as it liveth” keeps the same openness from the Latin quanto tempore vivit (checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org), where “it” can be the law. Verse 2 settles it: the one who dies is the husband, and the law goes on. The whole phrase stands in three verses (eph’ hoson chronon, searched): here, of a man; in 1 Corinthians 7:39, of a husband (“a wife is bound for as long as her husband lives”); and in Galatians 4:1, of an heir “for as long as” he is a child (these two not yet on these pages). That a death ends what the law demands of a person is also a saying of the Talmud, quoted at Romans 6:7.

“The married woman” (hē hypandros gynē, Romans 7:2). Hypandros is “under a man,” the same hypo as in “under law” and “under grace” (6:14), and it stands once in the New Testament, here (hypandros, searched). The Greek Old Testament has it three times in Rahlfs’s text, Proverbs 6:24 and Sirach 9:9 and 41:21 (verse numbers by the usual division; these three not yet on these pages), each time in a warning about another man’s wife. The verb is the perfect dedetai, “has been bound and stays bound”: she is held, in the way verse 6 will say “we were held.”

“She has been released from the law of the husband” (katērgētai apo tou nomou tou andros). The verb is katargeō, “make idle, void,” which this translation prints “abolish” where an object follows (3:3, 3:31, 4:14, 6:6). Here it takes “from,” and English cannot say “abolished from.” What is made void is the bond; neither the woman nor the law is abolished, since the law holds for the living. The same verb, with the same “from,” comes back in verse 6, so that the woman’s release and ours are one word. The shelf has four words for it: KJV “she is loosed from the law of her husband” and Douay “she is loosed from the law of her husband”; ASV “she is discharged from the law of the husband” and NWT 1984 “she is discharged from the law of her husband”; Geneva “she is delivered from the Law of the man”; and NIV “she is released from the law that binds her to him,” which adds the explaining clause the genitive “of the husband” leaves unsaid.

“She will be called an adulteress” (moichalis chrēmatisei, Romans 7:3). Chrēmatizō is used in nine verses of the New Testament (searched; the related noun at Romans 11:4 is not counted), and in two of them it means “be called”: here and Acts 11:26 (“the disciples were first called Christians”). In the other seven it is about a message from God, and the shipped pages print it “warned” (Matthew 2:12, Hebrews 11:7), “disclosed” (Luke 2:26) and “divinely instructed” (Acts 10:22). So in the two cases of “be called,” the word is about a title that is announced, not one that is chosen. Here the name is one the law gives for an act. The act is stated in the idiom “if she becomes another man’s” (ean genētai andri heterō), which uses the very words (genētai andri heterō) of the Greek of Deuteronomy 24:2, where the page prints “goes and becomes another man’s,” and the sense there is marriage. NIV “if she has sexual relations with another man” says something the Greek does not, and KJV “she be married to another man” says what the idiom means. The noun moichalis stands in six verses (moichalis, searched), and in three of them a generation is called by it (Matthew 12:39, 16:4, Mark 8:38); here it is a woman, in two clauses: she is one if she goes to another man while her husband lives, and she is not one after he dies. The law made the name, and a death removes the law’s claim to give it, which is the point of the picture.

Verse 4 · καὶ ὑμεῖς ἐθανατώθητε τῷ νόμῳ διὰ τοῦ σώματος τοῦ Χριστοῦ, εἰς τὸ γενέσθαι ὑμᾶς ἑτέρῳ kai hymeis ethanatōthēte tō nomō dia tou sōmatos tou Christou, eis to genesthai hymas heterō — Romans 7:4: “you also were put to death to the law through the body of Christ.” Who dies in the picture, what “the body of Christ” is here, and the marriage that Paul names without using the word

“You also were put to death to the law” (ethanatōthēte tō nomō). The picture of verses 2–3 had a husband who dies and a wife who is released. Verse 4 does not say that the law died, and it does not say that the husband was the law. It says that the readers were put to death, so the picture shows what a death does to a bond, and it is not an allegory in which each person stands for a part (that is a reading of the verse; it fits verse 6, where the ones released from the law are the ones who died). The verb is the passive thanatoō, which stands in eleven verses of the New Testament (thanatoō, searched). In six of them people put someone to death (Matthew 10:21, 26:59, 27:1, Mark 13:12, 14:55, Luke 21:16), and the other five turn it on believers or on Christ: here; 8:13 (“you put to death the practices of the body”); 8:36 (“we are being put to death all day long”); and 2 Corinthians 6:9 and 1 Peter 3:18, where it is said of Christ, “put to death in the flesh” (these two not yet on these pages). The passive does not name who does it. The dative “to the law” is the dative of 6:2 (“we who died to sin”), with the same open relation: death puts the person out of reach of a claim, and the verse does not yet say what the claim was.

The shelf makes a state of it. KJV “ye also are become dead to the law”, Geneva “are dead also to the Law” and Douay “you also are become dead to the law” describe where the readers now stand, and the NIV “you also died to the law” makes it something they did. ASV “ye also were made dead to the law” and NWT 1984 “YOU also were made dead to the Law” keep the passive, and are the two closest to the Greek. The TLB “as it were, with Christ on the cross” puts in “with Christ” and “on the cross,” which verse 4 does not have; they are the words of 6:6 (“crucified with him”) brought forward.

“Through the body of Christ” (dia tou sōmatos tou Christou). Readers differ over which body. One reading is the body that died: a body is how Christ died, and the letter to the Colossians says that God “reconciled you in the body of his flesh through death” (Colossians 1:22), and Hebrews 10:10 has “through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (Greek checked; not yet on these pages). The other is the body of which the readers are members, the congregation, “one body in Christ” (Romans 12:5), so that the death to the law is something they went through by being joined to him. The verse, by itself, does not decide, and this translation keeps the words as they stand. Six of the seven print the phrase and leave the choice open: KJV “by the body of Christ”, Geneva “by the body of Christ”, Douay “by the body of Christ”, ASV “through the body of Christ”, NIV “through the body of Christ” and NWT 1984 “through the body of the Christ” (it prints the article that the Greek has). The TLB drops the phrase for “with Christ on the cross,” which settles the question for the first reading.

“That you might become another’s” (eis to genesthai hymas heterō). The marriage is never named; it is in the grammar. The clause is built from the words of verse 3, genētai andri heterō, “she becomes another man’s,” with the “man” dropped, and heterō can mean not just “a second” but “one of a different kind” (Koine does not always keep that line; reported, not checked), which would suit the context: the new husband is the one “raised from the dead,” so that, unlike the first, he cannot die and release her again. The verb egerthenti is the passive of 6:4 and 6:9, again without naming who does the raising. The shelf supplies the word the Greek leaves out: KJV “that ye should be married to another” says it outright, and the TLB says it with a hedge, “so to speak,” after “married” in quotation marks; while ASV “ye should be joined to another”, NIV “you might belong to another”, Douay “you may belong to another” and NWT 1984 “YOU might become another’s” leave it as Paul does. The same dative with a verb of dying is Galatians 2:19, “through law I died to law, so that I might live to God” (Greek checked; not yet on these pages).

“Bear fruit for God” (karpophorēsōmen tō theō). The verb karpophoreō stands in eight verses (karpophoreō, searched): four in the Gospels (Matthew 13:23, Mark 4:20, 4:28, Luke 8:15), this verse and the next, and two in Colossians 1:6 and 1:10. Its picture is a plant, as at Mark 4:28 (“the earth bears fruit by itself”), and a marriage invites the hearer to take the fruit as children; the Greek never says so. What it does say is that fruit is owed to someone, and in verse 5 the dative is tō thanatō, “for death.” The same verb, the same dative, two owners: that is the contrast of the section. The change from “you” to “we” in this verse is real, too: Paul puts himself in the fruit-bearing but not in the dying that is addressed to his readers. KJV “bring forth fruit unto God”, ASV “bring forth fruit unto God” and Douay “bring forth fruit to God” use the English of birth for a verb about plants; NIV “bear fruit for God” and NWT 1984 “bear fruit to God” do not. The TLB adds the explanation “good deeds,” which is not in the verse.

Verses 5–6 · ὅτε ἦμεν ἐν τῇ σαρκί, τὰ παθήματα τῶν ἁμαρτιῶν … κατηργήθημεν ἀπὸ τοῦ νόμου … ἐν καινότητι πνεύματος καὶ οὐ παλαιότητι γράμματος hote ēmen en tē sarki, ta pathēmata tōn hamartiōn … katērgēthēmen apo tou nomou … en kainotēti pneumatos kai ou palaiotēti grammatos — Romans 7:5–6: “when we were in the flesh” and “newness of spirit, not oldness of letter.” Passions that worked through the law, a release, and a variant that puts the death on the law

“When we were in the flesh” (hote ēmen en tē sarki, Romans 7:5). The phrase is the past tense of what Romans 8:8 says in the present (“those who are in flesh cannot please God”) and of the denial of 8:9 (“you are not in flesh but in Spirit”): the readers were in the flesh, and the “when” marks that it is over. The shelf is split on what it means. KJV “when we were in the flesh”, ASV “when we were in the flesh”, Geneva “when we were in the flesh” and Douay “when we were in the flesh” keep the preposition. NIV “in the realm of the flesh” and NWT 1984 “in accord with the flesh” decide what “in” means, the first a sphere and the second a way of living, and the NWT’s wording is the one it prints for “according to the flesh” at Romans 8:4, 5, 12 and 13 (checked in the 1984 text), although this Greek is en, “in,” and not kata, “according to.” The Greek does not say whether the flesh is the body, a power or an age, and the word is left as it stands, to be filled in by the use of the next chapter.

“The passions of sins that were through the law” (ta pathēmata tōn hamartiōn ta dia tou nomou). Pathēma stands in sixteen verses of the New Testament (pathēma, searched), and in fourteen of them it is “sufferings,” as in Romans 8:18 (“the sufferings of the present season”); the other two are this verse and Galatians 5:24 (“the passions and the desires,” not yet on these pages). The word is built on paschō, “undergo, suffer,” and both uses are about what a person undergoes; the genitive “of sins” says whose they are: passions that belong to sins in the plural, the acts, and not to sin in the singular, the power, which is the word from verse 7 on. KJV “the motions of sins” uses “motions” in its old sense of movements within; Geneva “the affections of sins” has another old word; Douay “the passions of sins” is the one that keeps the genitive. ASV “the sinful passions”, NIV “the sinful passions” and NWT 1984 “the sinful passions” turn the noun “sins” into an adjective. The TLB “sinful desires” reads the passions as desires, epithymia, the word of verses 7–8.

“Through the law” is the preposition dia that the next verses will repeat, “through the commandment” (vv. 8, 11, 13). Here the Greek states only that the passions came by way of the law; it does not say how. NIV “aroused by the law” and NWT 1984 “excited by the Law” supply a verb for the preposition, and it is the right verb, as verse 8 shows, but it is the interpreter’s at this point. The same claim has been made in the letter more than once, in other words: “through law comes the recognition of sin” (3:20), “the law works out wrath” (4:15), “law came in alongside, so that the trespass might increase” (5:20). Enērgeito, “were at work,” is a verb that stands in Romans only here (energeō, searched), and the place where they worked is “our members”: the members that were to be presented to God as weapons of righteousness (6:13, 6:19) are where the passions were at work, and where, in verse 23, another law is seen.

“We have been released from the law” (katērgēthēmen apo tou nomou, Romans 7:6). The verb and the “from” are those of verse 2, so that the readers’ release is the woman’s, and its cause is the same, a death: “having died to that by which we were held.” Katechō, “hold,” stands in Romans in two verses (katechō, searched): here, and 1:18, where people “hold the truth down in unrighteousness.” In chapter 1 it is humankind that holds; here the readers are the ones who were held. ⚠ Who died, in the Greek? The printed text has apothanontes, “having died,” of the readers, and the SBLGNT has no apparatus note at the word. Scrivener’s Textus Receptus (1894) has apothanontos, a genitive singular (checked on Biblehub’s reproduction of the editions, where Tischendorf’s eighth edition has apothanontes), and with it the death falls on the thing that held them: KJV “that being dead wherein we were held”, and Geneva “he being dead in whom we were holden.” The other versions that print the sentence put it the way this text has it: ASV “having died to that wherein we were held” and NIV “by dying to what once bound us.” Douay “from the law of death” has yet another: the Clementine Latin printed at vulgate.org reads a lege morientes in quo detinebamur, “from the law, dying in that by which we were held,” so the Latin behind “law of death” is not the one printed there (the older Latin reading a lege mortis is reported, not checked). Verse 4 settles what matters for the sense: the readers were put to death to the law, so that they are not released by the law dying.

“Slaves in newness of spirit and not in oldness of letter” (douleuein en kainotēti pneumatos kai ou palaiotēti grammatos). Douleuō is the verb of 6:6 (“no longer be slaves to sin”), of the same family as the slaves and the enslaving of 6:16–22, and this translation keeps “slaves.” The release is from one master’s claim into another’s, and not into having none. NWT 1984 “slaves in a new sense” agrees, and KJV “we should serve”, ASV “we serve”, NIV “we serve” and Douay “we should serve” use the softer word. Kainotēs, “newness,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this and Romans 6:4 (“newness of life”), and palaiotēs, “oldness,” in this verse alone (kainotēs and palaiotēs, searched). The contrast of spirit and letter is Paul’s in three verses: Romans 2:29 (“by spirit, not by letter”), this, and 2 Corinthians 3:6 (“the letter kills, but the spirit gives life,” Greek checked; not yet on these pages). Gramma is the thing written; NIV “the written code” and NWT 1984 “the written code” print the meaning of a code, which the noun does not have. The word is printed “spirit” in lower case, as at 2:29; NIV “the new way of the Spirit” and Geneva “newness of Spirit” use the capital, and the oldest manuscripts, written in capitals throughout, do not mark the difference. The verse does not say who gives the spirit; it says only that the service is of a new kind, and the chapter that follows is where “Spirit” is the word again and again (8:2, 4, 9, 11).

Verse 7 · Τί οὖν ἐροῦμεν; ὁ νόμος ἁμαρτία; μὴ γένοιτο … τήν τε γὰρ ἐπιθυμίαν οὐκ ᾔδειν εἰ μὴ ὁ νόμος ἔλεγεν· Οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις Ti oun eroumen? ho nomos hamartia? mē genoito … tēn te gar epithymian ouk ēdein ei mē ho nomos elegen: Ouk epithymēseis — Romans 7:7: “Is the law sin?” and the commandment “You shall not covet,” quoted without its objects. Why the one commandment about the inside is the one Paul chooses, and where the “I” begins

“What shall we say, then? Is the law sin? May it never be!” The formula opens a turn in the argument six times in Romans: ti oun eroumen, “What shall we say, then?”, stands in six verses (ti oun eroumen, searched): 4:1, 6:1, here, 8:31, and 9:14 and 9:30. And mē genoito, “May it never be!”, stands in ten verses of Romans, of which this is the sixth (3:4, 3:6, 3:31, 6:2, 6:15, here); the seventh is verse 13. The objection grows out of verses 5–6: if the passions of sins worked “through the law,” and the release from sin is a release from the law, then law and sin look like one thing. The Greek is a noun, “Is the law sin?”, and not an adjective, and the shelf mostly keeps it: KJV “Is the law sin?”, ASV “Is the law sin?”, Geneva “Is the Law sin?”, Douay “Is the law sin?” and NWT 1984 “Is the Law sin?” are five of the seven. NIV “Is the law sinful?” softens the noun to an adjective, and the TLB “am I suggesting that these laws of God are evil?” turns Paul’s question about the law into one about what he himself is saying.

“I did not know sin except through law” (tēn hamartian ouk egnōn ei mē dia nomou). The claim is about knowing, and not about existing. Sin was in the world before law (Romans 5:13: “until law, sin was in the world”); what the law gave was the means to identify it, as in 3:20 (“through law comes the recognition of sin”). The verse says it in the first person, and the first person is new: the pronoun “I, me, my” stands once in verses 1–6 (“my brothers,” verse 4) and twenty-four times in verses 8–25 (counted in the Greek). The shelf differs on the tense and the object. KJV “I had not known sin, but by the law”, ASV “I had not known sin, except through the law” and Geneva “I knew not sin, but by the Law” keep it as a fact about sin. NIV “I would not have known what sin was” changes the object, from sin to the concept of sin. NWT 1984 “I would not have come to know sin” keeps the object and spells out the process. Douay “But I do not know sin, but by the law” is in the present tense, where the Clementine Latin it translates has the perfect, peccatum non cognovi (checked at vulgate.org). The TLB “it was the law that showed me my sin” makes the law the actor.

“Coveting” (epithymia) and the commandment. The noun epithymia stands in thirty-seven verses of the New Testament (epithymia, searched), in Romans at 1:24, 6:12, here, in verse 8, and at 13:14 (now on these pages), and it is the word for “desire” in itself: it is Jesus’ own in “I have earnestly desired to eat this Passover with you” (Luke 22:15), where the Greek sets the noun beside its verb. This translation prints “desires” at 1:24 and 6:12, and “coveting” here, because the noun is the noun of the commandment’s own verb, epithymeō, which stands in Romans in two verses, here and 13:9 (searched; now on these pages). The shelf cannot keep the pair together. ASV “coveting” and the verb “covet”, NWT 1984 “covetousness” and “covet,” and NIV “coveting” and “covet” do. KJV “lust” for the noun and “covet” for the verb breaks the pair here and then prints the noun again as “concupiscence” in verse 8, so that one Greek noun has two English words in two verses; Geneva “lust” carries it into the commandment (“Thou shalt not lust”); and Douay “concupiscence” for the noun and “covet” for the verb breaks the pair as well. The TLB “evil desires” adds “evil,” and has the law say “You must not have evil desires in your heart,” which is not what the commandment says.

“You shall not covet” with no object. The commandment in the Law has objects. The Hebrew of Exodus 20:17 (the site’s verse 13) has chamad twice, for the neighbour’s house and wife, and the Hebrew of Deuteronomy 5:21 (the site’s verse 17) has two verbs, chamad for the wife and hit’avvah for the house (searched). The Greek Old Testament has the one verb for them all, ouk epithymēseis, in both books, in Rahlfs’s text, and Paul keeps that verb and cuts the objects. Everything the commandment names is something another person has; Paul’s version names none, and so the commandment reads as a prohibition of a state of the person, not of an act (a reading of the bare verb). A Greek Jewish discourse, 4 Maccabees (a work of uncertain date, usually placed in the first century; reported, not checked), quotes the same commandment (4 Maccabees 2:5, the verse number by the usual division, the Greek read in Rahlfs’s text) to argue the opposite: that because the law says “you shall not covet,” reason must be able to rule the desires. Paul uses the same sentence the other way round, as the place where sin finds its opportunity (verse 8), and nothing in the letter shows that he knew that work.

Who is “I”? From here the chapter speaks in the first person singular, and readers have taken that “I” in at least four ways, which this translation sets down and does not vote on (the history of each is reported here, not checked): Paul himself, looking back on a life under the law; Adam, whose story (a commandment, a deception, a death) the verses echo, with the echo sounded at verses 9 and 11; Israel at Sinai; and a speaking “I” that stands for anyone under the law, a figure of speech and not a biography. Verses 14–25 add the further question whether the “I” is the one who was or the one who is. The notes on those verses take each reading where the text gives it a foothold.

Verses 8–11 · ἀφορμὴν λαβοῦσα ἡ ἁμαρτία διὰ τῆς ἐντολῆς … χωρὶς γὰρ νόμου ἁμαρτία νεκρά … ἐγὼ δὲ ἔζων … ἡ ἁμαρτία ἀνέζησεν, ἐγὼ δὲ ἀπέθανον … ἐξηπάτησέν με καὶ διʼ αὐτῆς ἀπέκτεινεν aphormēn labousa hē hamartia dia tēs entolēs … chōris gar nomou hamartia nekra … egō de ezōn … hē hamartia anezēsen, egō de apethanon … exēpatēsen me kai di’ autēs apekteinen — Romans 7:8–11: sin takes an opportunity through the commandment, comes to life, deceives and kills. Six verbs with sin for their subject, and the sound of Genesis 2–3 under them

“Taking an opportunity” (aphormēn labousa, Romans 7:8 and 7:11). Aphormē is a starting point, a base from which something is launched (the sense the lexica give; not checked here), and it stands in six verses of the New Testament, seven times (aphormē, searched): here twice, and 2 Corinthians 5:12, 2 Corinthians 11:12 (twice), Galatians 5:13 and 1 Timothy 5:14 (these four verses not yet on these pages). “Opportunity” is the English word that carries it. The shelf has three: KJV “taking occasion by the commandment”, Douay “taking occasion by the commandment” and Geneva “took an occasion by the commandment” have the older word; NIV “seizing the opportunity afforded by the commandment” has this translation’s and adds “afforded,” which the Greek does not say; and NWT 1984 “receiving an inducement through the commandment” has a motive, where its own revision of 2013 prints “finding the opportunity afforded by the commandment” (checked in both). The phrase “through the commandment” can go with the taking or with the working out. ASV “sin, finding occasion, wrought in me through the commandment all manner of coveting” attaches it to the working, and the other versions to the taking; in either, the commandment is the means by which sin acts, and this translation keeps it beside the participle. The TLB “sin used this law against evil desires by reminding me that such desires are wrong” makes the commandment a reminder, and the Greek has sin acting by means of it and not the commandment doing the reminding.

“Worked out in me all coveting” (katergasato en emoi pasan epithymian). The verb is the katergazomai of 4:15 (“the law works out wrath”), 5:3 and 2:9, and it will be the verb of verses 13, 15, 17, 18 and 20; whatever the agent works out, it works out to a result. Pasan is “all” or “every kind of.” KJV “all manner of concupiscence”, ASV “all manner of coveting”, NWT 1984 “covetousness of every sort” and NIV “every kind of coveting” take it as “every kind,” and the translation keeps the plain “all.” Then comes a sentence with no verb: chōris gar nomou hamartia nekra, “for apart from law, sin dead.” This translation supplies the present, “is dead,” and so do ASV “apart from the law sin is dead” and Geneva “without the Law sin is dead”; KJV “without the law sin was dead”, Douay “without the law sin was dead” and NWT 1984 “apart from law sin was dead” supply the past, which turns a general statement into a story about the writer. “Dead” cannot mean that sin did not exist: the letter has said that “until law, sin was in the world” (5:13). It means that sin is idle, with no starting point, until the commandment gives it one.

“I was alive apart from law once” (egō de ezōn chōris nomou pote, Romans 7:9). The “once” is the question. Three answers have been given, each tied to a reading of “I” (reported, not checked for their history): Paul as a child, before he had to keep the commandments; Adam, before the commandment of Genesis 2 (“on the day you eat from it, you will surely die,” Genesis 2:17); and Israel or the human race before the law came, in the stretch the letter has described as “from Adam until Moses” (Romans 5:14). The text offers one hold on the choice, in its next clause: anezēsen, “came to life again,” says that sin had lived once before. The verb anazaō stands in two verses of the printed Greek text of the New Testament, here and Luke 15:24, where the father says of the son in the parable, “was dead and has come back to life” (anazaō, searched); in Luke the one who was dead is the person, and here it is sin. KJV “sin revived”, ASV “sin revived”, Geneva “sin revived” and Douay “sin revived” are four of the seven; NWT 1984 “sin came to life again” keeps the prefix; NIV “sin sprang to life” drops “again”; and the TLB “I realized that I had broken the law and was a sinner, doomed to die” takes sin out of the sentence, so that nothing comes back to life.

“And I died” (egō de apethanon, Romans 7:10). In the exchange of verses 8–9 the roles trade places: apart from law, sin is dead and I am alive; with the commandment, sin lives and I die. The commandment that was “for life” is the commandment of the Law’s own promise, “the one who does them will live by them” (Leviticus 18:5) and “so choose life” (Deuteronomy 30:19). The Greek is a passive, heurethē moi, “was found by me,” and the shelf is divided: Geneva “was found to be unto me unto death” and Douay “was found to be unto death to me” keep it, and KJV “I found to be unto death”, ASV “this I found to be unto death” and NWT 1984 “this I found to be to death” make the speaker the finder. The NIV “the very commandment that was intended to bring life actually brought death” adds “intended,” “bring” and “actually,” and puts a purpose where the Greek has only “the one for life.” The prepositions are the same, eis zōēn and eis thanaton, so that the sentence has the shape of the two roads of Deuteronomy 30 with the commandment standing on the wrong one.

“Deceived me and through it killed me” (exēpatēsen me kai di’ autēs apekteinen, Romans 7:11). In verses 8–11 sin is the subject of six verbs: two of “taking,” and “worked out,” “came to life,” “deceived” and “killed.” The speaker is the object of the last two, “me” twice, and “I died” of verse 10 is the same event told from the other side. Exapataō, “deceive,” stands in six verses of the New Testament (exapataō, searched): here, Romans 16:18, 1 Corinthians 3:18, 2 Corinthians 11:3, 2 Thessalonians 2:3 and 1 Timothy 2:14 (these five not yet on these pages). Two of those five, 2 Corinthians 11:3 and 1 Timothy 2:14, are about Eve. The Greek of Genesis 3:13, where the woman says “The serpent deceived me,” has ēpatēsen me, the same verb without its prefix, with the same “me” (checked in Rahlfs’s text). Nothing in Romans 7 names Adam or Eve, and the verses are not a quotation. What they have is the order of events of Genesis 2–3: a commandment; a deceiver who works through it; a death. In the Hebrew of Genesis 3:6 the tree is nechmad, “desirable,” from the root chamad of the commandment “you shall not covet”; the form nechmad stands in two verses of Genesis, 2:9 and 3:6 (searched). That order of events, together with that Hebrew, is the strongest ground of the reading in which the “I” of verse 9 is Adam. It does not settle that reading, and a Greek reader would not see the Hebrew link. For the verb of killing, KJV “slew me”, ASV “slew me” and Geneva “slew me” keep the older word; Douay “killed me” and NWT 1984 “killed me” use this translation’s; NIV “put me to death” uses the verb of verse 4; and the TLB “Sin fooled me by taking the good laws of God and using them to make me guilty of death” turns a death into a verdict.

Verses 12–13 · ὁ μὲν νόμος ἅγιος, καὶ ἡ ἐντολὴ ἁγία καὶ δικαία καὶ ἀγαθή … ἵνα φανῇ ἁμαρτία … ἵνα γένηται καθʼ ὑπερβολὴν ἁμαρτωλὸς ἡ ἁμαρτία ho men nomos hagios, kai hē entolē hagia kai dikaia kai agathē … hina phanē hamartia … hina genētai kath’ hyperbolēn hamartōlos hē hamartia — Romans 7:12–13: “the law is holy, and the commandment holy and righteous and good,” and sin made “sinful beyond all measure.” The answer to the question of verse 7, and a purpose clause with no one named

“The law is holy” (ho men nomos hagios, Romans 7:12). This is the answer to “Is the law sin?” in verse 7, and it is given in the verse’s first word, hōste, “so,” as a conclusion from verses 8–11 and not as a new claim. It is the only verse of the New Testament that calls the law itself holy; the other verses that set “law” and “holy” side by side are about the holy place (Acts 6:13, 21:28, searched and read). The law gets one adjective and the commandment three, “holy and righteous and good,” and one way to hear the three is against the charges the chapter has met: that the law is itself a thing of sin (holy), that it is unjust to the one it kills (righteous), and that it is the cause of death (good, the question of verse 13). The particle men, “for its part,” opens a contrast that this sentence does not finish; if it is finished anywhere, it is by verse 14, “but I.” NWT 1984 “on its part, the Law is holy” and Douay “the law indeed is holy” are the two on the shelf that print the men. Three versions give the middle adjective as “just”: KJV “holy, and just, and good”, Geneva “holy, and just, and good” and Douay “holy, and just, and good”; ASV “holy, and righteous, and good”, NIV “holy, righteous and good” and NWT 1984 “holy and righteous and good” print the word this translation uses for dikaios. The TLB “the law itself was wholly right and good” drops “holy.”

“Did what is good, then, become death to me?” (to oun agathon emoi egeneto thanatos, Romans 7:13). ⚠ The apparatus has one variant here. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print egeneto, the aorist, “did it become,” and Robinson–Pierpont prints gegonen, the perfect, “has it become” (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on egeneto). The aorist asks about the event of verse 11, and the perfect would ask about a standing result; the printed text asks about the event. The question is a second objection, and its answer is the second mē genoito of the chapter (verse 7 has the first); KJV “Was then that which is good made death unto me?”, Geneva “Was that then which is good, made death unto me?” and Douay “Was that then which is good, made death unto me?” print the verb as a passive, “made,” which the Greek does not have; ASV “Did then that which is good become death unto me?”, NIV “Did that which is good, then, become death to me?” and NWT 1984 “Did, then, what is good become death to me?” print what it says.

“It was sin” (alla hē hamartia). The Greek is a sentence with a subject and no verb: alla hē hamartia, hina phanē hamartia, “but sin, so that it might be shown to be sin.” Translations must put a verb in, and which one they choose decides how much the sentence says. NWT 1984 “But sin did” supplies the verb of the question, “became.” NIV “it used what is good to bring about my death” and the TLB “it was sin, devilish stuff that it is, that used what was good” supply “used,” and the TLB adds “devilish,” “cunning” and “damnable,” none of which is in the verse. Geneva “wrought death in me by that which is good” and Douay “wrought death in me” make a main verb out of the participle katergazomenē, “working out,” the verb of verses 8, 15, 17, 18 and 20; KJV and ASV print the participle and leave the sentence open, as the Greek leaves it. This translation answers the question with It was sin, the subject and no more, and keeps “working out” as the participle it is.

“So that it might be shown to be sin” and “sinful beyond all measure” (hina phanē hamartia … kath’ hyperbolēn hamartōlos). There are two purpose clauses, and no one is named as having the purpose. The grammar is that of Romans 5:20 (“law came in alongside, so that the trespass might increase”), which 6:1 then turned into an objection. Hamartōlos is the word for “sinner,” the word of 5:8 and 5:19, and here it is said of sin itself: the word that goes with persons is put on sin, and the personification of verses 8–11 is completed. Kath’ hyperbolēn stands in five verses of the New Testament (kath’ hyperbolēn, searched): here, 1 Corinthians 12:31 and 2 Corinthians 4:17 (these two not yet on these pages), 2 Corinthians 1:8 (“weighed down utterly”) and Galatians 1:13 (“beyond all measure I was persecuting”), and this translation uses the wording of Galatians. KJV “exceeding sinful” and ASV “exceeding sinful”, Geneva “out of measure sinful” and Douay “sinful above measure”, NIV “utterly sinful” and NWT 1984 “far more sinful” are five wordings for one phrase; the last, “far more,” turns a limit that cannot be measured into a comparison.

Verse 14 · Οἴδαμεν γὰρ ὅτι ὁ νόμος πνευματικός ἐστιν· ἐγὼ δὲ σάρκινός εἰμι, πεπραμένος ὑπὸ τὴν ἁμαρτίαν Oidamen gar hoti ho nomos pneumatikos estin; egō de sarkinos eimi, pepramenos hypo tēn hamartian — Romans 7:14: “the law is spiritual; but I am made of flesh, sold under sin.” Carnal, fleshly or unspiritual? A variant of one syllable, a slave-sale idiom of the Old Testament, and the verse where the tense changes

“For we know that the law is spiritual” (oidamen gar hoti ho nomos pneumatikos estin). Oidamen, “we know,” is the opener of common ground, and it stands in six verses of Romans (oidamen, searched): 2:2, 3:19 (“Now we know that whatever the law says”), here, 8:22, 8:28 and, negated (“we do not know”), 8:26. The word pneumatikos stands in three verses of Romans: 1:11, here and 15:27 (not yet on these pages). Six of the seven versions print “spiritual” (KJV “the law is spiritual”, ASV “the law is spiritual”, Geneva “the Law is spiritual”, Douay “the law is spiritual”, NIV “the law is spiritual” and NWT 1984 “the Law is spiritual”), and the TLB replaces it with “The law is good, then,” which uses the word of verse 12 for a different adjective.

“But I am made of flesh” (egō de sarkinos eimi). ⚠ The apparatus shows the syllable. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print sarkinos, and Robinson–Pierpont prints sarkikos (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on sarkinos); the same two readings, with the same editions on each side, are in the apparatus at 1 Corinthians 3:1 and at Hebrews 7:16 (these two not yet on these pages), and in no other note of the SBLGNT apparatus (searched). The two endings do different work. Several New Testament adjectives in -inos name the stuff a thing is made of: “stone” in John 2:6 (the water-jars) and Revelation 9:20, and “of stone,” “of earthenware” and “of wood” at 2 Corinthians 3:3, 4:7 and 2 Timothy 2:20 (searched; these three not yet on these pages). An adjective in -ikos is the kind that says what a thing belongs to, like pneumatikos in the first half of the verse. So sarkinos can be read as “made of flesh,” set against “made of stone” as in 2 Corinthians 3:3, a statement about what the speaker is made of and not about how he behaves (a reading of the ending). The Greek Old Testament has the phrase “a heart of flesh,” kardian sarkinēn, twice in Rahlfs’s text, given in place of the “heart of stone” (Ezekiel 11:19 and 36:26, verses by the usual division; these two not yet on these pages), so the word is not in itself a bad one in the Greek Bible. This translation prints “made of flesh,” and the reader has the note for the other.

The shelf has four answers. KJV “I am carnal”, ASV “I am carnal”, Geneva “I am carnal” and Douay “I am carnal” use one English word (Douay’s comes from the Latin carnalis, checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org) for either Greek ending, and in current English “carnal” leans toward the sensual, which neither Greek word says. NWT 1984 “I am fleshly” is closer. NIV “I am unspiritual” makes the second half the denial of the first (“spiritual”; “unspiritual”), which is a contrast the Greek does not draw, since its second adjective is about stuff and not about the Spirit. And the TLB drops the adjective for “the trouble is not there but with me.”

“Sold under sin” (pepramenos hypo tēn hamartian). The participle is the perfect passive of pipraskō, “having been sold, and remaining sold,” and the preposition is the hypo of 3:9 (“all under sin”) and of “under law” and “under grace” in 6:14. The picture is a person sold into slavery, as in Matthew 18:25, where the master “ordered him to be sold, with his wife and his children and everything he had.” The Old Testament has an idiom close to it, and it makes the person the one who sells himself. In the Hebrew of 1 Kings 21:20 Elijah says to Ahab, hitmakkerkha, “you have sold yourself to do what is evil in the eyes of Jehovah,” and 21:25 says of him that he hitmakker, “sold himself” for the same; Israel in 2 Kings 17:17 vayyitmakkeru, “sold themselves” to the same end (searched; these two chapters not yet on these pages). The Greek Old Testament has eprathē poiēsai to ponēron, “was sold to do the evil,” at 1 Kings 21:25 (English numbering) and eprathēsan with the same clause at 2 Kings 17:17 and again in 1 Maccabees 1:15 (Rahlfs’s text; chapter and verse by the usual division). Paul’s participle is passive and gives no seller. KJV “sold under sin”, ASV “sold under sin”, Geneva “sold under sin”, Douay “sold under sin” and NWT 1984 “sold under sin” keep it; NIV “sold as a slave to sin” adds “as a slave” and changes “under” to “to”; and the TLB “sold into slavery with Sin as my owner” turns sin into an owner, which the preposition hypo does not say.

The tense changes. Up to verse 13 the “I” is told in the past: “I did not know,” “I was alive,” “I died.” From verse 14 to verse 25 the verbs about the speaker are in the present: “I am,” “I do not know,” “I wish,” “I do” (read in the Greek). Readers have taken that change in at least two ways, and the history of each is reported here and not checked: that the present is the Christian Paul, describing the struggle of a believer, a reading that goes back at least to Augustine’s later writings and was held by Luther and Calvin; or that it is a vivid present, a way of telling a past or an imagined experience of someone under the law, which was the earlier reading in the Greek church and in Augustine himself before he changed his mind. Each reading has a verse set against it in the letter. If the speaker is a believer, “sold under sin” stands beside “having been set free from sin” (6:18, 22) and “sin will not be lord over you” (6:14). If the speaker is not, “I delight in the law of God” (verse 22) and “thanks be to God” (verse 25) stand beside “sold under sin.” This translation does not choose, and it prints the verbs as they are.

Verses 15–17 · ὃ γὰρ κατεργάζομαι οὐ γινώσκω … οὐ γὰρ ὃ θέλω τοῦτο πράσσω, ἀλλʼ ὃ μισῶ τοῦτο ποιῶ … σύμφημι τῷ νόμῳ … οὐκέτι ἐγὼ κατεργάζομαι αὐτὸ ἀλλὰ ἡ οἰκοῦσα ἐν ἐμοὶ ἁμαρτία ho gar katergazomai ou ginōskō … ou gar ho thelō touto prassō, all’ ho misō touto poiō … symphēmi tō nomō … ouketi egō katergazomai auto alla hē oikousa en emoi hamartia — Romans 7:15–17: “what I work out I do not know,” three verbs for doing, “I agree with the law,” and “no longer I, but the sin that dwells in me”

“What I work out I do not know” (ho gar katergazomai ou ginōskō, Romans 7:15). The shelf reads ginōskō, “know,” in three ways. NWT 1984 “I do not know” and ASV “I know not” keep the word. NIV “I do not understand what I do”, Douay “I understand not” and the TLB “I don’t understand myself at all” make it a failure of comprehension. And KJV “I allow not” and Geneva “I allow not” use “allow” in its old sense of “approve.” “Know” can mean to acknowledge as one’s own, as in “Jehovah knows the way of the righteous” (Psalm 1:6), and the letter has used the nearby verbs of approval: “they did not approve of holding God in accurate knowledge” (1:28) and “approve of those who practice them” (1:32). So the sentence can say either “I cannot make sense of what I do” or “I do not acknowledge what I do as mine”; the second prepares verse 17, and the verse does not choose. Douay “I do not that good which I will; but the evil which I hate” puts in “good” and “evil,” which verse 15 does not have; they are the words of verse 19.

Three verbs for doing. Verses 15–20 do not say “do” once. They use katergazomai, “work out” (15, 17, 18, 20); prassō, “practice” (15, 19); and poieō, “do” (15, 16, 19, 20). Prassō is in Romans the verb of those who practice what deserves death, “those who practice such things” (1:32), those who judge and “practice the same things” (2:1–3), and of “if you practice the law” (2:25); so the speaker of verses 15 and 19, who wishes the good and practices the evil, does what the people of chapters 1–2 are charged with, and that is one thing the readers point to who take the speaker to be a person under the law. The verb stands in Romans in nine verses (prassō, searched, with the nouns at 8:13 and 12:4 set aside). Three of the seven print one word for all three verbs: NIV “what I want to do I do not do, but what I hate I do”, KJV “what I would, that do I not; but what I hate, that do I” and Geneva “that which I do” have “do” throughout. NWT 1984 “what I wish, this I do not practice” and ASV “that do I practise” are the two that keep “practice” for prassō.

“I agree with the law that it is good” (symphēmi tō nomō hoti kalos, Romans 7:16). Symphēmi, “say along with,” stands once in the New Testament, here (symphēmi, searched). The speaker’s failure is the witness: what I do against my wish shows that I side with the law. The law said “you shall not covet”; the speaker says “amen.” NIV “I agree that the law is good” and NWT 1984 “I agree that the Law is fine” keep the weight of the verb; KJV “I consent unto the law”, ASV “I consent unto the law”, Geneva “I consent to the Law” and Douay “I consent to the law” use the verb of the Vulgate’s consentio (checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org), which in English now sounds like permission. The TLB adds “my bad conscience proves that I agree,” and a conscience is not in the verse. Kalos is another word than the agathos of verses 12, 13 and 18–19, and this translation prints “good” for both, as it does for kalos in “the good shepherd” (John 10:11); NWT 1984 “fine” is the only version on the shelf that keeps the two words apart.

“No longer I, but the sin that dwells in me” (ouketi egō … alla hē oikousa en emoi hamartia, Romans 7:17). The words ouketi egō, “no longer I,” stand in three verses of the New Testament (ouketi egō, searched): verses 17 and 20 here, and Galatians 2:20, “it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me” (Greek checked; not yet on these pages). The two sentences have one shape and opposite occupants. The word “dwell” is oikeō, which stands in Romans in five verses (oikeō, searched): sin dwells in me (7:17, 7:20); nothing good dwells in my flesh (7:18); and, in the next chapter, “God’s Spirit dwells in you” (8:9) and “the Spirit of the one who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you” (8:11). ⚠ The apparatus shows the prefix: Treg, NA28 and RP print oikousa, and Westcott–Hort prints enoikousa, “indwelling” (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on oikousa); the meaning is the same. A picture of sin as something that crouches at the door is older: “sin is crouching at the door — its desire is for you” (Genesis 4:7); in Romans it has moved indoors.

“But now” (nyni de) can be heard in two ways. It is the phrase that turns time in 3:21 (“But now, apart from law”), 6:22 and verse 6, and read so it points to the speaker’s present, after the past of verses 7–13. It can also be logical, “as things are,” and NIV “As it is, it is no longer I myself who do it” prints it so. ASV “So now it is no more I that do it”, KJV “Now then it is no more I that do it”, Geneva “Now then, it is no more I, that do it” and Douay “Now then it is no more I that do it” take it as an inference, and NWT 1984 “But now the one working it out is no longer I” keeps the time word. The TLB “It is sin inside me that is stronger than I am that makes me do these evil things” adds three things the Greek does not say: “stronger,” “makes me” and “evil.” Whether “no longer I” excuses or only distinguishes is the question the verse leaves open; the next verses will say that the speaker still wishes, and verse 25 will join the divided “I” with “I myself.”

Verses 18–20 · οὐκ οἰκεῖ ἐν ἐμοί, τοῦτʼ ἔστιν ἐν τῇ σαρκί μου, ἀγαθόν· τὸ γὰρ θέλειν παράκειταί μοι, τὸ δὲ κατεργάζεσθαι τὸ καλὸν οὔ … οὐκέτι ἐγὼ κατεργάζομαι αὐτὸ ouk oikei en emoi, touth’ estin en tē sarki mou, agathon; to gar thelein parakeitai moi, to de katergazesthai to kalon ou … ouketi egō katergazomai auto — Romans 7:18–20: “nothing good dwells in me, that is, in my flesh.” Wishing “lies beside” the speaker, the working out does not, and the argument of 15–17 goes round again

“In me—that is, in my flesh” (en emoi, touth’ estin en tē sarki mou, Romans 7:18). The speaker corrects himself in mid-sentence, and the correction is the point: nothing good dwells “in me,” and by “me” is meant the flesh, which leaves room for something else that is the speaker’s, the “mind” and the “inner man” of verses 22–23, where the wish is. KJV “(that is, in my flesh,)” sets the correction in parentheses, and Douay “that is to say, in my flesh” has it as a gloss. The Greek noun sarx stands in four verses of this chapter (5, 14 in the adjective, 18 and 25), and the shelf’s NIV prints it four ways: “in the realm of the flesh” at verse 5, “unspiritual” at verse 14, and “my sinful nature” at verses 18 and 25. The TLB “I know I am rotten through and through so far as my old sinful nature is concerned” adds “rotten through and through,” the opposite of the limit the Greek puts on the claim. The oikeō of the verse is that of verses 17 and 20, and its other occupant is the Spirit (8:9, 8:11). KJV “dwelleth no good thing”, ASV “dwelleth no good thing” and Geneva “dwelleth no good thing” have “no good thing” for the neuter agathon, and NIV “good itself does not dwell in me” has made it an abstraction.

“Wishing lies beside me” (to gar thelein parakeitai moi). Parakeimai, “lie beside, be at hand,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this and verse 21 (parakeimai, searched), and it is said of two things: the wish lies beside the speaker here, and evil lies beside the speaker there. Both are near. The wish is within reach, and the working out is not (to de katergazesthai to kalon ou, “but to work out the good, no”), with the verb left to be supplied. Thelō, “wish,” stands seven times in verses 15–21 (counted in the Greek). The shelf renders the wish in four ways. KJV “to will is present with me”, ASV “to will is present with me”, Geneva “to will is present with me” and Douay “to will, is present with me” use the old “to will.” NIV “I have the desire to do what is good” makes it a desire, a word this translation keeps for epithymia. NWT 1984 “ability to wish is present with me, but ability to work out what is fine is not [present]” puts in “ability” twice, so that the verse is about capacities, where the Greek has the wish and the work; its 2013 revision reads “I have the desire to do what is fine but not the ability to carry it out” (checked). And the TLB “I want to but I can’t” keeps the sense in the plainest words.

⚠ The end of verse 18. The printed Greek ends at ou, “not,” and the apparatus has Robinson–Pierpont adding ouch heuriskō, “I do not find” (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on ou; Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 end at ou). The longer reading is the one behind KJV “how to perform that which is good I find not”, Geneva “I find no means to perform that which is good” and Douay “to accomplish that which is good, I find not”, and Douay’s Latin has it too (perficere autem bonum non invenio, checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org); KJV adds “how” as well. The shorter reading is that of ASV “but to do that which is good is not”. So the two readings differ in whether the verse ends with the speaker not finding the means or simply with the working out not being there; the wish and the lack stand either way.

The refrain (verses 19–20). Verse 19 is verse 15 again, with the two objects named: “the good that I wish I do not do, but the evil that I do not wish, this I practice.” Verse 20 is verse 16’s “if” clause (ei de ho ou thelō touto poiō) followed by verse 17’s conclusion, word for word (ouketi egō katergazomai auto alla hē oikousa en emoi hamartia). ⚠ The apparatus has one word here: Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont add egō after thelō (“what I do not wish, I do”), and Westcott–Hort and Tregelles do not (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on thelō). The printed text has no egō, which keeps the clause exactly as it was in verse 16 and leaves the only “I” of the verse for the denial. The argument has gone round once: the speaker finds his deeds opposed to his wish (15, 19), reasons from the wish to the law (16) and from the deed to the occupant (17, 20), and says in the middle (18) what it is about him that cannot produce the good. The shelf’s TLB “sin still has me in its evil grasp” turns the dwelling of verse 20 into a grip, and says “evil” where verse 20 has none.

A divided self in other voices. The complaint of verses 15 and 19 is not unique to Paul’s page, and three others are close to it. In Euripides’ Medea, before she kills her children, the speaker says, “I understand what evils I am about to do, but my passion is stronger than my plans” (kai manthanō men hoia dran mellō kaka, thymos de kreissōn tōn emōn bouleumatōn, lines 1078–1079). In Ovid’s Metamorphoses 7.20–21, Medea in love says, “I see the better and approve it; I follow the worse” (video meliora proboque, deteriora sequor). And Epictetus, the Stoic teacher who taught after Paul’s time (reported), says in Discourses 2.26.4 that the teacher’s skill is to show a person “how what he wishes he does not do, and what he does not wish he does” (pōs ho thelei ou poiei kai ho mē thelei poiei; the Greek of Euripides and Epictetus and the Latin of Ovid read in Perseus’s texts; the English is this translation’s). The likeness is real, and so are three differences. In the three the opposition is inside the speaker: passion against reason. In Paul it is the speaker against an occupant, “the sin that dwells in me.” Paul’s speaker also agrees with a law (verse 16), and none of the three has one. And none of the three says “no longer I.” Whether Paul knew any of them is not shown, and the likeness proves a shared experience, not a source.

Verses 21–23 · Εὑρίσκω ἄρα τὸν νόμον … συνήδομαι γὰρ τῷ νόμῳ τοῦ θεοῦ κατὰ τὸν ἔσω ἄνθρωπον … ἕτερον νόμον ἐν τοῖς μέλεσίν μου ἀντιστρατευόμενον τῷ νόμῳ τοῦ νοός μου καὶ αἰχμαλωτίζοντά με Heuriskō ara ton nomon … synēdomai gar tō nomō tou theou kata ton esō anthrōpon … heteron nomon en tois melesin mou antistrateuomenon tō nomō tou noos mou kai aichmalōtizonta me — Romans 7:21–23: “I find the law … I delight in the law of God according to the inner man … I see a different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind.” Four laws in three verses, and the army in the members

“I find, then, the law” (heuriskō ara ton nomon, Romans 7:21). The sentence can be read two ways, and the Greek does not choose. “I find the law … that evil lies beside me” can mean that the speaker finds a rule, a regularity, as one speaks of a law of nature; it can also mean that the speaker finds something about the law he has been speaking of, that when he wishes to do the good, evil is at hand. The shelf reads it both ways. KJV “I find then a law, that, when I would do good, evil is present with me” and Douay “I find then a law, that when I have a will to do good, evil is present with me” take it as a rule, and change the article to “a.” NIV “I find this law at work” and NWT 1984 “I find, then, this law in my case” add “this.” ASV “I find then the law, that, to me who would do good, evil is present” keeps the article and the dative “to me who would do good.” Geneva “I am thus yoked” drops the word “law” and puts a yoke in its place, and the TLB “It seems to be a fact of life” drops it as well. This translation keeps the article and the order, and leaves the two readings open. The letter already speaks of laws that are rules or regimes and not the law of Moses: “through a law of faith” (3:27), and, after this chapter, “the law of the Spirit of life … the law of sin and of death” (8:2).

Seven uses of “law” in verses 21–25. The noun nomos stands twenty-three times in this chapter, more than in any other chapter of the New Testament (counted in the Greek; Romans 2 has nineteen and Galatians 3 fifteen), and seven of them are in verses 21–25. They are: the law found in verse 21; “the law of God” in which the speaker delights (22, and again in 25); “a different law in my members” (23); “the law of my mind” (23); and “the law of sin” (23, 25). How many laws these are depends on two questions the text does not answer: whether “the different law in my members” and “the law of sin” are one, and whether “the law of God” and “the law of my mind” are one. If both are, there are two sides, as verse 25 puts it, the law of God and the law of sin; if not, there are more.

“I delight in the law of God according to the inner man” (synēdomai … kata ton esō anthrōpon, Romans 7:22). Three verbs in these verses begin with a preposition of company or opposition and take “law” as their object in the dative, and each stands once in the New Testament (searched): symphēmi in verse 16, “say along with”; synēdomai here, “take delight along with”; and antistrateuomai in verse 23, “campaign against.” The first two put the speaker beside the law, and the third puts something in his members against the law of his mind. “The inner man” (ho esō anthrōpos) stands in three verses of the New Testament (esō anthrōpos, searched): here, Ephesians 3:16 and 2 Corinthians 4:16 (these two not yet on these pages). It is not in Rahlfs’s Greek Old Testament (searched). A close phrase is in Plato’s Republic (589a–b), in the argument of the one who praises justice, that one should act so that “the man within the man” (ho entos anthrōpos, a different word for “within”) will be strongest and will tend “the many-headed beast” as a farmer would; the likeness is of the phrase and not of the thought (Plato’s Greek read in Perseus’s text; whether Paul knew it is not shown). It stands against the “old man” of 6:6, and the verse does not say whether the inner man and the old man are one person looked at in two ways. The shelf: KJV “after the inward man”, ASV “after the inward man” and Douay “according to the inward man” keep “man”; Geneva “concerning the inner man” changes the preposition; NWT 1984 “according to the man I am within” keeps “man”; NIV “in my inner being” drops it; and the TLB “I love to do God’s will so far as my new nature is concerned” replaces the law with “will,” the delight with “love to do,” and the inner man with a “new nature,” and so decides the question of who the speaker is, a believer, in a word.

“A different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind” (heteron nomon en tois melesin mou antistrateuomenon tō nomō tou noos mou, Romans 7:23). Heteron is often “different in kind” and not just a second of the same (Koine does not always keep that line; reported, not checked), and this translation prints “a different law,” as Galatians 1:6 prints “a different gospel.” The verb antistrateuomai is military, and so is the next one, aichmalōtizō, “take captive,” which stands in four verses of the New Testament (aichmalōtizō, searched): Luke 21:24 (“will be led captive into all the nations”), here, 2 Corinthians 10:5 and 2 Timothy 3:6 (these two not yet on these pages). The army has been in the argument since the “weapons” of 6:13 and the “wages” of 6:23, and the place of the campaign is the same, the members, which are to be presented to God (6:13, 6:19) and in which the passions were at work (verse 5). Two other letters put war and the members or the soul together: “the pleasures that wage war in your members” (James 4:1) and “the fleshly desires that wage war against the soul” (1 Peter 2:11; the Greek of both checked; not yet on these pages). Nous, “mind,” stands in six verses of Romans (nous, searched): 1:28, 7:23, 7:25, and three more at 11:34, 12:2 and 14:5. In 1:28 God gave people over “to an unapproved mind,” and here the mind has a law, and the speaker serves it; whether it is the same mind is a part of the same question as who the “I” is.

The shelf has one verb each. KJV “warring against the law of my mind”, ASV “warring against the law of my mind” and NWT 1984 “warring against the law of my mind” keep the army; NIV “waging war against the law of my mind” does too; Douay “fighting against the law of my mind” and Geneva “rebelling against the law of my mind” use a fight and a revolt. The Greek has en before the law of sin, “taking me captive in the law of sin” (the apparatus has Robinson–Pierpont without the en; the SBLGNT apparatus, note on en), and Douay “captivating me in the law of sin” keeps it from the Latin in lege peccati (checked at vulgate.org). The others give a preposition of their own: KJV “bringing me into captivity to the law of sin”, ASV “bringing me into captivity under the law of sin”, Geneva “leading me captive unto the law of sin” and NWT 1984 “leading me captive to sin’s law.” “Law of sin” is the phrase of 8:2, where the answer to this verse begins: “set you free from the law of sin and of death.”

Verses 24–25 · ταλαίπωρος ἐγὼ ἄνθρωπος· τίς με ῥύσεται ἐκ τοῦ σώματος τοῦ θανάτου τούτου; χάρις τῷ θεῷ διὰ Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ … αὐτὸς ἐγὼ τῷ μὲν νοῒ δουλεύω νόμῳ θεοῦ, τῇ δὲ σαρκὶ νόμῳ ἁμαρτίας talaipōros egō anthrōpos; tis me rhysetai ek tou sōmatos tou thanatou toutou? charis tō theō dia Iēsou Christou … autos egō tō men noi douleuō nomō theou, tē de sarki nomō hamartias — Romans 7:24–25: “Wretched man that I am! Who will rescue me from the body of this death?” and “Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” A cry, a thanksgiving with three readings, and a last sentence that rejoins the divided “I”

“Wretched man that I am!” (talaipōros egō anthrōpos, Romans 7:24). The adjective talaipōros stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Revelation 3:17 (“you are the wretched one, and pitiable, and poor, and blind, and naked”), and the noun of the same root is in Romans 3:16 (“ruin and misery are in their ways”) and James 5:1, with the verb in James 4:9 (talaipōr-, searched; the two James verses not yet on these pages). So the root of the cry is the root of the “misery” that the string of quotations in chapter 3 puts in “their ways.” The shelf: KJV “O wretched man that I am!”, Geneva “O wretched man that I am” and ASV “Wretched man that I am!” and NIV “What a wretched man I am!” have the word this translation uses here and at Revelation 3:17; NWT 1984 “Miserable man that I am!” and Douay “Unhappy man that I am” use others, and Douay’s comes from the Latin infelix (checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org).

“Who will rescue me from the body of this death?” (tis me rhysetai ek tou sōmatos tou thanatou toutou). The verb rhyomai stands in Romans in three verses (rhyomai, searched): here, 11:26 (“the one who rescues,” in a quotation usually traced to Isaiah 59:20; reported, not checked) and 15:31 (these two not yet on these pages). It is a future, and the question is answered in verse 25 not with a “who” but with thanks. KJV “who shall deliver me”, ASV “who shall deliver me”, Geneva “who shall deliver me” and Douay “who shall deliver me” keep the old word, and NIV “Who will rescue me” and NWT 1984 “Who will rescue me” have this translation’s, as at Matthew 27:43.

The phrase tou sōmatos tou thanatou toutou has a grammar the shelf cannot all keep. Toutou, “this,” has the same ending for the masculine thanatou, “of death,” and the neuter sōmatos, “of the body,” so it can go with either: “the body of this death” or “this body of death.” Its place, next to thanatou, favors the first, and this translation keeps the order. KJV “the body of this death”, ASV “the body of this death”, Geneva “the body of this death” and Douay “the body of this death” keep it, and NWT 1984 “the body undergoing this death” keeps the “this” and makes the genitive a participle. NIV “this body that is subject to death” moves “this” to the body and turns “of death” into “subject to.” The genitive itself is open: a body that belongs to death, that brings death, or that is dying. The letter has been naming bodies since chapter 6: the “body of sin” to be abolished (6:6), the “mortal body” in which sin is not to reign (6:12), the “body of Christ” through which the readers died to the law (verse 4); and it will go on with “the body is dead because of sin” (8:10), the mortal bodies God will also make alive (8:11) and “the redemption of our body” (8:23).

“Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” (charis tō theō dia Iēsou Christou tou kyriou hēmōn, Romans 7:25). ⚠ The apparatus has three readings of the opening. The printed text has charis, “thanks” (Tregelles); Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 have charis de, “but thanks”; Robinson–Pierpont has eucharistō, “I thank” (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on charis). The de would make the thanks an answer to the cry, as at 6:17 (“But thanks be to God that you were slaves of sin”), the same words. “I thank God” is the reading behind KJV “I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord” and ASV “I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord”. Geneva “I thank God through Jesus Christ our Lord” has it too. Douay “The grace of God, by Jesus Christ our Lord” follows the Latin gratia Dei per Iesum Christum (checked at vulgate.org), which has “grace of God” and no verb at all. NWT 1984 “Thanks to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!” and NIV “Thanks be to God, who delivers me through Jesus Christ our Lord!” have the printed text, and the NIV adds a clause, “who delivers me,” that makes the rescue present. The TLB, which runs verses 23–25 together, has “Thank God! It has been done by Jesus Christ our Lord. He has set me free,” a sentence in the perfect tense that the Greek does not contain. The question of verse 24 is in the future and the thanks has no tense. Readers take the thanks as for a rescue already given, which points to a speaker who has been rescued, or as thanks given in advance for a rescue still to come, such as “the redemption of our body” of 8:23. The verse supports either, and the answer that the next chapter gives begins “There is therefore now no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus” (8:1).

“I myself” (autos egō). The last sentence, ara oun autos egō tō men noi douleuō nomō theou, tē de sarki nomō hamartias, puts the two halves in one speaker: “I myself” with the mind am a slave to the law of God, and with the flesh to the law of sin. The verb is douleuō for both masters, the verb of 6:6 and of verse 6 (“slaves in newness of spirit”), and the family of the slaves of 6:16–22, where a person is the slave of one master or of another; here one speaker is a slave in two ways at once. NIV “I myself in my mind am a slave to God’s law, but in my sinful nature a slave to the law of sin” and NWT 1984 “with [my] mind I myself am a slave to God’s law, but with [my] flesh to sin’s law” keep “slave.” KJV “with the mind I myself serve the law of God”, ASV “I of myself with the mind, indeed, serve the law of God”, Geneva “I myself in my mind serve the Law of God” and Douay “I myself, with the mind serve the law of God” use “serve.” The datives noi and sarki can be the instrument or the sphere: KJV “with the mind” and Douay “with the mind” give the instrument, and Geneva “in my mind” and NIV “in my mind” give the sphere. The chapter that began with a woman released by a death ends with a speaker who, in this verse, serves both, and the chapter after it is the one that says what the release is (8:1–2).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “May it never be!” for mē genoito (vv. 7 and 13), as at 3:31 and 6:15; “lord over” for kyrieuō (v. 1), as at 6:9 and 14; “worked out” for katergazomai (vv. 8, 13, 15, 17, 18, 20), as at 1:27, 2:9, 4:15 and 5:3; “practice” for prassō (vv. 15 and 19), as at 1:32 and 2:1; “slaves” for douleuō (vv. 6 and 25), as at 6:6; “deceived” for exapataō (v. 11), the word the site prints for the serpent at Genesis 3:13; “came to life again” for anazaō (v. 9), as “come back to life” at Luke 15:24; “beyond all measure” for kath’ hyperbolēn (v. 13), as at Galatians 1:13; “a different” for heteron (v. 23), as at Galatians 1:6; “wretched” for talaipōros (v. 24), as at Revelation 3:17; “rescue” for rhyomai (v. 24), as at Matthew 27:43; and “thanks be to God” for charis tō theō (v. 25), as at 6:17. Four pairs are split on purpose: katargeō is “abolish” where it has an object (6:6) and “released” where it has “from” (vv. 2 and 6); epithymia is “desires” at 1:24 and 6:12 and “coveting” in verses 7 and 8, because it is the noun of the commandment’s own verb; pathēma is “sufferings” at 8:18 and “passions” at verse 5; and agathos and kalos are both “good” (vv. 12, 16 and 18), the one place where the Greek has two words and the English one.

Echoes paid. “What shall we say, then?” (4:1, 6:1) — v. 7. The three lords, death and sin (6:9, 14) and now the law — v. 1. “Died to sin” (6:2, 10) — put to death to the law, vv. 4 and 6. “Newness of life” (6:4) — “newness of spirit,” v. 6. “By spirit, not by letter” (2:29) — v. 6. The members presented as weapons (6:13, 19) and the soldier’s wages (6:23) — the members in which the passions worked (v. 5), and the war and the captivity of v. 23. Fruit (1:13, 6:21–22) — fruit for God and fruit for death, vv. 4 and 5. The recognition of sin through law (3:20), the law that works out wrath (4:15), sin in the world before law (5:13) and the law that came in alongside (5:20) — the whole of vv. 5 and 7–13. “Under sin” (3:9) and “under law” (6:14) — the woman “under a husband,” v. 2, and the one “sold under sin,” v. 14. The “body of sin” (6:6) and the mortal body (6:12) — “the body of this death,” v. 24. The “law of faith” (3:27) — the laws of vv. 21–25. Sinners (5:8, 19) — sin made “sinful,” v. 13. The unapproved mind (1:28) — the mind with a law, vv. 23 and 25. “Thanks be to God” (6:17) — v. 25.

Echoes planted. The sin that dwells in me (vv. 17 and 20) and the good that does not (v. 18) are answered by the Spirit who dwells in you (8:9, 11). “In the flesh” (v. 5) is taken up at 8:8 and 9; “weak through the flesh” is the diagnosis of 8:3. “The law of sin” (vv. 23 and 25) returns at 8:2, with the rescue that verse 24 asked for; “no condemnation” (8:1) answers the whole of vv. 24–25. The body of this death (v. 24) is the mortal body made alive (8:11) and the body whose redemption is awaited (8:23). “Sold under sin” (v. 14) and the double slavery of verse 25 meet “a spirit of slavery” and “a spirit of adoption” at 8:15. The tenth commandment (v. 7) is quoted again at Romans 13:9 (now on these pages). “May it never be!” has three verses left, at Romans 9:14, 11:1 and 11:11. Dead to the law, and “no longer I” (vv. 4 and 17) are the sentences of Galatians 2:19 and 2:20 (not yet on these pages), the letter that kills (v. 6) is 2 Corinthians 3:6 and the inner man (v. 22) is 2 Corinthians 4:16 (these two not yet on these pages). The war in the members (v. 23) is James 4:1 and 1 Peter 2:11 (these two not yet on these pages). “For as long as he lives” (v. 1) is the wording of 1 Corinthians 7:39 (not yet on these pages).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Who the “I” of verses 7–25 is (Paul himself, Adam, Israel at Sinai, or anyone under the law) and whether the present tense of verses 14–25 is the speaker as he now is or as he was; what “the body of Christ” is in verse 4, the body that died or the body of the congregation; what “in the flesh” is in verse 5, a body, a power, an age or a sphere; whether the law of verse 1, which has no article in Greek, is the Law of Moses or law as such; who died in verse 6, the readers (apothanontes) or the thing that held them (apothanontos); whom the “purpose” clauses of verse 13 belong to; whether sarkinos in verse 14 is “made of flesh” or “fleshly”; whether nomos in verse 21 is a rule or the Law, and how many laws there are in verses 21–25; whether the “inner man” of verse 22 is a new nature or the same person as the old man; whether “this” in verse 24 belongs to the body or to the death; and whether the thanks of verse 25 is for a rescue given or one promised. The SBLGNT apparatus has seven notes in this chapter, at verses 13, 14, 17, 18, 20, 23 and 25, and each is treated in the note for its verse.
