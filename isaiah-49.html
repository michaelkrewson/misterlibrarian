“Listen to me, you islands”: the servant who was called from the womb, hidden like an arrow in a quiver, named Israel and sent to Israel, and told that the task is too light, so he is made a light of nations. A time of favor, prisoners told to come out, a road made of mountains; Zion says she is forsaken, a nursing mother cannot forget, and the barren city is too cramped for the children who come back.
Undated in the chapter itself, and filed with the exile and return because it speaks to a Zion with no children and a gathering still to come. The chapter names no king, year or event. ⚠ Isaiah 53, with the same servant, and Isaiah 54, with the same barren woman, are on these pages beside it.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–6 · Isaiah 49:1–6 “Listen to me, you islands” — the servant speaks, is called Israel, and is sent to Israel; and the task is called too light, so he is made a light of nations

⭐ “You are my servant, Israel”, and the one sent to Israel. The words avdi attah, “you are my servant”, stand in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Isaiah 41:9 (now on these pages), Isaiah 44:21 and this one. Here the speaker is named Israel (v3), and in the next breath he is to bring Jacob back and gather Israel (v5) and to raise up the tribes of Jacob (v6). The chapter puts the name and the mission in one speech, which is why the servant of Isaiah 53 (now on these pages) is argued both ways; the note there sets out both readings, and this chapter does not vote either.

⭐ Called from the womb. The words mibeten qeraani, “from the womb he called me”, stand in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one. The second half has hizkir shemi, “he made my name remembered”. The NWT 1984 has “From the inward parts of my mother he has made mention of my name”, the Douay “from the bowels of my mother he hath been mindful of my name”, and the NIV “he has spoken my name”. Paul writes of the God “who separated me from my mother’s womb and called me through his undeserved kindness” (ek koilias mētros mou, Galatians 1:15, now on these pages; the NWT 2013); he does not say he is quoting, and the Greek has no formula of citation.

⭐ Sword and arrow, hidden. The mouth is made a sharp sword and the servant a polished arrow, and both are kept hidden until used: hechbi’ani, “he hid me”, in the shadow of his hand, and histirani, “he concealed me”, in his quiver. The KJV has “a polished shaft”, the ASV “a polished shaft”, the NIV “a polished arrow”, the NWT 1984 “a polished arrow”, the Geneva “a chosen shaft” and the Douay “a chosen arrow”. The Hebrew word barur is “polished” or “cleansed”, and “chosen” reads it as selected.

⭐ “For nothing”. The servant answers: lerik yaga’ti, “for nothing I have toiled”, and “for emptiness and vapor”, letohu vahevel, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Tohu is the word of the earth before the first day, “formless and empty” in Genesis 1:2 (now on these pages), and hevel is Ecclesiastes’ breath. The KJV has “for nought, and in vain”, the ASV “for nought and vanity”, the NIV “for nothing at all” and the NWT 1984 “For unreality and vanity”. And then the turn, aken, “yet surely”: “my justice is with Jehovah, and my wage with my God”. The KJV has “my judgment … and my work”, the ASV “the justice due to me … and my recompense”, and the NIV “what is due me … and my reward”.

⭐ “Israel” gathered, or not gathered. The text as written in verse 5 has lo, “not”, and the reading tradition says lo, “to him”: the same sound, two words, and the archive prints both. This translation follows the reading, “that Israel be gathered to him”, as the ASV does (“and that Israel be gathered unto him”) and the NIV (“gather Israel to himself”) and the NWT 1984 (“in order that to him Israel itself may be gathered”). The KJV follows the written word: “Though Israel be not gathered, yet shall I be glorious in the eyes of the Lord”. The Geneva has “though Israel be not gathered”, and the Douay “and Israel will not be gathered together”. Verse 6 has the same sort of pair, unetsiray written and unetsurey read, and both mean the kept ones, “the preserved of Israel”, as the KJV and the ASV have it.

⭐ Too light a thing, and a light of nations. Nakel mihyotkha li eved: it is too light for you to be my servant, only to raise up the tribes. The ASV has “it is too light a thing that thou shouldest be my servant”, the NIV “It is too small a thing for you to be my servant”, and the NWT 1984 “It has been more than a trivial matter for you to become my servant”. The KJV drops the “too”: “It is a light thing that thou shouldest be my servant”, and the Douay has “It is a small thing”; so those two read as if the task were easy, and the Hebrew says it is not hard enough. “A light of nations”, le’or goyim, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Isaiah 42:6 and this one. The KJV has “a light to the Gentiles”, the NWT 1984 “a light of the nations” and the NWT 2013 “a light of nations”. Paul and Barnabas say, “For Jehovah has commanded us in these words: ‘I have appointed you as a light of nations, for you to be a salvation to the ends of the earth’” (Acts 13:47, now on these pages; the NWT 2013), turning a command given to “you” into one given to “us”. Simeon’s “a light for revelation to nations” (phōs eis apokalypsin ethnōn, Luke 2:32, now on these pages) has the same two Greek words as Paul’s line, phōs and ethnōn, and no formula of citation.

Verses 7–13 · Isaiah 49:7–13 “In a time of favor I answered you” — the despised one whom kings rise for, a covenant of the people, prisoners told to come out, and a road made of mountains

⭐ “Despised in soul”. Livzoh nefesh, “to one despised in soul”, is said to a person and not to a city. The Geneva has “to him that is despised in soul”, the NWT 1984 “to him that is despised in soul”, and the Douay “to the soul that is despised”; the KJV has “to him whom man despiseth” and the NIV “to him who was despised and abhorred by the nation”. The servant of Isaiah 53:3 (now on these pages) is also called despised. Here the line turns on kings: they see and rise, and the princes bow, “because of Jehovah, who is faithful”; the KJV has “princes also shall worship”, the NIV “princes will see and bow down”.

⭐ “A time of favor”, and Paul’s “now”. “In a time of favor I answered you, and in a day of salvation I helped you.” Paul quotes it: “For he says: ‘In an acceptable time I heard you, and in a day of salvation I helped you.’ Look! Now is the especially acceptable time. Look! Now is the day of salvation” (2 Corinthians 6:2, now on these pages; the NWT 2013). His verb is “heard” (epēkousa) where the Hebrew has “answered” (aniticha); the KJV has “In an acceptable time have I heard thee”, the ASV “In an acceptable time have I answered thee”, and the NWT 1984 “In a time of goodwill I have answered you”. The word is ratson, “favor”, and the “now” belongs to Paul.

⭐ “A covenant of the people”. The words livrit am, “for a covenant of the people”, stand in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: Isaiah 42:6 and this one, both joined to the light of nations. The servant is himself the brit. The aim is “to raise up the land” (lehaqim eretz) and to cause the desolate inheritances to be inherited; the ASV has “to raise up the land” and the NWT 1984 “to rehabilitate the land”, while the KJV has “to establish the earth”, since erets is both “land” and “earth”.

⭐ Prisoners, a road, hunger and sun. “To say to the prisoners, ‘Come out,’ to those in the darkness, ‘Show yourselves.’” The pasture is “on all the bare heights”: the ASV has “on all bare heights” and the KJV “in all high places”. Verse 10 is the list of those who are led: “They will not hunger and will not thirst, and neither scorching heat nor sun will strike them”, in the order hunger, thirst, heat, sun. John’s list has the same four and a different order, hunger, thirst, sun, heat: ou peinasousin eti oude dipsēsousin eti, oude mē pesē ep’ autous ho hēlios oude pan kauma (Revelation 7:16, now on these pages), and it ends with the Lamb, who “will shepherd them and guide them to springs of the waters of life” (my rendering of the Greek), a shepherd where Isaiah has Jehovah.

⭐ Sinim, and the mountains that sing. “These from the north and from the west, and these from the land of Sinim.” The word Sinim stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one, and no one knows where it was. The KJV, the ASV, the Geneva and the NWT 1984 keep the name (“the land of Sinim”); the NIV prints “the region of Aswan”, which is an identification and not a translation; the Douay has “the south country”. Verse 13 calls on heavens, earth and mountains: “mountains, break out”, pitschu harim, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and Isaiah 44:23; the same verb sends the mountains and the hills “breaking out in song” at Isaiah 55:12 (now on these pages).

Verses 14–21 · Isaiah 49:14–21 “Zion said, Jehovah has forsaken me” — a nursing mother’s memory, a name engraved on the palms, and the barren city crowded with children

⭐ Forsaken and forgotten, and four “forgets”. Zion says azavani, “has forsaken me”, and shechechani, “has forgotten me”; the answer uses the same root three more times: tishkach, “can a woman forget”, tishkachna, “these may forget”, and eshkachekh, “I will not forget you”. That is four in two verses, and the last one carries the weight: the picture is the nursing child, the most helpless person in the household, and a mother who does not forget her. The KJV has “Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee”, where the ASV has “yea, these may forget” and the NWT 1984 “Even these women can forget”, keeping the Hebrew plural, gam elleh, “even these”. The NIV has “Though she may forget, I will not forget you!” and the Douay “and if she should forget”, both in the singular. The TLB opens the answer with “Never!”, which is not in the Hebrew. And racham, “have compassion”, stands in verses 10, 13 and 15, so that compassion frames the whole passage.

⭐ Engraved on the palms. Hen al-kappayim chaqotikh: “Look, on the palms I have engraved you.” The verb chaqah is to cut into a surface, and the thing cut is Zion; the next line is “your walls are before me continually”, a city seen as stone. The KJV has “I have graven thee upon the palms of my hands”, the NIV “I have engraved you on the palms of my hands”, the Douay “I have graven thee in my hands” and the TLB “I have tattooed your name upon my palm”, which says what the Hebrew does not: the verb does not name a tattoo, and the object is the city, not a name.

⭐ Sons who hurry, or builders. “Your sons hurry; those who tore you down and laid you waste go out from you.” Banayikh is written bnyk, and the same consonants can be read as “your sons” or “your builders” (bonayikh); this translation follows the reading with “sons”, as the NIV does (“Your children hasten back”). The Douay has “Thy builders are come”, the Geneva “Thy builders make haste” and the TLB “Soon your rebuilders shall come”, and each of those three then sets them against the destroyers, since “builders” and “those who tore you down” are opposites in one line. The sons are the ones of the next verses, who come back to a city that had thought them lost.

⭐ Too cramped for the children. The woman who is told in verse 18 to put the gathered ones on like an ornament and bind them on like a bride is the one who in verse 21 says, “Who bore me these, when I was bereaved and barren, exiled and set aside?” The word galmudah, “barren”, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one. The ruin of verse 19 turns into crowding: “surely now you will be too cramped for the inhabitants”, and the children of her bereavement say in her ears, “The place is too cramped for me; make room for me, that I may dwell”. The barren woman of Isaiah 54:2 (now on these pages) is told to “enlarge the place of your tent”, and that command, spoken to a woman in the same plight, is the other side of this chapter’s complaint.

Verses 22–26 · Isaiah 49:22–26 “I will lift up my hand to the nations” — sons carried in the bosom, kings as foster fathers, and the prey taken from the mighty

⭐ The signal, and the carried sons. “I will lift up my hand to the nations, and to the peoples I will raise my signal.” The nes, the signal raised for the nations, is also the signal of Isaiah 11:12 (now on these pages), where it gathers the outcasts of Israel. The sons come “in the bosom” (bechotsen) and the daughters “on the shoulder” (al katef); the NWT 1984 has “in the bosom”. Kings and princesses become the household of a child: omenayikh is the word for a guardian and nurse of a child, and the Geneva and the Douay have “nursing fathers”, the NIV “foster fathers” and the NWT 1984 and the NWT 2013 “caretakers”. They bow with their faces to the earth and lick the dust of her feet: the rising and bowing that verse 7 gave kings before the servant is now given before the city.

⭐ “Those who hope in me will not be ashamed”. The recognition formula comes again, “and you will know that I am Jehovah”, and then the reason: asher lo yevoshu qovay, “those who hope in me will not be ashamed”. The Douay has “they shall not be confounded that wait”. The line has a close parallel in Psalm 25:3 (now on these pages), where it is “none who wait for you” (kol qovekha), and the same verb, qavah, is the waiting.

⭐ The captives of a righteous one. Verse 24 asks two questions. The first is plain: can the prey be taken from a mighty man? The second reads in the Hebrew shevi tsaddiq, “the captives of a righteous one”, and the answer in verse 25 speaks of “the captives of a mighty man” and “the prey of a tyrant”, so many versions print “tyrant” in verse 24 as well, which is not what tsaddiq says. The KJV has “the lawful captive”, the ASV “the lawful captives”, the Geneva “the just captivity”; the NIV has “captives be rescued from the fierce”, the TLB “a tyrant let his captives go”, the NWT 1984 “the body of captives of the tyrant” and the NWT 2013 “the captives of the tyrant be rescued”, and the Douay has “that which was taken by the mighty”. This translation keeps “a righteous one”, because the Hebrew says it, and leaves the difference visible: the first group of versions translates the word, and the second group translates the answer.

⭐ “I will contend”, and the Mighty One of Jacob. “I will contend with the one who contends with you” is et yerivekh anokhi ariv, the same root twice, riv, “quarrel”. The NIV has “I will contend with those who contend with you”, the Geneva “for I will contend with him that contendeth with thee”, and the Douay “But I will judge those that have judged thee”. Verse 26 is the hardest line in the chapter and this translation leaves it as hard as it is: those who oppress her eat their own flesh and are drunk on their own blood as on sweet wine, and then all flesh knows who has done it. “Your savior and your redeemer, the Mighty One of Jacob”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and Isaiah 60:16 (now on these pages). Avir Ya’aqov, the Mighty One of Jacob, stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, Isaiah 60:16, Genesis 49:24 (now on these pages), and Psalm 132:2 and 5.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “you are my servant, Israel” for avdi attah Yisra’el; “from the womb” and “made mention of my name”; “too light a thing” for nakel; “a light of nations” for le’or goyim; “a time of favor” for et ratson; “a covenant of the people”; “bare heights”; “Sinim”, left as a name; “forget” for the four times of shakach; “engraved” for chaqah; “too cramped”; “signal” for nes; “foster fathers” and “nursing mothers”; “a righteous one” for tsaddiq in verse 24; “contend” for riv; and “the Mighty One of Jacob”, as at Isaiah 60:16.

Echoes paid. “You are my servant” (Isaiah 41:9); the despised servant (Isaiah 53:3); the barren woman who must enlarge her tent (Isaiah 54:2); the mountains that break into song (Isaiah 55:12); the signal that gathers the outcasts (Isaiah 11:12); the Mighty One of Jacob (Genesis 49:24); “none who wait for you” (Psalm 25:3); and the raised eyes of Isaiah 60:4, whose line is the same as verse 18 here.

Echoes planted. The light of nations and the covenant of the people (Isaiah 42:6); “you are my servant” (Isaiah 44:21); the mountains that break out (Isaiah 44:23); and the Mighty One of Jacob in Psalm 132:2 and 5. None of these is translated here yet.

⚠ A note on order: Isaiah stands on these pages at chapters 1, 6, 7, 9, 11, 14, 26, 35, 40, 41, 43, 49, 53, 54, 55, 58, 60 and 61, chosen by how often a passage is read rather than in sequence. The sequential thread is unchanged: next in sequence is Isaiah 2.
