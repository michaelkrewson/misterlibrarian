The same letter, still from Ephesus. Paul defends his apostleship and his right to support, a defence that the Corinthians may have been questioning. Acts 18:3 has Paul working as a tentmaker with Aquila and Priscilla, and Acts 18:5 has him occupied with the word when Silas and Timothy arrive from Macedonia; the chapter explains why he took no support.
c. AD 54–55, the same letter and date as chapters 1 to 8, written from Ephesus. Nothing in Acts dates the defence; the chapter gives no date of its own.

Verses 1–3 · free, an apostle, and a seal on the Corinthians

“Am I not free? Am I not an apostle?” Ouk eimi eleutheros; ouk eimi apostolos, the two questions open a defence, and the answer each time is yes. The first is about freedom, the second about office (apostolos). Paul has been accused of something, and the chapter asks the Corinthians to test his standing. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “Am I not free? Am I not an apostle?” and the KJV “Am I not an apostle? am I not free?”, which swaps the order. The translation keeps the Greek order.

“Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?” The apostle’s qualification is a sight: Iēsoun ton kyrion hēmōn heoraka, I have seen Jesus our Lord. The verb is the verb of seeing (horaō); Paul uses a form of it for his own sight of the risen Lord when he lists the appearances in 15:8. The Lord is Jesus here. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “Have I not seen Jesus our Lord?”

“A seal of my apostleship.” Hē sphragis mou tēs apostolēs, the seal of my apostleship, is the mark a document or a cargo carries to show it is genuine (sphragis). The Corinthians are the proof of his office. The shelf: the KJV “the seal of mine apostleship are ye in the Lord,” the ASV the same wording.

Verses 4–6 · the right to eat, the right to a wife, and the right not to work

“The right to eat and drink.” Exousian, the right or authority, is the word of exousia, the word Paul used of the Corinthians’ freedom in 8:9. Here it is the apostle’s right to be supported. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “we have authority to eat and drink, do we not?” and the KJV “Have we no right to eat and to drink?”

“A believing wife, a sister.” Adelphēn gynaika periagein, to lead about a sister as a wife, is an unusual phrase: the word for “sister” means a fellow believer, and the other apostles and Cephas are named as the ones who do it. The sentence does not say whether the wife travels with the apostle for the work or for household needs. The shelf: the ASV “to lead about a wife that is a believer,” the KJV “to lead about a sister, a wife.” The translation keeps “believing wife” to name the sense of “sister.”

“Barnabas and I.” Ē monos egō kai Barnabas ouk echomen exousian mē ergazesthai, or is it only Barnabas and I who do not have the right to refrain from working. Paul has chosen not to exercise the right he names. The shelf: the ASV “Or I only and Barnabas, have we not a right to forbear working?” and the NWT 1984 “Or is it only Barʹna·bas and I that do not have authority to refrain from [secular] work?”

Verses 7–12 · the soldier, the vineyard, the ox, and the sowing

“Who serves as a soldier at his own expense?” Tis strateuetai idiois opsōniois pote, who ever serves as a soldier at his own charges. The three images are a soldier, a planter, a shepherd, each of whom eats from what he works on. The shelf: the KJV “Who goeth a warfare any time at his own charges?” and the NWT 1984 “Who ever serves as a soldier at his own expense?”

“The Law says the same.” Mē kata anthrōpon tauta lalō ē kai ho nomos tauta ou legei, am I saying this in the human manner, or does not the Law say the same. Paul appeals to scripture. The verse he quotes is Deuteronomy 25:4: ou kēmōseis boun aloōnta, you shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “You must not muzzle a bull when it is threshing out the grain,” the ASV “Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn.”

“Is it for oxen that God is concerned?” Mē tōn boōn melei tō theō, is it the oxen God cares about. Paul asks the question and then answers it: di’ hēmas pantōs legei, he surely speaks for our sake. The argument runs from the literal sense of the law to a claim about its purpose. The shelf: the KJV “Doth God take care for oxen?” and the NWT 2013 “Is it bulls that God is concerned about?”

“Sown spiritual things, reap material things.” Ei hēmeis hymin ta pneumatika espeiramen, mega ei hēmeis hymōn ta sarkika therisomen, if we sowed spiritual things among you, is it a great thing if we reap material things from you. The sowing and reaping pattern is the argument of the whole section. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “if we have sown spiritual things to YOU, is it something great if we shall reap things for the flesh from YOU?”

“We have not used this right.” Alla ouk exrēsamethā tē exousia tautē, alla panta stegomen, but we have not used this authority; we bear all things. The verb stegō is to bear or endure, and the reason is hina mē tina egkopēn dōmen tō euangeliō tou Christou, so that we give no hindrance to the good news of Christ. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “we have not made use of this authority, but we are bearing all things, in order that we might not offer any hindrance to the good news about the Christ.”

Verses 13–18 · the temple, the gospel, and the boast that cannot be emptied

“The temple and the altar.” Hoi ta hiera ergazomenoi ta ek tou hierou esthiousin, those who work at the sacred things eat from the temple, and those who attend the altar share with the altar. Paul appeals to the priests, who were fed from the sacrifices. The shelf: the KJV “they which minister about holy things live of the things of the temple,” and the NWT 1984 “the men performing sacred duties eat the things of the temple.”

“The Lord ordained that they live from the gospel.” Houtōs kai ho kyrios diētaxen tois to euangelion katangellousin ek tou euangeliou zēn, so the Lord commanded that those who proclaim the good news live from the good news. The sentence claims an instruction of the Lord, and the Gospels record a saying like it: Luke 10:7, “the labourer is worthy of his hire.” The shelf: the NWT 1984 “the Lord ordained for those proclaiming the good news to live by means of the good news,” and the KJV “the Lord ordained that they which preach the gospel should live of the gospel.”

“I have used none of these rights.” The sentence breaks off. Paul begins to say that he would rather die than—and then the clause resumes: to kauchēma mou oudeis kenōsei, no one will empty my boast. The shape of the sentence is the point: the boast is Paul’s refusal of the payment. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “it would be finer for me to die than— no man is going to make my reason for boasting void!” and the KJV “for it were better for me to die, than that any man should make my glorying void.”

“Woe is me.” Anankē gar moi epikeitai; ouai gar moi estin ean mē euangelisōmai, necessity is laid upon me; woe is me if I do not proclaim the good news. The necessity is the commission, and the woe is the cost of refusing it. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “necessity is laid upon me. Really, woe is me if I did not declare the good news!”, and the NWT 2013 “woe to me if I do not declare the good news!”

“A reward and a stewardship.” Ei gar hekōn touto prassō, misthon echō; ei de akōn, oikonomian pepisteumai, if I do this willingly, I have a reward; if unwillingly, I have a stewardship entrusted to me. The two words split work into wages and trust. The word oikonomia is the household office of a steward (oikonomia), the word of 4:1. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “if I do it against my will, all the same I have a stewardship entrusted to me,” the KJV “a dispensation of the gospel is committed unto me.”

“What is my reward?” Tís oûn moú estin ho misthós, what then is my reward? The RV60 reads “What, then, is my reward?”, the NVI “What then is my reward?”, the TNM 1987 “Then what is my reward?” and the TNM 2019 “What is my reward?”

Verses 19–23 · free and a slave to all: to the Jews, the lawless, and the weak

“Free from all, a slave to all.” Eleutheros gar ōn ek pantōn pasin emauton edouleisa, for being free from all I made myself a slave to all. The sentence reverses the freedom of verse 1 into a service: the free man sells his freedom for the gain of others. The verb doulooō is the verb for enslaving. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “though I am free from all persons, I have made myself the slave to all,” the KJV “yet have I made myself servant unto all, that I might gain the more.”

“To the Jews as a Jew.” Egenomēn tois Ioudaiois hōs Ioudaios, to the Jews I became as a Jew. The sentence is a list of the groups Paul was to win: Jews, those under the law, those outside the law, and the weak. For those under the law he was not under it, and for those outside the law he was not outside God’s law but under the law of Christ (ennomos Christou). The shelf: the NWT 1984 “to those under law I became as under law, though I myself am not under law,” and the KJV “being not without law to God, but under the law to Christ.”

“To the weak I became weak.” Egenomēn tois asthenesin asthenēs, to the weak I became weak, to win the weak. The adjective is the one of 8:7, the weak conscience. The sentence matches the weak of 8:9–11 with a claim to have become like them. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “To the weak I became weak, that I might gain the weak. I have become all things to people of all sorts,” and the KJV “To the weak became I as weak, that I might gain the weak: I am made all things to all men, that I might by all means save some.”

Verses 24–27 · the race, the crown, and the body kept under

“Run so as to win.” Hoi en stadiō trechontes pantes men trechousin, heis de lambanei to brabeion, the runners in a stadium all run, but one receives the prize. The brabeion, the prize, is the award given to a winner (brabeion). The race is the image of the community’s effort. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “the runners in a race all run, but only one receives the prize? Run in such a way that you may attain it,” the KJV “Know ye not that they which run in a race run all, but one receiveth the prize? So run, that ye may obtain.”

“Self-control in all things.” Pas de ho agōnizomenos panta egkrateuetai, everyone who competes exercises self-control in all things (egkrateia). The word is the word of 7:9, where the unmarried are told to marry if they lack it. The crown is perishable for the athlete and imperishable for Paul. The shelf: the KJV “And every man that striveth in the games exerciseth self-control in all things,” and the NWT 1984 “Moreover, every man taking part in a contest exercises self-control in all things.”

“Not aimlessly.” Ouch hōs aderōs, not uncertainly, not aimlessly; ouch hōs aera derōn, not beating the air. The boxing picture is a fight with a visible target. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “the way I am running is not uncertainly; the way I am directing my blows is so as not to be striking the air,” and the ASV “I therefore so run, as not uncertainly; so fight I, as not beating the air.”

“I discipline my body.” Hypōpiazō mou to sōma kai doulagōgō, I pummel my body and lead it as a slave. The verb hypōpiazō, to strike under the eye, is a boxer’s word (hypōpiazō). The end of the sentence is the fear the chapter has been building: mē pōs adokimos genōmai, that I myself should not be disqualified (adokimos), the word of 3:13 for work that fails the test. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “I pummel my body and lead it as a slave,” the KJV “I keep under my body, and bring it into subjection,” and the ASV “I buffet my body, and bring it into bondage.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “right” for exousia at 9:4–18 and 9:12, the word of 8:9 and 7:37; “seal” for sphragís at 9:2; “stewardship” for oikonomia at 9:17, the word of 4:1; “weak” for asthenēs at 9:22, the word of 8:7; “self-control” for egkrateúetai at 9:25; “disqualified” for adokimos at 9:27, the word of 3:13.

Echoes paid. The right of 8:9 (8:9), now Paul’s own; the weak of 8:7–11, now the apostle who becomes weak (9:22); the temple of 3:16–17 and 6:19, now the temple whose workers eat (9:13); the self-control of 7:5 and 7:9 (9:25); the steward of 4:1–2, now the office Paul has been entrusted with (9:17); the boast of 1:31 and 4:7 (9:15–16); and the fire test of 3:13 (9:27).

Echoes planted. Paul’s apostolic sight of Jesus (9:1) returns in chapter 15, where he lists the appearances. Eating what is sacrificed returns in chapter 10 with the table of the Lord and the table of demons. The perishable crown (9:25) returns in chapter 15 as the body that is raised imperishable.

Next in this book: 1 Corinthians 10, already on these pages. Paul turns from his own rights to the people of the desert, who ate the same food and drank the same drink, and to the table of the Lord and the table of demons.
