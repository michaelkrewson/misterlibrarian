🕰
Jabin king of Hazor raises a northern coalition -- as numerous, the text says, as the sand on the seashore -- and Israel falls on it by surprise at the waters of Merom, hamstringing the captured horses and burning the chariots as commanded. Joshua turns back and burns Hazor alone, 'for it was formerly the head of all these kingdoms'; every other captured city is left standing on its own mound. The campaign runs 'many days', not a single set-piece day; no city makes peace except Gibeon, Jehovah hardens the northern kings' hearts against surrender, and Joshua cuts the Anakim off from the whole hill country, leaving only a remnant in Gaza, Gath and Ashdod. The chapter closes on Joshua giving the whole land as an inheritance by tribe, and the land's first rest from war.
Follows the southern campaign of chapters 9-10 as the second and final campaign of the conquest narrative, but is explicitly NOT continuous with it in the way chapters 6-10 are with each other: v18 states the northern war 'made war a long time... many days', in contrast to the single day-and-night compression of chapter 10. The book gives no year or duration for the interval. Same contested early/late-date question as chapters 1-10 (c. 1406 BC on the early date this project foregrounds, c. 1230-1210 BC on the late); the conquest and the judges have no era of their own in this timeline, so the chapter is filed under the Exodus era.

Verses 1–3 · Jabin king of Hazor raises the mirror of the last coalition, and Hermon gets a fourth people at its foot

★★ The northern campaign opens exactly as the southern one did. Joshua 10:1–5 (now on these pages) had Adoni-zedek king of Jerusalem hear of Joshua's victories and summon four fellow Amorite kings by name; this chapter has Jabin king of Hazor hear and summon three by name, then a whole region besides. Two coalitions, two kings acting first out of fear rather than being attacked, and two lists of names the narrator troubles to preserve.

★ The geography this time is a full compass, not a city list. North, hill country, Arabah, lowland, coast, east and west — the same fourfold vocabulary (hill country/Negev/Shephelah/Arabah) that closed the southern campaign's own summary (Joshua 10:40–41, now on these pages) reappears here to open the northern one, before a single battle is fought.

★★ The Hivite is now at Hermon, not at Gibeon. The last Hivite this book named made peace by cunning (Joshua 9, now on these pages); this one is drawn into the coalition instead, at the opposite end of the land, under the tallest mountain in the region. See Mizpah (under Hermon) — not the Mizpah of Gilead or of Benjamin, both later in the story.

Verses 4–5 · A coalition as numerous as the sand on the seashore — Abraham's own promise, turned against his descendants

★★★ The army arrayed against Israel is measured in Abraham's own words. Ka-chol asher al sefat, ‘as the sand that is on the shore of’, stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: Genesis 22:17 (already on these pages), where it names how many descendants Jehovah swears to give Abraham; this verse, where it names the enemy drawn up to stop those descendants from taking the land they were promised; 1 Samuel 13:5, where it becomes the Philistines' own army against Israel; and 1 Kings 5:9, where it turns positive again as the breadth of Solomon's own understanding. The same picture serves a blessing and a threat, depending only on which side of it a reader stands.

★ ‘They met together by appointment.’ Vayyiva'adu is a deliberate rendezvous, not a chance muster — five verses earlier the southern kings simply gathered and went up (Joshua 10:5, now on these pages); this coalition sets a meeting point first, the waters of Merom, and only then marches to it.

Verses 6–9 · Hamstring the horses — a rare verb shared with Jacob's curse on Simeon and Levi, and with David after them

★★★ The order is not simply to defeat the horses but to cripple them for good. Te'aqqer, ‘you shall hamstring’, is the same rare verb Jacob used, dying, of Simeon and Levi's massacre at Shechem: in their self-will they hamstrung an ox (Genesis 49:6, already on these pages) — there, gratuitous cruelty condemned in the same breath as murder; here, a divine command carried out twice over, once given (v6) and once done (v9), in the identical verb both times. The horses are not killed outright, only permanently disabled, so that the chariots they pulled become useless without the animals ever being butchered as spoil.

★ The shelf splits on the word itself. KJV and Geneva both keep the old English verb, ‘hough’ (rhymes with ‘how’); ASV modernizes it only slightly, ‘hock’; NWT, NIV and Douay-Rheims all read ‘hamstring’, the plainest modern equivalent, which this translation follows. The RV60 and RV 1909 both read «desjarretarás», and the NVI paraphrases it as a broken-legged image, «romperán las patas».

★★ David does the same thing later, to horses he could have kept. Generations on, David captures the chariot force of Hadadezer of Zobah and hamstrings all the chariot horses, sparing only a hundred chariots for himself (2 Samuel 8:4; 1 Chronicles 18:4, neither yet on these pages) — the identical verb, but now a king's own choice rather than a command relayed from Jehovah, and a choice that anticipates Deuteronomy 17:16's later warning that a king of Israel must not multiply horses for himself.

★ ‘They left no survivor.’ Sarid closes this engagement exactly as it will close the chapter's own summary refrain (v14, v22); the word that measures how total a victory was here measures how total a dispossession is by the chapter's end.

Verses 10–11 · Hazor, ‘formerly the head of all these kingdoms’ — the one city Joshua burns, and a king's title that will not stay buried

★★★ Hazor is singled out by rank before it is singled out by fire. Ki-Chatsor lefanim hi rosh kol ha-mamlakhot ha-elleh, ‘for Hazor was formerly the head of all these kingdoms’, is the narrator's own explanation for why this city alone, of the whole campaign, is burned outright (v11, v13) — the same distinction Jericho and Ai received in the southern campaign, and no other northern city does. Excavation has borne the claim out: Bronze Age Hazor was the largest city in the whole land, dwarfing every neighbour it is said here to have led. See Hazor.

★★ A king's title survives what a king does not. Jabin king of Hazor stands in only one other verse of the whole Hebrew Bible — generations later, and in Hazor again: Sisera, commander of a king Jabin's army, flees the battle Deborah and Barak win (Judges 4:17, not yet on these pages), and that same chapter says outright that this later Jabin reigned in Hazor (4:2). Readers have taken the recurrence three ways — a city rebuilt under a fallen dynasty's old name, a throne-title that survived any one king, or two separate strands of memory the Bible never lines up — and this translation does not choose among them. See Jabin.

Verses 12–15 · ‘He left nothing undone’ — the charge given to Joshua paid in full, and a mound that means the opposite of Deuteronomy's mound

★★★ The verse that closes this section is the verse Joshua's whole book has been building toward. Lo-hesir davar mikkol asher-tsivvah Jehovah et-Moshe, ‘he left nothing undone of all that Jehovah had commanded Moses’, answers the charge given at the very start of the book — be careful to do according to all the law… do not turn from it (Joshua 1:7–8, now on these pages) — with a narrator's verdict rather than a command. The identical sentence, almost word for word, will close the whole campaign a second time at v23.

★ A mound that is not a ruin. He-arim ha-omedot al tillam, ‘the cities standing on their own mound’, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible and nowhere else — and it means almost the opposite of the mound this book has used twice before. A tel olam, ‘a mound forever’, is Deuteronomy's own sentence for an apostate town and what Joshua made of Ai (Joshua 8:28, now on these pages): a place permanently reduced to a ruin. Here the same noun names a city's ordinary foundation, the rise it has always stood on, left standing and inhabited rather than burned. One word, two fates, and only the sentence around it tells a reader which.

★★ The shelf disagrees sharply on what ‘their mound’ even means. NIV and ASV both keep the geography, ‘their mounds’, and the NWT keeps it too, ‘their own mounds’; but KJV and Geneva both read ‘stood still in their strength’, an old construal that turns a landform into a military posture, and the Douay-Rheims reads ‘on hills and high places’. The Spanish shelf splits the same way: TNM reads «sus propios montículos», while RV60 and RV 1909 both read «colinas»/«cabezos», hills rather than mounds, and the NVI «cerros».

Verses 16–17 · From the bare mountain to Baal-gad — the whole land measured south to north, and measured again in reverse a chapter later

★★ The two endpoints of the conquest name a god of fortune and a bare mountain, one apiece. Baal-gad pairs the storm-god's name with gad, ‘fortune, luck’ — a name built on chance, at the northern edge of a campaign this book insists at every turn was never left to chance. Mount Halak, ‘the bare mountain’, at the southern edge, gets that plain description in only one other place in the Bible.

★★★ The whole formula recurs, reversed, as Joshua 12's own summary. ‘The bare mountain’ stands in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, running south-to-north, Mount Halak to Baal-gad; and Joshua 12:7 (now on these pages), running north-to-south, Baal-gad to Mount Halak — the identical two place-names, in the identical pairing, stated a second time in the opposite direction, as if the totality of what was taken needed saying twice to be believed.

Verses 18–20 · Many days, not one day — the one city that made peace, and a hardening of hearts that echoes Pharaoh's own

★ This campaign is not measured in a single day. Where the southern campaign turned on one day long enough for a sun to stand still (Joshua 10:12–14, now on these pages), this one is only ever dated many days — the northern war has no single set-piece miracle to anchor it, only a stated duration.

★★ ‘Except the Hivites, the inhabitants of Gibeon’ reaches back two chapters to name the sole exception again. Gibeon's peace by cunning (Joshua 9, now on these pages) is restated here as the standing exception to a rule that otherwise holds without a single further case: no other city in the whole conquest narrative makes peace with Israel.

★★★ Jehovah hardens a heart against peace for the second time in this translation, and it is the same verb Pharaoh's hardening used, not Sihon's. Le-chazzeq, ‘to harden’, is the verb of I will harden his heart, said of Pharaoh at the very start of the exodus story (Exodus 4:21, already on these pages); it is not the word Deuteronomy used of Sihon, which paired a different root, hiqshah, with amats (Deuteronomy 2:30, already on these pages). Two different hardenings, two different verbs, the same purpose both times: an enemy denied the option of surrender so that a war already underway can run to its full, commanded end. Readings differ on how a heart can be hardened by its own owner and by God in the same story without contradiction; this translation states both without resolving them, as Exodus itself does.

Verses 21–23 · The Anakim cut off, except where Goliath will come from — and the land's first rest from war

★★★ This is the narrative answer to a question the wilderness generation once asked and never heard answered. Who can stand before the sons of Anak? (Deuteronomy 9:2, already on these pages) was rhetorical, unanswerable fear when the spies brought it back from Hebron (Numbers 13:22, 28, 33, already on these pages); here Joshua simply cuts them off from Hebron, Debir, Anab, and the whole hill country of Judah and Israel. See the Anakim.

★★ The remnant is left in exactly the three cities a giant will later come from. Only in Gaza, in Gath, and in Ashdod, some remained — and Gath is Goliath's own hometown (1 Samuel 17, not yet on these pages). Whether his family is literally descended from this remnant or only grouped with the Anakim by size and by this same geography, the Bible never says outright; the overlap of place is exact either way.

★★★ ‘The land had rest from war’ is a sentence Joshua repeats once more of Caleb's own inheritance (Joshua 14:15, not yet on these pages), and it is the root the whole book of Judges will build its recurring hinge-sentence from — but never quite the same way. The Judges refrain, ‘and the land was quiet’, stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible (Judges 3:11, 3:30, 5:31, 8:28, none yet on these pages), each one naming a fixed count of years before the cycle of apostasy starts again. Here the same root states a fact at a campaign's end, not a lease with a term; Judges will spend a whole book showing how short that lease keeps turning out to be.

Echoes paid. Joshua 9’s Hivite treaty at Gibeon (now on these pages) is named twice as the one exception peace ever won in this book (vv12, 19). Joshua 10’s coalition-by-summons opens this chapter’s own coalition almost sentence for sentence, and its closing four-part geography (hill country/Negev/Shephelah/Arabah, 10:40–41) reopens this chapter at v16. Joshua 1:7–8’s charge to keep the whole law is answered twice, word for word, at v15 and v23. Genesis 22:17’s promise to Abraham of descendants like the sand of the seashore returns, turned against those same descendants, at v4; Genesis 49:6’s hamstrung ox returns as a command at vv6 and 9; Exodus 4:21’s hardened Pharaoh returns at v20; and Numbers 13/Deuteronomy 9:2’s unanswerable fear of the Anakim is answered at last at v21.

Echoes planted. Joshua 12 will list every king struck down in both campaigns, including Jabin, Madon, Shimron and Achshaph by name, and will repeat this chapter’s Mount-Halak-to-Baal-gad formula in reverse (v7). Joshua 14:15 repeats v23’s exact sentence of Caleb’s own inheritance. Joshua 19:15 and 19:25 give Shimron and Achshaph their settled place inside Zebulun and Asher; Madon, struck down at 12:19, never recurs again under this name. Judges 4 brings a king named Jabin, ruling in Hazor, back onto the page, and the whole book of Judges turns this chapter’s the land had rest from war into its own recurring, expiring refrain. 1 Kings 9:15 has Solomon refortify Hazor itself. And 1 Samuel 17 brings a Philistine giant out of Gath, the one city besides Gaza and Ashdod where this chapter’s Anakim survive.

Decisions. Hebrew and English verse numbers align through the whole chapter. The Masoretic text carries one open break, {פ} after v5, and three closed breaks, {ס} after v9, v20 and v23. ‘Jehovah’ for the tetragrammaton continues this project’s own convention. Chinneroth is kept as the Old Testament’s own name rather than anticipated as ‘the Sea of Galilee’; ha-Aravah and ha-Shefelah are kept as names, as at Joshua 10; te'aqqer/iqqer is ‘hamstring’, as at Genesis 49:6; chalalim is ‘slain’; sarid is ‘survivor’, as at Joshua 10; lefanim is ‘formerly’; cherem/hecherim is ‘devote to destruction’, as at Joshua 6 and 10; tillam is ‘their own mound’, kept distinct in the note from tel olam; lo-hesir davar is ‘left nothing undone’; ha-har he-chalaq is ‘Mount Halak’, kept as a proper name rather than translated ‘the bare mountain’ in the verse itself, with the description given in the note; yamim rabbim is ‘a long time’, literally ‘many days’; hishlimah is ‘made peace’, as at Joshua 9 and 10; le-chazzeq et-libbam is ‘to harden their hearts’, kept distinct in the note from Deuteronomy 2:30’s amats; techinnah is ‘favor’; vayyakhret is ‘cut off’; anaqim is kept as ‘Anakim’; and shaqetah mimmilchamah is ‘had rest from war’, kept distinct in the note from Judges’ own recurring vatishqot.

Library. Two new dictionary entries in both languages: aqar, to hamstring; and shaqat, to be quiet, at rest. Three existing dictionary entries extended in both languages: chazak, with this chapter’s hardened northern kings; amats, distinguishing the two roots; and tel, with this chapter’s opposite-sense mound. Nine new encyclopedia entries in both languages: Hazor, Jabin, the waters of Merom, Chinneroth, Madon, Shimron, Achshaph, Mizpah (under Hermon) and Baal-gad. Five existing encyclopedia entries extended in both languages: Mount Hermon, with Chinneroth’s own three names; the Anakim, with this chapter’s resolution; and Gath, Gaza and Ashdod, with the surviving remnant.

⚠ A note on order: Joshua stands on these pages at chapters 1–13. Joshua 14–24 remain the open sequence; next in this book is Joshua 14, where the lot is finally cast west of the Jordan and Caleb steps forward to claim the one inheritance in this book that chance does not decide.
