Torn away from the Ephesian elders, Paul and his party make a straight run by Cos, Rhodes and Patara and a ship to Phoenicia, sight Cyprus and put in at Tyre, where disciples tell him “through the Spirit” not to set foot in Jerusalem; after seven days they kneel on the beach and sail on to Ptolemais and Caesarea, and stay at the house of Philip the evangelist, one of the Seven, whose four virgin daughters prophesy. The prophet Agabus comes down from Judea, binds his own feet and hands with Paul’s belt and says the Jews will bind its owner in Jerusalem and hand him over to the nations; the party and the local people beg Paul not to go, and he answers that he is ready to be bound and even to die for the name of the Lord Jesus. At Jerusalem James and the elders tell him of the tens of thousands of zealous believers among the Jews and ask him to pay the expenses of four men with a vow and to be purified with them; he does, and goes into the temple. Jews from Asia seize him there and cry out that he has brought Greeks in; the doors are shut, the tribune of the cohort runs down with soldiers and centurions, has him bound with two chains, and has him carried up the steps to the barracks, where Paul asks, in Greek, to speak and, once allowed, motions to the people and addresses them in the Hebrew dialect.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are the days of the voyage (‘the next day,’ v1; ‘seven days’ at Tyre, v4; ‘one day’ at Ptolemais, v7), ‘more days’ at Caesarea (v10), ‘the next day’ at Jerusalem (v18), and the seven days of the vow, which are ‘about to be completed’ when the riot begins (v27). Luke gives no total, and this library dates the events no more closely than the book does. It follows Acts 20, where Paul is hurrying to be at Jerusalem for Pentecost; Acts 22 follows.

Verses 1–3 · ἀποσπασθέντας … εὐθυδρομήσαντες … Κῶ … Ῥόδον … Πάταρα … διαπερῶν εἰς Φοινίκην … ἀναφάναντες … εὐώνυμον … Συρίαν … Τύρον … ἀποφορτιζόμενον τὸν γόμον apospasthentas … euthydromēsantes … Kō … Rhodon … Patara … diaperōn eis Phoinikēn … anaphanantes … euōnymon … Syrian … Tyron … apophortizomenon ton gomon — Acts 21:1–3: why did Paul’s party have to “tear themselves away” from Miletus, and what is the sea route through Cos, Rhodes, Patara, past Cyprus and into Tyre?

Torn away (Acts 21:1). Apospasthentas ap’ autōn is literally “having been torn away from them,” a passive participle. English has no easy way to say “having been torn,” so “torn ourselves away” turns it into a reflexive; the Greek form is passive: the party is pulled loose. “Them” are the elders of the congregation whom Paul called over from Miletus (20:17), who wept, fell on his neck and kissed him, and then “escorted him to the ship” (20:37–38); the participle looks straight back at that scene. The verb apospaō stands in four verses of the New Testament: Matthew 26:51 (apespasen, a sword “drawn”), Luke 22:41 (Jesus “withdrawn” from the disciples “about a stone’s throw,” and then kneeling to pray), Acts 20:30 and here. The Acts 20:30 use is Paul’s own warning to these same elders, that men would arise “to draw the disciples away after themselves.” The same verb therefore names what Paul feared from outsiders and what Luke says happened to the travelers when they left the elders. The two are different in kind (a pulling-away that someone does, and a separation that someone suffers), and the verb itself says only that something is pulled. Luke does not comment on the repetition. NIV and NWT 1984 “torn ourselves away” keep the force of the verb. ASV “were parted from them,” Douay “being parted from them,” Geneva “were departed from them” and KJV “were gotten from them” soften it to a leave-taking; TLB “After parting from the Ephesian elders” also names them, where the Greek has only “from them.”

A straight run, three ports (Acts 21:1). Euthydromēsantes, “having run a straight course,” comes from the adjective euthys, “straight,” and the verb of running. The verb euthydromeō stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Acts 16:11, and both belong to a “we” voyage. The two sentences have the same shape: anachthentes there and anachthēnai here, “put out to sea,” then the straight run to a first port, then the next day to a second. At 16:11 that was “a straight run to Samothrace, and on the next day to Neapolis,” and the “next day” is tē epiousē; here it is tē hexēs, another phrase for the same thing. Three ports are named in one clause: Cos, Rhodes, Patara. Rhodon stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and so does Patara. Luke gives no distance, no number of days at sea and no reason for these stops. The apparatus gives the island’s name as Kō to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and as Kōn to Robinson–Pierpont; both are one island, and the choice changes nothing in English. The English spellings differ: KJV “Coos” and Douay “Coos” keep the older form, NIV prints “Kos,” and ASV, Geneva, NWT 1984 and TLB “Cos” agree with this translation. For the run itself, KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay have “a straight course”, NWT 1984 “ran with a straight course,” and NIV and TLB “sailed straight” to the first port. See Cos, Rhodes and Patara.

A ship crossing to Phoenicia (Acts 21:2). Heurontes ploion diaperōn eis Phoinikēn is “having found a ship crossing over to Phoenicia.” The participle diaperaō stands in five verses of the New Testament: in Matthew 14:34, Mark 5:21 and 6:53 it is a crossing of the lake by boat, in Luke 16:26 it is the crossing that cannot be made over the great chasm, and here it is a crossing of the open water. Luke does not say why the party passes from the short hops of v1 to a ship on a crossing; the verse says only that they found it and boarded. Phoenicia is a name Acts has already used, in 11:19 (“as far as Phoenicia and Cyprus and Antioch”) and 15:3 (“both Phoenicia and Samaria”); see Phoenicia. ASV “crossing over unto Phoenicia,” NIV “crossing over to Phoenicia” and NWT 1984 “crossing to” keep the crossing; KJV “sailing over unto Phenicia,” Douay “sailing over to Phenice” and Geneva “went over unto Phoenicia” keep a form of “over”; TLB “sailing for the Syrian province of Phoenicia” drops the crossing and adds a province, a phrase the Greek does not have. NWT 1984 also reads “boat” where the other six versions have “ship”; the Greek is ploion, the same word as at 20:38 (“the ship”).

Sighted, and left on the left (Acts 21:3). Anaphanantes is the verb of a land coming into view, and it is rare: anaphainō stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Luke 19:11, where the disciples think that “the kingdom of God was going to appear at once.” The apparatus gives anaphanantes to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 and anaphanentes to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont; the two are different aorist participles of one verb, the first active in form (“having brought Cyprus into view”) and the second passive in form, and English has one natural word for both. This translation prints “sighted.” ASV “come in sight of,” NWT 1984 “coming in sight of,” NIV “sighting” and TLB “sighted” are close to it; KJV “discovered,” Geneva “discovered” and Douay “discovered” use the older English word for first seeing something. Then katalipontes autēn euōnymon, “having left it left.” The adjective euōnymos stands in nine verses of the New Testament. Seven of the other eight set it against the right hand in the same verse (Matthew 20:21, 20:23, 25:33, 27:38, Mark 10:40, 15:27, and Revelation 10:2); Matthew 25:41 has it alone, “those on the left.” Here it is a bearing at sea, with no right hand beside it. The word is built from eu, “well,” and onoma, “name,” and the usual explanation, reported here and not checked on this page, is that “well-named” was a polite word for the unlucky side. For the ship, Cyprus on the left means it passed to the south of the island, which is what NIV “passing to the south of it” says; the Greek gives only the left hand. ASV, KJV and Geneva “on the left hand,” NWT 1984 “on the left side” and TLB “on our left” keep it; Douay has “leaving it on the left hand” as well. Cyprus is not new to Luke’s readers: it is Barnabas’s own island (“a Cypriot by birth,” 4:36), the first sea-destination of Barnabas and Paul (13:4, “sailed away to Cyprus”), where Barnabas went with Mark after the sharp disagreement (15:39), and a place the scattered believers reached (11:19). The verse says only that Paul’s ship saw it and kept going. The island comes back with a named man, Mnason “a Cypriot,” at 21:16, and with the winds at 27:4 (not yet on these pages).

Syria, Tyre and the cargo (Acts 21:3). Epleomen eis Syrian, “we sailed on to Syria,” follows “Phoenicia” in v2 with a second region name, and then katēlthomen eis Tyron gives the port. Luke does not say how the two names fit. TLB folds one name into the other twice, “the Syrian province of Phoenicia” at v2 and “Tyre, in Syria” here; that Tyre lay in the Roman province of Syria is reported here, not checked on this page. Syria was also where Paul had meant to sail when the plot turned him back (20:3, “about to set sail for Syria”), so Syria, named here, is the destination 20:3 gave before the plot changed his route. Tyre has appeared before in Luke’s writing as a country “fed from the king’s” land (12:20, the Tyrians and Sidonians, with Sidon), as a seacoast from which a crowd came to Jesus (Luke 6:17), and in a woe of Jesus (Luke 10:13–14). On the verb, the apparatus gives katēlthomen, “we came down,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and katēchthēmen, “we were brought down,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The second is the form of the arrival at Sidon at 27:3 (not yet on these pages), and the first is the form of the arrival at Myra at 27:5 (not yet on these pages); so both readings are verbs Luke uses for putting in at a port. ASV “landed at Tyre,” KJV “landed at Tyre,” NWT 1984 “landed at Tyre,” NIV “landed at Tyre” and Geneva “arrived at Tyre” do not show which reading they follow. The reason for the stop is the last clause: ekeise gar to ploion ēn apophortizomenon ton gomon, “for there the ship was unloading its cargo”; the order to ploion ēn is Westcott–Hort’s, Tregelles’s and Nestle–Aland’s, and Robinson–Pierpont has ēn to ploion, a word-order variant only. The verb apophortizomai stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so its sense rests on its parts (apo, “off,” and a verb of loading) and on the context, and no second use confirms it. It is a periphrastic present, “was unloading”; “was to unload” reads it as the purpose of the stop, and Luke has just said why they stopped. NWT 1984 “was to unload [its] cargo” and NIV “was to unload its cargo” read it so; TLB “the ship unloaded” and Geneva “the ship unladed the burden” state it as a fact. The noun gomon, “cargo,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Revelation 18:11 (not yet on these pages) and Revelation 18:12 (not yet on these pages), where the merchants of the earth mourn because their cargo has no buyer, and the cargo is gold, silver, precious stone and pearls. KJV “burden,” ASV “burden” and Douay “burden” use the old English word for a load; the cargo of the ship at Tyre is not described.

Verses 4–6 · ἀνευρόντες … διὰ τοῦ πνεύματος … μὴ ἐπιβαίνειν … ἐξαρτίσαι … προπεμπόντων … θέντες τὰ γόνατα … προσευξάμενοι … ἀπησπασάμεθα … ἐνέβημεν … εἰς τὰ ἴδια aneurontes … dia tou pneumatos … mē epibainein … exartisai … propempontōn … thentes ta gonata … proseuxamenoi … apēspasametha … enebēmen … eis ta idia — Acts 21:4–6: what does “through the Spirit” mean when the disciples at Tyre tell Paul not to set foot in Jerusalem, and what happened in the seven days, on the beach and at the ship?

Found the disciples at Tyre (Acts 21:4). Aneurontes de tous mathētas epemeinamen autou hēmeras hepta: “And having found the disciples, we stayed there seven days.” The ship put in at Tyre to unload (see the note at v1), and Tyre sits in Phoenicia, where Luke has already put believers: those scattered after Stephen “went through as far as Phoenicia” (11:19), and Paul and Barnabas “passed through both Phoenicia and Samaria” with the news of the nations (15:3). In Acts, the Tyrians come up before this chapter at 12:20 only, as the people who were fed from the king’s country and came asking Herod for peace (12:20), and the disciples of this verse are not that crowd. The verb is anheuriskō, the finding verb heuriskō with a prefix, and it stands in two verses of the New Testament, Luke 2:16, where the shepherds “came in haste and found” Mary, Joseph and the infant, and this one; both are in Luke’s own writing. Whether the prefix adds the sense of searching is a lexical judgment that is reported here and not checked on this page, and it is what the English versions decide for the reader: NIV “sought out the disciples” and NWT 1984 “By a search we found the disciples” print the search, while ASV “having found the disciples,” KJV “finding disciples,” Geneva “found disciples” and Douay “finding disciples” print only the finding, and TLB “found the local believers” adds “local” to the people. The Greek has the article, tous mathētas, “the disciples”; the apparatus string gives aneurontes de tous to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and kai aneurontes to Robinson–Pierpont, so that reading has “and” for de and no tous; the shelf’s “the” or no “the” follows the same line, though English articles do not show which Greek stood behind a version. The seven days are a second week-long stay in these chapters, after the seven at Troas (20:6), where the verb is a different one (diatribō); here it is epimenō, “to stay on.”

“Through the Spirit” and Paul’s going (Acts 21:4). Hoitines tō Paulō elegon dia tou pneumatos mē epibainein eis Hierosolyma, “they kept telling Paul through the Spirit not to set foot in Jerusalem.” The verb is an imperfect, elegon, “were saying, kept saying,” and the prohibition is mē with an infinitive. The phrase is dia tou pneumatos, with the article and without hagion, “holy.” It stands in five verses of the New Testament: Acts 11:28, where Agabus “signified through the Spirit” that a famine was coming, this verse, and 1 Corinthians 2:10, 1 Corinthians 12:8 and Ephesians 3:16 (not yet on these pages). In the one other place in Acts, then, a person speaks and the Spirit is the one through whom the word is given; the Greek of this verse does not say more than that the Spirit stands behind what the disciples said. What makes the verse hard is its neighbors. At 20:22 Paul says he is going to Jerusalem “bound in the spirit” (the note there leaves open whether that is his own spirit or the Holy Spirit), and at 20:23 “the Holy Spirit testifies solemnly to me city by city, saying that bonds and afflictions await me.” Tyre is one of the cities, and Paul goes on from it. Later in this chapter the prophet Agabus speaks with Tade legei to pneuma to hagion, “Thus says the Holy Spirit” (the note at v10), and the verses after that are Paul’s answer to a plea not to go up (vv12–14). Readings differ, and this page does not choose between them. On one, the Spirit made known what awaited Paul, the “bonds and afflictions” of 20:23, and the disciples drew from it their own conclusion that he should not go; dia then marks the channel of their knowledge and the Greek leaves the prohibition itself with them. On a second, the Spirit’s own word to Paul came as a prohibition, and the reader then has to relate it to 20:22, where Paul has already described himself as bound to go; whether that is the Spirit pulling two ways or the Spirit’s word being weighed by Paul is a question the Greek does not answer, and readers who take it this way have divided on whether Paul, in going on, is following that word or resisting it. On a third, what is told at Tyre is a warning of cost and not a command, a repeat of the “city by city” testimony. Luke reports no reply from Paul at Tyre, and the seven days pass without one. These are the readings the Greek leaves open; which commentators or communions hold which is reported here in outline and was not checked against their own texts. The shelf shows the same range in small marks. KJV “said to Paul through the Spirit,” ASV “said to Paul through the Spirit,” Geneva “through the Spirit” and Douay “through the Spirit” print the capital; NIV “Through the Spirit they urged Paul” prints the capital and makes the telling an urging; NWT 1984 “through the spirit they repeatedly told Paul” prints the lower case (the doctrinal rendering of “holy spirit” as God’s active force flagged at 20:22) and keeps the imperfect; TLB “the Holy Spirit prophesying through them” adds both “Holy” and “prophesying,” neither of which the Greek has here, and turns the telling into a “warned.” This translation prints “the Spirit” with a capital, as at 11:28, because the phrase there and here is the Spirit as the one through whom people speak; at 20:22, where Paul’s own spirit is open, it prints “spirit” in lower case. The capital says who is meant by the phrase and leaves open what the prohibition was.

“Set foot” or “go up” (Acts 21:4). The infinitive is epibainein in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and anabainein in Robinson–Pierpont. Epibainō is “to step onto, to set foot on,” and anabainō is “to go up,” the usual verb for the trip up to Jerusalem; both are about arriving there, and the difference is that the first looks at the stepping and the second at the road. The first verb stands in six verses of the New Testament: Matthew 21:5 (the king “mounted on a donkey”), Acts 20:18, 21:2 and 25:1 and 27:2, and this verse; of those, 21:2 and 25:1 and 27:2 are not yet on these pages. The echo to take with the reader is 20:18, where Paul says “from the first day on which I set foot in Asia” (20:18): the verb he used for his first step into Asia is the verb in which the Tyre disciples now tell him not to take his next one. ASV “set foot in Jerusalem” and NWT 1984 “set foot in Jerusalem” keep the stepping, and this translation’s “set foot” does too; KJV “go up to Jerusalem,” Geneva “go up to Jerusalem” and Douay “go up to Jerusalem” give the sense of the second verb, and NIV “go on to Jerusalem” and TLB “go on to Jerusalem” say neither. The English can match one Greek reading and not be evidence that its translators had it. In the printed text the second verb stands at v12 (anabainein, “to go up”), where the plea is made again by the people of Caesarea and Paul’s companions; on the Robinson–Pierpont reading v4 and v12 use one verb, and on the other three they use two. The apparatus also gives Hierosolyma in this verse to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and Hierousalēm to Robinson–Pierpont, a variation of the city’s name only; the printed text of v12 has Hierousalēm.

“Completed the days” (Acts 21:5). Hote de egeneto exartisai hēmas tas hēmeras, “And when it came about that we had completed the days,” has egeneto with an infinitive and an accusative subject, and the days, the seven of v4, as the thing completed. Exartizō stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 2 Timothy 3:17 (not yet on these pages), where the participle exērtismenos says that the person of God is “equipped” for every good work; “equipped” is this note’s gloss of that Greek, and the same verse has artios just before it. Whether the verb means to finish, to fit out or both is not decided by this verse; what it has is days as the object, and so time to be finished. The shelf prints the finishing or the end of the time: KJV “accomplished those days,” ASV “accomplished the days,” NWT 1984 “completed the days,” Geneva “the days were ended,” Douay “the days being expired,” NIV “When it was time to leave” (which names what the end of the days led to, and not the days) and TLB “At the end of the week” (which says “week” for the seven days of v4). The apparatus gives the order exartisai hēmas to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles and hēmas exartisai to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont; that is word order only, and the sense is the same.

Escorting, with wives and children (Acts 21:5). Exelthontes eporeuometha propempontōn hēmas pantōn syn gynaixi kai teknois heōs exō tēs poleōs is a genitive absolute, “all of them escorting us, with wives and children, until outside the city.” The verb propempō (see the dictionary entry) stands in nine verses of the New Testament: Acts 15:3 (“sent on their way by the congregation”), 20:38 (proepempon, “they escorted him to the ship”), this verse, Romans 15:24, 1 Corinthians 16:6 and 16:11 (not yet on these pages), 2 Corinthians 1:16 (“to be sent on my way by you to Judea”), Titus 3:13 (not yet on these pages) and 3 John 6. The three Acts verses take two shapes: at 15:3 the congregation sends and the verse describes no walk, and at 20:38 and here the group goes with the travellers to the ship or the shore. At Miletus the company is the elders Paul summoned, and they escorted him after the kneeling and the weeping (20:17, 20:36–38); at Tyre it is “all of them,” and Luke names the women and the children. The shelf renders the verb in four ways, none of them showing the genitive absolute: KJV “brought us on our way,” ASV “brought us on our way” and Douay “bringing us on our way” use one English idiom; Geneva “accompanied us” and NIV “accompanied us” the second; NWT 1984 “conducted us” the third; and TLB “the entire congregation including wives and children walked down to the beach with us” supplies a noun, “congregation,” which the Greek does not have here (only pantōn, “all”) and moves the walk to the beach.

Kneeling on the beach (Acts 21:5). Kai thentes ta gonata epi ton aigialon proseuxamenoi, “and kneeling down on the beach, we prayed,” is the second kneeling of Paul’s company on the road to Jerusalem, after Miletus, and the fifth in Luke’s two books to use the idiom of 20:36 (see the note there): Luke 22:41 (Jesus, “kneeling down, he prayed”), Acts 7:60 (Stephen, “kneeling down, he cried out”), 9:40 (Peter, “kneeling down, prayed” before he spoke to the body), 20:36 (Paul, with the elders) and here. The idiom stands in six verses of the New Testament if Mark 15:19 is counted, where the soldiers put their knees down in mock homage; the count was reached by reading the hits for gonata, which also finds Ephesians 3:14 and Hebrews 12:12 with other verbs, and those are not the idiom. Of the five Luke–Acts verses, this is the one where the place is named as a beach (aigialos). The apparatus gives the participle proseuxamenoi, “having prayed,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the finite prosēuxametha, “we prayed,” to Robinson–Pierpont. On the participle, kneeling and praying are two steps leading to the main verb of the next verse (apēspasametha, “we bade farewell”); on the finite verb, the prayer is a separate main action and the farewell is another. The shelf is split on where the sentence ends: KJV “we kneeled down on the shore, and prayed” and Douay “we kneeled down on the shore, and we prayed” end the verse with the prayer; ASV “kneeling down on the beach, we prayed, and bade each other farewell” and TLB “where we prayed and said our farewells” run the farewell into it; and NIV “on the beach we knelt to pray” turns the second participle into a purpose.

Farewell, boarding and home (Acts 21:6). Apēspasametha allēlous, kai enebēmen eis to ploion, ekeinoi de hypestrepsan eis ta idia: “and bade one another farewell, and we went aboard the ship, and they returned to their own homes.” The farewell is apaspazomai, the greeting verb of 20:1 with apo- on it, “to say goodbye from.” The compound stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Robinson–Pierpont has kai aspasamenoi allēlous, “and having greeted one another,” with the simple verb as a participle, the form it has at 20:1; the other three have the finite compound, so on one reading the departure is a participle leading into the boarding, and on the other it is a main verb of its own. The shelf: KJV “taken our leave one of another,” Douay “bid one another farewell,” NWT 1984 “said good-bye to one another” and NIV “saying goodbye to each other” read the word as the spoken parting, and Geneva “embraced one another” reads it as the gesture, the same split that stands at 20:1. For the boarding the three witnesses differ in one prefix. Westcott–Hort and Tregelles have enebēmen from embainō, “to step in”; Nestle–Aland 28 has anebēmen from anabainō, “to go up,” the up of climbing on board; Robinson–Pierpont has epebēmen, from the verb of v4 and of Luke’s ship scenes at 21:2 and 27:2 (the last not yet on these pages). All three mean they went aboard. The shelf reads this way: NWT 1984 “went up into the boat” has the “up,” and the sense of Nestle–Aland 28’s verb; ASV “went on board the ship,” NIV “went aboard the ship” and TLB “went aboard” say the sense only; and KJV “took ship,” Geneva “took ship” and Douay “took ship” use the old idiom. In Acts the verb embainō stands here alone, and elsewhere the New Testament has it of boats in the Gospels (Matthew 8:23 and Luke 8:22 among them). The last phrase is eis ta idia, “to their own,” a neuter plural that this translation prints “to their own homes.” The phrase stands in four verses of the New Testament: John 1:11 (“He came to his own”), 16:32 (“each to his own home”), 19:27 (“into his own home”) and this one. The neuter can mean “their own things, their own place,” and the context decides: the Tyre families go home. ASV “returned home again,” KJV “returned home again,” Geneva “returned home,” Douay “returned home,” NIV “returned home” and TLB “returned home” make it one home; NWT 1984 “returned to their homes” makes it many, as this translation does. The next verse takes the ship on to Ptolemais; see the note at v7.

Verses 7–9 · Ἡμεῖς … διανύσαντες … Πτολεμαΐδα … ἀσπασάμενοι … Φιλίππου τοῦ εὐαγγελιστοῦ ὄντος ἐκ τῶν ἑπτὰ … θυγατέρες τέσσαρες παρθένοι προφητεύουσαι Hēmeis … dianysantes … Ptolemaida … aspasamenoi … Philippou tou euangelistou ontos ek tōn hepta … thygateres tessares parthenoi prophēteuousai — Philip the evangelist and his four daughters who prophesied: who was Philip in Acts 21:8, what does “one of the Seven” mean, and what does Acts 21:9 say, and not say, about the prophesying daughters?

“Finished the voyage” and Ptolemais (Acts 21:7). Hēmeis de ton plōun dianysantes apo Tyrou opens, as 20:13 did, with the pronoun hēmeis, “we,” put in for the party whose ship this is; the Tyre of v3–6 is left behind. The participle dianysantes, “having finished,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; the other hit for the stem (Luke 6:12) is dianyktereuōn, “spending the night,” a different word. The noun plous, “voyage,” stands in two verses of Acts, here and 27:10 (not yet on these pages). The English shelf splits on the verb. ASV “finished the voyage”; Douay “finished the voyage by sea”; NWT 1984 “completed the voyage”; KJV “finished our course”; Geneva “ended the course”; NIV “continued our voyage” names a leg of a longer trip rather than the end of one; TLB “The next stop” drops the verb. Ptolemais, in the accusative Ptolemaida, stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and Luke says nothing about it beyond the arrival and the stay. That it is the city older sources call Acco (a name this translation prints at Judges 1:31) is reported in standard reference works and is not checked on this page. The visit is a single day, hēmeran mian, after aspasamenoi, “having greeted,” the adelphoi, “the brothers”: the same greeting-verb as at 18:22 and 20:1, and again at 21:19. NIV “brothers and sisters” adds a word the Greek masculine plural does not print; TLB “believers” names the people by what they are rather than by the family word. The apparatus lists no variant at v7.

Caesarea, and the house of Philip (Acts 21:8). Tē de epaurion exelthontes ēlthomen eis Kaisareian, “on the next day, having gone out, we came to Caesarea.” Caesarea is already on these pages as the place Philip “came to” at 8:40, the home of Cornelius at 10:1 and 10:24, the place Herod went down to at 12:19 and Paul’s stop at 18:22. Luke has not named Philip since 8:40 (the Philippoi of 16:12 and 20:6 is the city), so the verse picks him up where Acts 8 left him: “Philip was found at Azotus, and passing through, he was bringing the good news to all the cities until he came to Caesarea.” The party goes eis ton oikon Philippou, “into the house of Philip,” and the same verb as in v7 gives the stay, emeinamen par’ autō, “we stayed with him.” The “we” who enter his house is the narrator’s own; the verse does not say how many they were or how Philip knew they were coming.

“The evangelist” (Acts 21:8). Euangelistēs stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Ephesians 4:11, where Christ gives “apostles,” “prophets,” euangelistas, and then “shepherds and teachers” (4:11 not yet on these pages), and 2 Timothy 4:5, where Timothy is told ergon poiēson euangelistou, “do the work of an evangelist” (4:5 not yet on these pages). None of the three defines the word. Here it is a title Luke attaches to the man whose own doings Acts 8 told with the verb euangelizomai, “to bring good news”: Philip is the subject of it at 8:12, 8:35 and 8:40, in Samaria (8:5), on the Gaza road (8:26) and then through the coastal cities. Whether Luke means an office, a calling or simply a description of what Philip did is not said in the verse. KJV, ASV, Douay and NIV “Philip the evangelist” take the Greek word over as the English one; Geneva and TLB “Philip the Evangelist” capitalise it as a title; NWT 1984 “evangelizer” builds the noun from the verb instead.

“One of the Seven” (Acts 21:8). Ontos ek tōn hepta, literally “being out of the seven,” carries the article and no noun. The phrase tōn hepta stands in one verse of Acts, this one; in 6:3 the apostles ask for “seven men” without a title, and 6:5 names them, Philip second after Stephen, so “the Seven” is a label the reader supplies from there (see The Seven and Philip the evangelist). Acts 6 calls their work “the daily service” (6:1) and, in the Twelve’s words, to “serve tables” (6:2), with diakonia and its verb; the Greek of 21:8 has no word for what the Seven were. The shelf supplies one in two versions. NIV “one of the Seven” keeps the numeral as a name; NWT 1984 “one of the seven men” fills the gap with the noun of 6:3; Geneva “one of the seven Deacons” and TLB “one of the first seven deacons” put in an English office-word that this verse does not print, and the shipped English of 6:1–5 does not use it either; KJV, ASV and Douay “one of the seven” stay with the bare numeral. This translation’s “one of the Seven” keeps the bare numeral, as the version above does, with the capital marking it as a name.

Four daughters, virgins, prophesying (Acts 21:9). Toutō de ēsan thygateres tessares parthenoi prophēteuousai: “to this man were four daughters, virgins, prophesying,” a possessive dative, with prophēteuousai a present feminine plural participle, so “prophesying” or “who prophesied” are both within what the form allows, and the verse says nothing about when or how often. Parthenos and its relatives stand in fifteen verses of the New Testament, in Matthew, Luke, Acts (this verse only), 1 and 2 Corinthians and Revelation; Luke himself has it in the noun for Anna’s youth at Luke 2:36, “having lived with a husband seven years from her virginity,” where Anna is called prophētis, “prophetess” (see Anna). Which sense of parthenos is live in a verse depends on the verse, and the shelf divides: ASV “four virgin daughters” turns the noun into an adjective; KJV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 “four daughters, virgins” keep the Greek’s apposition; NIV and TLB “four unmarried daughters” read parthenoi as marital state; TLB “the gift of prophecy” also turns the verb prophēteuousai into a noun and a gift, which the Greek does not print. The Greek says only the word; it gives neither their names nor their ages, and it does not say how long they stayed unmarried.

What the daughters prophesy, and what Acts does not say (Acts 21:9). The verb prophēteuō stands in four verses of Acts, and this verse is the last of them. Two are Joel’s: 2:17, “your sons and your daughters will prophesy,” and 2:18, where the male and female slaves “will prophesy.” The third is 19:6, where the twelve men at Ephesus “were speaking in tongues and prophesying.” The plural thygateres, “daughters,” stands in two verses of Acts, 2:17 and this one, and in both the daughters are the ones who prophesy. The Greek of 21:9 has no object and no speech: the daughters say nothing here, and the prophecy of the next verses comes from Agabus, who “came down from Judea” (21:10) and speaks at 21:11. Acts also does not say that the four spoke to Paul, that they agreed or disagreed with Agabus, or what they said to anyone. The same verb is used of a woman at 1 Corinthians 11:5, pasa de gynē proseuchomenē ē prophēteuousa, “every woman praying or prophesying” (not yet on these pages), the same feminine participle as here. Later Christian writers tell more about Philip’s daughters and where they were buried; that is reported here, not checked on this page.

Apparatus: “we came” against “those around Paul came” (Acts 21:8), and word order (21:9). At v8 Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print ēlthomen, “we came,” and Robinson–Pierpont prints hoi peri ton Paulon ēlthon, “those around Paul came,” so the editions differ on whether the sentence names Paul in the third person before it returns to “we stayed with him.” Nothing in the apparatus lists a difference at emeinamen, the verb of the stay. The shelf is split along the same line: KJV “we that were of Paul’s company departed” and Geneva “Paul and we that were with him” carry the wording of the second reading; NIV “we reached Caesarea,” NWT 1984 “we set out” and ASV “we departed” follow the first. What the variant shows about the “we” is only this: one edition prints a third-person way of naming the group where the other three print the first person, and the narrator’s “we” here is the same one that 20:5, 20:6 and 20:13 brought back after 16:10–17; Luke does not say who “we” are. At v9 the editions differ in order only: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print tessares parthenoi, “four virgins,” and Robinson–Pierpont parthenoi tessares, “virgins four.” That is a difference of word order, not of substance, and the English renderings quoted above do not differ on the number.

Verses 10–14 · Ἅγαβος … τὴν ζώνην … Τάδε λέγει τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον … παραδώσουσιν εἰς χεῖρας ἐθνῶν … ἐντόπιοι … συνθρύπτοντές μου τὴν καρδίαν … ἑτοίμως ἔχω … ἡσυχάσαμεν … Τοῦ κυρίου τὸ θέλημα γινέσθω Hagabos … tēn zōnēn … Tade legei to pneuma to hagion … paradōsousin eis cheiras ethnōn … entopioi … synthryptontes mou tēn kardian … hetoimōs echō … hēsychasamen … Tou kyriou to thelēma ginesthō — Acts 21:10–14: what did Agabus’s belt prophecy say about Paul, what does “crushing my heart” mean, and was Paul wrong to go up to Jerusalem anyway?

Agabus, who “came down” (Acts 21:10). Epimenontōn de hēmeras pleious, “as we stayed on for more days,” uses the verb of v4, where the party “stayed” seven days at Tyre (epemeinamen), and the comparative pleious says “more” days without giving a number. The apparatus gives de to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and has Robinson–Pierpont add hēmōn, “our,” so that the genitive absolute names its subject; the English “we” is the same either way. On the shelf, KJV “many days” and Geneva “many days” put it strongly, ASV “some days” and Douay “some days” weakly, NIV “a number of days” and NWT 1984 “quite a number of days” in between, and TLB “several days” likewise. Hagabos stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 11:28, where he “stood up” at Antioch and “signified through the Spirit” a great famine. The “came down” is katēlthen, from the verb of 11:27 (katēlthon), where “prophets came down from Jerusalem to Antioch” and Agabus is introduced as “one of them”; the place of origin is now “Judea,” not “Jerusalem.” Luke has just said, at v9, that Philip had four daughters who “prophesied” and gives no words of theirs there; the prophet who speaks is a visitor, and the verse does not say why. TLB “who also had the gift of prophecy” turns the noun prophētēs, “prophet,” into a description.

The belt as a sign-act (Acts 21:11). He comes, takes up (aras, from airō) Paul’s zōnē, and binds heautou tous podas kai tas cheiras, “his own feet and hands,” before he says a word. The noun zōnē stands in seven verses of the New Testament (Matthew 3:4, 10:9, Mark 1:6, 6:8, this verse, Revelation 1:13 and 15:6), and this verse is the only one of the seven in Acts; it has the noun twice, “Paul’s belt” and “whose belt this is.” KJV “girdle,” ASV “girdle,” Geneva “girdle,” Douay “girdle” and NWT 1984 “girdle” keep the older English word for the cloth sash; NIV “belt” and TLB “belt” say what it is today. The order is feet first, and KJV “hands and feet,” Geneva “hands and feet” and NIV “hands and feet” turn it around, where ASV “feet and hands” keeps it. A prophet who enacts his message is a figure of the Hebrew Bible. In Isaiah 20:2–4 (not yet on these pages) Isaiah is told to take the sackcloth off his loins and the sandal off his foot and to walk naked and barefoot, and the oracle that follows is built on ka’asher … ken, “as … so”: as Isaiah has walked, so the king of Assyria will lead away the captives of Egypt. In Jeremiah 13:1–11 (not yet on these pages) the prophet buys a linen girdle (ezor), wears it, hides it at Perath, and finds it ruined, and the explanation (13:9) begins with koh amar YHWH, “thus says Jehovah,” and goes on to say that so Judah’s pride will be marred. In Ezekiel 4:1–8 (not yet on these pages) the sign (ot, 4:3) includes a drawn siege, lying on one side and, at 4:8, “bands” (avotim) laid on the prophet so that he cannot turn. In all three the object is the prophet’s own and the act is reported as commanded (Isaiah 20:2, Jeremiah 13:1, Ezekiel 4:1). Here the object is Paul’s, no command is reported in the verse, and the clause that sets it going, ton andra hou estin hē zōnē hautē, houtōs dēsousin, “the man whose belt this is — thus will they bind,” has the Isaiah shape: the act, then “so.”

“This is what the Holy Spirit says” (Acts 21:11). Tade legei to pneuma to hagion is literally “these things says the Holy Spirit.” At 11:28 Agabus’s words are reported in the third person (he “signified through the Spirit”); here they are quoted as an oracle. The Hebrew prophets’ formula is koh amar YHWH, “thus says Jehovah,” and as spelled in the archive that string stands in 291 verses of the Hebrew Bible (a search of the consonants; it matches that spelling only). The Greek Old Testament is not among the texts that can be fetched here, so that its translators render the formula with tade legei is reported, not checked on this page. In the New Testament tade legei stands in eight verses: this one and the seven letters of Revelation (2:1, 2:8, 2:12, 2:18, 3:1, 3:7 and 3:14), printed on these pages “These things says” and followed in each by a description of the speaker: “the one who holds the seven stars” (2:1), “the one who has the seven spirits of God” (3:1), “the Amen, the faithful and true witness” (3:14). Only in Acts 21:11 is the speaker the Spirit alone; the letters close each with a different clause, “what the Spirit says to the congregations,” to pneuma legei, in seven verses (2:7, 2:11, 2:17, 2:29, 3:6, 3:13, 3:22). The shelf: KJV “Thus saith the Holy Ghost,” Douay “Thus saith the Holy Ghost” and ASV “Thus saith the Holy Spirit” keep the prophetic “thus saith”; NIV “The Holy Spirit says” and TLB “The Holy Spirit declares” drop it; NWT 1984 “Thus says the holy spirit” keeps it and prints “spirit” in lower case, which is that version’s own doctrinal choice about the Spirit and is flagged here as such. The apparatus has heautou, “his own,” for Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and te autou for Robinson–Pierpont, who would have the prophet “binding his feet and hands” with a plain “his” that, by itself, does not say whose; the printed reading says it is Agabus’s own. On the English shelf, NIV “his own hands and feet” and ASV “his own feet and hands” both print “his own” (as do the other five versions), so the printed reading is not in dispute among them.

“Hand him over into the hands of the nations” (Acts 21:11). Paradōsousin eis cheiras ethnōn uses the verb that Luke’s Gospel puts in Jesus’s own predictions. Luke 9:44 has paradidosthai eis cheiras anthrōpōn, “delivered into the hands of men”; Luke 18:31 says anabainomen eis Ierousalēm, “we are going up to Jerusalem,” and 18:32 paradothēsetai tois ethnesin, “he will be handed over to the Gentiles” (the noun is ethnē, printed “nations” at Matthew 20:19 and “Gentiles” at Luke 18:32 on these pages); Mark 10:33 has the two steps, first to the chief priests and scribes and then “they will hand him over to the nations.” The exact phrase “into hands of nations” (cheiras ethn–) stands in one verse, this one. Whether Luke means the wording to recall those predictions is not said; the words are there to be compared. The narrative that follows is the text’s own check on the oracle, and it is set out here without a verdict. At 21:27 “the Jews from Asia” see Paul in the temple and lay hands on him; at 21:30 “they seized Paul” and drag him out of the temple; 21:31 has them “seeking to kill him.” Then the tribune takes hold of him and, at 21:33, orders him “to be bound” (dethēnai, the verb of Paul’s own words in v13) “with two chains.” In that sequence it is the Roman officer who binds, and no verse from 21:27 to 21:36 says the Jews hand Paul over; at Acts 28:17 (not yet on these pages) Paul himself says he was paredothēn, “handed over,” “from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans.” The shelf puts the “who” and the “to whom” in different words: NIV “the Jewish leaders” has a noun (“leaders”) the Greek hoi Ioudaioi, “the Jews,” does not have; TLB “turned over to the Romans” names the Romans where the Greek says “nations”; KJV “Gentiles,” NWT 1984 “people of the nations” and Douay “Gentiles” keep the plural noun. How closely the oracle and its outcome match, and in what sense “the Jews” are the subject of both verbs, is a question readers answer differently (see the last paragraph).

“Both we and the local people” (Acts 21:12). Parekaloumen hēmeis te kai hoi entopioi: the imperfect of parakaleō is “kept begging,” with tou mē anabainein, “that he not go up”; it is the verb this translation prints “encouraged” at 20:1, here with the sense of appeal. Entopioi, “those of the place,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; it is built from en, “in,” and topos, “place.” The speakers are “we,” Luke’s own party, and Paul is “him,” so the narrator is among those pleading. The Greek does not say “believers” or “disciples” of the locals; TLB “the local believers and his traveling companions” supplies both nouns. The verb divides the shelf: KJV “besought,” ASV “besought” and Geneva “besought” are one word; Douay “desired” is milder, NIV “pleaded with” and TLB “begged” stronger, and NWT 1984 “began entreating” keeps the imperfect as a start. For “those of the place,” ASV “they of that place,” NWT 1984 “those of that place” and NIV “the people there” stay with the Greek. The request is the same one the disciples at Tyre made at 21:4, “through the Spirit,” and the apparatus at 21:4 has Robinson–Pierpont reading anabainein, the verb of this verse, where the other three editions print epibainein, “set foot.” The city is also Ierousalēm in vv11–13 and Hierosolyma at v15.

“Crushing my heart,” “ready,” “for the name” (Acts 21:13). Ti poieite klaiontes kai synthryptontes mou tēn kardian is “what are you doing, weeping and crushing my heart?” Synthryptō stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so its sense rests on this verse and on its parts, syn- and thryptō; that thryptō means “to break in pieces, crumble” is reported here, not checked on this page, and the English has to choose whether the heart is being grieved or the resolve weakened. Four versions use the English idiom for the grief: ASV “breaking my heart,” NIV “breaking my heart,” Geneva “breaking mine heart” and TLB “breaking my heart”; KJV “to break mine heart” puts it as an infinitive; Douay “afflicting my heart” and NWT 1984 “making me weak at heart” go different ways, the first to distress, the second to loss of nerve. Hetoimōs echō, “I hold myself ready,” is, as adverb plus verb (hetoimōs echō), found in three verses of the New Testament: here, 2 Corinthians 12:14, where Paul says it is the third time he is “ready” to come to the Corinthians, and 1 Peter 4:5, “the one ready to judge” (both not yet on these pages). The adjective stands near it elsewhere: Luke 22:33, Peter to Jesus, “Lord, with you I am ready to go both to prison and to death” (hetoimos eimi), 1 Peter 3:15 (“always ready” for an answer) and 2 Corinthians 10:6 (en hetoimō echontes, “having in readiness”) (the last two not yet on these pages). Peter’s sentence and Paul’s have the same two stations, being bound or imprisoned and dying; Paul’s adds “not only … but even.” “For the name of the Lord Jesus” is hyper tou onomatos, which stands in five verses: 5:41 (“dishonored for the Name,” no name given), 9:16 (the Lord says how much Paul “must suffer for the sake of my name”), 15:26 (“for the sake of the name of our Lord Jesus Christ”), here and Romans 1:5. “Lord Jesus” names Jesus, and Paul named him at 20:24 as the one he received his ministry from; this translation therefore prints “Lord” and not “Jehovah.” KJV “for the name of the Lord Jesus,” NWT 1984 “for the name of the Lord Jesus” and Geneva “for the Name of the Lord Jesus” keep the noun; TLB “for the sake of the Lord Jesus” leaves it out, and TLB “jailed” for dethēnai, “to be bound,” narrows the verb to a prison.

“We quieted down” and “The Lord’s will be done” (Acts 21:14). Mē peithomenou de autou, “he not being persuaded,” ends the appeal, and hēsychasamen eipontes is “we quieted down, saying.” Hēsychazō stands in five verses of the New Testament: Luke 14:4 (they “kept silent”), 23:56 (the women “rested” on the Sabbath), Acts 11:18, here and 1 Thessalonians 4:11 (not yet on these pages). The nearest match is 11:18, where the Jerusalem believers, having heard Peter, “quieted down and glorified God, saying”; the verb, the group and the “saying” are the same here. KJV “ceased,” ASV “ceased,” Geneva “ceased” and Douay “ceased” say that the begging stopped; NIV “gave up” and TLB “gave up” add defeat; NWT 1984 “acquiesced” adds consent, and the Greek says only that they fell silent. The next clause is tou kyriou to thelēma ginesthō, with “of the Lord” first, as NIV “The Lord’s will be done” has it. The apparatus has the word order tou kyriou to thelēma for Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and to thelēma tou kyriou for Robinson–Pierpont (a difference of order only), and ginesthō, a present imperative, for the first three against genesthō for Robinson–Pierpont, the same split the footnotes record at Luke 22:42. There Jesus prays “not my will, but yours, be done” (to thelēma mou alla to son ginesthō), with the same verb form; Matthew 6:10 and 26:42 have genēthētō to thelēma sou, a different form of the verb. So Acts 21:14 shares its form, not its words, with Matthew’s two, and shares the verb form and the noun thelēma, though not the possessive, with Luke’s; and the exact words “the will of the Lord” are not in any of those verses. Who the kyrios is, the Greek does not say. The nearest Lord is the Lord Jesus of v13, but the group may mean God, and the translation keeps “Lord” and does not decide. NWT 1984 “Let the will of Jehovah take place” does decide: it prints “Jehovah” for kyrios here, though it keeps “the Lord Jesus” one verse earlier; KJV “The will of the Lord be done” and TLB “The will of the Lord be done” leave it open.

Three warnings and one decision (Acts 20:23, 21:4, 21:11). Three passages say the Spirit has told someone about Paul’s road. In 20:22–23 Paul says he is going “bound in the spirit” and that “the Holy Spirit testifies solemnly to me city by city, saying that bonds and afflictions await me”; the content is what is coming, and nothing is said of staying away. At 21:4 the Tyre disciples “kept telling Paul through the Spirit not to set foot in Jerusalem”; the content is a prohibition. At 21:11 the Spirit’s words, as quoted, are again a prediction: they say what the Jews will do, and the pleading in v12 is the hearers’ response, not part of the quoted oracle. Paul answers in v13 with readiness, as at 20:24 with “I hold my life of no account,” and at 21:15 “we went up.” Readers have taken this in at least three ways, and the text does not choose among them (these positions are reported here, not checked against particular commentaries on this page). One holds that Paul went in obedience, because the same Spirit that foretold the bonds had “bound” him to go, so that the warnings are information, and what the Tyre disciples said at 21:4 is their own conclusion from it. A second holds that the Spirit’s word at 21:4 is a real prohibition and that Paul went against it. A third holds that Luke leaves the question open on purpose: he reports the warnings, the weeping and the decision, and closes with the group’s own sentence, “The Lord’s will be done,” without adding a verdict. What the text itself shows is that the three warnings differ in content, that nobody in vv10–14 quotes the Spirit as saying “do not go,” and that the narrator and his party, who pleaded, accompany Paul.

Verses 15–19 · ἐπισκευασάμενοι … ἀνεβαίνομεν … Μνάσωνί τινι Κυπρίῳ, ἀρχαίῳ μαθητῇ … ξενισθῶμεν … ἀσμένως ἀπεδέξαντο … πρὸς Ἰάκωβον … πάντες οἱ πρεσβύτεροι … ἐξηγεῖτο … διὰ τῆς διακονίας αὐτοῦ episkeuasamenoi … anebainomen … Mnasoni tini Kypriō, archaiō mathētē … xenisthōmen … asmenōs apedexanto … pros Iakōbon … pantes hoi presbyteroi … exēgeito … dia tēs diakonias autou — Acts 21:15–19: who was Mnason of Cyprus, what does “early disciple” mean, who was the James Paul met with the elders, and what did Paul report to Jerusalem?

“Made ready” (Acts 21:15). Meta de tas hēmeras tautas, “and after these days,” closes the stay at Caesarea: the days at Philip’s house (21:8) and the “more days” of the prophet Agabus’s arrival (21:10). The verb episkeuasamenoi stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so no other verse can be set beside it. It is a middle participle, “having made (ourselves or our things) ready,” and it has no object: the Greek does not say whether the thing made ready was baggage, animals, money, or the travellers themselves. That it is built on skeuos, “vessel, gear,” is reported here, not checked on this page. The English shelf fills the gap in three ways. ASV “took up our baggage,” KJV “took up our carriages” (“carriages” reads as things carried, the same sense as the ASV’s word), Geneva “trussed up our fardels” and TLB “packed our things” supply an object; NWT 1984 “prepared for the journey” and Douay “being prepared” supply none; NIV “started on our way” has no verb of preparing at all. This translation prints “made ready” and leaves the object open, as the Greek does. The second verb is anebainomen, an imperfect, “we were going up,” and it is the verb of the plea at 21:12, where the local believers begged Paul tou mē anabainein, “not to go up” to Jerusalem; between that plea and this verse stand Paul’s answer (21:13) and the company’s “The Lord’s will be done” (21:14), and the verse reports no further argument. The apparatus gives Hierosolyma to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and Hierousalem to Robinson–Pierpont, two spellings of the same city; the chapter itself prints Hierousalem at 21:11, 12 and 13 and Hierosolyma here and at 21:17, so the editions differ only over which of Luke’s two forms stands in this verse.

Mnason of Cyprus, an “early” disciple (Acts 21:16). Sunēlthon de kai tōn mathētōn apo Kaisareias sun hēmin: “some of the disciples from Caesarea also came with us.” Then agontes par’ hō xenisthōmen Mnasoni tini Kypriō, which the English must reorder. Agontes, “bringing,” has no stated object; the relative clause “with whom we were to lodge” comes next, and the name follows it in the dative (Mnasoni, where an accusative, like Simōna in 10:32, would make him the one who was brought). This translation reads it as the disciples bringing “us” to him, since the name is dative. The shelf splits on it: ASV and Douay “bringing with them one Mnason” and KJV and Geneva “brought with them one Mnason” make Mnason a companion on the road from Caesarea, while NIV “brought us to the home of Mnason” and NWT 1984 “to bring us to the man” make the disciples guides to his house. The name Mnasōn stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and Luke gives nothing else about him. He is a Cypriot (Kyprios), and the word Kyprios stands in three verses of Acts: Barnabas, “a Cypriot by birth” (4:36), the “Cypriot and Cyrenian men” who spoke to the Hellenists at Antioch (11:20), and here. Luke does not say how Mnason came to be in Jerusalem or whether he knew Barnabas, and the verse does not connect the three.

“Early” (Acts 21:16). The word that matters in the verse is archaiō. Archaios stands in eleven verses of the New Testament. Five are on these pages: Matthew 5:21, “it was said to the ancients” (tois archaiois), and 5:33; Luke 9:8 and 9:19, “one of the prophets of old”; and Revelation 12:9, “the ancient serpent.” Two are in Acts and are the nearest comparison: 15:7, where Peter says aph’ hēmerōn archaiōn, “in the early days God made a choice among you, that by my mouth the nations should hear” (the story of that hearing is told in Acts 10), and 15:21, “from ancient generations” of Moses. The other three are 2 Corinthians 5:17, 2 Peter 2:5 and Revelation 20:2 (not yet on these pages). So the word reaches from the “early days” of Peter’s own life, to the prophets, to Moses; it speaks of when, not of how old a person is. For Mnason the verse says only that his discipleship began early. Whether that means he was among those gathered at the beginning in Jerusalem, or won in the spread of the message that Luke tells at 11:19–20, the text does not say. The English divides on the word: ASV “an early disciple,” NWT 1984 “an early disciple,” NIV “one of the early disciples” and TLB “one of the early believers” keep the time; KJV, Geneva and Douay “an old disciple” will read, to a modern ear, as a disciple who is aged, which this word does not say in any verse above.

Where they were to lodge (Acts 21:16). Xenisthōmen is the passive of xenizō, “to be entertained as a guest.” Xenizō stands in seven verses of Acts. Four are Peter’s lodging at Joppa with Simon the tanner: 10:6, 10:18 and 10:32 (“is a guest”) and 10:23, where Peter “hosted” the men sent to him. One is 17:20, where the same verb means something strange (“you are bringing some strange things to our ears”); the others are here and 28:7 (not yet on these pages). Luke twice names the host as “a certain” man after this verb: para tini Simōni at 10:6, with “a certain” before the name, and par’ hō… Mnasoni tini here, with it after. The dictionary entry xenos has the word’s root. The shelf words for the lodging are KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “with whom we should lodge”; NWT 1984 “at whose home we were to be entertained”; TLB “we were guests at the home of Mnason”; NIV “where we were to stay.” The Greek names no house; it names the host.

Received gladly (Acts 21:17). Asmenōs, “gladly,” is an adverb that stands in one verse of the New Testament (asmenōs), this one. Apedexanto, “received,” is from apodechomai, which stands in six verses: Luke 8:40 and 9:11 (the crowds and Jesus), Acts 2:41 (“received his word”), 18:27 (the brothers write to the disciples “to welcome” Apollos), here, and 24:3 (not yet on these pages). The apparatus gives apedexanto to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and edexanto, the plain verb without the prefix, to Robinson–Pierpont; the meaning is the same. The welcome at the earlier visit used another word: at 15:4 Paul and Barnabas “were welcomed by the congregation and the apostles and the elders” (paredechthēsan), whereas here the receivers are only “the brothers” (hoi adelphoi). KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 read “received us gladly”; NIV “received us warmly” and TLB “welcomed us cordially” give the tone and drop the plain “received.” NIV “the brothers and sisters” prints the masculine plural adelphoi as including women, which the plural form can do; the verse does not say who among the believers at Jerusalem were present.

James, and all the elders (Acts 21:18). Tē epiousē, “on the next day,” stands in five verses of Acts (epiousē): 7:26, 16:11, 20:15, here, and 23:11 (not yet on these pages). Pros Iakōbon is “to James” with no title and no father’s name. The James who is meant is the one Peter names at 12:17 (“Report these things to James and the brothers”) and who answers at 15:13 (“James responded”); Luke had already told the death of the other James, “the brother of John,” at 12:2. Acts itself does not call this James anyone’s brother; the label “the Lord’s brother” comes from Paul’s words in Galatians 1:19, and the encyclopedia entry follows that identification. How “brother” is to be understood is a question on which the traditions answer differently, and it is not decided here. Then pantes te paregenonto hoi presbyteroi, “and all the elders were present.” The form paregenonto stands in four verses of the New Testament, and one is 20:18, where the Ephesian elders “had arrived” at Paul; the word that brought them to him at Miletus is the one that has the Jerusalem elders “present” here. The text names two parties here, James and the elders. It does not name any apostle at this meeting, and it does not say there was none, or where any apostle was. At the earlier meeting the text did name them: 15:4, 15:6 and 15:22 each have “the apostles and the elders,” as do 15:2, 15:23 and 16:4, while 11:30, where the relief from Antioch arrived, has “the elders” alone. The shelf: KJV, ASV and NIV “all the elders were present”; Geneva “all the Elders were there assembled”; Douay “all the ancients were assembled”; NWT 1984 “all the older men were present,” the literal sense of presbyteros (an “older man”) put where the others have a title; TLB “the elders of the Jerusalem church” adds a “church” that this clause does not have (ekklēsia, which this translation prints “congregation,” is not in the verse).

“Related” and “ministry” (Acts 21:19). Aspasamenos, “having greeted,” is the greeting that Luke also opens Paul’s departure with at 20:1 and the brothers at Ptolemais with at 21:7; Geneva “embraced them” reads it as the embrace (see the note at 20:1), KJV and ASV “saluted them,” Douay “saluted,” NIV and NWT 1984 “greeted them” and TLB “greetings were exchanged.” Next, exēgeito, an imperfect, “he was relating”: exēgeomai stands in six verses of the New Testament, and four are in Acts: 10:8 (Cornelius relating “everything” to his men), 15:12, 15:14 and here; the others are Luke 24:35, where the two from Emmaus “related the things that had happened on the road,” and John 1:18, “he has made him known.” The nearest echo is 15:12, where the whole assembly listened to Barnabas and Paul “relating all the signs and wonders God had done among the nations through them.” The clause is almost the same: hosa epoiēsen ho theos… en tois ethnesin there, hōn epoiēsen ho theos en tois ethnesin here, “the things God had done among the nations.” Two things differ. The 15:12 account names “signs and wonders” and says “through them”; the 21:19 clause has neither, and says “through his ministry.” The imperfect and kath’ hen hekaston, “one by one,” make it a report in parts, a long one. ASV “rehearsed one by one,” KJV “declared particularly,” Douay “related particularly,” Geneva “told by order,” NIV “reported in detail,” NWT 1984 “began giving in detail an account” (it reads the imperfect as a beginning; the other six do not) and TLB “recounted the many things” each choose their own word for the detail.

Then diakonia, which stands in eight verses of Acts, and this is the last of them. This translation prints “ministry” for the share that Judas had and lost (1:17, 1:25), for the one Paul says he received from the Lord Jesus (20:24) and here; it prints “service” at the “daily service” and the “service of the word” (6:1, 6:4), at the disciples’ sending to the brothers in Judea (11:29), and at 12:25, where Barnabas and Saul “fulfilled the service at Jerusalem.” See the dictionary. So the one noun that names Paul’s commission to the nations is also the noun for the relief the Antioch disciples sent up to the Jerusalem elders (11:30). KJV and Douay “by his ministry,” ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 “through his ministry,” Geneva “by his ministration” and TLB “through his work.” Luke does not tie the two uses together in this verse. What the verse reports is a telling and not a hand-over. Paul’s letters name a gift for Jerusalem: Romans 15:25–26 (the saints at Jerusalem, and a koinōnia made by Macedonia and Achaia for their poor), 15:31 (“my ministry to Jerusalem,” hē diakonia mou hē eis Ierousalēm) and 1 Corinthians 16:1–4, the logeia for the saints, which Paul says he will send by men the Corinthians approve (all three not yet on these pages). None of the forty verses of Acts 21 mentions a gift brought to Jerusalem or handed over (v24 speaks of paying the expenses of four men, a different matter); the nearest text is Acts 24:17 (not yet on these pages), where Paul says eleēmosynas poiēsōn eis to ethnos mou paregenomēn kai prosphoras, “I came to bring alms to my nation, and offerings.” Whether the “ministry” reported to James includes that gift, and whether the elders of 21:18 are the ones who received it, is an inference some readers draw from the letters (as the note on Acts 20:1–6 says) and is not in the verses.

Verses 20–22 · ἐδόξαζον τὸν θεόν … πόσαι μυριάδες … ζηλωταὶ τοῦ νόμου … κατηχήθησαν … ἀποστασίαν διδάσκεις ἀπὸ Μωϋσέως … μὴ περιτέμνειν … τοῖς ἔθεσιν περιπατεῖν … πάντως ἀκούσονται edoxazon ton theon … posai myriades … zēlōtai tou nomou … katēchēthēsan … apostasian didaskeis apo Mōuseōs … mē peritemnein … tois ethesin peripatein … pantōs akousontai — Acts 21:20–22: who were the “zealots for the law,” how many are “tens of thousands,” and what is the “apostasy from Moses” Paul was said to teach?

They glorified God, and said (Acts 21:20). Hoi de akousantes edoxazon ton theon, “and they, when they heard, glorified God,” answers Paul’s account of what God had done among the nations (21:19); the speakers are James and the elders of 21:18, though the verse itself names no one. The apparatus gives theon, “God,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and kyrion, “Lord,” to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “glorified the Lord” follows the second reading; ASV “glorified God,” Geneva “glorified God,” Douay “glorified God,” NIV “praised God,” NWT 1984 “glorify God” and TLB “praised God” read the first, so the KJV is the only one of the seven that prints “Lord.” Which of the two stood in the oldest copies is a question about the manuscripts, and where kyrios is the reading its referent would be open between Jesus and the God of Israel. Two smaller variants sit in the same verse. Eipon te autō, “and said to him,” is the reading of the three critical editions and eipontes, “saying,” of Robinson–Pierpont; and en tois Ioudaiois, “among the Jews,” is theirs where Robinson–Pierpont has the genitive Ioudaiōn, “of Jews.” The English phrase “thousands of Jews” has the shape of the genitive and “among the Jews” has the shape of en with the dative, but an English wording does not prove which Greek lay behind it: KJV “thousands of Jews there are which believe,” NIV “thousands of Jews have believed” and TLB “thousands of Jews have also believed” read one way; ASV “among the Jews,” Douay “among the Jews” and NWT 1984 “among the Jews” the other; Geneva “thousand Jews there are which believe” sits with the first group. The Greek does not say where these believers live or whether the count is of Jerusalem or of the whole Jewish world.

“Tens of thousands” (Acts 21:20). Posai myriades, “how many tens of thousands,” is a plural question: myrias is a unit of ten thousand, and “how many” asks for a number of such units. The noun myrias stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Luke 12:1, Acts 19:19, here, Hebrews 12:22 (not yet on these pages), Jude 14, Revelation 5:11 and 9:16. In Luke the crowd has gathered “by the tens of thousands, so that they were trampling one another”; in Acts 19 the burned books were reckoned at argyriou myriadas pente, “fifty thousand pieces of silver” (five units of ten thousand); in Revelation 5:11 the number of the angels is myriades myriadōn kai chiliades chiliadōn, “myriads of myriads and thousands of thousands”; and in Jude 14 the Lord comes “with his holy myriads.” So the word is exact in one of those verses (a sum of money) and an immense, uncounted number in the others. Whether the elders mean a figure or a great many is not said. Each of the seven turns the unit into “thousands,” which is a tenth of the Greek unit: KJV “thousands of Jews,” ASV “thousands,” Douay “thousands,” NIV “thousands of Jews,” NWT 1984 “thousands of believers,” TLB “thousands of Jews” and Geneva “thousand Jews” (singular). Greek has its own word for a thousand, chilioi, and Revelation 5:11 sets the two units side by side, which is why this translation keeps “tens of thousands” here as it does at Luke 12:1. How many Jewish believers there were is a historical question this verse answers only with the elders’ own words, and the verse is not corroborated here from any other source.

“Zealots for the law” (Acts 21:20). Zēlōtai tou nomou is a noun, zēlōtēs, with the genitive of what the person is zealous for. The noun stands in eight verses of the New Testament: Luke 6:15, Acts 1:13, here, Galatians 1:14, Acts 22:3, and 1 Corinthians 14:12, Titus 2:14 and 1 Peter 3:13 (none of the three yet on these pages), all forms of zēlōtēs. In the first two it is a title: Simon “who was called the Zealot” (Luke 6:15) and “Simon the Zealot” (Acts 1:13), both read here as Ζηλωτήν and ζηλωτής in the Greek. In the others it takes an object: zēlōtai pneumatōn, “zealous for spirits” (1 Corinthians 14:12), zēlōtēn kalōn ergōn, “a zealot of good works” (Titus 2:14, Greek only here), and, of Paul himself, zēlōtēs tōn patrikōn mou paradoseōn, shipped in this translation as “far more zealous for the traditions of my fathers” (Galatians 1:14). So the word the elders use of the believing Jews is the one Paul uses of his own earlier life; at Acts 22:3 the Greek has him say zēlōtēs hyparchōn tou theou kathōs pantes hymeis este sēmeron, “being a zealot of God, as all of you are today.” Whether “zealot” here already names a party, as the English word came to, is not decided by the Greek, which is a plain noun of zeal; in Luke 6:15 and Acts 1:13 it is Simon’s title. Six versions print the adjective: ASV “zealous for the law,” Douay “zealous for the law,” NIV “zealous for the law,” NWT 1984 “zealous for the Law,” KJV “zealous of the law” and Geneva “zealous of the Law.” TLB “very insistent that Jewish believers must continue to follow the Jewish traditions and customs” is a paraphrase: it gives the zealots a demand (“must continue”) and says what the law consists of, neither of which is in the elders’ sentence. The “all” (pantes) is also theirs: they say it of every one of the tens of thousands.

“They have been instructed” (Acts 21:21). Katēchēthēsan de peri sou is a passive of katēcheō, which this translation prints as “instructed” at Luke 1:4 (“the things about which you were instructed”) and Acts 18:25 (Apollos, “instructed in the way of the Lord”); it is the verb behind English “catechize” and “catechism.” The verb katēcheō stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Luke 1:4, Acts 18:25, this verse, 21:24, Romans 2:18 (“being instructed out of the law”), 1 Corinthians 14:19 and Galatians 6:6 (the last two not yet on these pages). A search for the letters also turns up Acts 27:3, but that is katēchthēmen, a form of a different verb, katagō, “to bring down” (a ship to a port). Where Paul uses it the sense is teaching by word of mouth: hina kai allous katēchēsō, “so that I might instruct others also,” and koinōneitō de ho katēchoumenos ton logon tō katēchounti en pasin agathois, in which one man is instructed and another instructs (both Greek only here). Here the instruction is hearsay, since the passive names no teacher, and the same verb comes back in 21:24, where the elders say that what “they have been instructed” about Paul is nothing. Seven versions, seven ways to avoid “instructed”: KJV “they are informed of thee,” ASV “they have been informed concerning thee,” Geneva “they are informed of thee,” NIV “informed,” NWT 1984 “heard it rumored about you,” Douay “they have heard of thee” and TLB “have been told.” Only the NWT says that it is a rumor, which the Greek does not say (peri sou, “about you”), though the passive without an agent leaves room for it.

“Apostasy from Moses” (Acts 21:21). Apostasian didaskeis apo Mōuseōs, “you teach apostasy from Moses,” has a noun built from aphistēmi, “to stand away from.” The noun apostasia stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and ean mē elthē hē apostasia prōton in 2 Thessalonians 2:3 (not yet on these pages). A search for the first letters also catches apostasion, a different noun, the “certificate of divorce” of Matthew 5:31, 19:7 and Mark 10:4. In Acts the noun has no article and is followed by apo Mōuseōs, “from Moses,” so what is left is a person or a manner of life, not an event; the elders say “Moses,” not “the law,” and the charge is that it is taught to all the Jews, tous kata ta ethnē pantas Ioudaious, “all the Jews among the nations,” so that the people addressed are Jews, not the nations. NWT 1984 “an apostasy from Moses” is the one version on the shelf to keep the noun, and in English the word has a doctrinal weight in later usage that the Greek here does not itself carry; KJV, ASV and Geneva “forsake Moses” use a verb for it, Douay “depart from Moses” and NIV “turn away from Moses” use a motion, and TLB “against the laws of Moses” turns a leaving into an opposition and Moses into laws. The reports in this verse are not tied to a sentence of Paul’s own in Acts so far; whether the charge is true is not decided on these pages.

Circumcision and the customs (Acts 21:21). Legōn mē peritemnein autous ta tekna mēde tois ethesin peripatein, “saying that they should not circumcise their children nor walk by the customs,” is the content of the teaching, in two parts. Acts has already told several things on this ground. At 16:3 Paul “circumcised him because of the Jews who were in those places” of Timothy, whose mother was a believing Jewish woman and whose father was a Greek (patros de Hellēnos, 16:1, Greek). At 15:1 men from Judea taught, “Unless you are circumcised according to the custom of Moses, you cannot be saved” (tō ethei tō Mōuseōs, a singular custom), and at 15:5 the Pharisees who had believed said, “It is necessary to circumcise them, and to charge them to keep the law of Moses.” The decree that follows names four things to abstain from, in 15:20 and 15:29: what is polluted by idols (or, in the second list, idol sacrifices), sexual immorality, what is strangled and blood. Circumcision is not on either list. The same noun, ethē, “customs,” is the charge against Stephen at 6:14, “change the customs that Moses handed down to us,” and against Paul and Silas at Philippi at 16:21, “customs that it is not lawful for us to accept or to practice, being Romans.” It stands again at 26:3 and 28:17 (neither yet on these pages), the second of which has Paul say he did nothing against the people or the ethesi tois patrōiois, “customs of the fathers.” Paul’s letters are in the same dispute. In Galatians 2:3 (not yet on these pages) the Greek says of Titus, a Greek, oude Titos … ēnankasthē peritmēthēnai, that not even he was compelled to be circumcised; Galatians 5:2 and 5:6 (also not yet on these pages) read ean peritemnēsthe Christos hymas ouden ōphelēsei, “if you are circumcised, Christ will help you nothing,” and en gar Christō Iēsou oute peritomē ti ischyei oute akrobystia, “in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision counts for anything.” Those are Paul’s own sentences, written to others; the elders’ report is of what he is said to teach Jews, and the pages do not weigh the two against each other. The shelf renders the second half of the charge in a way that moves between “walk” and “live”: KJV “to walk after the customs,” ASV “to walk after the customs,” Douay “walk according to the custom,” NWT 1984 “walk in the [solemn] customs,” NIV “live according to our customs,” Geneva “to live after the customs”; the NIV adds “our,” which is not in the Greek, and TLB “against our Jewish customs” adds it too and drops the verb. The verb is peripatein, the plain verb for walking about, which Luke uses of the lame man at 3:8 and 3:9; with the dative of the customs it speaks of a manner of life.

“What then?” (Acts 21:22). Ti oun estin; is “What then is it?” and pantōs akousontai hoti elēlythas is “they will surely hear that you have come.” Pantōs, “surely, by all means,” carries the weight: NWT 1984 “In any case they are going to hear,” ASV “They will certainly hear,” NIV “They will certainly hear” and TLB “they will certainly hear” keep a word for it, while KJV, Geneva and Douay have none. The apparatus has a longer reading in Robinson–Pierpont: where Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have akousontai, “they will hear,” Robinson–Pierpont has dei plēthos synelthein, akousontai gar, “a multitude must come together, for they will hear.” Three versions print the addition: KJV “the multitude must needs come together,” Geneva “the multitude must needs come together” and Douay “the multitude must needs come together”; the other four do not: ASV “What is it therefore?” with nothing after it, NWT 1984 “What, then, is to be done about it?”, NIV “What shall we do?” and TLB “Now what can be done?” What the elders propose after it begins at 21:23.

Verses 23–26 · εὐχὴν ἔχοντες ἀφʼ ἑαυτῶν … ἁγνίσθητι … δαπάνησον … ξυρήσονται τὴν κεφαλήν … στοιχεῖς φυλάσσων τὸν νόμον … ἡμεῖς ἀπεστείλαμεν κρίναντες … ἁγνισθεὶς … διαγγέλλων τὴν ἐκπλήρωσιν τῶν ἡμερῶν τοῦ ἁγνισμοῦ … ἡ προσφορά euchēn echontes aph’ heautōn … hagnisthēti … dapanēson … xyrēsontai tēn kephalēn … stoicheis phylassōn ton nomon … hēmeis apesteilamen krinantes … hagnistheis … diangellōn tēn ekplērōsin tōn hēmerōn tou hagnismou … hē prosphora — Acts 21:23–26: was Paul’s vow in the temple a Nazirite vow, what does “be purified” mean, who paid, what does “walk in line, keeping the law” say about Paul, and why do James and the elders repeat the decree of Acts 15?

Four men “having a vow” (Acts 21:23). The speakers are James and the elders of 21:18, and their plan is one sentence long: eisin hēmin andres tessares euchēn echontes, “we have four men who have a vow.” The noun euchē stands in three verses of the New Testament: Acts 18:18, where Paul had his head shorn at Cenchreae “for he had a vow” (eichen gar euchēn), here, and James 5:15 (not yet on these pages). Luke does not write “Nazirite” here or there: a search for the stem nazir- finds no verse of the Greek New Testament. The word existed in Greek, since Josephus writes naziraioi and naziraiōn (see below), but Luke says only “vow.” What the text does carry is the shape of the Torah’s vow with a head attached. The Nazirite vow of Numbers 6:2 keeps the razor off the head “until the days are fulfilled” (6:5); on the day the days are fulfilled the man is brought “to the entrance of the tent of meeting” (6:13) with “one male lamb a year old without blemish, for a burnt offering, and one ewe lamb a year old without blemish, for a sin-offering, and one ram without blemish, for a peace offering,” a basket of unleavened bread and the grain-offering and drink offerings (6:14–15); he “shall shave the head of their separation at the entrance of the tent of meeting” and put the hair on the fire under the peace offering (6:18); and after the priest has waved the portions “the Nazirite may drink wine” (6:20). Acts 21 has a vow, a shaving of heads (v24), “the days” coming to a completion and an offering for each man (v26). Whether the four men, or Paul at Cenchreae, had taken the Nazirite vow of Numbers 6 is therefore an inference from that shape, which the Greek does not state, and Luke does not say what was vowed or for how long. The Hebrew letters nazir stand in thirteen verses of the Hebrew Bible: six in Numbers 6 (6:2, 13, 18, 19, 20, 21), three about Samson (Judges 13:5, 13:7, 16:17) and four elsewhere (Genesis 49:26, Leviticus 25:5, Deuteronomy 33:16, Lamentations 4:7), so the Hebrew has the word and the New Testament Greek does not print it. The shelf follows the Greek’s plainness in most places: KJV “a vow on them,” ASV “a vow on them,” Douay “a vow on them,” Geneva “made a vow” and NIV “made a vow.” TLB “preparing to shave their heads and take some vows” makes the vows still to come, where the Greek participle echontes is present (“having”); and the same version adds at v26 “publicizing his vow,” which gives Paul a vow of his own, where the Greek of v26 says only that he was purified “with them.” Paul’s own Cenchreae vow, the shorn head of 18:18, is not described either, so the two scenes can be compared but not equated.

“Of their own,” or “upon themselves” (Acts 21:23). The apparatus gives aph’ heautōn to Westcott–Hort, the reading printed here, and eph’ heautōn to Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. The first is apo with the genitive, “from themselves”: a vow that comes from the men, of their own accord, which this translation prints as “of their own.” The second is epi with the genitive, “upon themselves”: a vow that rests on them, as an obligation they carry. The two readings differ by one letter, and they can overlap: a vow that comes from a man can also rest on him. English wording does not prove which Greek lay behind it, but the shelf sorts by the preposition’s picture: NWT 1984 “a vow upon themselves,” KJV “a vow on them,” ASV “a vow on them” and Douay “a vow on them” use the “on” shape, while Geneva “made a vow,” NIV “made a vow” and TLB “take some vows” print neither preposition. Four of the seven, then, have the “on” shape; none has “of their own.” Either way the vow is the men’s, and the elders’ request to Paul is that he be purified and pay with them, not that he take the vow.

“Be purified” and “pay the expenses” (Acts 21:24). Hagnisthēti, “be purified,” is an aorist passive imperative of hagnizō. The verb stands in seven verses of the New Testament, four of them in a temple or festival setting and three figurative. Four: John 11:55, where many went up to Jerusalem before the Passover “to purify themselves,” here and v26, and Acts 24:18, Paul’s own words, “they found me purified in the temple” (not yet on these pages). Three: James 4:8, “purify hearts” (not yet on these pages), 1 Peter 1:22, “Having purified your souls by obedience to the truth,” and 1 John 3:3, “purifies himself” (not yet on these pages). The noun hagnismos, “purification,” stands in one verse, v26. Luke does not say what the purification consisted of, how long it took, or why Paul in particular needed it, and the imperative asks him to be purified “with them,” syn autois, which does not say that Paul takes the four men’s vow. The shelf: KJV, ASV and Geneva “purify thyself,” Douay “sanctify thyself,” NWT 1984 “cleanse yourself ceremonially” (the adverb is the version’s own gloss), NIV “join in their purification rites” (“rites” is added), and TLB “have your head shaved too,” which drops the verb and gives Paul the shaving, while the Greek has hina xyrēsontai tēn kephalēn, “so that they may shave their heads,” with the four men as subject. Dapanēson ep’ autois, “pay the expenses for them,” is a verb of spending: the stem dapan- stands in eight verses, and in the others it is what the woman of Mark 5:26 spent on physicians, what the younger son spent in Luke 15:14, and the Samaritan’s promise to the innkeeper in Luke 10:35, “whatever more you spend, I will repay you”; it stands also at 2 Corinthians 12:15 and James 4:3 (neither yet on these pages) and, as the noun, Luke 14:28, “calculate the cost,” and Paul uses its negative, adapanon, of preaching “free of charge” at 1 Corinthians 9:18 (not yet on these pages). The Greek does not say what the money bought. If the vow was the one of Numbers 6, the offerings of 6:14–15 are a lamb, a ewe lamb, a ram and a basket of bread for each man, which is a real expense, but that is the inference again. The shelf: KJV “be at charges with them,” ASV “be at charges for them,” NWT 1984 “take care of their expenses,” NIV “pay their expenses,” Geneva “contribute with them” and Douay “bestow on them.” Josephus has a sentence about a king and Nazirite shaving, and it does not mention payment. In Antiquities 19.294 (Whiston’s chapter 6, section 1, which covers Niese’s sections 292–296) Agrippa has come to Jerusalem and completed his thanksgiving sacrifices (exeplērōse, from the verb that the noun ekplērōsis of v26 belongs to), omitting nothing “according to the law” (293), and then: dio kai naziraiōn xyrasthai dietaxe mala sychnous, “for which reason he also ordered very many of the Nazirites to be shaved.” Whiston has “he ordained that many of the Nazarites should have their heads shorn,” and the king is Agrippa I in Whiston’s note (compare Herod Agrippa I, the Herod of Acts 12). The verb is xyraomai, the same verb as xyrēsontai in Acts 21:24, which the New Testament has in three verses (Acts 21:24 and 1 Corinthians 11:5 and 11:6, the last two not yet on these pages; Acts 18:18 uses a different verb, keirō). So Josephus shows a king arranging for many Nazirites to be shaved; the sentence does not say that he paid, or who paid. Later rabbinic law on the vow is gathered in the Mishnah’s tractate Nazir; what it says about paying for another man’s offerings is reported here, not checked on this page.

“So that they may shave,” “all will know” (Acts 21:24). Two small variants sit inside the purpose clause. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have xyrēsontai, the future, “they will shave,” and gnōsontai, “they will know”; Robinson–Pierpont has the subjunctives xyrēsōntai and gnōsin, “may shave” and “may know.” The difference is one of mood, and both readings give a purpose, and a result that everyone is to see. In English the shelf mostly shows the future: ASV “all shall know,” Geneva “all shall know,” Douay “all will know,” NWT 1984 “everybody will know” and NIV “everyone will know,” while KJV “all may know” has the shape of the subjunctive. The content of the knowing is hōn katēchēntai peri sou ouden estin, “there is nothing in what they have been told about you,” the same verb katēcheō as in 21:21 (dictionary): the rumour of v21 is answered by an act that everyone can see, not by a statement of Paul’s.

“Walk in line, keeping the law” (Acts 21:24). Stoicheis kai autos phylassōn ton nomon, “you yourself also walk in line, keeping the law.” The verb stoicheō stands in five verses, all in Paul or Luke, and the other four are Paul’s: here; Romans 4:12, those who walk in line with the footsteps of the faith of Abraham; Galatians 5:25, if we live by the Spirit, let us also walk in line by the Spirit; Galatians 6:16, as many as will walk in line by this rule; and Philippians 3:16, to walk in line by the same (none yet on these pages). In each of Paul’s the verb has something to walk in line with, a footprint, the Spirit, a rule; here it is the law. It is not peripateō, the plain “walk” of v21 (“nor walk by the customs”), so the elders answer a charge phrased with one walking-verb in a second. In the apparatus the word order of the closing phrase differs (phylassōn ton nomon in the critical editions, ton nomon phylassōn in Robinson–Pierpont), and nothing else. Phylassō, “to keep,” with “law” in the same verse stands in four verses: Acts 7:53 (“you who received the law as ordinances of angels, and did not keep it”), here, Romans 2:26 (“If then the uncircumcised keeps the requirements of the law”) and Galatians 6:13 (“not even those who are circumcised themselves keep the law,” not yet on these pages). The same verb is Luke’s for the decree: the decrees “to keep” at 16:4 and the nations “that they keep themselves” from the four things in v25. At 15:5 the believing Pharisees use a different verb, tērein, “to keep the law of Moses.” The shelf: KJV “walkest orderly, and keepest the law,” ASV “walkest orderly, keeping the law” and NWT 1984 “walking orderly” keep a word for the walk; Geneva “walkest and keepest the Law” and Douay “walkest keeping the law” keep it without the adverb; NIV “living in obedience to the law” and TLB “you yourself obey the Jewish laws” fold the walk into the keeping, and the TLB adds “Jewish.” The participle can be read as the way the walking is done (“walk in line, by keeping”) or as a second statement beside it, and the Greek does not choose.

“We ourselves have sent word” (Acts 21:25). The sentence repeats the decree of Acts 15. Hēmeis, “we ourselves,” is a pronoun Greek need not print, so it is emphatic; the speakers are James and the elders, who are putting their own act beside the plan of v23–24. Paul was not absent from that act. At 15:22 the apostles and elders chose men to send “with Paul and Barnabas,” and at 16:4 “as they were going through the cities, they were handing on to them, to keep, the decrees that had been decided by the apostles and elders who were in Jerusalem”; the “they” are Paul’s party of 16:1–3. So the elders tell him about a decision he helped to carry, and the Greek does not say whether it is a reminder, an assurance or a contrast with the plan just proposed. The list matches 15:29 in order: food sacrificed to idols (eidōlothyton), blood, what is strangled (pnikton), sexual immorality (porneia), and pnikt- stands in three verses, 15:20, 15:29 and here. 15:20 has the same four in another order and another first word: “the things polluted by idols” (alisgēmatōn tōn eidōlōn), sexual immorality, what is strangled, blood. The apparatus has three marks in v25. Westcott–Hort and Tregelles read apesteilamen, “we sent word,” and Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont epesteilamen, “we wrote”; epistellō stands in two verses in the Greek printed here, 15:20 (James’s own “that we write”) and Hebrews 13:22 (not yet on these pages), and would stand in a third with the other reading. Robinson–Pierpont also adds mēden toiouton tērein autous ei mē, “that they observe nothing of the kind, except,” before the list, and, at the mark before “and blood,” a to (“the blood”). Seven versions, three shapes for that addition: KJV “observe no such thing, save only that they keep themselves” and Geneva “observe no such thing, but that they keep themselves” print it; TLB “at all—except for the ones we wrote to them about” has the same sense in a paraphrase, though the critical editions have no such clause; Douay “should only refrain themselves” has an “only” and no “such thing”; ASV “giving judgment that they should keep themselves,” NIV “our decision that they should abstain” and NWT 1984 “rendering our decision that they should keep themselves” go straight to the list. On the verb itself, six of the seven versions have a verb of writing: KJV “we have written and concluded,” ASV “we wrote, giving judgment,” Geneva “we have written, and determined,” Douay “we have written, decreeing,” NIV “we have written to them our decision” and TLB “the ones we wrote to them about”; only NWT 1984 “we have sent out, rendering our decision” keeps “sent” and also prints “nations” (“believers from among the nations”) where the six others have “Gentiles.” Krinantes, “having decided,” is the verb of James’s egō krinō, “my judgment is,” at 15:19.

“Completion” and “the offering” (Acts 21:26). The verse carries out v24 clause by clause. Paralabōn tous andras, “having taken the men,” is the instruction’s own verb, paralabōn, “take these men”; hagnistheis, “having been purified,” is the second hagniz- form of the verse, beside the noun hagnismos. The verb diangellō, “announcing,” stands in three verses: Luke 9:60 (“go and proclaim the kingdom of God”), here, and Romans 9:17 (“that my name might be proclaimed in all the earth,” not yet on these pages), so it is a verb of public declaration that here has a smaller occasion. Luke does not say to whom Paul announced, but the temple is the setting and the priests are the natural audience. Ekplērōsis, “completion,” is not found in any other verse of the Greek New Testament, so it is a hapax in this text; whether it is common elsewhere in Greek is not checked here, but Josephus uses its verb, ekplēroō, of Agrippa completing his sacrifices (above). Hebrew has the fulfilling of days in the Nazirite law: ad melot hayyamim, “until the days are fulfilled” (Numbers 6:5), and melot yemei nizro, “the days of their separation are fulfilled” (6:13); which Greek the Septuagint puts there is not checked on this page. The shelf: NWT 1984 “to give notice of the days to be fulfilled” and NIV “to give notice of the date when the days of purification would end” make it a notice of an end date; KJV “to signify the accomplishment of the days of purification,” ASV “declaring the fulfilment of the days of purification,” Geneva “declaring the accomplishment of the days of the purification” and Douay “giving notice of the accomplishment of the days of purification” keep the noun. Hē prosphora, “the offering,” is the noun of prosphereō, “to bring to,” whose passive prosēnechthē stands in the same clause (“was presented”); the noun stands in nine verses: here, Acts 24:17 (Paul says he came to do acts of charity for his nation, “and offerings”), Romans 15:16 (“the offering of the nations,” hē prosphora tōn ethnōn, which Paul hopes will be acceptable), Ephesians 5:2 (Christ gave himself for us, “an offering and sacrifice to God”) and Hebrews 10:5, 8, 10, 14 and 18, where “the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all” (10:10) is set against “no longer an offering for sin” (10:18); Acts 24:17, Romans 15:16, Ephesians 5:2 and Hebrews 10 are all not yet on these pages. The same noun, then, names the temple offering here, Paul’s own account of his gift in Acts 24, the nations Paul presents, and Christ. Of the shelf, KJV “an offering,” ASV “the offering,” Geneva “an offering,” NIV “the offering” and NWT 1984 “the offering” keep it, Douay “an oblation” has the Latin-derived word, and TLB “a sacrifice” changes the noun. Huper henos hekastou autōn, “for each one of them,” does not say whether Paul is counted: “them” most naturally takes up “the men,” and Paul is described as being purified “with them.” That the seven days of v27 follow, and the riot that starts in the temple, is for the next note (21:27–30).

What the scene shows about Paul’s practice. Luke reports three facts, in order: the elders ask, Paul does, and what Paul does is the thing the elders said would show that he “walks in line, keeping the law.” He pays, is purified, goes into the temple and has an offering made. Other texts bear on it and point in different directions. In Acts, Paul circumcised Timothy “because of the Jews who were in those places” (16:3), is said to have had a vow at Cenchreae (18:18), whose it was the Greek does not say, sailed “after the days of Unleavened Bread” (20:6) and was “hurrying… to be in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost” (20:16). In his letters, 1 Corinthians 9:20 has tois hypo nomon hōs hypo nomon, mē ōn autos hypo nomon, “to those under law as under law, not being myself under law,” and the apparatus marks the last clause, mē ōn autos hypo nomon, as absent from Robinson–Pierpont; the ASV (“not being myself under the law”) and the 1984 NWT (“though I myself am not under law”) print it at that verse, while the KJV’s “to them that are under the law, as under the law” ends without it. In Galatians 2:11–14 Paul tells of opposing Cephas at Antioch for withdrawing from the table of the nations out of fear of “those of the circumcision,” and asks him, ei sy Ioudaios hyparchōn ethnikōs kai ouk Ioudaikōs zēis, “If you, being a Jew, live like the nations and not like the Jews” (Galatians and 1 Corinthians 9 are not yet on these pages, and the Galatians sentence is Paul’s own report of a different occasion). The scene can be read at least three ways from these texts. One takes the elders’ present tense, stoicheis, “you walk,” as true of Paul’s whole life, so that the temple act displays what was already so. A second takes it as 1 Corinthians 9:20’s “as under law” in action, a practice adopted for those under the law without Paul himself being under it. A third takes it as a concession to the Jerusalem congregation, made at their request, to answer a rumour. Luke does not say which Paul would have said, and these pages do not choose.

Verses 27–30 · οἱ ἀπὸ τῆς Ἀσίας Ἰουδαῖοι … συνέχεον … ἐπέβαλον … τὰς χεῖρας … κατὰ τοῦ λαοῦ καὶ τοῦ νόμου καὶ τοῦ τόπου τούτου … πανταχῇ … Ἕλληνας εἰσήγαγεν … κεκοίνωκεν … Τρόφιμον τὸν Ἐφέσιον … προεωρακότες … ἐνόμιζον … ἐκινήθη … συνδρομή … εἷλκον … ἐκλείσθησαν αἱ θύραι hoi apo tēs Asias Ioudaioi … synecheon … epebalon … tas cheiras … kata tou laou kai tou nomou kai tou topou toutou … pantachē … Hellēnas eisēgagen … kekoinōken … Trophimon ton Ephesion … proeōrakotes … enomizon … ekinēthē … syndromē … heilkon … ekleisthēsan hai thyrai — Why was Paul seized in the temple at Jerusalem: who were the Jews from Asia, what does “made common this holy place” mean, who was Trophimus, and what did the warning stones forbid?

The Jews from Asia (Acts 21:27). Hōs de emellon hai hepta hēmerai synteleisthai, “and as the seven days were about to be completed,” picks up the days of purification that Paul announced in the temple at v26 (tēn ekplērōsin tōn hēmerōn, “the completion of the days”); the vow and its seven days are the subject of the note at vv23–26. The ones who see him are hoi apo tēs Asias Ioudaioi, “the Jews from Asia.” Luke names no one, and says only that they “saw” (theasamenoi) him in the temple; he does not say how they knew him. The reader has met such people before. At 6:9 some “of those from Cilicia and Asia” rose up “disputing with Stephen,” and the false witnesses of 6:11–14 come next. In Ephesus some in the synagogue were “hardening themselves and disobeying, speaking evil of the Way before the multitude” (19:9), and the word then reached “all those living in Asia, both Jews and Greeks” (19:10; see Asia). Paul’s reminder to the elders of his time “from the first day on which I set foot in Asia” (20:18) includes trials “through the plots of the Jews” (20:19), and the plot of 20:3 was laid in Greece. Luke does not say that the Jews of 21:27 are the people of any of these verses. At 24:19 (not yet on these pages) Paul speaks of “some Jews from Asia,” tines de apo tēs Asias Ioudaioi, who “ought to be present before you and accuse” if they had anything against him; that is the Greek of the verse as fetched, and this page reports it without reading further into it. NIV “some Jews from the province of Asia” adds “province” and “some”; TLB “some Jews from Turkey” puts a modern country where Luke has the name Asia (the same swap that version made at 20:4).

Throwing the crowd into confusion, and laying on hands (Acts 21:27). Synecheon, an imperfect, is “began throwing … into confusion” or “were throwing”: the Greek gives a process that has started, not a completed result. This is the verb syncheō (in its two spellings, syncheō and synchynnō), which stands in five verses of the New Testament, and all five are in Acts: 2:6, where the multitude at Pentecost “was bewildered” because each heard his own language, 9:22, where Saul “was bewildering the Jews who lived in Damascus,” 19:32, where the assembly at Ephesus “was in confusion,” this verse, and 21:31, where word goes up that “all Jerusalem was in confusion.” The noun sygchysis is at 19:29. In the first two the confusion is of astonishment or argument; in the last three it is a crowd being set going. Most of the shelf prints “stir” or “rouse” for it here: KJV and Douay “stirred up all the people,” ASV “stirred up all the multitude,” NIV “stirred up the whole crowd,” Geneva “moved all the people” and TLB “roused a mob against him.” None of those six carries the sense of confusion. NWT 1984 “began to throw all the crowd into confusion” does, and also keeps the imperfect. The next verb is epebalon … tas cheiras, “laid hands on.” Epiballō stands in four verses of Acts, and all four have “hands”: 4:3, where they “laid hands on them” and put them in custody until the next day, 5:18, “laid hands on the apostles,” 12:1, where Herod “laid his hands to afflict” some of the congregation, and here. The same crowd that is “confused” is therefore now doing what authorities did at 4:3 and 5:18. KJV, ASV and Geneva “laid hands on him” and Douay “laid hands upon him” keep the idiom; NWT 1984 “laid their hands upon him” keeps it with a possessive; NIV “seized him” and TLB “grabbed him” give only the force, as the revision NWT 2013 does with “seized him.” The apparatus gives ep’ auton tas cheiras, “on him the hands,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and tas cheiras ep’ auton to Robinson–Pierpont; this is word order only, and the English is the same either way.

“Men of Israel, help!” (Acts 21:28). Andres Israēlitai, “men of Israel,” is an address that stands in five verses of Acts, and this is the fifth: Andres Israēlitai opens Peter’s speeches at 2:22 and 3:12, Gamaliel’s warning at 5:35, and Paul’s own speech at 13:16. Each time it is a speaker gaining a hearing; here it is a rallying cry. Boētheite, “help,” is the imperative of the verb that the Macedonian in Paul’s vision used toward him: “Cross over into Macedonia and help us” (16:9); the root boēthe- stands in three verses of Acts, 16:9, this one, and 27:17 (not yet on these pages, where it is a noun for ship’s gear). NIV “Fellow Israelites, help us!” changes the address and adds “us”; the Greek has no object after boētheite. The cry continues: houtos estin ho anthrōpos ho kata tou laou kai tou nomou kai tou topou toutou pantas pantachē didaskōn, “this is the man who teaches everyone everywhere against the people and the law and this place.” KJV and Douay “against the people, and the law, and this place” follow the Greek order; NIV “against our people and our law and this place” adds “our” twice, where the Greek has the article only; NWT 1984 “against the people and the Law and this place” does not; TLB “tells everybody to disobey the Jewish laws” turns the accusation into a command, and then “He even talks against the Temple” makes the third item the temple alone. The apparatus gives pantachē, “everywhere,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and pantachou to Robinson–Pierpont, and the English does not change. The adverb in either spelling stands in four verses of Acts: 17:30, 21:28, 24:3 and 28:22 (the last two not yet on these pages). The first is Paul’s own sentence at Athens, pantas pantachou, “all people everywhere,” whom God “commands … to repent”; here the same two words, pantas pantachē, are what Paul is said to teach “against.” Luke does not remark on the likeness.

The same charge as Stephen’s? (Acts 21:28, compare 6:13–14, 21:21, 25:8). At 6:13 the false witnesses say that Stephen “does not stop speaking words against the holy place and the law,” kata tou topou tou hagiou kai tou nomou, and at 6:14 they add that Jesus “will destroy this place” (ton topon touton) and change the customs Moses handed down. The same elements recur at 21:28 (kata with the genitive, nomos, topos, houtos), with the people added and the order changed. The apparatus at 6:13 matters here: Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont print tou hagiou, “the holy,” which this translation follows, and Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 add toutou, so that those two editions read “this holy place,” the same three words (ton hagion topon touton) that the end of 21:28 has. At 6:13 Luke labels the witnesses “false” (pseudeis); at 21:28 he gives the shout without a label, and goes on at v29 to say why they shouted. The charge also differs from the one reported to Paul at 21:21, apostasian didaskeis apo Mōuseōs tous kata ta ethnē pantas Ioudaious, “you teach apostasy from Moses to all the Jews among the nations,” where the hearers are “all the Jews” and the subject is circumcision and customs; at 21:28 the hearers are only pantas, “everyone,” and the subject is people, law and place. Paul’s later answer, at 25:8 (not yet on these pages), has the same three-part shape in different nouns: oute eis ton nomon tōn Ioudaiōn oute eis to hieron oute eis Kaisara ti hēmarton, “neither against the law of the Jews nor against the temple nor against Caesar have I sinned in anything,” with eis where the accusers say kata. At 24:6 (not yet on these pages) the speaker for the high priest, Tertullus (24:1–2), says of Paul ho kai to hieron epeirasen bebēlōsai, “who even tried to profane the temple,” with a different verb, bebēlōsai, from the one in v28.

“Greeks,” “made common,” and the warning stones (Acts 21:28). Hellēnas eisēgagen eis to hieron, “he brought Greeks into the temple,” has a plural where v29 will name one man; and kekoinōken ton hagion topon touton, “has made common this holy place,” is a perfect, a state brought about and still standing. Koinoō stands in eleven verses of the New Testament: Matthew 15:11, 18 and 20, Mark 7:15, 18, 20 and 23, Acts 10:15, 11:9, this verse, and Hebrews 9:13 (not yet on these pages). The voice at Joppa, “What God has cleansed, you must not make common” (10:15, repeated at 11:9), uses the same verb as the Asian Jews’ accusation (dictionary entry). In Acts 10 it is about what a Jew may eat and, as Peter then explains at 10:28, whom he may approach: mēdena koinon ē akatharton legein anthrōpon, “to call no person common or unclean,” with allophylō, “one of another tribe,” for the foreigner. Allophylos stands in one verse of the New Testament, 10:28. Here a Jewish crowd uses the verb the other way: not that a Jew has been told to stop treating a person as common, but that a place has been made common by a person’s entering it. Luke gives the accusation and does not say, in v28, that it was true. None of the seven shelf versions prints “made common” here: KJV and Geneva “polluted this holy place,” ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 “defiled this holy place,” Douay “violated this holy place” and TLB “defiles it” each choose a word of moral or physical dirt or injury; the house rendering “make common” (as at 10:15) keeps the root and lets the reader see that it is the word of 10:15, a place or person taken out of the set-apart class. On “Greeks”: KJV and ASV “brought Greeks also into the temple,” NIV and NWT 1984 “brought Greeks into the temple” keep the Greek word; Geneva “hath brought Grecians” keeps it in an older form; Douay “brought in Gentiles” and TLB “bringing Gentiles in” use “Gentiles,” a wider word for all non-Jews than Luke’s Hellēnas. At Ephesus Luke pairs “Jews and Greeks” (19:10, 19:17), so there “Greeks” are the non-Jews of the city; whether the plural of v28 means that, or only men of Greek descent, the verse does not say. The Greek says “temple” (hieron), not a court, and nothing in the verse names the line that the crowd thinks was crossed. That there was one, with a notice, is reported by Josephus, not by Luke. In the Greek text of Niese on Perseus, Jewish War 5.193–194 says that on going on to the second temple there ran around it a stone balustrade (dryphaktos), three cubits high and finely worked, and that on it stood pillars (stēlai) at equal intervals “announcing the law of purity,” some in Greek and some in Roman letters: mēdena allophylon entos tou hagiou pariēnai, that no foreigner go within “the holy.” At 6.124–125 Titus, in Josephus’s account, asks John’s men whether they did not set up the pillars “inscribed in Greek letters and ours,” telling men not to cross the geision; and Antiquities 15.417 describes the second enclosure’s stone balustrade with an inscription forbidding the alloethnēs to enter, “the penalty of death being threatened” (thanatikēs apeilioumenēs tēs zēmias). Those lines are Josephus’s report; the stones themselves are mentioned in the note at Ephesians 2:14, which says two have been excavated (reported there, not checked here), and that note ties the “dividing wall of the fence” to this balustrade and to Acts 21:28–29. Whether the Asian Jews were thinking of that line, and whether the temple of Acts is the one Josephus describes in these passages, is not said in Acts. See also the Second Temple and the soreg.

“They supposed”: Trophimus the Ephesian (Acts 21:29). The verse is an explanation (gar, “for”) for the shout of v28, and it says on what the shout rested: ēsan gar proeōrakotes Trophimon ton Ephesion en tē polei syn autō, “for they had previously seen Trophimus the Ephesian in the city with him,” hon enomizon hoti eis to hieron eisēgagen ho Paulos, “whom they supposed Paul had brought into the temple.” Trophimus is named in three verses of the New Testament, Trophimos at 20:4, among the Asianoi who went with Paul, here, and 2 Timothy 4:20 (not yet on these pages): Trophimon de apelipon en Milētō asthenounta, “Trophimus I left in Miletus, sick.” Miletus is where Paul met the Ephesian elders in Acts 20 (20:17); whether the 2 Timothy 4:20 visit is that same one is not said in either text. What Luke gives about him here is a city, Ephesion, and that he had been seen “in the city” with Paul. Luke does not say in this verse that Trophimus was a Gentile, or that he entered the temple, or that he did not; the supposition belongs to the accusers, and the Greek verb is nomizō, which stands in seven verses of Acts. In the others it is: Moses “supposed” his brothers would understand (7:25), with the next clause “but they did not understand”; Simon “thought” the gift of God could be bought (8:20); those at Lystra stoned Paul “supposing him to be dead” (14:19), and in the next verse he “rose up” (14:20); the party “supposed there was a place of prayer” at the river (16:13); the jailer, “supposing that the prisoners had escaped” (16:27), is answered, “we are all here” (16:28); and Paul tells the Athenians that “we ought not to suppose” the divine is like gold or silver or stone (17:29). In 7:25, 8:20, 14:19 and 16:27 a nearby verse shows the supposition wrong or rebuked; 16:13 is a supposition that the passage does not correct; 17:29 is a prohibition. Which kind this one is, v29 does not say; v30 reports what the supposition set off. Proeōrakotes, “having previously seen,” is the perfect of the “see beforehand” verb that stands in four verses of the New Testament: Acts 2:25 (proorōmēn, “I kept … before me”), 2:31 (proidōn, “he foresaw”), this verse, and Galatians 3:8 (proidousa, not yet on these pages). In the first two the prefix is read as “before one” or “ahead of time”; here, as “earlier.” The apparatus gives proeōrakotes to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and heōrakotes, “having seen,” to Robinson–Pierpont, without the “previously.” The English divides accordingly: NIV and NWT 1984 “previously seen” and KJV and Geneva “had seen before” have it; ASV “had before seen” has it; Douay “they had seen Trophimus” does not print it (that version is made from the Latin, and whether the Latin or the shorter Greek lies behind its wording is not checked here); TLB “earlier that day” adds a time of day the Greek does not give. For the supposition: KJV, Geneva, ASV and Douay “they supposed that Paul had brought” use the verb the Greek has; NIV “assumed that Paul had brought him” and TLB “assumed that Paul had taken him” choose “assume”; NWT 1984 “were imagining Paul had brought him” chooses a word that suggests invention rather than inference. TLB also prints “a Gentile from Ephesus in Turkey” for ton Ephesion, which puts into the text what the verse leaves for the reader to supply; the Greek names a city and no ethnicity.

The whole city stirred, dragged out, the doors shut (Acts 21:30). Ekinēthē te hē polis holē, “and the whole city was stirred,” is a passive, and the root kine- stands in four verses of Acts: 6:12, where the opponents of Stephen “stirred up the people and the elders and the scribes” (synekinēsan, 6:12), 17:28 (“in him we live and move,” 17:28), this verse, and 24:5 (not yet on these pages), where Tertullus calls Paul one “stirring up” staseis among all the Jews. Other words for the same event in Acts are the etaraxan of 17:8, “they troubled the crowd and the politarchs,” and 17:13, “shaking and troubling the crowds.” KJV, ASV and Geneva “all the city was moved” follow the verb literally; Douay “the whole city was in an uproar,” NWT 1984 “the whole city was set in an uproar,” NIV “The whole city was aroused” and TLB “electrified” give it noise or heat. Egeneto syndromē tou laou, “there was a running together of the people,” has a noun, syndromē, that stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; its verb is at 3:11, where “all the people ran together” to Peter and John at Solomon’s portico, in the same temple, astounded. Then epilabomenoi tou Paulou heilkon auton exō tou hierou, “seizing Paul, they dragged him outside the temple.” The verb helkō stands in two verses of Acts, 21:30 and 16:19, where it has the same participle, epilabomenoi: Paul and Silas were seized and “dragged into the marketplace before the rulers.” Two other verbs for dragging are on these pages: syrō, in three verses of Acts: Saul “dragging off men and women” at 8:3, the Jews “dragged” Paul “outside the city” at Lystra (14:19), and Jason “dragged” before the politarchs at 17:6; and ekbalontes exō tēs poleōs, “having thrown out of the city,” for Stephen (7:58). Each of those three scenes puts the victim outside (exō): Stephen outside the city, Paul outside the city at Lystra, Paul outside the temple here. Luke says nothing about a verdict here and does not tell which court or gate Paul was dragged from. ASV “dragged him out of the temple,” NIV “dragged him from the temple” and NWT 1984 “dragged him outside the temple” keep the force; KJV and Douay “drew him out of the temple” and Geneva “drew him out of the Temple” use a milder word. The last clause, kai euthyōs ekleisthēsan hai thyrai, “and immediately the doors were shut,” has no named doer, and the verse does not say which doors: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “the doors were shut” follow the Greek noun; NWT 1984 “the doors were closed” does too; NIV “the gates were shut” and TLB “the gates were closed” use “gates,” which is a different noun in English from Luke’s thyrai. The shutting of doors is not new to the book: at 5:23 the officers report the prison found “locked” with the guards standing at the doors, in the same verb family (kekleismenon), though that is a prison and not a temple. None of the Josephus lines read for this note mentions these doors, so the shutting of them is left as Luke tells it: Paul is outside, and the next verse has the crowd seeking to kill him.

Verses 31–36 · τῷ χιλιάρχῳ τῆς σπείρης … φάσις … συγχύννεται … κατέδραμεν … ἁλύσεσι δυσί … ἐπυνθάνετο … ἐπεφώνουν … θόρυβον … παρεμβολήν … ἀναβαθμούς … Αἶρε αὐτόν tō chiliarchō tēs speirēs … phasis … sygchynnetai … katedramen … halysesi dysi … epynthaneto … epephōnoun … thorybon … parembolēn … anabathmous … Aire auton — Who was the tribune who arrested Paul in Jerusalem, what were the cohort, the chains and the barracks, and how does the cry “Away with him!” echo Luke 23 and John 19?

The tribune (Acts 21:31–33). Anebē phasis tō chiliarchō tēs speirēs, “word went up to the tribune of the cohort,” gives the officer a rank and no name. Chiliarchos is built from chilioi, “a thousand,” and archos, “ruler,” and it stands in twenty-one verses of the New Testament: Mark 6:21, John 18:12, Revelation 6:15 and 19:18 (not yet on these pages), and seventeen verses of Acts. Three of the seventeen are in this note, vv31, 32 and 33; the other fourteen are 21:37, and 22:24, 22:26–29, 23:10, 23:15, 23:17–19, 23:22, 24:22 and 25:23 (the last eight not yet on these pages). The officer who is named in these verses by his rank alone is therefore the one Paul addresses at 21:37 and the one the next four chapters keep returning to. This translation prints “tribune” for him throughout Acts, so that one officer is one word. The same Greek word is “military commanders” at Mark 6:21, “its commander” at John 18:12 and “the commanders” at Revelation 6:15 on these pages. Luke gives no name in these verses: Claudius Lysias is named at 23:26, and at 24:22 he is “Lysias the tribune” (both not yet on these pages). On the English shelf, one of the seven versions prints “tribune”: Douay “tribune” at v31, v32 and v33. KJV, ASV and Geneva “chief captain” keep the idea of a high officer in an older English; NIV and TLB “commander” are the plain modern word; NWT 1984 varies, “commander of the band” at v31 and “military commander” at v32 and v33, one Greek word in two English phrases.

The cohort (Acts 21:31). The tribune is the tribune tēs speirēs. Speira, “cohort,” the Greek word for a body of soldiers, stands in seven verses of the New Testament, counted by reading the hits and not by tallying the string: Matthew 27:27 (“gathered the whole cohort against him”), Mark 15:16, John 18:3 and 18:12, Acts 10:1 (“a centurion of the cohort called Italian”), here, and Acts 27:1, speirēs Sebastēs, the “Augustan” cohort (not yet on these pages). The two words, chiliarchos and speira, stand together in two verses, this one and John 18:12, where “the cohort and its commander” seize Jesus with the officers of the Jewish leaders. Neither verse says how many men the cohort was. KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 “band” (“of the band” in each) keep a word that does not say Roman; NIV “Roman troops” and TLB “Roman garrison” supply “Roman,” which is not in the Greek of this verse, though the context (a tribune, centurions, a barracks) supplies it, and TLB’s “garrison” is a body stationed in a place, which the Greek word does not say.

Word went up, and all Jerusalem in confusion (Acts 21:31). Phasis, “word, report,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so there is no second use to compare. It “went up” (anebē), the verb of climbing that Luke uses throughout this chapter for going up to Jerusalem; ASV “tidings came up” and NWT 1984 “information came up” keep the “up”; KJV “tidings came unto,” Douay “it was told,” NIV “news reached” and TLB “word reached” do not. The report is hoti holē sygchynnetai Ierousalēm, “that all Jerusalem is in confusion,” a present passive of the root Luke used of the crowd at 21:27, where the Jews from Asia “began throwing the whole crowd into confusion” (synecheon). The root stands in six verses of the New Testament, all in Acts (sygchynnō and its noun, in the forms searched): 2:6, 9:22, 19:29 (the noun, sygchysis), 19:32 (“the assembly was in confusion”), 21:27 and here. The apparatus gives sygchynnetai, present, to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and sygkechytai, the perfect, “has been thrown into confusion,” to Robinson–Pierpont; at the verse’s opening the first three print te, “and,” and Robinson–Pierpont de, a connective with no change of sense. ASV, Douay and NWT 1984 “in confusion” keep the word; KJV “in an uproar” and Geneva “on an uproar” do not, and NIV and TLB “in an uproar” use again at v34 the same English for a different Greek noun (see the paragraph on the uproar below). The city’s name has a question of its own. The verse prints Ierousalēm, and Acts uses two spellings of the name: Hierosolyma stands in twenty-three verses of Acts and Ierousalēm in thirty-five, and no verse of Acts has both; these are counts inside Acts only and do not show how the rest of the New Testament divides. In this chapter the first is at 21:4, 21:15 and 21:17 and the second at 21:11, 21:12, 21:13 and here. At 21:4 and 21:15 the apparatus gives Hierosolyma to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and Ierousalēm to Robinson–Pierpont, the same two spellings that were noted at 16:4. Luke does not say why he alternates, and this translation prints “Jerusalem” for both; the encyclopedia entry has the city.

Ran down upon them (Acts 21:32). Hos exautēs paralabōn stratiōtas kai hekatontarchas katedramen ep’ autous: the tribune, “at once,” took “soldiers and centurions” (the plural hekatontarchas means at least two) and “ran down upon them.” Katedramen is the aorist of katatrechō, “to run down,” and the forms searched (katedram-, katatrech-) stand in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Luke does not say where the soldiers ran from, only that the movement is downward and that the crowd stopped when it saw them. A place can be tested against Josephus, whose Greek was read for this note (Jewish War 5.238–245, in the Perseus edition). He describes the Antonia, the work of Herod the king, as lying “at the corner of two porticoes” of the first (outer) temple, the western and the northern (kata gōnian … dyo stoōn, 5.238); where it joined the temple’s porticoes it had katabaseis, “descents,” into both, “by which the guards came down” (di’ hōn katēiesan hoi phrouroi, 5.243); and “a unit of the Romans” (tagma Rhōmaiōn) always sat upon it, and at the festivals the men stood in arms around the porticoes to watch the people, “lest anything be stirred up” (hōs mē ti neōteristheiē, 5.244). A fortress at the corner of the temple, with a garrison and ways down into the porticoes, fits the picture of soldiers coming down into a crowd. Acts does not name the Antonia in these verses, and Josephus does not mention Paul; the fit is an inference from two texts, and neither identifies the barracks of v34 with it. The steps of v35 are anabathmoi, in two verses of the New Testament: here and 21:40, where Paul stands on them to speak. Josephus’s word for the ways between fortress and porticoes is katabaseis, “descents”; Luke’s is “ascents,” and whether they are the same stairs is what neither text says. NIV “steps” is this translation’s word; KJV, ASV, Douay, NWT 1984 and TLB “stairs” are the same thing in another word, and Geneva “grieces” is an older English word for steps. In v35 the verb of carrying, bastazesthai, is “to be carried” (KJV, ASV and Geneva “borne”), and the reason Luke gives is dia tēn bian tou ochlou, “because of the violence of the crowd.” The noun bia stands in three verses of the New Testament, counted by reading the hits: Acts 5:26, 21:35, and 27:41, where the printed text has it of the force breaking up a ship’s stern (the last not yet on these pages). At 5:26 the officers bring the apostles in “not with force, for they feared the people, lest they be stoned”; here the force is the crowd’s, and the soldiers’ answer is to carry him. NWT 1984 and ASV “violence of the crowd” keep ochlos; KJV “violence of the people,” Geneva and Douay print “people,” which is the English this translation keeps for laos in v36; TLB “lifted Paul to their shoulders” adds the shoulders and a purpose, “to protect him,” that the Greek does not state.

Bound with two chains (Acts 21:33). Epelabeto autou kai ekeleuse dethēnai halysesi dysi: the tribune “laid hold of him and ordered him to be bound with two chains.” The words halysesi dysi are the ones Luke wrote of Peter at 12:6, halysesin dysin, “bound with two chains” between two soldiers; the two phrases differ only in the final nu of each word. Halysis stands in four verses of Acts, counted by reading the hits: 12:6 and 12:7 (where the chains fall from Peter’s hands), here, and 28:20, tēn halysin tautēn perikeimai, “I wear this chain” (not yet on these pages), Paul’s own word for his condition at the end of the book. KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and NWT 1984 “two chains”; TLB “double chains” is a different phrase. The infinitive dethēnai, “to be bound,” is the same form Paul used of himself at 21:13, “I am ready not only to be bound but even to die,” so the tribune orders at v33 the first thing Paul said at v13 he was ready for. It is also the verb of Agabus’s words at 21:11: “thus will the Jews bind him in Jerusalem and hand him over into the hands of the nations.” Set side by side, the text shows this and no more. In v11 the Jews bind him and hand him over; in vv27–32 Jews from Asia lay hands on him and the crowd seizes him, drags him out and beats him; and the binding of v33 is ordered by a Roman officer, with no scene in vv31–36 in which anyone hands Paul to anyone, for the tribune takes hold of him himself. How closely v33 answers v11, and whether Luke means it to, is a question these verses do not answer; the Spirit’s words, and whether Paul was right to go on, are for the note at v11.

Kept inquiring, and the shouting (Acts 21:33–34). Epynthaneto tis eiē kai ti estin pepoiēkōs, “kept inquiring who he might be and what he had done,” is the imperfect of pynthanomai, and epynthaneto stands in five verses of the New Testament: Matthew 2:4, Luke 15:26, 18:36, here and Acts 23:19 (not yet on these pages), where the same tribune takes a young man by the hand and asks him in private. At Luke 18:36 the man who hears a crowd going by “asked what this meant,” with the same optative eiē; Luke 15:26 has ti an eiē, with an, and Robinson–Pierpont add an after tis here, while Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print tis eiē without it, as Luke 18:36 prints ti eiē. In v33 the apparatus also gives tote engisas to the first three and engisas de to Robinson–Pierpont, a particle that moves. KJV, Geneva and Douay “demanded who he was, and what he had done” make the question a demand; ASV “inquired,” NIV “he asked” and NWT 1984 “proceeded to inquire” do not; TLB “asked the crowd” adds the crowd as the ones asked, which the Greek leaves open. The answer is v34: alloi de allo ti epephōnoun en tō ochlō, “some shouted one thing, some another, in the crowd.” Epiphōneō stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Luke–Acts: Luke 23:21, where the crowd “kept shouting, ‘Crucify, crucify him!’”; Acts 12:22, where the people “kept shouting, ‘A god’s voice, and not a man’s!’”; here; and 22:24. The apparatus gives epephōnoun to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and eboōn, “were shouting,” to Robinson–Pierpont. A second variant in the same verse is the participle of the tribune’s inability: mē dynamenou de autou gnōnai in the first three, with autou in the genitive, and dynamenos in the nominative in Robinson–Pierpont. The first is a genitive absolute, the construction Greek uses for a participle whose subject is not the subject of the main verb, and here the autou is the tribune, who is also the subject of ekeleusen; the second agrees with him as an ordinary participle would. KJV and Douay “cried one thing” and ASV and NIV “shouted one thing” take the verb as plain crying out; Geneva “one cried this, another that” makes it one voice after another; NWT 1984 “began shouting out” adds a beginning.

The uproar and the barracks (Acts 21:34). The tribune “could not learn the certainty because of the uproar”: to asphales dia ton thorybon, with the article, “the” uproar, as at 20:1. Thorybos, the noun, stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Matthew 26:5 and Mark 14:2, where the leaders say “not during the festival,” lest there be an uproar among the people; Matthew 27:24, where Pilate sees “that an uproar was rather breaking out” and washes his hands; Mark 5:38; Acts 20:1; here; and 24:18 (not yet on these pages), where Paul says he was found in the temple with neither a crowd nor an uproar. Its verb, thorybeō, is the Jews’ act at 17:5 and Paul’s word at 20:10. On the shelf: ASV, NIV and TLB “uproar” match this translation (NIV and TLB used the same English at v31 for a different Greek word); KJV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 “tumult” keep the older word. Then the tribune orders him taken eis tēn parembolēn, “into the barracks.” Parembolē stands in ten verses of the New Testament, six of them in Acts: here, 21:37, 22:24, 23:10, 23:16 and 23:32 (the last three not yet on these pages), all of the place where the soldiers are lodged; and four elsewhere: Hebrews 11:34, where the faithful “put foreign armies to flight” (parembolas), Hebrews 13:11 and 13:13, the camp outside which the bodies of the sacrificial animals are burned and to which the reader is told to go out, and Revelation 20:9, “the camp of the saints” (the last three not yet on these pages). Outside Acts the word is a camp or an army in the field, so “barracks” is the sense the garrison of this scene supplies, and “camp” is the word’s other face. NIV “barracks” agrees; NWT 1984 “soldiers’ quarters” names the same place by who lives there; TLB “armory” is a place for storing arms, which the Greek word does not say; KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva “castle” use the older English word for a fortress.

“Away with him!” (Acts 21:36). Ēkolouthei gar to plēthos tou laou krazontes, Aire auton: the multitude “followed, crying out, ‘Away with him!’” Aire is the present imperative of airō, the verb for lifting and taking away, and the same verb is in the cries at Luke 23:18, Aire touton, which this translation prints “Away with this man!”, and at John 19:15, Aron aron, the doubled aorist imperative of the same verb, printed “Away with him! Away with him!” What is the same, then, is the verb and its mood, the command. What differs is the pronoun (touton, “this man,” in Luke; auton, “him,” here); the tense (aire, present, in Luke and Acts; aron, aorist, in John); the doubling, which John has and Luke and Acts do not; and what follows in the same breath: “Release Barabbas to us!” in Luke, “Crucify him!” in John, and in Acts nothing, since the verse stops. The cry next appears at 22:22, Aire apo tēs gēs ton toiouton, “Away from the earth with such a man”, which adds the words that say what the verb is asking for. The verb has one more use in this chapter, with a different object: Agabus “taking” (aras) Paul’s belt at 21:11. In the Gospel scenes a Roman governor is present when the cry is made: at Luke 23:20 Pilate is “wanting to release Jesus,” at 23:22 he says “I have found in him no guilt deserving of death,” and at 23:24 he “decided that their demand should be granted.” Here a Roman tribune, whose men are at that moment carrying Paul, has not been asked for a verdict at all; what he does after the cry is the matter of v37 (not yet on these pages). The aim of the cry is given in v31: they were “seeking to kill him.” The apparatus gives the participle krazontes, plural, to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and krazon, singular and neuter, to Robinson–Pierpont: the plural agrees with the multitude by sense, the singular with the word plēthos by form. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “Away with him” match this translation; TLB “Away with him, away with him!” doubles it, which is John’s shape and not Luke’s or Acts’; NIV “Get rid of him!” and NWT 1984 “Take him away!” each choose another English verb for it. NWT 2013, a different witness from NWT 1984, prints “Do away with him!” at the same place, a different English choice again for the same Greek.

Verses 37–40 · Ἑλληνιστὶ γινώσκεις … ὁ Αἰγύπτιος … ἀναστατώσας … τῶν σικαρίων … Ταρσεὺς τῆς Κιλικίας … οὐκ ἀσήμου πόλεως πολίτης … ἐπίτρεψόν μοι … κατέσεισε τῇ χειρί … σιγῆς … τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ Hellēnisti ginōskeis … ho Aigyptios … anastatōsas … tōn sikariōn … Tarseus tēs Kilikias … ouk asēmou poleōs politēs … epitrepson moi … kateseisen tē cheiri … sigēs … tē Hebraidi dialektō — Acts 21:37–40: what does “Do you know Greek?” mean, who was the Egyptian with the dagger-men, and what language did Paul speak to the crowd?

“Do you know Greek?” (Acts 21:37). Hellēnisti ginōskeis is a verb of knowing with an adverb of language, “do you know (how to speak) in Greek?” The adverb Hellēnisti stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and John 19:20, where the title on the cross was “written in Hebrew, in Latin, in Greek” (Hebraisti, Rhōmaisti, Hellēnisti). It is not the word for the Hellenists: Hellēnistēs stands in three verses, 6:1, 9:29 and 11:20, where the apparatus gives Hellēnistas to Westcott–Hort, Robinson–Pierpont and Nestle–Aland 28 and Hellēnas, “Greeks,” to Tregelles; the three verses are separate from the adverb here. Paul’s own question, Ei exestin moi eipein ti pros se, carries a small apparatus note: ti, “something,” is printed by Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and absent from Robinson–Pierpont, so “May I say to you?” is the Robinson–Pierpont shape. ASV “May I say something unto thee?” and NIV “May I say something to you?” keep the “something”; KJV and Geneva “May I speak unto thee?” leave it out, and Douay “May I speak something to thee?” puts it back. The tribune’s reply is rendered three ways. KJV, Geneva and Douay “Canst thou speak Greek?” and NWT 1984 “Can you speak Greek?” turn ginōskeis, “you know,” into “speak”; NIV “Do you speak Greek?” does the same; ASV “Dost thou know Greek?” and TLB “Do you know Greek?” keep the verb, as this translation does. The Greek does not say what language Paul had been speaking, or what had led the tribune to think the question needed asking; the tribune’s next words give his guess, and it is a mistaken identity.

“Are you not, then, the Egyptian” (Acts 21:38). Ouk ara sy ei ho Aigyptios: ara, “then,” ties the question to what has just happened, the crowd, the violence and the cry of v36, and the article makes him the Egyptian, a figure the tribune treats as known. Aigyptios stands in five verses of the New Testament: 7:22 (“wisdom of the Egyptians”), 7:24 and 7:28 (the Egyptian whom Moses struck), here, and Hebrews 11:29. The charge uses three words that Luke has used before. Anastatoō, “to stir up a revolt,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: Acts 17:6, where it is the accusation made against Paul and Silas at Thessalonica, here, and Galatians 5:12 (not yet on these pages). Exagagōn eis tēn erēmon, “having led out into the wilderness,” uses the verb of the Exodus in Stephen’s speech, houtos exēgagen autous, “this man led them out” of Moses (7:36), and of God’s own act in Paul’s at 13:17; whether the tribune’s words are meant to recall the Exodus, Luke does not say. Tetrakischilioi, “four thousand,” stands in five verses of the New Testament, and four of them are the numbers fed in the Gospels (Matthew 15:38, 16:10, Mark 8:9, 8:20); the fifth is here. Sikarioi stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and this translation prints “dagger-men.” The word is a Greek spelling of the Latin sicarii, from sica, “dagger”; Josephus himself derives the name from the Roman sicae (see the next paragraph). The shelf splits on how to put it into English. NWT 1984 “dagger men” and ASV “the Assassins” keep the name of a group, the second with a capital; TLB “4,000 members of the Assassins” does the same; KJV, Geneva and Douay “men that were murderers” give a description, and NIV “terrorists” a modern one. The verb is rendered “stirred up a revolt” here; NIV “started a revolt” is close, KJV “madest an uproar” is milder, and NWT 1984 “stirred up a sedition” and Douay “didst raise a tumult” sit between them.

What Josephus says, and where it differs (Acts 21:38). Josephus tells of both the dagger-men and the Egyptian in the years of the procurator Felix. In his Jewish War (2.254–257, numbered 2.13.3 in Whiston, as marked in the Perseus edition of Niese’s Greek) hoi kaloumenoi sikarioi, “the so-called sicarii,” are “a different kind of brigands” at Jerusalem who kill by day in the middle of the city, mostly at festivals, mixing with the crowd and hiding mikra xiphidia, “small daggers,” in their clothes; the first victim he names is the high priest Jonathan. Then comes a separate group of deceivers, planoi, who lead the crowd into the wilderness for signs of freedom, and Felix sends soldiers (2.258–260). Then (2.261–263, numbered 2.13.5 in Whiston) ho Aigyptios pseudoprophētēs, “the Egyptian false prophet,” gathers peri trismyrious, “about thirty thousand” of the deceived, brings them round from the wilderness to the Mount of Olives and means to force his way into Jerusalem; Felix meets him with the Roman infantry, the whole people joins in, and ton men Aigyption pheugein met’ oligōn, “the Egyptian fled with a few,” most of his men killed or taken and the rest scattered. In the Antiquities (20.169–172, numbered 20.8.6 in Whiston) a man ex Aigyptou, “from Egypt,” claiming to be a prophet, leads the people to the Mount of Olives, five stades from the city, promising that the walls will fall at his command; Felix kills four hundred and takes two hundred alive, and ho d’ Aigyptios autos diadras ek tēs machēs aphanēs egeneto, “the Egyptian himself escaped from the battle and disappeared.” This account gives no total for his following. Where the sicarii are named again, under the procurator Festus (Antiquities 20.186–187, numbered 20.8.10 in Whiston), Josephus says they used short curved daggers homoiais tais hypo Rhōmaiōn sikais kaloumenais, “like what the Romans call sicae,” and that the brigands took their name from them. So the accounts differ, and the page does not join them. Acts has four thousand men and calls them sicarii; Josephus has about thirty thousand in War, no figure in Antiquities (only four hundred killed and two hundred captured), and keeps the sicarii, the assassins who kill in the city, separate from the Egyptian’s crowd, who are deceived people following a prophet. Both place the man in Felix’s time and both have him escape. Whether the tribune’s figure is another count of the same event, a rumour, or a different gathering, and whether he is joining two groups that Josephus keeps apart, the texts do not say; that is reported here as an open question, not decided.

“A Tarsian of Cilicia, a citizen of a city not without distinction” (Acts 21:39). Paul answers the question about who he is, not the one about language. Ego anthrōpos men eimi Ioudaios, “I am a Jewish man,” is set against the next clause with men … de, so the first thing he gives is that he is a Jew. Tarseus stands in two verses, here and 9:11, where Ananias is told to look for “Saul, a Tarsian.” The city, Tarsos, stands in five verses in all: 9:11, 9:30, 11:25, here, and 22:3. Kilikia stands in eight verses of the New Testament, seven of them in Acts: 6:9 (the synagogue of the Cilicians that argued with Stephen), 15:23 and 15:41, here, 22:3, 23:34 and 27:5 (the last two not yet on these pages), and Galatians 1:21. Luke does not say that Paul was among those who argued with Stephen. The litotes ouk asēmou poleōs politēs, “citizen of a not-unmarked city,” is a figure of understatement: asēmos is built on a-, “not,” and sēma, “mark,” and the word stands in this verse alone in the New Testament (the search also finds parasēmos at 28:11, a different word, not yet on these pages). Politēs, “citizen,” stands in four verses: Luke 15:15, where the prodigal attaches himself to a citizen of that country, 19:14, where the nobleman’s citizens hate him, here, and Hebrews 8:11 (not yet on these pages). (The search for the stem also finds politarchēs, politeia and sympolitēs, which are other words.) Here it is of a city; the tribune’s own word at 22:28 for what he bought is a different noun, politeia. KJV, ASV and Douay “a citizen of no mean city” keep the double negative in English, where “mean” is the older sense of “petty”; NIV “a citizen of no ordinary city” and NWT 1984 “a citizen of no obscure city” keep it too, with different words for the missing mark; Geneva “a famous city of Cilicia” turns the denial into praise and puts “citizen” with Tarsus; TLB “no small town” says it about size and drops “citizen.”

“Allow me” (Acts 21:39–40). Epitrepson moi lalēsai pros ton laon, “allow me to speak to the people,” and then epitrepsantos de autou, “and when he had allowed it”: the verb of permission is asked for and granted in two verses running. Epitrepō stands in five verses of Acts: here and at 21:40, 26:1 (where Agrippa tells Paul he is permitted to speak for himself), 27:3 (where Julius lets him go to his friends) and 28:16 (where he is permitted to stay by himself at Rome), the last three not yet on these pages. In each of the later three Paul is in custody and what is permitted comes from someone else. ASV “give me leave to speak” and KJV, Geneva and Douay “suffer me to speak” are the older phrasing; NWT 1984 “permit me to speak” and NIV “Please let me speak” are the modern ones.

The hand, and the hush (Acts 21:40). Kataseiō, “to motion with the hand,” stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts and all of a speaker getting quiet before he speaks: Peter at 12:17, where he motions “to be silent” (sigan), Paul at 13:16 in the synagogue at Pisidian Antioch, Alexander at 19:33 in the theater at Ephesus, and Paul here, on the steps. Peter’s gesture carries an infinitive, sigan, and Paul’s here is followed by pollēs de sigēs genomenēs, “a great hush having come about.” The noun sigē stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Revelation 8:1, the silence in heaven; the verb sigaō is the one of the hush at the council in 15:12 and 15:13, of the listeners in Luke 20:26, and, in the infinitive, of Peter at 12:17. In Acts 22:2 the crowd’s quiet is not this word but hēsychian, in mallon parescon hēsychian, “they gave the more quiet.” KJV, ASV and Geneva “beckoned with the hand” and Douay “beckoned with his hand” make the gesture a call; NWT 1984 “motioned with his hand” and NIV “motioned to the crowd” keep it as a signal; TLB “motioned to the people to be quiet” adds what the gesture asked for, which the Greek does not say here. This translation’s “motioned with his hand” is the one printed at 12:17 and 19:33.

The Hebrew dialect (Acts 21:40). Prosephōnēsen tē Hebraidi dialektō, “he addressed them in the Hebrew dialect.” The apparatus gives prosephōnēsen to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and prosephōnei, the imperfect, to Robinson–Pierpont. Hebrais stands in three verses of the New Testament, all with the same noun: here, 22:2 and 26:14 (the last not yet on these pages), where Paul says that the voice on the road spoke to him tē Hebraidi dialektō. Dialektos stands in six verses, all in Acts: 1:19 (the field called Hakeldama in tē idia dialektō autōn, “their own dialect”), 2:6 and 2:8 (“each in his own dialect” at Pentecost), here, 22:2 and 26:14. It means a language as spoken in a place, not a script. The Greek names the language Hebrais and does not say whether Hebrew or Aramaic is meant, and the question is argued. A related adverb, Hebraisti, stands in seven verses and is used for words that are often classed as Aramaic in form, John 5:2, 19:13, 19:17, 20:16 Revelation 9:11, and Revelation 16:16 (not yet on these pages), as well as the title at 19:20; that classification is reported here, not examined on this page. The shelf divides. KJV, Geneva and Douay “the Hebrew tongue” print Hebrew; so do ASV “the Hebrew language,” NWT 1984 “the Hebrew language” and TLB “in Hebrew”; NIV “in Aramaic” prints Aramaic and adds a footnote offering Hebrew as a possibility, one that also covers 22:2. What the verse sets down is the contrast: the tribune asked whether Paul knew Greek, and Paul, with a Roman officer listening, turns to the people and speaks another language. Acts 22 gives the speech.
