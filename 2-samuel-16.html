Just beyond the summit David is met by Ziba, the young man of Mephibosheth, with a pair of saddled donkeys, bread, raisins, summer fruit and a skin of wine; Ziba says Mephibosheth is staying in Jerusalem hoping that the house of Israel will give back to him the kingdom of his father, and David gives Ziba all that was Mephibosheth’s. At Bahurim Shimei son of Gera, of the clan of the house of Saul, comes out cursing and throwing stones, calling David a man of bloods and a man of worthlessness; Abishai asks leave to take off his head, and David tells him to let Shimei curse, since Jehovah may have told him to, and says Jehovah may turn back good for the cursing. David and the people come weary to an unnamed place and draw breath. Absalom enters Jerusalem with Ahithophel, Hushai the Archite says to him ‘Long live the king’ and answers his question about friendship with ‘his I will be’, and Ahithophel counsels him to go in to his father’s concubines, which he does in a tent on the roof before all Israel; the chapter ends by saying that Ahithophel’s counsel in those days was as when a man inquires of the word of God.
Immediately after 2 Samuel 15, on the road east of Jerusalem toward the Jordan, while Absalom is entering the city.

Y David había pasado un poco más allá de la cumbre, y mira, Siba, el joven de Mefiboset, a su encuentro, con un par de asnos aparejados, y sobre ellos doscientos panes y cien racimos de pasas y cien de fruta de verano, y un odre de vino.

Y el rey dijo: «¿Y dónde está el hijo de tu señor?» Y Siba dijo al rey: «Mira, se queda en Jerusalén; porque dijo: “Hoy la casa de Israel me devolverá el reino de mi padre”».

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · 2 Samuel 16 Ziba on the road — a pair of donkeys, two hundred loaves, “the kingdom of my father”, and everything that was Mephibosheth’s

Bread and raisins on a road. “A pair of saddled donkeys, and on them two hundred loaves of bread and a hundred raisin clusters and a hundred of summer fruit, and a skin of wine”: Abigail’s gift to David was two hundred loaves, a hundred raisin clusters and two hundred fig cakes on donkeys (1 Samuel 25:18, already on these pages), so the road brings David the same bread and raisins a second time. “a pair of donkeys” stands in two verses, this one and Judges 19:3 (already on these pages). The Hebrew qayits is “summer fruit”. The ASV has “a hundred of summer fruits”. The NIV has “a hundred cakes of figs”. The TLB has “one hundred bunches of grapes”. The NWT 1984 has “a hundred loads of summer fruit”. Rahlfs’ Greek has phoinikes, “dates”. For the wine the ASV has “a bottle of wine”. The TLB has “a small barrel of wine”. The Douay has “a vessel of wine”.

The young man of Mephibosheth. “Ziba, the young man of Mephibosheth”: the Hebrew is na‘ar, and in 2 Samuel 9:10 (already on these pages) the same man has fifteen sons and twenty servants, so the word is a rank and not an age. The NIV has “the steward of Mephibosheth”. The TLB has “the manager of Mephibosheth’s household”. The ASV has “the servant of Mephibosheth”. The NWT 1984 has “the attendant of Mephibosheth”. Rahlfs’ Greek puts the meeting “a little beyond Rhos”, apo tēs Rhōs, the name it gave the summit in the last chapter.

The kingdom of my father. “Look, he is staying in Jerusalem; for he said: ‘Today the house of Israel will give back to me the kingdom of my father’”: the only witness to these words is Ziba, and the chapter does not say that Mephibosheth spoke them. His father was Jonathan, who never reigned, and Saul was his grandfather; the Hebrew av can say either. The NIV has “my grandfather’s kingdom”. The TLB has “the kingdom of my father, Saul”. The NWT 1984 has “the royal rule of my father”. The Hebrew also has “the son of your master”. The NIV has “your master’s grandson”. The ASV has “thy master’s son”.

Yours is all. “Look, yours is all that is Mephibosheth’s”: in 2 Samuel 9:9 (already on these pages) David had said to this same Ziba “All that pertained to Saul and to all his house have I given unto thy master’s son”, and the sentence is now turned the other way with no question asked. Ziba’s answer is hishtachaveti, “I bow down”. The NIV has “I humbly bow”. The ASV has “I do obeisance”. The NWT 1984 has “I do bow down”. The TLB has “Thank you, thank you, sir”. Mephibosheth’s own account is in 2 Samuel 19:24–30.

Verses 5–8 · Shimei at Bahurim — a man who keeps coming out, stones, “man of bloods”, and a kingdom given into Absalom’s hand

Bahurim, and a man of Saul’s clan. “King David came as far as Bahurim; and look, from there a man was coming out from the clan of the house of Saul”: Bahurim is where Paltiel followed Michal weeping and Abner told him “Go, return” (2 Samuel 3:16, already on these pages). Shimei’s father’s name, Gera, is also the name of Ehud’s father, a Benjaminite (Judges 3:15, already on these pages), and the text gives Shimei nothing more. The Hebrew yotse yatso is a participle doubled by its infinitive, “coming out and coming out”, a man who does not stop. The ASV has “he came out, and cursed still as he came”. The NWT 1984 has “coming out and calling down evil as he came out”. The NIV has “he cursed as he came out”. The Douay has “coming out he cursed as he went on”.

The mighty men at his right and left. “He threw stones at David and at all the servants of King David; and all the people and all the mighty men were at his right and at his left”: the Hebrew has a plain “and”. The ASV has “and all the people and all the mighty men were on his right hand and on his left”. The NIV has “though all the troops and the special guard were on David’s right and left”. The Douay has “walked on the right, and on the left side of the king”. The TLB has “all the mighty warriors who surrounded them”.

Man of bloods. “Get out, get out, man of bloods, and man of worthlessness!”: the Hebrew damim is the plural of blood, and it means shed blood and the guilt for it. “the man of bloods”, with the article, stands in one verse, this one; verse 8 repeats it without the article. The NIV has “you murderer, you scoundrel”. The NWT 1984 has “you bloodguilty man and good-for-nothing man”. The ASV has “thou man of blood, and base fellow”. The Douay has “thou man of Belial”.

All the bloods of the house of Saul. “Jehovah has turned back upon you all the bloods of the house of Saul, in whose place you have reigned”: the Hebrew says that the blood is turned back on David and does not say that he shed it. The ASV has “Jehovah hath returned upon thee all the blood of the house of Saul”. The NWT 1984 has “brought back upon you all the bloodguilt for the house of Saul”. The NIV has “for all the blood you shed in the household of Saul”. The Douay has “thou hast usurped the kingdom in his stead”. The TLB has “for murdering King Saul and his family”. The book has David say “I and my kingdom are guiltless before Jehovah for ever from the blood of Abner” (2 Samuel 3:28) and say to the Amalekite “Your blood be upon your head” (2 Samuel 1:16), both already on these pages; in this chapter he does not answer the charge. “And look, you are in your evil”: the noun is ra‘ah, the evil David feared Absalom would drive down on the city (2 Samuel 15:14, now on these pages). The ASV has “taken in thine own mischief”. The NWT 1984 has “in your calamity”. The NIV has “You have come to ruin”.

Verses 9–14 · Abishai and the dead dog — “What to me and to you”, a curse that Jehovah may have sent, my eye, and dust

This dead dog. “Why should this dead dog curse my lord the king? Let me cross over, please, and take off his head”: “the dead dog” stands in two verses, this and 2 Samuel 9:8 (already on these pages), where it is Mephibosheth’s word for himself, and in verse 4 of this chapter David has just given Mephibosheth’s property away. The ASV has “take off his head”. The NIV has “cut off his head”. The TLB has “strike off his head”.

What to me and to you. “What to me and to you, sons of Zeruiah?”: mah-li valakhem is the idiom of Jephthah to the king of Ammon (Judges 11:12, already on these pages), and David has said of the same brothers that they are “too hard for me” (2 Samuel 3:39, already on these pages); he says it to them again at 2 Samuel 19:23. The ASV has “What have I to do with you, ye sons of Zeruiah?”. The NIV has “What does this have to do with you, you sons of Zeruiah?”. The NWT 1984 has “What do I have to do with YOU men, YOU sons of Zeruiah?”. “Because he curses thus, because Jehovah has said to him: ‘Curse David’”: the written text has vekhi ki and the reading tradition has ki, and the Hebrew has no “let him”. The ASV has “Because he curseth, and because Jehovah hath said unto him, Curse David”. The NIV has “If he is cursing because the Lord said to him”. The NWT 1984 has “Thus let him call down evil”. The Douay has “Let him alone and let him curse”.

My inward parts, my eye. “My son, who came out of my inward parts, seeks my soul”: me‘ay is the inward organs and nefesh is the soul or life. The ASV has “who came forth from my bowels, seeketh my life”. The NWT 1984 has “out of my own inward parts, is looking for my soul”. The NIV has “My son, my own flesh and blood, is trying to kill me”. “Perhaps Jehovah will look upon my eye”: the written text of verse 12 has b‘wny, which the consonants let be read “my iniquity” (avoni) or “my affliction” (oni), and the reading tradition has be‘eini, “my eye”, which I translate. The ASV has “the wrong done unto me”. The Geneva has “mine affliction”. The NIV has “my misery”. The NWT 1984 has “see with his eye”. The Greek has en tē tapeinōsei mou, “in my humiliation”. The verb of Shimei’s curse, heshiv, “has turned back” the bloods on David, returns in David’s hope, “Jehovah will turn back to me good in place of his cursing”. The NIV has “restore to me his covenant blessing instead of his curse”. The NWT 1984 has “actually restore to me goodness instead of his malediction”.

The side of the hill, and breath. “Shimei was going along the side of the hill opposite him, going and cursing, and throwing stones opposite him, and dusting with dust”: tsela is “side”, the word of Genesis 2:21, and here it is a hill’s. “dusting with dust” (ve‘ippar be‘afar) stands in one verse, a verb made from the noun. The NIV has “showering him with dirt”. The ASV has “cast dust”. The NWT 1984 has “threw a lot of dust”. “The king and all the people who were with him came weary; and he drew breath there”: the weary are ayefim, from the root of Ziba’s “the weary” in verse 2, and the verb vayinnafesh is the Sabbath verb of Exodus 31:17 (already on these pages). The Hebrew does not name where they arrived. The NIV has “arrived at their destination exhausted”. The ASV has “he refreshed himself there”. The NWT 1984 has “they refreshed themselves”.

Verses 15–19 · Hushai before Absalom — “Long live the king”, a friend’s kindness, and “his I will be”

The man of Israel. “And Absalom, and all the people, the man of Israel, came to Jerusalem; and Ahithophel with him”: the Hebrew has the singular, ish yisrael, as in 15:13 (2 Samuel 15:13, now on these pages), with a plural verb. The ASV has “all the people, the men of Israel”. The NIV has “all the men of Israel”. The Douay has “all his people”.

Long live the king. “Long live the king! Long live the king!”: “long live the king” (yehi hamelekh) stands in seven verses: 1 Samuel 10:24, this verse, 1 Kings 1:25, 34 and 39 (all already on these pages), 2 Kings 11:12 and 2 Chronicles 23:11. The Hebrew says “the king” and does not say whose. The ASV has “Long live the king, Long live the king”. The NWT 1984 has “Let the king live! Let the king live!”. The Geneva has “God save the King”. The Douay has “God save thee, O king”.

Your kindness to your friend. “Is this your kindness to your friend? Why did you not go with your friend?”: Absalom uses chesed, the word David used to Ittai in 15:20, and he says “your friend” twice. The NIV has “So this is the love you show your friend?”. The NWT 1984 has “This is the loving-kindness of yours toward your companion, is it?”. The ASV has “Is this thy kindness to thy friend?”.

His I will be. “No; but whom Jehovah chose, and this people, and every man of Israel — his I will be, and with him I will stay”: the words “whom Jehovah has chosen” are Samuel’s in 1 Samuel 10:24, just before the people shout “Long live the king”. The written text has lo, “not”, and the reading tradition has lo, “his”; they sound the same, and the Greek has autō esomai, “I will be his”. The ASV has “his will I be, and with him will I abide”. The NIV has “his I will be, and I will remain with him”. The Geneva has “with them will I dwell”. “And secondly, whom shall I serve? Is it not before his son?”: the words can be heard two ways, and Absalom hears one of them. The ASV has “should I not serve in the presence of his son?”. The NIV has “Should I not serve the son?”. The Douay has “is it not the king’s son?”.

Verses 20–23 · Ahithophel’s counsel — a tent on the roof, “a stench to your father”, and counsel like the word of God

Give counsel. “Give for yourselves counsel: what shall we do?”: the verb havu is plural, though Absalom has turned to Ahithophel. The NIV has “Give us your advice”. The NWT 1984 has “YOU men, give counsel on YOUR part”. The ASV has “Give your counsel what we shall do”.

Go in to your father’s concubines. “Go in to your father’s concubines, whom he left to keep the house”: they are the ten of 15:16, left “to keep the house” (2 Samuel 15:16, now on these pages), and “go in to” is the Hebrew idiom. The ASV has “Go in unto thy father’s concubines”. The NIV has “Sleep with your father’s concubines”. The NWT 1984 has “Have relations with the concubines of your father”. “You have made yourself a stench to your father”: nivasheta is the third verse in which the form “has become a stench” stands, after 1 Samuel 13:4 and 2 Samuel 10:6 (both already on these pages), and all three are in Samuel. The ASV has “thou art abhorred of thy father”. The NIV has “you have made yourself obnoxious to your father”. The NWT 1984 has “you have made yourself foul-smelling to your father”. The Douay has “thou hast disgraced thy father”. “The hands of all who are with you will be strong” is David’s word to Jabesh-gilead, “let your hands be strong” (2 Samuel 2:7, already on these pages).

A tent on the roof. “They spread a tent for Absalom on the roof; and Absalom went in to his father’s concubines before the eyes of all Israel”: “the tent on the roof” stands in one verse. The roof is where David walked and saw Bathsheba (2 Samuel 11:2), and Nathan had said “I will take your wives before your eyes and give them to your neighbor, and he shall lie with your wives in the sight of this sun” and “I will do this thing before all Israel” (2 Samuel 12:11–12), all already on these pages; Nathan’s word is “neighbor”, and it is not named here. The ASV has “in the sight of all Israel”. The NWT 1984 has “under the eyes of all Israel”. The Douay has “before all Israel”. For the tent the NIV has “pitched a tent”. The Geneva has “spread Absalom a tent”.

As when a man inquires of the word of God. “The counsel of Ahithophel, which he counseled in those days, was as when a man inquires of the word of God”: “as when a man inquires of the word” stands in one verse, and the comparison is to an oracle, not to wisdom. The ASV has “as if a man inquired at the oracle of God”. The NIV has “like that of one who inquires of God”. The NWT 1984 has “just as when a man would inquire of the word of the [true] God”. The Douay has “as if a man should consult God”. It was so “both to David and to Absalom”, one adviser for two kings, and David’s prayer in 2 Samuel 15:31 (now on these pages) was against exactly this. Rahlfs’ Greek has the counsel given “in the first days”, en tais hēmerais tais prōtais, where the Hebrew has “in those days”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “the young man of Mephibosheth”; “a pair of donkeys”; “summer fruit”; “coming out and coming out”; “man of bloods” and “man of worthlessness”; “all the bloods of the house of Saul”; “this dead dog”; “What to me and to you”; “my inward parts”; “the side of the hill”; “dusting with dust”; “drew breath”; “Long live the king”; “a stench”; “before the eyes of all Israel”; “the word of God”; and the pointed readings “my eye”, “his I will be” and “because he curses thus”, with the written forms in the notes.

Echoes paid. Abigail’s loaves and raisins (1 Samuel 25:18); the Levite’s pair of donkeys (Judges 19:3); Ziba’s fifteen sons and the land given to Mephibosheth (2 Samuel 9:9–10); Paltiel at Bahurim (2 Samuel 3:16); Ehud son of Gera (Judges 3:15); the blood of Abner and the Amalekite’s blood on his head (2 Samuel 3:28; 1:16); the evil David feared (2 Samuel 15:14); the dead dog (2 Samuel 9:8); “What to me and to you” (Judges 11:12) and the sons of Zeruiah too hard for David (2 Samuel 3:39); the Sabbath verb for drawing breath (Exodus 31:17); the ten concubines left to keep the house (2 Samuel 15:16); “a stench” (1 Samuel 13:4; 2 Samuel 10:6); “let your hands be strong” (2 Samuel 2:7); “Long live the king” (1 Samuel 10:24; 1 Kings 1:25, 34, 39) and “whom Jehovah has chosen” (1 Samuel 10:24); the roof and Nathan’s word (2 Samuel 11:2; 12:11–12); and David’s prayer against Ahithophel (2 Samuel 15:31).

Echoes planted. Mephibosheth’s answer and the land divided (2 Samuel 19:24–30); “What to me and to you, sons of Zeruiah” again (2 Samuel 19:23); Shimei at the Jordan, forgiven and then judged (2 Samuel 19:16–24; 1 Kings 2:8–9, 36–46); Jehovah ordaining that Ahithophel’s counsel be defeated (2 Samuel 17:14); the concubines shut up until the day of their death (2 Samuel 20:3); Ahithophel’s end (2 Samuel 17:23); and “Long live the king” for Joash (2 Kings 11:12; 2 Chronicles 23:11). None of these is translated here yet.

Next in this book: 2 Samuel 17 — Ahithophel counsels twelve thousand men that night against a weary David; Absalom calls Hushai, who counsels gathering all Israel and going in person, and Absalom and all the men of Israel say that Hushai’s counsel is better, because Jehovah had ordained to defeat the good counsel of Ahithophel; Hushai tells Zadok and Abiathar, Jonathan and Ahimaaz carry the word from En-rogel and are hidden in a well at Bahurim, David crosses the Jordan, Ahithophel puts his house in order and strangles himself, and David at Mahanaim is brought beds and bread by Shobi, Machir and Barzillai.
