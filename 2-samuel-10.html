Hanun succeeds his father Nahash as king of the sons of Ammon; David sends servants to comfort him, and Ammon’s princes persuade him they are spies, so he shaves half their beards and cuts their garments to the buttocks; David tells the men to stay at Jericho until their beards grow; Ammon hires Aram of Beth-rehob and Zobah, the king of Maacah and Ish-tob; Joab and Abishai divide the army, Ammon is shut in its city and Joab returns to Jerusalem; Hadadezer calls Aram from beyond the River, David crosses the Jordan to Helam and strikes Aram and its commander Shobach, and Hadadezer’s servants make peace with Israel.
After the wars of 2 Samuel 8 and the kindness of 2 Samuel 9; the chapter gives no date, only “after this” and, in 11:1, the turn of the year when the war resumes.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–2 · Hanun son of Nahash — “I will do kindness with Hanun”, and a kindness the Hebrew never describes

Hanun and Nahash. “And it happened after this that the king of the sons of Ammon died, and Hanun his son reigned in his place”: the king is not named until the next verse, where he is Nahash. A Nahash “the Ammonite” came up against Jabesh-gilead and offered to cut a covenant on the condition of every right eye (1 Samuel 11:1–2, already on these pages), and Samuel reminds Israel that “Nahash king of the sons of Ammon came against you” when they asked for a king (1 Samuel 12:12, already on these pages). The Hebrew gives this man the same title but does not say that he is the same, nor how long he had reigned. Hanun is spelled with the letters of channun, “gracious”, the word of “merciful and gracious” (Exodus 34:6, already on these pages), and Nahash with the letters of nachash, “serpent”; the verses that follow do not give either name an opening to be used. The ASV, the KJV and the NIV have “Hanun”; the Douay has “Hanon” and, in the next verse, “the son of Daas”. The Greek has Annōn and Naas.

Do kindness with. “I will do kindness with Hanun son of Nahash, as his father did kindness with me”: this is the verb of the chapter before, where David asked whether anyone is left of Saul’s house “that I may do kindness with him for Jonathan’s sake” (2 Samuel 9:1, already on these pages), and the kindness is again for a father’s sake. “Do kindness with” (asah chesed im) stands in four verses. They are Genesis 24:12 (not yet in Spanish on these pages), 2 Samuel 3:8, this verse, and 1 Chronicles 19:2. Abner used it of his own loyalty to Saul’s house, and Abraham’s servant asked it of God. What Nahash had done for David is not told anywhere in the Hebrew Bible. The NIV has “just as his father showed kindness to me”; the NWT 1984 “just as his father exercised loving-kindness toward me”; the KJV “as his father shewed kindness unto me”; the ASV “as his father showed kindness unto me”; the Douay “as his father shewed kindness to me”; the TLB “because his father, Nahash, was always so loyal and kind to me”, which gives David a long acquaintance that the Hebrew does not mention. The Greek has eleos for both kindnesses, the word it used at 2 Samuel 9:1.

To comfort him. “And David sent to comfort him by the hand of his servants concerning his father”: nacham is the verb of condolence, and the servants who go are the “servants” of the verse, the word the Ammonite princes will turn against them. The ASV has “to comfort him concerning his father”; the NIV “to express his sympathy to Hanun concerning his father”; the NWT 1984 “to comfort him over his father”; the TLB “to express regrets to Hanun about his father’s death”.

Verses 3–5 · Spies, half a beard and a cut garment — and “Stay at Jericho until your beards grow”

Spies. “Is David honoring your father in your eyes, that he has sent comforters to you? Is it not in order to search the city, and to spy it out, and to overturn it, that David has sent his servants to you?”: “in your eyes” is the Hebrew, a question whether Hanun sees it as honor. The NIV has “Do you think David is honoring your father”; the NWT 1984 “Is David honoring your father in your eyes”; the ASV and the Geneva “Thinkest thou that David doth honor thy father”; the Douay “Thinkest thou that for the honour of thy father”; the TLB “These men aren’t here to honor your father!” For the second question the ASV has “to search the city, and to spy it out, and to overthrow it”; the NIV “to explore the city and spy it out and overthrow it”; the NWT 1984 “searching through the city and to spy it out and to overthrow it”; the TLB “to spy out the city before attacking it”. The verb of the last is hafakh, “overturn”. The Greek asks whether David “glorifies” (doxazein) the father, and has ereunēsōsin, “search”, and kataskopēsōsin, “spy out”.

Half the beard. “And Hanun took David’s servants, and shaved off half of their beards, and cut off their garments in the middle, to their buttocks; and sent them away”: the insult is in the halves, a half-shaved face and a half-cut garment, so that the men cannot go as they came and cannot be taken for anything but what they were done to. The prophet will use the beard as a mark of shame too, when the Lord “will shave with the razor hired beyond the River — with the king of Assyria — the head and the hair of the feet; and it will sweep away the beard as well” (Isaiah 7:20, already on these pages), and he will show captives “with buttocks uncovered, to the shame of Egypt” (Isaiah 20:4). The NIV has “shaved off half of each man’s beard, cut off their garments at the buttocks”; the ASV “shaved off the one half of their beards, and cut off their garments in the middle, even to their buttocks”; the NWT 1984 “shaved off half their beards and cut their garments in half to their buttocks”; the Douay “cut away half of their garments even to the buttocks”; the TLB “cut their robes off at the buttocks and sent them home half naked”. The Greek has ischia, “hips”, where the Hebrew has the buttocks.

Greatly humiliated. “For the men were greatly humiliated”: niklamim, from the verb of shame and disgrace. “Humiliated” (niklamim) stands in three verses. They are this one, 1 Chronicles 19:5 and 2 Samuel 19:4, where the people steal into the city “as people that are ashamed steal away when they flee in battle”. The ASV has “the men were greatly ashamed”; the NIV “they were greatly humiliated”; the NWT 1984 “very humiliated”; the TLB “very embarrassed over their appearance”; the Douay “sadly put to confusion”. David “sent to meet them”: the king goes out to them before they reach the court. The Greek has ētimasmenoi sphodra, “dishonored exceedingly”.

Stay at Jericho. “Stay at Jericho until your beards grow, and then return”: the Hebrew verb is tsamach, “sprout”, as of grass, and the Greek keeps that image, anateilai, “rise”. Jericho is on the west bank of the Jordan, across from Ammon, “the city of palm trees” (Deuteronomy 34:3, already on these pages); in the days of the judges Eglon of Moab gathered “the sons of Ammon and Amalek” and “they took the City of Palms” (Judges 3:13, already on these pages). The ASV has “Tarry at Jericho until your beards be grown, and then return”; the NIV “Stay at Jericho till your beards have grown”; the NWT 1984 “until YOUR beards grow abundantly”.

Verses 6–8 · “A stench to David” — Ammon hires Aram, and the battle is set at the gate

A stench to David. “And the sons of Ammon saw that they had become a stench to David”: the verb of becoming a stench (ba’ash) to someone stands in six verses. They are Jacob’s “making me a stench to the inhabitants of the land” (Genesis 34:30), the foremen’s “you have made us a stench in the eyes of Pharaoh” (Exodus 5:21), Israel “become a stench to the Philistines” (1 Samuel 13:4), Achish’s “he has surely made himself a stench among his people” (1 Samuel 27:12, all already on these pages), this verse, and Ahithophel’s word to Absalom (2 Samuel 16:21). The KJV has “they stank before David”; the Geneva “they stank in the sight of David”; the ASV “were become odious to David”; the NIV “they had become obnoxious to David”; the NWT 1984 “they had become foul-smelling to David”; the Douay “they had done an injury to David”; the TLB “how seriously they had angered David”. The Greek turns it round: katēschynthēsan ho laos Dauid, “David’s people were put to shame”, as if the Ammonites saw only what they had done to the envoys.

Hired Aram. “And the sons of Ammon sent and hired Aram of Beth-rehob and Aram of Zobah, twenty thousand on foot, and the king of Maacah, a thousand men, and Ish-tob, twelve thousand men”: “Beth-rehob” stands in two verses. They are this one and Judges 18:28 (already on these pages), where it is the valley in which Laish lay. The Hebrew has ish tov, “man of Tob”, as a name; Judges 11:3 and 5 (already on these pages) have the land of Tob, where Jephthah lived when the elders of Gilead fetched him to fight Ammon, so Ammon now hires from the place that supplied its enemy. Zobah is Hadadezer’s kingdom (2 Samuel 8:3, already on these pages). The ASV has “the men of Tob twelve thousand men”; the KJV “of Ishtob twelve thousand men”; the NIV “twelve thousand men from Tob”; the Douay “of Istob twelve thousand men”; the TLB “ten thousand from the land of Tob”. The Chronicles tell it differently: “a thousand talents of silver to hire them chariots and horsemen” from Aram-naharaim, Aram-maacah and Zobah, and “thirty and two thousand chariots” (1 Chronicles 19:6–7). The Greek has “Syria of Beth-rob” with no Zobah.

At the gate and in the field. “And David heard, and sent Joab and all the army, the mighty men. And the sons of Ammon came out and drew up for battle at the entrance of the gate; and Aram … were by themselves in the field”: the Ammonites stand at the gate of their own city and Aram a little way off, in the open, levadam, “by themselves”. The ASV has “all the host of the mighty men”; the NIV “the entire army of fighting men”; the NWT 1984 “all the army [and] the mighty men”; the Douay “the whole army of warriors”. For the line the ASV has “put the battle in array at the entrance of the gate”; the NIV “drew up in battle formation at the entrance of their city gate”; the NWT 1984 “draw up in battle formation at the entrance of the gate”; the Douay “set their men in array at the entering in of the gate”.

Verses 9–14 · Joab’s two fronts — “it shall be salvation for me”, “Be strong, and let us be strong”, and what is good in Jehovah’s eyes

Before and behind. “And Joab saw that the face of the battle was toward him in front and behind; and he chose from all the picked men of Israel, and drew up to meet Aram. And the rest of the people he gave into the hand of Abishai his brother”: the written Hebrew has “in Israel” (beyisrael), which the pointing reads as “Israel”; the NWT 1984 follows the written form, “some of all the choice men in Israel”. The ASV and the KJV have “he chose of all the choice men of Israel”; the Douay “chose of all the choice men of Israel”; the NIV “selected some of the best troops in Israel”; the TLB “selected the best fighters in his army”. The Greek has neaniskōn, “young men”. Joab and Abishai are brothers and sons of Zeruiah (2 Samuel 2:18, already on these pages), and the two who killed Abner (2 Samuel 3:30, already on these pages) are here covering each other in one battle.

It shall be salvation for me. “If Aram is stronger than I, then it shall be salvation for me; and if the sons of Ammon are stronger than you, then I will go and save you”: the verb of the first half is feminine, “and it shall be”, with no “you”, so the Hebrew says that there shall be rescue and not who brings it; the second half is “I will go to save you”, lehoshia lakh. The NWT 1984 has “you must serve as a salvation for me” and “I must also come to save you”; the NIV “then you are to come to my rescue”; the ASV “then thou shalt help me; but if the children of Ammon be too strong for thee, then I will come and help thee”; the TLB “come out and help me”. The Greek has eis sōtērian in the first half, “for salvation”, and tou sōsai se, “to save you”. Yasha is the word of “Jehovah saved David wherever he went” (2 Samuel 8:6, 14, already on these pages); here two brothers promise each other the same, and in verse 19 Aram will fear to do it for Ammon.

Be strong. “Be strong, and let us be strong for our people and for the cities of our God; and Jehovah will do what is good in his eyes”: “Be strong, and let us be strong” (chazak venitchazzak) stands in two verses, this one and the verse in the Chronicles that repeats the speech (1 Chronicles 19:13). “What is good in his eyes” stands in three verses, and the other two are in the Chronicles as well (1 Chronicles 19:13; 21:23). Eli’s “He is Jehovah; let him do what is good in his eyes” (1 Samuel 3:18, already on these pages) is the same words, spelled bə‘eynō without the yod, which is why a search does not find it. Eli said it of a judgment; Joab says it before a battle, after doing all that men can. The NWT 1984 has “and as for Jehovah, he will do what is good in his own eyes”; the NIV “will do what is good in his sight”; the ASV “let us play the man for our people, and for the cities of our God”, as does the KJV (“let us play the men for our people”); the Geneva “let us be valiant for our people”; the Douay “let us fight for our people, and for the city of our God”; the TLB “We must really act like men today”. The Greek has andrizou, “act like a man”.

Fled from before him. “And Joab and the people who were with him drew near for the battle against Aram; and they fled from before him”: Aram flees first and Ammon after it, and Ammon goes back inside the city; Joab does not enter it, but returns to Jerusalem. The ASV has “Then Joab returned from the children of Ammon, and came to Jerusalem”; the NIV “Joab returned from fighting the Ammonites and came to Jerusalem”; the NWT 1984 “Joab returned from the sons of”; the TLB “Afterwards Joab returned to Jerusalem”. The war with Ammon is left standing at the city gate.

Verses 15–19 · Hadadezer sends for Aram “beyond the River” — Helam, Shobach, and kings who make peace

Beaten before Israel. “And Aram saw that it had been beaten before Israel; and they gathered together”: the verb is nagaf, “be struck”, here of a defeat. The ASV has “put to the worse before Israel”; the KJV “smitten before Israel”; the NIV “routed by Israel”; the NWT 1984 “defeated before Israel”; the Douay “fallen before Israel”.

Beyond the River. “And Hadadezer sent and brought out Aram who were beyond the River”: “From beyond the River” stands in four verses. They are Joshua’s “I took your father Abraham from beyond the River” and “the gods your fathers served beyond the River” (Joshua 24:3, 15, already on these pages), this verse, and 1 Chronicles 19:16. The River is the Euphrates, where David had struck Hadadezer (2 Samuel 8:3); here the same king calls up the country on its far bank, the country Abraham came from. Ammon had “hired” Aram in verse 6, and the razor of Isaiah 7:20 is “hired beyond the River” as well. The ASV has “brought out the Syrians that were beyond the River”; the KJV “brought out the Syrians that were beyond the river”; the NIV “from beyond the Euphrates River”; the TLB “from the other side of the Euphrates River”. The ASV and the KJV print the king’s name “Hadarezer”, the Douay “Adarezer”; the Hebrew here has Hadadezer, as in chapter 8.

Helam and Shobach. “And they came to Helam, with Shobach the commander of Hadadezer’s army before them”: the letters of verse 16 can be read as “and their army came” (chelam), where verse 17 has a place, Chelamah, and the versions divide on it. The ASV and the Geneva have “and they came to Helam”; the NIV “they went to Helam”; the Douay “and brought over their army”. The Chronicles have no place, “came upon them” (1 Chronicles 19:17), and call the general Shophach. The Greek has Chalamak after the River and Ailam as the place. The ASV has “Shobach the captain of the host of Hadarezer at their head”; the TLB “Shobach, the commander-in-chief of all of Hadadezer’s forces”.

Seven hundred chariots. “And David gathered all Israel and crossed the Jordan … and David killed of Aram the men of seven hundred chariots and forty thousand horsemen; and Shobach the commander of their army he struck down, and he died there”: in verse 7 David sends Joab; now he goes himself, with all Israel. The Chronicles give “the men of seven thousand chariots, and forty thousand footmen” (1 Chronicles 19:18). The ASV, the KJV and the Douay have “the men of seven hundred chariots”; the Geneva “seven hundred chariots of the Aramites, and forty thousand horsemen”; the NWT 1984 “seven hundred charioteers and forty thousand horsemen”; the TLB “seven hundred charioteers dead on the field, also forty thousand cavalrymen”; and the NIV “forty thousand of their foot soldiers”, the Chronicles’ reading. The Greek has hippeōn, “horsemen”.

Made peace, and feared to save. “And all the kings, servants of Hadadezer, saw that they had been beaten before Israel, and they made peace with Israel and served them; and Aram feared to save the sons of Ammon any more”: Hadadezer’s own servants become Israel’s, as Aram of Damascus had in 8:6. The ASV has “made peace with Israel, and served them”; the NIV “they made peace with the Israelites and became subject to them”; the NWT 1984 “promptly made peace with Israel and began to serve them”; the TLB “they surrendered to David and became his servants”; the Douay “eight and fifty thousand men before Israel”, a number the Hebrew does not have. For the last clause the ASV has “feared to help the children of Ammon any more”; the NIV “afraid to help the Ammonites anymore”; the NWT 1984 “afraid to try saving the sons of”. The Hebrew has lehoshia, the verb of verse 11, and the Greek says that the kings “deserted” to Israel (ēutomolēsan). Ammon is left with no help and a gate to hold, and the chapter ends without taking it.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “do kindness with”, as in 2 Samuel 3:8 and 9:1; “comfort”; “a stench” for the verb of Genesis 34:30 and 1 Samuel 13:4; “greatly humiliated”; “hired”; “the face of the battle”; “picked men”; “salvation” and “save”, the root of 2 Samuel 8:6, 14; “Be strong, and let us be strong”; “what is good in his eyes”; “beyond the River”; “Helam”; “beaten before Israel”; “made peace”.

Echoes paid. David’s kindness for a father’s sake (2 Samuel 9:1, 3; 3:8); Nahash and Jabesh-gilead (1 Samuel 11:1–2; 12:12); the stench of Jacob, the foremen, Israel and Achish (Genesis 34:30; Exodus 5:21; 1 Samuel 13:4; 27:12); the hired razor beyond the River (Isaiah 7:20); the City of Palms (Judges 3:13; Deuteronomy 34:3); Beth-rehob and Laish (Judges 18:28); Jephthah in Tob (Judges 11:3, 5); Hadadezer at the Euphrates and “Jehovah saved David” (2 Samuel 8:3, 6, 14); Abner’s death and the sons of Zeruiah (2 Samuel 3:30; 2:18); Eli’s “what is good in his eyes” (1 Samuel 3:18); “merciful and gracious” (Exodus 34:6); Abraham and “beyond the River” (Joshua 24:3, 15).

Echoes planted. The Chronicles’ own account, with thirty-two thousand chariots, Shophach and the speech repeated (1 Chronicles 19:1–19); the stench again, said by Ahithophel to Absalom (2 Samuel 16:21); Machir’s neighbor Shobi son of Nahash at Mahanaim (2 Samuel 17:27); “humiliated” in the people’s return (2 Samuel 19:4); the buttocks uncovered in Isaiah 20:4; and the siege of Rabbah that picks the war up again (2 Samuel 11:1, now on these pages; 12:26–31). None of these is translated here yet.

Next in this book: 2 Samuel 11 — at the turn of the year David sends Joab and all Israel against Ammon and stays in Jerusalem; from his roof he sees a woman washing, and the chapter turns on Bathsheba, her husband Uriah, and a letter carried by Uriah’s own hand.
