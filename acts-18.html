Paul leaves Athens for Corinth and lodges and works with the tentmakers Aquila and Priscilla, lately come from Italy under Claudius’s order; Silas and Timothy come down from Macedonia, Paul testifies to the Jews that the Christ is Jesus, shakes out his garments and moves next door to Titius Justus, and Crispus the ruler of the synagogue believes with his household. After a night vision he settles a year and six months, until the Jews bring him before the proconsul Gallio, who dismisses the case and lets Sosthenes be beaten in front of the judgment seat. Paul sails with Priscilla and Aquila, has his head shorn at Cenchreae, leaves them at Ephesus, goes by Caesarea and Antioch through the Galatian country, and Apollos, taught more accurately by Priscilla and Aquila, crosses to Achaia.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are ‘a year and six months’ at Corinth (v11), ‘many days’ (v18) and ‘some time’ (v23). It follows Acts 17 at Athens. Claudius’s edict is reported to fall in AD 49 and an inscription at Delphi is reported to date Gallio’s term to AD 51–52; this library has not checked either and dates the events no more closely than the book does.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–4 · χωρισθεὶς … Κόρινθον … Ἀκύλαν, Ποντικὸν τῷ γένει … Πρίσκιλλαν … διατεταχέναι Κλαύδιον χωρίζεσθαι … σκηνοποιοὶ τῇ τέχνῃ … διελέγετο … ἔπειθέν chōristheis … Korinthon … Akylan, Pontikon tō genei … Priskillan … diatetachenai Klaudion chōrizesthai … skēnopoioi tē technē … dielegeto … epeithen — Corinth: the edict out of Rome, Aquila and Priscilla, a shared trade, and a synagogue every sabbath

“Departed,” and the edict that sent Aquila (Acts 18:1–2). Chōristheis, “having departed,” is the verb Luke gives the emperor’s order two lines later: Claudius had ordered chōrizesthai pantas tous Ioudaious apo tēs Rōmēs, “all the Jews to depart from Rome.” This translation keeps “depart” for both, so that Paul’s leaving Athens (17:34 is the last scene there) and the Jews’ leaving Rome are said with one word, and Corinth is where the two departures meet. The edict is known outside Luke. Suetonius, in his life of Claudius, writes Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit, “he expelled from Rome the Jews, who were constantly making disturbances at the instigation of Chrestus” (Claudius 25.4, read in the Latin). His sentence carries no year; AD 49 is often given for the edict, and that date is reported here and has not been checked. He does not say who Chrestus was; some have taken the name to be a spelling of “Christ,” and this page reports that reading and does not decide it. A second ancient account, also reported here and not read, is said to have Claudius closing the Jews’ meetings rather than expelling them. Luke calls Aquila only Ioudaion tina, “a certain Jew”: the verse does not say that he and Priscilla were believers when Paul found them, or that they became so through him. Pontikon tō genei, “a man of Pontus by birth,” and prosphatōs, “lately,” each stand in this verse alone in the New Testament; “from Italy” is the only word of how recent “lately” was.

Aquila and Priscilla (Acts 18:2, 18 and 26). The pair are named in six verses of the New Testament: here, at vv18 and 26 below, and in Romans 16:3, 1 Corinthians 16:19 and 2 Timothy 4:19 (the last three not yet on these pages). Luke writes Priscilla; Paul, in all three of his verses, writes Prisca. The order of the names moves: Aquila comes first here and at 1 Corinthians 16:19, and Priscilla first at v18, at v26 in the text printed here (Robinson–Pierpont reverses v26), at Romans 16:3 and at 2 Timothy 4:19, so that in the text printed here four of the six verses name the wife first. The Greek gives no reason for the order, and this page offers none.

Tentmakers (Acts 18:3). Homotechnon, “of the same trade,” and skēnopoioi, “tentmakers,” each stand in this verse alone in the New Testament. The Greek says the trade, not what a tentmaker made tents of, and this page adds nothing about it. The apparatus gives the verb as singular, ērgazeto, “he worked,” to Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and as plural, ērgazonto, “they worked,” to Westcott–Hort; and “by trade” is the dative tē technē in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the accusative tēn technēn in Robinson–Pierpont. Two versions on the shelf say “they”: ASV “he abode with them, and they wrought” and NWT 1984 “he stayed at their home, and they worked.” Two make Paul the worker: NIV “he stayed and worked with them” and TLB “Paul lived and worked with them.” KJV “he abode with them, and wrought,” Geneva “he abode with them and wrought” and Douay “he remained with them, and wrought” leave the number to the reader, as the English of “wrought” does.

“Reasoned” every sabbath, and “was persuading” (Acts 18:4). Dielegeto, “was reasoning,” is the verb of 17:2 and 17:17, and it closes the Corinth story at v19 below; it stands in ten verses of Acts, those three and 19:8, 19:9, 20:7, 20:9, 24:12 and 24:25 (the last six not yet on these pages). Epeithen is an imperfect, “was persuading” or “was trying to persuade,” and says nothing of whether he succeeded; at Thessalonica Luke used the passive of the same verb for the result, epeisthēsan, “were persuaded” (17:4), and the accusation of v13 below uses a compound of it. Here the audience is Ioudaious te kai Hellēnas, “both Jews and Greeks,” with no word for “worshiping” as in 17:4. KJV, ASV and Douay “reasoned in the synagogue every sabbath,” NIV “Every Sabbath he reasoned in the synagogue,” while Geneva has “disputed in the Synagogue every Sabbath day” and, for the second verb, “exhorted the Jews, and the Grecians,” NWT 1984 “would give a talk in the synagogue every sabbath” and TLB “trying to convince the Jews and Greeks alike.” The Douay alone of the seven adds that he reasoned “bringing in the name of the Lord Jesus”; those words stand in none of the editions in the apparatus, which carries no note at this verse, and the Douay translates the Vulgate, whose Latin this page has not checked.

Verses 5–8 · συνείχετο τῷ λόγῳ … εἶναι τὸν χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν … ἀντιτασσομένων … ἐκτιναξάμενος τὰ ἱμάτια … Τὸ αἷμα ὑμῶν ἐπὶ τὴν κεφαλὴν ὑμῶν … καθαρὸς ἐγώ … Τιτίου Ἰούστου … συνομοροῦσα … Κρίσπος ὁ ἀρχισυνάγωγος syneicheto tō logō … einai ton christon Iēsoun … antitassomenōn … ektinaxamenos ta himatia … to haima hymōn epi tēn kephalēn hymōn … katharos egō … Titiou Ioustou … synomorousa … Krispos ho archisynagōgos — Gripped by the word: “the Christ was Jesus,” shaken garments, “I am clean,” the house next door, and Crispus

“Gripped by the word” (Acts 18:5). Silas and Timothy “came down” (katēlthon) from Macedonia; at 17:14 both had stayed behind at Beroea, and at 17:15 the men who brought Paul to Athens took back a command for them to come to him as quickly as possible. The Greek does not say what they brought. Syneicheto is the passive of synechō, “to hold together, to press, to grip,” the verb of being held by something stronger than oneself: Simon’s mother-in-law “held by a great fever” (Luke 4:38), Jesus’s own “how I am pressed until it is accomplished” (Luke 12:50) and Paul’s “I am pressed from the two” (Philippians 1:23); this page keeps “gripped” for the verb here and treats the word as the thing that held him. The apparatus gives tō logō, “by the word,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and tō pneumati, “in the spirit,” to Robinson–Pierpont, and that is where the shelf splits: KJV “pressed in the spirit” and Geneva “forced in spirit” follow the second; ASV “constrained by the word,” NWT 1984 “intensely occupied with the word” and the Douay “earnest in preaching” keep the word or its sense; NIV “devoted himself exclusively to preaching” and TLB “spent his full time preaching” make it a matter of how Paul spent his days.

“That the Christ was Jesus” (Acts 18:5). The Greek reads einai ton christon Iēsoun, “the Christ to be Jesus,” with the Christ first and Jesus last, and this page keeps that order, which makes the Christ the figure the Jews were expecting and Jesus the answer; the same four Greek words stand in one other verse of the New Testament, the last of this chapter (v28), where Apollos does the same showing from the Scriptures. Robinson–Pierpont omits einai, “to be,” and so leaves “the Christ Jesus” with no verb. The shelf reverses the order, all seven of its versions: KJV “Jesus was Christ,” ASV “Jesus was the Christ,” Geneva “Jesus was the Christ,” Douay “Jesus is the Christ,” NWT 1984 “Jesus is the Christ,” NIV “Jesus was the Messiah” and TLB “Jesus is the Messiah.” At Thessalonica the sentence was “This is the Christ — the Jesus whom I proclaim to you” (17:3), the same two names in the same order. Diamartyromenos, “solemnly testifying,” is the verb of 8:25 and 10:42.

Opposition, and the shaken garments (Acts 18:6). Antitassomenōn, “setting themselves in array against,” is a military word of five verses of the New Testament: here, Romans 13:2, James 4:6, 5:6 and 1 Peter 5:5 (the last four not yet on these pages). Blasphēmountōn has no object in the Greek, and the shelf supplies one in one place: KJV “opposed themselves, and blasphemed,” ASV “opposed themselves and blasphemed,” Geneva “resisted and blasphemed,” Douay “gainsaying and blaspheming,” NIV “became abusive,” NWT 1984 “speaking abusively,” and TLB “hurling abuse at Jesus.” Ektinaxamenos ta himatia, “having shaken out his garments,” uses ektinassō, a verb of four verses of the New Testament: Matthew 10:14, Mark 6:11, Acts 13:51, where Paul and Barnabas shake off “the dust of their feet,” and here (dictionary). The Greek says garments and no dust, and the shelf follows it except in two places: KJV “shook his raiment,” ASV “shook out his raiment,” Geneva “shook his raiment,” Douay “shook his garments,” NWT 1984 “shook out his garments,” while the NIV adds a motive, “shook out his clothes in protest,” and the TLB an object, “shook off the dust from his robe.”

“Your blood be on your own heads. I am clean.” The Greek has the singular, epi tēn kephalēn hymōn, “on your head,” and this page puts it into the plural English needs for several hearers; Geneva keeps the singular, “Your blood be upon your own head,” and the KJV, ASV and Douay “Your blood be upon your own heads,” NIV “Your blood be on your own heads!” and TLB “Your blood be upon your own heads.” The Hebrew phrase behind the idiom, damkha al roshekha, “your blood be on your head,” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, David’s sentence on the man who said he had killed Saul (2 Samuel 1:16); and damo berosho, “his blood on his head,” stands in two, Joshua 2:19, where Rahab tells the spies that whoever leaves the house is responsible for his own blood, and Ezekiel 33:4 (not yet on these pages). That is what “I am clean” (katharos egō) answers: Paul declares himself free of the consequence. He says it again in Acts 20:26, “I am clean from the blood of all” (not yet on these pages). “From now on I will go to the nations” is the sentence of 13:46, “we are turning to the nations,” said again in another city, and it is as local as the first: after 13:46 and 13:51 Paul and Barnabas go into the next synagogue at Iconium (14:1), and within this chapter Paul “entered the synagogue and reasoned with the Jews” at Ephesus (v19). NWT 1984 “people of the nations”; KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and TLB all print “Gentiles,” six of the seven.

Titius Justus, and the house next door (Acts 18:7). Metabas ekeithen, “having moved on from there,” does not say from where: ekeithen can be the synagogue or the house of Aquila and Priscilla, and the verse does not say whether Paul moved his lodging, his place of teaching, or both. NIV decides it with “Paul left the synagogue and went next door,” and the NWT 1984 keeps the Greek: “he transferred from there.” The man is Titiou Ioustou: the apparatus gives Titiou, a Latin name, Titius, to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and omits it from Robinson–Pierpont, so that KJV and Geneva read “named Justus”; NIV and NWT 1984 print “Titius Justus,” while ASV, Douay and TLB print “Titus Justus,” and Titos, “Titus,” is a different name in Greek. Justus stands in three verses of the New Testament, naming three men as far as the verses show: Joseph called Justus at 1:23, this man, and Colossians 4:11 (not yet on these pages). He is sebomenou ton theon, “worshiping God” (dictionary), the participle that stands in six verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 13:43, 13:50, 16:14, 17:4, 17:17 and here. The accusation of v13 turns on the same verb and the same object, sebesthai ton theon, “to worship God.” KJV, ASV and Douay “one that worshipped God,” Geneva “a worshipper of God,” NIV and NWT 1984 “a worshiper of God,” and the TLB “a Gentile who worshiped God,” where the word “Gentile” is its own. Synomorousa, “bordering on,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament: KJV, ASV and Geneva “joined hard to the synagogue,” Douay and NWT 1984 “adjoining,” TLB “next door to the synagogue.”

Crispus, and “believed the Lord” (Acts 18:8). Archisynagōgos, “ruler of the synagogue,” is the title of Crispus here and of Sosthenes at v17: two men with one title in one city, and Luke does not say whether they held it together or one after the other. Crispus is named in one other verse of the New Testament, where Paul says he baptized “none of you except Crispus and Gaius” (1 Corinthians 1:14); Acts says only that “many of the Corinthians, hearing, were believing and being baptized” (imperfects: episteuon kai ebaptizonto) and does not say who baptized them. Episteusen tō kyriō has the dative, “believed the Lord,” as 16:34 has “having believed God” of the jailer; all seven versions on the shelf say “in the Lord,” and the KJV “believed on the Lord.” He believes syn holō tō oikō autou, “with his whole household,” as the household of Cornelius is said to fear God “with all his household” (10:2), Lydia’s is baptized (16:15) and the jailer is told “you and your household” (16:31). KJV “chief ruler of the synagogue,” Geneva “chief ruler,” ASV and Douay “ruler of the synagogue,” NIV “synagogue leader,” NWT 1984 “presiding officer of the synagogue,” TLB “leader of the synagogue.”

Verses 9–11 · ἐν νυκτὶ διʼ ὁράματος … Μὴ φοβοῦ … διότι ἐγώ εἰμι μετὰ σοῦ … ἐπιθήσεταί σοι τοῦ κακῶσαί σε … λαός ἐστί μοι πολύς … ἐκάθισεν … ἐνιαυτὸν καὶ μῆνας ἓξ en nykti di’ horamatos … Mē phobou … dioti egō eimi meta sou … epithēsetai soi tou kakōsai se … laos esti moi polys … ekathisen … eniauton kai mēnas hex — “I am with you”: a night vision, “I have a numerous people in this city,” and a year and six months

A vision in the night, and “I am with you” (Acts 18:9–10). This is the second night vision in Paul’s story since the Macedonian man at Troas (16:9, horama dia nyktos), and here Luke does not say who stands in it: ho kyrios, “the Lord,” speaks, and Crispus has just “believed the Lord” (v8). This page keeps “the Lord” in both verses and does not decide the referent. Three commands, mē phobou, alla lalei kai mē siōpēsēs, “do not be afraid, but speak, and do not be silent,” are given two reasons, each introduced by dioti, “because”: “I am with you” and “I have a numerous people in this city.” “Do not be afraid, for I am with you” is old speech in Jehovah’s mouth: to Isaac (Genesis 26:24), to Joshua (Joshua 1:9, “Jehovah your God is with you wherever you go”) and to Jeremiah, “Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you” (Jeremiah 1:8). The Hebrew clause ki ittekha ani, “for I am with you,” stands in six verses of the Hebrew Bible, Isaiah 43:5, Jeremiah 1:8, 1:19, 15:20, 30:11 and 46:28 (Isaiah 43, Jeremiah 15 and Jeremiah 46 not yet on these pages); Genesis 26:24 has the fuller ittekha anokhi. Luke does not say that the words are drawn from any of them; this page reports the pattern and does not claim a quotation.

“Set upon you to harm you” (Acts 18:10). Epithēsetai soi tou kakōsai se, with oudeis before it as the subject, is a promise of freedom from harm, and it names no enemy. Kakoō, “to harm, to mistreat,” stands in five verses of Acts: 7:6, 7:19, 12:1, 14:2 and this one. At 12:1 the infinitive is the same, kakōsai tinas, “to harm some of those from the congregation,” and the subject there is a king who lays hands on them; here the subject is the negative pronoun oudeis and no person is named. In the verses that follow, the Jews rise against Paul and bring him to the judgment seat (v12), and it is Sosthenes who is beaten (v17); Luke does not connect the promise to either. KJV “no man shall set on thee to hurt thee,” ASV “no man shall set on thee to harm thee,” Douay “no man shall set upon thee, to hurt thee,” Geneva “no man shall lay hands on thee to hurt thee,” NIV “going to attack and harm you,” NWT 1984 “no man will assault you so as to do you injury,” TLB “can harm you.”

“A numerous people.” Laos, “people” (dictionary), is Scripture’s word for Israel as the people of God, and here it is said of a Greek city, with the verb in the present: laos esti moi polys, “there is to me a numerous people.” It is the word of Acts 15:14, where God “concerned himself with taking from the nations a people for his name.” The verse does not say whether the people it means are those who have already believed (v8 reports a household and “many of the Corinthians”) or those still to come, and this page does not decide. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “I have much people in this city,” NIV and NWT 1984 “I have many people in this city,” and the TLB turns the possession around, “Many people here in this city belong to me.”

A year and six months (Acts 18:11). Ekathisen is the verb “to sit,” and in Greek it is also the verb of staying put and of a teacher’s posture: it is what Jesus said the disciples were to do in the city (kathisate en tē polei, Luke 24:49) before they were clothed with power. This page keeps “settled.” The length, eniauton kai mēnas hex, “a year and months six,” is a year and a half; the verse says he taught “among them,” en autois, and gives the length of the stay, not where in it the hearing of v12 fell. KJV “continued there a year and six months,” ASV “dwelt there a year and six months,” NIV “a year and a half,” NWT 1984 “stayed set there a year and six months,” TLB “stayed there the next year and a half.”

Verses 12–17 · Γαλλίωνος … ἀνθυπάτου … κατεπέστησαν ὁμοθυμαδὸν … βῆμα … ἀναπείθει … ῥᾳδιούργημα πονηρόν … κατὰ λόγον … περὶ λόγου … ὄψεσθε αὐτοί … κριτὴς … Σωσθένην … οὐδὲν τούτων ἔμελεν Galliōnos … anthypatou … katepestēsan homothymadon … bēma … anapeithei … rhadiourgēma ponēron … kata logon … peri logou … opsesthe autoi … kritēs … Sōsthenēn … ouden toutōn emelen — Gallio: the proconsul, “with reason” and “about a word,” and the beating in front of the judgment seat

The proconsul, and “with one accord” (Acts 18:12). Anthypatos, “proconsul,” stands in five verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 13:7, 13:8 and 13:12, of Sergius Paulus on Cyprus, here, of Gallio, and 19:38 (not yet on these pages), where the town clerk of Ephesus says that proconsuls “are available.” Suetonius says that Claudius “gave back to the senate the provinces of Achaia and Macedonia, which Tiberius had taken over to his own care” (provincias Achaiam et Macedoniam, quas Tiberius ad curam suam transtulerat, senatui reddidit, Claudius 25.3, read in the Latin); the governor Luke names is the senator’s title, anthypatos, over Achaia. The apparatus gives the noun phrase anthypatou ontos, “being proconsul,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the single participle anthypateuontos to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV and Geneva print “deputy,” ASV, Douay, NIV and NWT 1984 “proconsul,” and TLB “governor.” Luke gives no year; an inscription found at Delphi is reported to date Gallio’s term to AD 51–52, and this page has not read it. He is reported to have been the brother of the philosopher Seneca; that, too, is reported here and not checked. Homothymadon, “with one accord,” stands in eleven verses of the New Testament, ten in Acts (1:14, 2:46, 4:24, 5:12, 7:57, 8:6, 12:20, 15:25, this verse and 19:29) and Romans 15:6 (dictionary); it is said of the believers at prayer and of the crowd that rushed on Stephen, and here of the Jews against Paul. Katepestēsan, “rose up against,” stands in this verse alone: KJV “made insurrection,” ASV and Douay “rose up,” NWT 1984 “rose up with one accord,” NIV “made a united attack” and TLB “rose in concerted action.”

The judgment seat (Acts 18:12, 16 and 17). Bēma stands in eight verses of Acts: 7:5, where it is only the measure of a foot’s breadth, 12:21, where Herod sits on it to make his public speech, three times in this passage, and 25:6, 25:10 and 25:17 (the last three not yet on these pages). KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 keep “judgment seat” at v12; the NIV has “place of judgment” there and at v17 loses the word, “in front of the proconsul,” and the TLB has “before the governor” at v12 and “the courtroom” at vv16–17.

“Contrary to the law” (Acts 18:13). Anapeithei, “is talking into,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, a compound of peithō, the verb of “was persuading” at v4: what Paul does in the synagogue and what the accusers say he does are the same root, one with an object and one with a charge attached. Sebesthai ton theon, “to worship God,” is the phrase of v7 for Titius Justus, sebomenou ton theon. Para ton nomon, “contrary to the law,” does not say whose law; Gallio’s answer, nomou tou kath’ hymas, “the law that is yours,” takes it as theirs. The apparatus gives anapeithei houtos to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and houtos anapeithei to Robinson–Pierpont, an order only. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “persuadeth men,” NIV “persuading the people,” NWT 1984 “leads men to another persuasion in worshiping God,” and the TLB decides whose law it is: “contrary to Roman law.”

“With reason,” “about a word,” and no judge (Acts 18:14–16). Rhadiourgēma ponēron, “wicked villainy,” stands in this verse alone, and its root-mate rhadiourgia, “villainy,” stands in one other, 13:10, where Paul calls Elymas “full of all deceit and all villainy” before the proconsul Sergius Paulus: the only two verses of the New Testament with the root are both spoken in a proconsul’s presence. KJV “wrong or wicked lewdness,” ASV “wrong or of wicked villany,” NWT 1984 “wrong or a wicked act of villainy,” Douay “injustice, or an heinous deed,” Geneva “wrong, or an evil deed,” NIV “misdemeanor or serious crime” and TLB “a case involving some crime.” Gallio’s answer is built on one noun used twice: kata logon, “with reason” (v14), and peri logou, “about a word” (v15), both logos, and this page keeps the two as “reason” and “word” because that is all the Greek gives. The shelf makes the second plural or something else: KJV, ASV, Geneva and NIV “words and names,” Douay “word and names,” NWT 1984 “speech and names” and TLB “semantics and personalities.” The apparatus gives zētēmata, “questions,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the singular zētēma, “a question,” to Robinson–Pierpont, and it gives Robinson–Pierpont a gar, “for,” before the last clause; KJV “if it be a question of words and names, and of your law, look ye to it; for I will be no judge,” and Geneva “if it be a question of words and names” and “for I will be no judge” follow it, while the ASV “if they are questions about words and names” and the NIV “since it involves questions about words and names” follow the others. Opsesthe autoi, “you will see to it yourselves,” is the sentence the chief priests give Judas in Matthew 27:4 (sy opsē, “you see to it”) and Pilate gives the crowd in 27:24 (hymeis opsesthe). Kritēs, “judge,” is the word of 10:42, of the one “determined by God as judge of the living and the dead,” and stands in four verses of Acts (10:42, 13:20, this one and 24:10); Gallio says that he does not wish to be one, a kritēs of these things. Apēlasen, “drove away,” stands in this verse alone.

Sosthenes (Acts 18:17). The Greek says epilabomenoi de pantes, “and all, seizing,” and does not say who “all” were. The apparatus gives Robinson–Pierpont pantes hoi Hellēnes, “all the Greeks,” and the shelf shows the split: KJV “all the Greeks” and Geneva “all the Grecians” follow it; ASV and NWT 1984 “they all,” Douay “all laying hold,” NIV “the crowd there” and TLB “the mob” read the shorter text or supply a noun of their own. Whether the men who beat the ruler of the synagogue were Jews turning on their own leader, or Greeks, or a crowd, the Greek of the printed text does not say, and this page does not decide. Sosthenes is the second man at Corinth called archisynagōgos (v8), and the name stands in one other verse of the New Testament, where “Sosthenes the brother” is named with Paul in the first line of 1 Corinthians (1 Corinthians 1:1); Luke does not say that they are the same man. The last clause is kai ouden toutōn tō Galliōni emelen, “and none of these things mattered to Gallio.” Melei is the verb of “Teacher, does it not matter to you that we are perishing?” (Mark 4:38), and the clause does not say that Gallio watched the beating and approved; it says that none of the three, the charge, the dispute or the blows, was his concern. KJV and Douay “cared for none of those things,” ASV “cared for none of these things,” Geneva “cared nothing for those things,” NIV “showed no concern whatever,” NWT 1984 “would not concern himself at all” and TLB “couldn’t have cared less.” The apparatus gives emelen to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and emellen to Robinson–Pierpont.

Verses 18–23 · ἔτι προσμείνας ἡμέρας ἱκανάς … κειράμενος ἐν Κεγχρεαῖς τὴν κεφαλήν, εἶχεν γὰρ εὐχήν … διελέξατο … τοῦ θεοῦ θέλοντος … κατελθών … ἀναβάς … κατέβη … Γαλατικὴν χώραν καὶ Φρυγίαν … στηρίζων eti prosmeinas hēmeras hikanas … keiramenos en Kenchreais tēn kephalēn, eichen gar euchēn … dielexato … tou theou thelontos … katelthōn … anabas … katebē … Galatikēn chōran kai Phrygian … stērizōn — Cenchreae: whose head, and what vow; “God willing”; “gone up,” to where; and the Galatian country again

Whose head was shorn? (Acts 18:18). Paul stays on hēmeras hikanas, “many days,” takes leave of the brothers and sails for Syria, and with him go Priscilla and Aquila; then comes keiramenos en Kenchreais tēn kephalēn, eichen gar euchēn, “having had his head shorn at Cenchreae, for he had a vow.” The participle is masculine singular and stands right after the names “Priscilla and Aquila,” while Paul is the subject of the sentence, and the verb eichen, “he had,” carries no subject of its own: the Greek does not say whose head or whose vow, and this page keeps its order and its ambiguity. The shelf mostly keeps it too: KJV “having shorn his head in Cenchrea: for he had a vow,” ASV “having shorn his head in Cenchreae; for he had a vow,” Douay “having shorn his head in Cenchrae: for he had a vow,” Geneva “after that he had shorn his head in Cenchrea: for he had made a vow” and NWT 1984 “as he had the hair of his head clipped short” — “for he had a vow.” Two decide it: NIV “Before he sailed, he had his hair cut off at Cenchreae because of a vow he had taken,” which moves the clause ahead of the sailing, and the TLB “At Cenchreae Paul had his head shaved according to Jewish custom, for he had taken a vow,” which names Paul and adds the custom. The verb is keirō, which stands in three verses of the New Testament: Acts 8:32, of a sheep before its shearer, here, and 1 Corinthians 11:6 (not yet on these pages). Cenchreae is a place Paul names in Romans 16:1, “Phoebe … a servant of the congregation in Cenchreae” (not yet on these pages). Euchē, “vow,” stands in three verses of the New Testament (dictionary): here, Acts 21:23, where four men “having a vow” are to have their heads shaved (xyrēsontai tēn kephalēn, 21:24, not yet on these pages), and James 5:15. The Torah’s vow with a head attached is the Nazirite’s (Numbers 6:2), who “shall shave his consecrated head at the door of the tent of meeting” when the days end (6:18). Luke does not say “Nazirite,” does not say what Paul or Aquila had vowed, and does not say whether the days were ending at Cenchreae; this page does not decide.

Ephesus, a request refused, and “God willing” (Acts 18:19–21). At Ephesus Paul leaves Priscilla and Aquila (katelipen, “left them behind”) and goes alone into the synagogue, where he dielexato, “reasoned”: the verb that began the Corinth story at v4 closes it. The apparatus gives katēntēsan, “they reached,” and dielexato to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and the singular katēntēsen, “he reached,” and dielechthē to Robinson–Pierpont; the sense is the same. When they ask him epi pleiona chronon meinai, “to remain for a longer time,” he does not consent (epeneusen, a verb that stands in this verse alone in the New Testament); Robinson–Pierpont adds par’ autois, “with them,” and KJV “tarry longer time with them” and Geneva “tarry a longer time with them” have the words, as the NIV does in “spend more time with them.” At v21 the apparatus is longer: Robinson–Pierpont has Paul say Dei me pantōs tēn heortēn tēn erchomenēn poiēsai eis Hierosolyma, “I must by all means keep the coming feast at Jerusalem,” and has all’ apetaxato autois for the printed alla apotaxamenos kai, “but taking leave and.” Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have neither, and neither is in the text printed here. Three of the seven versions on the shelf carry the feast: KJV “I must by all means keep this feast that cometh in Jerusalem,” Geneva “I must needs keep this feast that cometh, in Jerusalem,” and TLB “I must by all means be at Jerusalem for the holiday”; the ASV “I will return again unto you if God will,” Douay “I will return to you again, God willing,” NIV “I will come back if it is God’s will” and NWT 1984 “I will return to YOU again” do not. This page’s “God willing” renders tou theou thelontos, and the Greek reads theos, “God”: KJV, ASV and Geneva “if God will,” TLB “if God permitted,” while the NWT 1984 prints the name, “if Jehovah is willing,” where the Greek has theou. Paul uses the same thought of the Lord (kyrios) in 1 Corinthians 4:19, “if the Lord wills,” and 16:7, “if the Lord permits” (neither yet on these pages).

“Come down,” “gone up,” “went down” (Acts 18:22). The Greek is katelthōn eis Kaisareian, anabas kai aspasamenos tēn ekklēsian, katebē eis Antiocheian, three movements in one verse, and this page keeps the up-and-down words. No city is named for the “going up.” At 11:2, 15:2 and 21:15 (the last not yet on these pages) anabainō is a going up to Jerusalem, and Jerusalem is named in each of those sentences; here it is not, and the shelf supplies it in four of seven versions: NIV “he went up to Jerusalem and greeted the church,” Geneva “he went up to Jerusalem,” Douay “he went up to Jerusalem” and TLB “visited the church at Jerusalem,” where KJV “gone up, and saluted the church,” ASV “he went up and saluted the church” and NWT 1984 “he went up and greeted the congregation” leave it out. Tēn ekklēsian, “the congregation” (dictionary), is not named either. Antioch is the city from which the first journey was sent out (13:1) and to which it came back (14:26), and “for Syria” in v18 points the same way; the verse itself says only “Antioch.”

The Galatian country again, and “strengthening” (Acts 18:23). Galatikēn chōran, “the Galatian country,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, 16:6 and here, with the order of the pair reversed: there “Phrygia and the Galatian country,” here “the Galatian country and Phrygia” (Galatia, Phrygia). Kathexēs, “in order,” stands in five verses of the New Testament, all Luke’s: Luke 1:3, Luke 8:1, Acts 3:24, 11:4 and here. The apparatus gives stērizōn to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles and the compound epistērizōn to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. The compound is the verb of 14:22, where Paul and Barnabas strengthen “the souls of the disciples,” and of 15:41, where Paul passes through Syria and Cilicia strengthening the congregations (dictionary); in Acts a form of the verb stands in four verses, 14:22, 15:32, 15:41 and this one. Jesus’s own use is the command to Peter, “strengthen your brothers” (Luke 22:32). This page prints “strengthening” for either form: KJV, Geneva, NIV and NWT 1984 “strengthening all the disciples,” ASV “establishing all the disciples,” Douay “confirming all the disciples,” and TLB “encouraging them and helping them grow in the Lord.”

Verses 24–28 · Ἀπολλῶς … Ἀλεξανδρεὺς τῷ γένει … ἀνὴρ λόγιος … δυνατὸς ἐν ταῖς γραφαῖς … κατηχημένος … ζέων τῷ πνεύματι … ἀκριβῶς … μόνον τὸ βάπτισμα Ἰωάννου … παρρησιάζεσθαι … προσελάβοντο … ἀκριβέστερον … συνεβάλετο … εὐτόνως διακατηλέγχετο Apollōs … Alexandreus tō genei … anēr logios … dynatos en tais graphais … katēchēmenos … zeōn tō pneumati … akribōs … monon to baptisma Iōannou … parrhēsiazesthai … proselabonto … akribesteron … synebaleto … eutonōs diakatēlegcheto — Apollos: “eloquent” or “learned,” “accurately” and “more accurately,” and “the Christ was Jesus” again

Apollos, “an eloquent man” (Acts 18:24). Apollos is named in ten verses of the New Testament: here and 19:1, seven in 1 Corinthians (1:12, 3:4, 3:5, 3:6, 3:22, 4:6 and 16:12) and Titus 3:13 (of the Pauline verses only the first, 1:12, is on these pages). Alexandreus tō genei, “an Alexandrian by birth,” has the same shape as Aquila’s Pontikon tō genei at v2, and “Alexandrians” stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and 6:9, among the men of a synagogue who rose to dispute with Stephen (Alexandria). Logios stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and comes from logos, “word”: it can mean skilled in words or learned, and the shelf splits on the two. KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 “an eloquent man,” five of the seven; NIV “a learned man”; TLB “a wonderful Bible teacher and preacher.” This page prints “eloquent” and says that the Greek does not choose. Dynatos en tais graphais, “powerful in the Scriptures,” uses the adjective Luke gives to Moses, “powerful in words and deeds” (dynatos en logois kai ergois, Acts 7:22), and to Jesus, “powerful in deed and word” (Luke 24:19). KJV “mighty in the scriptures,” ASV “mighty in the scriptures,” Geneva “mighty in the Scriptures,” Douay “mighty in the scriptures,” NIV “a thorough knowledge of the Scriptures” and NWT 1984 “well versed in the Scriptures.”

“Instructed in the way of the Lord,” and “accurately” (Acts 18:25). Katēchēmenos, “instructed,” is the verb behind English “catechize” and stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Luke 1:4, this verse, Acts 21:21, 21:24, Romans 2:18, 1 Corinthians 14:19 and Galatians 6:6 (the last five not yet on these pages). Akribōs, “accurately,” is Luke’s word in his preface, where he has followed everything akribōs kathexēs, “accurately, in order,” so that Theophilus may know the “words” in which he was katēchēthēs, “instructed” (Luke 1:3–4); all three of those words, akribōs, kathexēs and katēchēmenos, are in this chapter, the second at v23 and the others in this verse. Tēn hodon tou kyriou, “the way of the Lord” (dictionary), is the phrase that Luke 3:4 quotes from Isaiah for John the Baptist, Hetoimasate tēn hodon kyriou, “prepare the way of the Lord” (Luke 3:4, from Isaiah 40:3), and Apollos “knew only the baptism of John.” Here there is no quotation, and this page keeps “the Lord” and does not decide whether the Greek means Jesus or Jehovah; in v26 the same road is “the way of God.” “The Way” is also the name of the movement itself at 9:2, and again at 19:9, 19:23, 22:4, 24:14 and 24:22 (the last five not yet on these pages). KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NIV “instructed in the way of the Lord,” NWT 1984 “the way of Jehovah.” Zeōn tō pneumati, literally “boiling in the spirit,” uses zeō (dictionary), which stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Romans 12:11 (not yet on these pages). The Greek has the article but not the word “holy,” so that it is not said whether the spirit is Apollos’s own or the Spirit of God, and the shelf splits even in its capital letters: Geneva “fervently in the Spirit,” KJV “fervent in the spirit,” ASV and Douay “fervent in spirit,” NWT 1984 “aglow with the spirit,” NIV “with great fervor,” TLB “enthusiastically.” The apparatus gives “the things about Jesus,” ta peri tou Iēsou, to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and “the things of the Lord,” ta tou kyriou, to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “taught diligently the things of the Lord” and Geneva “taught diligently the things of the Lord” have the second; ASV “the things concerning Jesus,” Douay “the things that are of Jesus,” NIV “taught about Jesus accurately” and NWT 1984 “the things about Jesus” have the first. The adverb itself comes out as ASV “taught accurately,” NIV “accurately,” NWT 1984 “with correctness” and KJV, Geneva and Douay “taught diligently.” The verse sets “accurately” beside epistamenos monon to baptisma Iōannou, “knowing only the baptism of John” (1:22, 10:37 and 13:24 have it; 19:3 not yet on these pages); Luke does not say here what was missing, and he does not say that Apollos was baptized again.

“More accurately” (Acts 18:26). Parrhēsiazesthai, “to speak boldly,” stands in seven verses of Acts: 9:27, 9:28, 13:46, 14:3, this verse, 19:8 and 26:26 (the last two not yet on these pages) (dictionary). What they did is exethento autō tēn hodon tou theou akribesteron, “set out to him the way of God more accurately”; ektithēmi is the verb of Peter who “set it out to them in orderly sequence” (11:4), and akribesteron is the comparative of v25’s akribōs. The comparative stands also at 23:15, 23:20 and 24:22, where Felix is “knowing more accurately the things about the Way” (all three not yet on these pages). Proselabonto, “took to themselves,” is the verb of the marketplace men “taking along” the rabble at 17:5 and of Peter “taking him aside” (Matthew 16:22); the Greek does not say that Apollos was taken to a house, and the shelf is divided: KJV, ASV and Geneva “took him unto them,” Douay “took him to them,” NWT 1984 “took him into their company,” NIV “invited him to their home” and TLB “met with him.” The names are in the order Priskilla kai Akylas in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and Akylas kai Priskilla in Robinson–Pierpont: KJV and Geneva “Aquila and Priscilla”; ASV, Douay, NIV and TLB “Priscilla and Aquila,”. The comparative itself comes out as ASV “more accurately,” NWT 1984 “more correctly,” KJV and Geneva “more perfectly,” NIV “more adequately,” Douay “more diligently,” and the Douay prints “the way of the Lord” where all four editions in the apparatus read “of God”; the Douay translates the Vulgate, and this page has not checked the Latin. The TLB supplies the lesson itself, “explained what had happened to Jesus since the time of John,” which Luke does not say.

A letter to Achaia, and “through the grace” (Acts 18:27). Protrepsamenoi, “having urged him on,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the brothers write tois mathētais apodexasthai auton, “to the disciples to welcome him”; Luke records the letter and not its words. Apodechomai, “to welcome,” stands in five verses of Acts: 2:41, this one, 21:17, 24:3 and 28:30 (the last three not yet on these pages). Synebaleto (dictionary) is the verb of “throwing together,” and in the New Testament it stands in six verses: Luke 2:19, where Mary “pondered” the things in her heart, Luke 14:31, of a king who “meets” another in war, Acts 4:15, of the council “conferring,” 17:18, of the philosophers “engaging” Paul, here, “helped,” and 20:14, where Paul “met” the others at Assos. The last phrase, dia tēs charitos (dictionary), “through the grace,” can go with the helping or with the believing, and this page keeps the Greek order, synebaleto polu tois pepisteukosin dia tēs charitos, and does not decide. Five of the seven versions on the shelf put it with the believing: KJV “helped them much which had believed through grace,” ASV “helped them much that had believed through grace,” Geneva “helped them much which had believed through grace,” NIV “a great help to those who by grace had believed,” and NWT 1984 “greatly helped those who had believed on account of [God’s] undeserved kindness,” with the bracket marking the supplied word. The Douay “helped them much who had believed” has no “grace” in the verse, and the TLB “he was greatly used of God to strengthen the church” turns the whole phrase into something else.

“That the Christ was Jesus,” again (Acts 18:28). Eutonōs, “vigorously,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and of the chief priests and scribes “vehemently accusing” Jesus before Pilate (Luke 23:10). Diakatēlegcheto, “was refuting thoroughly,” stands in this verse alone. The last clause, epideiknys dia tōn graphōn einai ton christon Iēsoun, “showing through the Scriptures that the Christ was Jesus,” ends the chapter with the four Greek words of v5, where Paul testifies the same thing to the Jews of Corinth, and gives them now to another man in another province, with the Scriptures as the proof. KJV “mightily convinced the Jews, and that publickly,” ASV “powerfully confuted the Jews, and that publicly,” Geneva “mightily he confuted publicly the Jews,” Douay “with much vigour he convinced the Jews openly,” NWT 1984 “thoroughly proved the Jews to be wrong,” NIV “vigorously refuted his Jewish opponents in public debate” and TLB “powerfully refuted all the Jewish arguments in public debate,” the last two adding the word “debate.” The verse does not say which Jews they were, or where.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “depart” for chōrizō at vv1 and 2, so that Paul’s leaving Athens and the Jews’ leaving Rome are one word; “reasoned” for dialegomai at vv4 and 19, so that the verb that opens the Corinth story closes it; “gripped” for synechō at v5; “the Christ was Jesus” at vv5 and 28, in the order of the Greek; “the nations” at v6, as at 13:46; “worshiping God” at v7 and “to worship God” at v13, one verb and one object on both sides of the chapter; “believed the Lord” at v8, with the dative; “a numerous people” for laos at v10; “proconsul” and “judgment seat” at vv12, 16 and 17, one word each; “reason” and “word” for logos at vv14 and 15; “head shorn” at v18, with no subject chosen; “God willing” for tou theou thelontos at v21, with “God” kept for theos; “come down,” “gone up” and “went down” at v22; “instructed” and “accurately” at v25, and “more accurately” at v26.

Echoes paid. “I will go to the nations” — 13:46, at v6. The shaken-out gesture — 13:51, at v6. “Reasoned” — 17:2 and 17:17, at vv4 and 19. The household that believes — 10:2, 16:15 and 16:31, at v8. A vision in the night — 16:9, at v9. “A people for his name” — 15:14, at v10. The proconsul — 13:7, at v12. “Villainy” — 13:10, at v14. The judgment seat — 12:21, at vv12, 16 and 17. The judge of the living and the dead — 10:42, at v15. “Strengthening the congregations” — 14:22 and 15:41, at v23. The Galatian country — 16:6, at v23. “Powerful in words” — 7:22 and Luke 24:19, at v24. Luke’s preface — Luke 1:3–4, at vv23 and 25. The baptism of John — 1:22, 10:37 and 13:24, at v25. “Engaged” — 17:18, at v27.

Echoes planted. Apollos at Corinth and Ephesus, at 19:1. The baptism of John, at 19:3. “The Way”, at 19:9, 19:23, 22:4, 24:14 and 24:22, where Felix is “knowing more accurately the things about the Way.” “Reasoned,” at 19:8, 19:9, 20:7, 20:9, 24:12 and 24:25. The proconsuls, at 19:38. “With one accord,” at 19:29. “I am clean from the blood of all,” at 20:26. A vow and a shaved head, at 21:23–24. The judgment seat, at 25:6, 25:10 and 25:17. Three of this chapter’s people in the first chapter of 1 Corinthians: Sosthenes at 1:1, Apollos at 1:12 and Crispus at 1:14.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. What Claudius’s edict was, and when it fell, which the library reports from Suetonius’s one sentence and does not date. Whether “Chrestus” is Christ. When Gallio’s term fell, which an inscription at Delphi is reported to fix. Who beat Sosthenes, and whether he is the Sosthenes of 1 Corinthians 1:1. Whether Crispus and Sosthenes held the one office together or in turn. Whose head was shorn at Cenchreae, and what the vow was. Which feast, if any, Paul named at v21. Where “gone up” led at v22, and which congregation was greeted. Whether logios means eloquent or learned. Whether “in spirit” at v25 is Apollos’s own or the Spirit of God. Whether “through the grace” at v27 belongs to the helping or to the believing.
