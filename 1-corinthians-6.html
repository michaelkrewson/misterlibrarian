The same letter, still from Ephesus. Paul turns from the case inside the congregation to lawsuits between believers, heard before the civil courts of the city. Acts 18:12–17 has the Corinthian courts at work, with Gallio’s tribunal and the beating of Sosthenes before it, the proconsul who would not hear a dispute over the law.
c. AD 54–55, the same letter and date as chapters 1 to 5, written from Ephesus. Nothing in Acts dates these lawsuits; the chapter gives no date of its own.

Verses 1–4 · going to law before the unrighteous, and the holy ones who judge

“Does anyone among you dare.” Tolmā̂, dares, is a bold verb, and pragma echōn, having a matter, is the phrase for a legal dispute in hand. Paul uses the same word for the lawsuit that he will name in verse 6. The court is epi tōn adikōn, before the unrighteous, meaning unbelieving judges, against epi tōn hagiōn, before the holy ones. The holy ones are the members of the congregation, the word of 1:2, “called to be holy ones.” Hagios is the word that calls the believers saints, and the translation keeps “holy ones” so the contrast with the unrighteous is kept clear. The KJV has “Dare any of you, having a matter against another, go to law before the unjust, and not before the saints?” The ASV has “having a matter against his neighbor, go to law before the unrighteous, and not before the saints,” and the NWT 2013 “has a dispute with another dare to go to court before unrighteous men, and not before the holy ones.”

“The holy ones will judge the world.” Hoi hagioi ton kosmon krinousin, the holy ones will judge the world, is stated as a settled fact about the future. The chapter does not cite where it comes from, and commentators often point to Daniel 7:22, where judgment is given to the holy ones of the Most High. The chapter gives no reason for the claim, and this translation does not supply one. The claim is what makes the next question an argument: if the world will be judged by these people, they can decide the smaller matters.

“Unfit to try the smallest matters.” Anaxioi este kriteriōn elachistōn, unfit of the smallest courts. Kritērion is the word for a court or a place of judgment, and it names the case as well as the tribunal. The KJV has “are ye unworthy to judge the smallest matters?” The NWT 1984 has “are YOU unfit to try very trivial matters?” and the TNM 2019 in Spanish “no son capaces de juzgar asuntos de muy poca importancia?”

“Judge angels.” Angelous krinoumen, we will judge angels, with mēti ge biōtika, to say nothing of the matters of this life. The Greek of the second clause is an argument from the larger to the smaller: if the greater matter is theirs, the lesser matters of this life are easier. The chapter does not say which angels are meant, and the note records that it is silent on the point. The ASV has “how much more, things that pertain to this life?” and the NWT 2013 “Then why not matters of this life?”

“Those held in no account.” Tous exouthenēmenous en tē ekklēsia, the ones despised in the congregation, is the word for people treated as nothing. Kathizete, you seat, is the verb for seating a judge on a bench. Paul asks whether the congregation seats as judges the people it counts as nothing. The shelf has the question in its sharpest form: the KJV “set them to judge who are least esteemed in the church,” the ASV “set them to judge who are of no account in the church?” and the NWT 1984 “the men looked down upon in the congregation that YOU put in as judges?” This translation keeps “congregation” for ekklēsia, as the fixed rendering requires.

The shelf: the RV 1909 reads “having something with another”, the RV60 “when he has something against another”, and the NVI “how dare he bring a case before the unrighteous instead of before the believers.” In verse 2 the RV60 reads “you are unworthy to judge very small matters” and the NVI “trivial cases”; the NVI puts “believers will judge the world.”

Verses 5–8 · to your shame: no wise man, and a defeat already

“I say this to your shame.” Pros entropēn hymin legō, I say this to move you to shame. Entropē is the feeling that turns a person red, the shame that is meant to change behavior. The KJV has “I speak to your shame,” the ASV “I say this to move you to shame,” and the NWT 1984 “I am speaking to move YOU to shame.”

“Not one wise man among you.” Houtōs ouk eni en hymin oudeis sophos, is there not one wise man among you, is a question that expects the answer no. Sophos is a word for a person with skill in judging, and the sentence is ironic: the congregation has many members and claims wisdom, and Paul says it has no one who can settle a dispute between two brothers. The NWT 2013 has “Is there not one wise man among you who is able to judge between his brothers?” and the KJV “no, not one that shall be able to judge between his brethren?”

“Brother goes to law with brother.” Adelphos meta adelphou krinetai, brother goes to law with brother, and the case is heard epi apistōn, before unbelievers. The sentence states the shame plainly: the members of the one family are in the courts of outsiders. The ASV has “but brother goeth to law with brother, and that before unbelievers?” and the KJV “before the unbelievers.”

“A defeat.” Hēttēma, a defeat, is the noun from hēttaomai, to be worsted or to lose a contest. Paul says the lawsuits are already a loss, whatever the verdict. The NWT 2013 has “it is already a defeat for you when you have lawsuits,” and the KJV “utterly a fault among you,” which turns the defeat into a moral fault. The ASV has “altogether a defect in you.” The translation keeps “defeat” for the noun, because Paul is talking about the outcome of a contest, not a flaw.

“Why not rather be wronged?” Dia ti ouchi mallon adikeisthe; dia ti ouchi mallon apostereisthe, why not rather be wronged, why not rather be defrauded: the two verbs are passive, let yourselves be wronged, let yourselves be cheated. The reproach is a counsel to accept loss before a suit, and the note records that it is a counsel and not a command. The NWT 1984 has “Why do YOU not rather let yourselves be wronged?” and the TNM 2019 “¿Por qué no prefieren ser agraviados?”

“You yourselves wrong and defraud.” Hymeis adikeite kai apostereite, kai touto adelphous, you yourselves wrong and defraud, and that to your brothers. The reversal is the sharpest sentence in the paragraph: the congregation is not the victim of the courts but the one doing the wrong, to its own members. The NWT 1984 has “YOU wrong and defraud, and YOUR brothers at that,” and the ASV “Nay, but ye yourselves do wrong, and defraud, and that your brethren.”

In verse 5 the RV60 reads “I say this to your shame” and the NVI “I say this so that you may be ashamed”; the NVI adds that the wise person should judge “a dispute between believers.” In verse 6 the RV60 reads “brother goes to law with brother” and the NVI “one brother takes another to court.” In verse 7 the RV60 reads “it is already a fault among you” and the NVI “it is already a serious failing”; the RV60 has “you rather suffer wrong”, the NVI “to endure injustice.” In verse 8 the RV60 reads “you do wrong and defraud”, the NVI “it is you who defraud and do wrong.”

Verses 9–11 · do not be misled: the list, and what some of you were

“Do not be misled.” Mē planasthe, do not be misled, is a warning, and the question before it, ē ouk oidate, or do you not know, has already been asked in verse 2 and verse 3. The inheritance of basileia tou theou, the kingdom of God (basileia), is the Corinthians’ standing as heirs, and it is the test: those who do the listed things will not inherit it. The KJV has “Be not deceived,” and the NWT 2013 “Do not be misled.”

“The effeminate.” Malakoi, from the word for soft, is a word the English shelf translates several ways. It is used in Greek of luxury and softness, and the sense of the word in this list is debated among scholars. The translation keeps “effeminate”, which is the older shelf reading, and records that the word has been read in several ways. The KJV and ASV both have “effeminate.” The NWT 1984 has “men kept for unnatural purposes,” and the NWT 2013 “men who submit to homosexual acts.” The TNM 2019 has “los hombres que se someten a actos homosexuales” for this word.

“Men who lie with men.” Arsenokoitai, a compound of arsēn, male, and koitē, bed, is the rare word in this list, and it is already on the library’s entry for arsenokoitēs. The compound occurs also in 1 Timothy 1:10. The Greek of Leviticus 18:22, in the Greek Old Testament, uses the phrase ‘the bed of a woman’ for the same act, and the compound appears to draw on that phrase. The sense of the word, and whether it names a role or a general practice, is debated in the scholarship, and this translation states the readings and does not vote. The KJV has “abusers of themselves with mankind,” the ASV “abusers of themselves with men,” the NWT 1984 “men who lie with men,” and the TNM 1987 “hombres que se acuestan con hombres.”

“Thieves, the greedy, drunkards, revilers, extortioners.” The list in verse 10 is the list of 5:11, paid here as a test of the kingdom, with two changes: the fornicators and idolaters are not named again, and kleptai, thieves, is added. The greedy (pleonektai), the drunkards, the revilers (loidoroi) and the extortioners (harpages, harpax) are all from the earlier list. The KJV has “nor thieves, nor covetous, nor drunkards, nor revilers, nor extortioners,” and the NWT 2013 “thieves, greedy people, drunkards, revilers, and extortioners.”

“And some of you were such.” The verb ēte, were, is past tense, and the sentence turns the list into a memory: some in the congregation came from these lives. Then three passive verbs make the change: apelousasthe, you were washed; hēgiasthēte, you were sanctified; edikaiōthēte, you were declared righteous (dikaioō). The middle verb for washing is the reflexive, you washed yourselves in the Greek, but the passive forms of the other two make the grammar uneven; the translation keeps the three as they are. The KJV has “but ye are washed, but ye are sanctified, but ye are justified in the name of the Lord Jesus, and by the Spirit of our God,” and the ASV “and in the Spirit of our God.”

In verse 9 the RV60 reads “neither the effeminate, nor those who lie with men”, the NVI “neither sodomites nor homosexuals.” In verse 10 the RV60 reads “nor thieves will inherit”, and the NVI “slanderers, nor swindlers.” In verse 11 the RV60 reads “you have been washed”, and the NVI “But you were washed, you were sanctified and you were justified.”

Verses 12–14 · everything is lawful, and the body is for the Lord

“Everything is lawful for me.” Panta moi exestin, everything is lawful for me, is a slogan, and Paul quotes it twice, each time followed by a correction. The verb exestin is the word for what is permitted. The first correction is sympherei, is advantageous, which means it helps or profits, a word about benefit. The KJV has “All things are lawful unto me, but all things are not expedient,” the ASV “but not all things are expedient,” the NWT 1984 “but not all things are advantageous,” and the TNM 2019 “pero no todo es beneficioso.”

“Brought under the power of anything.” The second correction is exousiasthēsomai, I will be brought under authority. The word is from exousia, power, and it names the state of being mastered. The KJV has “I will not be brought under the power of any,” and the NWT 1984 “I will not let myself be brought under authority by anything.” The Greek is a claim about freedom, and the chapter turns it in verse 13: the freedom is for the Lord.

“Food for the stomach.” Ta brōmata tē koilia, foods are for the stomach, and the stomach for foods, is the second slogan, which Paul does not dispute, but limits: ho de theos kai tautēn kai tauta katargēsei, God will bring both to nothing. Katargeō, to bring to nothing, is the verb for making something inoperative. The shelf: the KJV “God shall destroy both it and them,” the ASV “God shall bring to nought both it and them,” the NWT 1984 “God will bring both it and them to nothing.”

“The body is for the Lord.” To de sōma ou tē porneia alla tō kyriō, the body is not for sexual immorality but for the Lord, and the Lord for the body: the word sōma, body, is the whole person, not the flesh alone, and it belongs to the Lord, who is in turn for it. The reciprocity is the point. The KJV has “Now the body is not for fornication, but for the Lord; and the Lord for the body,” and the TNM 2019 “El cuerpo no es para la inmoralidad sexual, sino para el Señor, y el Señor es para el cuerpo.”

“God raised up the Lord.” Ho de theos kai ton kyrion ēgeiren kai hēmas exegerei dia tēs dynamēs autou: God raised the Lord and will raise us through his power. The power is dynamis (dynamis), the word of 5:4, where the Lord’s power accompanies the assembly. Here it is the power that will raise the believers, and the verse plants the chapter 15 argument. The KJV has “And God hath both raised up the Lord, and will also raise up us by his own power,” and the NWT 1984 “God both raised up the Lord and will raise us up out of [death] through his power.” The brackets mark a word the NWT adds to make the sense plain.

In verse 12 the RV60 reads “not all are expedient” and “I will not be brought under the power of any”; the NVI “but not everything is good for me” and “but I will not let anything have power over me.” In verse 13 the RV60 reads “but God will destroy both one and the other”, and the NVI “God will destroy them both.” In verse 14 the NVI reads “By his power God raised the Lord.”

Verses 15–17 · members of Christ, a prostitute, one body, one spirit

“Your bodies are members of Christ.” Ta sōmata hymōn melē Christou estin, your bodies are members of Christ: melē, members, are the limbs of one body, and the picture is of a limb taken from the body of Christ. Paul’s argument depends on this image. The KJV has “Know ye not that your bodies are the members of Christ?” and the ASV “your bodies are members of Christ?”

“A prostitute.” Pornēs, of a prostitute, is the root of porneia, sexual immorality, as well as its own word. It is the plain noun for a woman who sells sex. The KJV and ASV have “harlot,” the NWT 2013 “prostitute,” and the TNM 2019 “una prostituta.” The translation keeps “prostitute,” the reading a present-day reader will take without a gloss.

“Never!” Mē genoito, may it never be. The phrase is Paul’s most emphatic refusal, and the shelf has it as “God forbid” in the KJV and the ASV, and “Never may that happen!” in the NWT 1984.

“The two will become one flesh.” Esontai gar, phēsin, hoi dyo eis sarka mian, for, he says, the two will be one flesh: the quotation is Genesis 2:24, the verse that makes the union of a man and a woman the origin of the one flesh. Paul applies the pattern to a sexual union with a prostitute, and in verse 17 he sets the union with the Lord beside it in the same grammar. The KJV has “for two, saith he, shall be one flesh,” the ASV “for, The twain, saith he, shall become one flesh,” and the NWT 1984 “‘The two,’ says he, ‘will be one flesh.’”

“One spirit.” Ho de kollōmenos tō kyriō hen pneuma estin, the one joined to the Lord is one spirit: the verb kollaō, to be glued to, is the same verb as in verse 16, so the one who is joined to a prostitute is one body and the one joined to the Lord is one spirit. The pairing is exact. The NWT 2013 has “is one with him in spirit,” and the TNM 2019 “llega a ser un solo espíritu con él.”

In verse 15 the RV60 reads “members of a harlot”, and the NVI “to unite them with a prostitute.” In verse 16 the RV60 reads “for it says, the two shall be one flesh”, and the NVI “The two will become one flesh.” In verse 17 the RV60 reads “one spirit with him”, and the NVI “is one with him in spirit.”

Verses 18–20 · flee sexual immorality, the temple, and the price

“Flee sexual immorality.” Pheugete tēn porneian, flee sexual immorality: the command is a single imperative, and porneia is the general word that Paul used of the man in 5:1. The KJV and ASV have “Flee fornication,” and the NWT 2013 “Flee from sexual immorality.”

“Outside the body.” Pan hamartēma ho ean poiēsē anthrōpos ektos tou sōmatos estin, every sin that a man does is outside the body, but the one who practices sexual immorality sins against his own body. The sentence is a claim about the body, and the note records the contrast the Greek draws, between sin done ektos, outside the body, and the sin of porneuōn eis to idion sōma, the one committed against the body itself. Commentators argue over whether the sentence is meant literally of every other sin. The translation states the Greek and does not settle the question.

“A temple of the holy spirit.” Naos, temple, is the sanctuary itself, the inner building where God is, not the courts around it (naos). The image returns from 3:16, where the congregation was the temple of God. Here it is the single body. The KJV has “Know ye not that your body is the temple of the Holy Ghost which is in you,” and the NWT 2013 “Do you not know that your body is the temple of the holy spirit within you.”

“Bought with a price.” Ēgorasthēte gar timēs, for you were bought with a price: the verb agorazō is the verb for buying in a market, and the price is named in general, without a sum. The KJV has “For ye are bought with a price,” and the ASV “for ye were bought with a price.” The conclusion, doxasate dē ton theon en tō sōmati hymōn, glorify God, then, in your body, is the command the chapter has been building.

“And in your spirit, which are God’s.” The KJV ends verse 20 with “and in your spirit, which are God’s.” That clause is in the Textus Receptus and in the printed editions that the KJV followed, and it is not in the critical text this translation reads. The translation keeps only the words the critical text prints, and records the clause here.

Patterns worth carrying forward

For verse 18 the RV60 reads “flee fornication”, and the NVI “flee from sexual immorality.” In verse 19 the RV60 and the NVI read “temple of the Holy Spirit.” In verse 20 the RV60 and the RV 1909 print the clause “and in your spirit, which are God’s”, as the KJV does though the critical text does not print it; the NVI reads “glorify God with your body.”

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “holy ones” for hagioi in 6:1–2, the word of 1:2; “defeat” for hēttema at 6:7; “advantageous” for sympherei at 6:12; “temple” for naos at 6:19, the word of 3:16; “bought with a price” for ēgorasthēte; “prostitute” for pornē; “members” for melē.

Echoes paid. The judging of 4:3–5 and 5:12–13, now turned to the holy ones who will judge the world (6:2); the list of 5:11, repeated here as the inheritance test (6:9–10); the temple of 3:16, now the single body (6:19); the power of 5:4, which raises the believers in 6:14; and the congregation that seats its own people as judges, with the wise man whom 6:5 says is missing from it.

Echoes planted. Foods for the stomach, a slogan Paul will limit again when he turns to meat offered to idols in chapters 8 to 10. The body for the Lord, and the belonging of the body to another, which chapter 7 treats as a question of marriage. God raises us through his power (6:14), which chapter 15 takes up as the argument for the resurrection.

Next in this book: 1 Corinthians 7, already on these pages. Paul turns from the body to marriage, and answers questions the Corinthians have written to him.
