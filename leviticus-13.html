🕰
Fifty-nine verses of priestly diagnostic law for a scaly skin affliction — traditionally 'leprosy', a word this translation retires — sorting swelling from scab from bright spot by depth, spreading and hair colour, then following the identical affliction into wool, linen and leather. It closes on the afflicted person's own public cry, 'Unclean! Unclean!', and a life lived outside the camp until the affliction lifts.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus, and — like Leviticus 11 — addressed to Moses AND Aaron together, since the priesthood executes this law in person. Still inside the same Sinai window between the tabernacle's completion (Exodus 40:17, already on these pages) and the census that opens Numbers (Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

Leviticus 13 — The Mister Translation

1
וַיְדַבֵּר יְהוָה, אֶל-מֹשֶׁה וְאֶל-אַהֲרֹן לֵאמֹר.
And Jehovah spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying:note
Y Jehová habló a Moisés y a Aarón, diciendo:
2
אָדָם, כִּי-יִהְיֶה בְעוֹר-בְּשָׂרוֹ שְׂאֵת אוֹ-סַפַּחַת אוֹ בַהֶרֶת, וְהָיָה בְעוֹר-בְּשָׂרוֹ, לְנֶגַע צָרָעַת--וְהוּבָא אֶל-אַהֲרֹן הַכֹּהֵן, אוֹ אֶל-אַחַד מִבָּנָיו הַכֹּהֲנִים.
When a person has, on the skin of his flesh, a swelling, or a scab, or a bright spot, and it becomes, on the skin of his flesh, an affliction of scaly disease, he shall be brought to Aaron the priest, or to one of his sons the priests.note
Cuando una persona tenga, en la piel de su cuerpo, una hinchazón, o una costra, o una mancha blanca, y se vuelva, en la piel de su cuerpo, una afección de enfermedad escamosa, será llevada a Aarón el sacerdote, o a uno de sus hijos los sacerdotes.

3
וְרָאָה הַכֹּהֵן אֶת-הַנֶּגַע בְּעוֹר-הַבָּשָׂר וְשֵׂעָר בַּנֶּגַע הָפַךְ לָבָן, וּמַרְאֵה הַנֶּגַע עָמֹק מֵעוֹר בְּשָׂרוֹ--נֶגַע צָרַעַת, הוּא; וְרָאָהוּ הַכֹּהֵן, וְטִמֵּא אֹתוֹ.
And the priest shall look at the affliction on the skin of the flesh: if the hair in the affliction has turned white, and the appearance of the affliction is deeper than the skin of his flesh, it is an affliction of scaly disease; and the priest shall look at him and pronounce him unclean.note
Y el sacerdote mirará la afección en la piel del cuerpo: si el pelo de la afección se ha vuelto blanco, y la apariencia de la afección es más profunda que la piel de su cuerpo, es una afección de enfermedad escamosa; y el sacerdote lo mirará y lo declarará impuro.
4
וְאִם-בַּהֶרֶת לְבָנָה הִוא בְּעוֹר בְּשָׂרוֹ, וְעָמֹק אֵין-מַרְאֶהָ מִן-הָעוֹר, וּשְׂעָרָה, לֹא-הָפַךְ לָבָן--וְהִסְגִּיר הַכֹּהֵן אֶת-הַנֶּגַע, שִׁבְעַת יָמִים.
But if the bright spot on the skin of his flesh is white, and its appearance is not deeper than the skin, and its hair has not turned white, the priest shall confine the afflicted person seven days.note
Pero si la mancha blanca en la piel de su cuerpo no es más profunda que la piel, y su pelo no se ha vuelto blanco, el sacerdote recluirá a la persona afectada siete días.
5

And the priest shall look at him on the seventh day, and if, in his eyes, the affliction has stayed as it was, not spread on the skin, the priest shall confine him seven more days, a second time.note
Y el sacerdote lo mirará al séptimo día, y si a sus ojos la afección ha permanecido igual, sin propagarse en la piel, el sacerdote lo recluirá siete días más, por segunda vez.
6
וְרָאָה הַכֹּהֵן אֹתוֹ בַּיּוֹם הַשְּׁבִיעִי, שֵׁנִית, וְהִנֵּה כֵּהָה הַנֶּגַע, וְלֹא-פָשָׂה הַנֶּגַע בָּעוֹר--וְטִהֲרוֹ הַכֹּהֵן מִסְפַּחַת הִוא, וְכִבֶּס בְּגָדָיו וְטָהֵר.
And the priest shall look at him again on the seventh day, and if the affliction has faded, and it has not spread on the skin, the priest shall pronounce him clean — it is a scab. He shall wash his clothes and be clean.note
Y el sacerdote lo mirará de nuevo al séptimo día, y si la afección se ha desvaído, y no se ha propagado en la piel, el sacerdote lo declarará puro —es una costra. Él lavará sus vestidos, y quedará puro.
7
וְאִם-פָּשֹׂה תִפְשֶׂה הַמִּסְפַּחַת בָּעוֹר, אַחֲרֵי הֵרָאֹתוֹ אֶל-הַכֹּהֵן לְטָהֳרָתוֹ; וְנִרְאָה שֵׁנִית, אֶל-הַכֹּהֵן.
But if the scab spreads further on the skin, after he has shown himself to the priest for his cleansing, he shall show himself to the priest again.note
Pero si la costra se propaga más en la piel, después de haberse mostrado al sacerdote para su purificación, se mostrará al sacerdote otra vez.
8
וְרָאָה, הַכֹּהֵן, וְהִנֵּה פָּשְׂתָה הַמִּסְפַּחַת, בָּעוֹר--וְטִמְּאוֹ הַכֹּהֵן, צָרַעַת הִוא. {פ}
And the priest shall look, and if the scab has spread on the skin, the priest shall pronounce him unclean — it is scaly disease.note
Y el sacerdote mirará, y si la costra se ha propagado en la piel, el sacerdote lo declarará impuro —es enfermedad escamosa.
9
נֶגַע צָרַעַת, כִּי תִהְיֶה בְּאָדָם; וְהוּבָא, אֶל-הַכֹּהֵן.
When an affliction of scaly disease is in a person, he shall be brought to the priest.note
Cuando haya una afección de enfermedad escamosa en una persona, será llevada al sacerdote.
10
וְרָאָה הַכֹּהֵן, וְהִנֵּה שְׂאֵת-לְבָנָה בָּעוֹר, וְהִיא, הָפְכָה שֵׂעָר לָבָן; וּמִחְיַת בָּשָׂר חַי, בַּשְׂאֵת.
And the priest shall look, and if there is a white swelling on the skin, and it has turned the hair white, and there is raw flesh alive in the swelling —note
Y el sacerdote mirará, y si hay una hinchazón blanca en la piel, que ha vuelto blanco el pelo, y hay carne viva y expuesta en la hinchazón —
11

it is an old scaly disease in the skin of his flesh, and the priest shall pronounce him unclean. He shall not confine him, for he is unclean.note

Nega and tzaraat — the mark, and the disease it might become

Like Leviticus 11 (already on these pages), this law is spoken to Moses AND Aaron together — one of only a handful of chapters that name Aaron as a direct addressee, and each time for the same reason: the priesthood has to execute this personally, not merely receive it secondhand. Leviticus 12, one chapter back, was addressed to Moses alone even though a priest performs its ritual too; the difference is diagnosis. A childbirth offering follows a fixed script. A skin affliction does not — the priest has to look, and decide, every single time, which is exactly what the next fifty-eight verses train him to do.

The chapter opens with three named marks and one word for what any of them MIGHT be. A se’et (‘a swelling’, from a root meaning to lift up), a sapachat (‘a scab’), or a baheret (‘a bright spot’) becomes an affliction — a nega, not yet a diagnosis — of scaly disease only if the priest’s examination says so. Keeping the two Hebrew words apart in English matters, because the chapter’s whole procedure depends on the gap between them: a mark is not yet a disease, and the entire point of this law is that only a trained, repeated examination is allowed to close that gap.

⚠ And that brings up the word every English Bible has used for two thousand years, and gets wrong: ‘leprosy’. Around the 2nd century BC, the Septuagint’s translators rendered tzaraat with Greek lepros, and it was a reasonable choice for its day — lepros simply meant ‘scaly’ in the Greek of their own era, and true Hansen’s disease (Mycobacterium leprae) went by an entirely different name, elephantiasis; ancient physicians kept the two apart. The narrowing happened centuries later, as Latin and then English ‘leprosy’ hardened into the name of one specific, dreaded disease and nothing else — so a modern reader meets the King James’s word and quietly imports a medical diagnosis the Hebrew text never makes. This translation reads ‘a scaly disease’ throughout instead. KJV, ASV, Douay-Rheims, Geneva and TLB all keep ‘leprosy’; NIV breaks from the pack with ‘a defiling skin disease’; and ⚠ even NWT, usually this shelf’s most exacting witness on precisely this kind of question, keeps ‘the disease of leprosy’ here — one of the rare places its own precision doesn’t win out over the King James tradition it usually corrects. The Spanish shelf splits the same way: RV60 keeps ‘llaga de lepra’, but NVI drops ‘lepra’ from its own main text entirely, for ‘infección’, footnoting the traditional word instead of printing it.

Old scaly disease — and the rule that turning entirely white makes you CLEAN

This case needs no seven-day wait: a disease that has already turned the hair white and shows raw flesh underneath is diagnosed on sight, once, no confinement. The chapter treats ‘raw flesh alive in the swelling’ as decisive precisely because it is the one sign that cannot be mistaken for an old scar or a passing rash — living tissue exposed inside the mark. ⚠ That detail turns out to be the key to the whole chapter’s strangest rule, three verses later.

Verse 13 is the paradox that stops most first-time readers: if the disease has spread until it covers the ENTIRE body, head to foot, the man is pronounced clean. More disease, not less, is what clears him. Read against vv10–11 it stops being strange: what the chapter is actually tracking is never the WHITENESS itself but whether any raw, living flesh is showing. A disease that has run its full course and covered everything, with nothing raw left exposed anywhere, has — on this law’s own logic — nothing left to spread; a disease still eating into new territory, visible as raw flesh at its advancing edge, is the one still active. v14 confirms it bluntly: the instant raw flesh reappears, even on an all-white body already pronounced clean, he is unclean again. The colour was never the real criterion. It was always the raw flesh, and whiteness was only ever a proxy for its absence.

Two accidental scars, examined by the identical procedure

A healed boil (vv18–23) and a healed burn (vv24–28) get their own separate Masoretic paragraphs, and then the identical seven-verse procedure, almost word for word, run on each. The boil is the same word Exodus uses for Egypt’s own sixth plague — an ordinary, everyday sore, nothing supernatural about getting one. What both cases share is the real question the law is answering: an accident has already happened to this skin, so how does the priest tell an innocent SCAR from a genuinely new disease using the old injury as its opening? The answer, both times, is the same three tests already established — white hair, depth below the skin, spreading — applied now to a spot with a known, harmless explanation sitting right next to it.

⚠ Notice what the priest never does across either case, or anywhere in this chapter: he does not treat, medicate, lance, or prescribe. The verb that carries the whole chapter is ra’ah, ‘to look’ — it recurs roughly thirty times in fifty-nine verses — and every diagnostic act ends in a spoken verdict, tame or tahor, unclean or clean, never in a cure. This is a chapter about EXAMINATION and STATUS, not therapy; whatever makes the disease actually go away happens off the page, and the priest’s whole job is to keep pronouncing on it, tick by tick, until it does.

Netek — the scale, where the tell is yellow hair, not white

The scalp and the beard get their own named subtype, and their own diagnostic marker: not white hair, but hair that has turned thin and yellow. Netek is the Hebrew, and this translation reads ‘a scale’ rather than the King James’s archaic ‘scall’ (a real, related English word, now simply dead outside this one verse of this one translation tradition). Some dermatologists have proposed favus, a fungal scalp infection that produces exactly this thin, discoloured hair, though nothing in the text lets that identification be checked against an actual case.

⚠ And v30 makes the relationship between the two Hebrew words explicit, in one breath: ‘it is a scale — it is scaly disease of the head or of the beard.’ Netek is not a rival category to tzaraat; it is named as a form of it, with its own local rule because hair, not skin, is what a priest can actually see clearly on a scalp. The seven-plus-seven confinement procedure runs again here almost verse for verse — this chapter is less interested in listing new diseases than in showing that one method, patiently repeated, covers all of them.

Bohaq — a hapax word, and the law’s own care not to over-diagnose

Two verses, and a diagnosis that needs no confinement at all: a faded white eruption is pronounced clean on sight. Bohaq occurs exactly once in the whole Hebrew Bible, here, so there is no second instance anywhere in Scripture to check its meaning against — printed untranslated in this edition rather than guessed at, matching this project’s own rule for a genuine hapax (see Leviticus 11’s solam and hargol, already on these pages). Modern dermatology’s best guess is vitiligo or leukoderma, a harmless loss of skin pigment. ⚠ KJV guesses ‘freckled spot’; ASV transliterates ‘tetter’ (itself just an old English word for a skin eruption, not a rendering of the Hebrew); NIV and NWT both converge, independently, on ‘a harmless rash’ — the one thing every version actually agrees the verse is sure of.

This is worth pausing on, because it is easy to read fifty-nine verses of diagnostic criteria as a chapter obsessed with FINDING disease. It is just as obsessed with the opposite: bohaq here, ordinary baldness three verses later (vv40–41), the healed scar of a boil or a burn earlier in the chapter — each one a condition the law goes out of its way to name and clear, guarding against exactly the kind of anxious over-diagnosis a purely fear-driven reading of this law would produce.

Two words for baldness, and neither one, by itself, means anything

Hair loss gets two Hebrew names — qereach, bald at the crown or back of the head, and gibbeach, bald at the front — and both verses end on the identical two words: ‘he is clean.’ A condition common enough that a large share of the adult population will eventually have it is stated, twice, to carry no ritual weight whatsoever on its own. Only when a reddish-white affliction breaks out ON TOP of the bald patch (v42) does the whole apparatus of examination restart, using the same criteria already established for skin elsewhere on the body.

‘Unclean, unclean’ — the cry two Gospels report Jesus answering with a touch

The diagnosed man tears his own clothes, lets his hair hang loose, covers his upper lip, and calls out his own condition to anyone who might come near. The torn clothes and loosened hair are, elsewhere in the Torah, mourning gestures — this law asks a living person to perform the outward signs of grief for himself. Jewish tradition reads the doubled cry, ‘tamei, tamei’, as doing two things at once: warning strangers away, and — per one old reading (b. Mo’ed Qatan 5a) — inviting anyone who hears it to pray for him. The custom outlived the ancient Near East by a long way: medieval European lepers carried a bell or clapper and were required to announce themselves in almost exactly this fashion, a direct if medically confused inheritance of this one verse.

⚠ A later, purely interpretive Jewish tradition ties this affliction to lashon hara, slander — reading the Hebrew for ‘one with the affliction’, metzora, as if it echoed motzi shem ra, ‘one who puts out a bad name.’ The pairing is a rabbinic wordplay (b. Arakhin 15b), not a claim this chapter itself makes — nothing in Leviticus 13 states or implies the disease is caused by, or a punishment for, speech. It is worth naming because Numbers 12, now on these pages, the very next appearance of this affliction in the Torah’s own narrative order, strikes Miriam with it directly after she speaks against Moses — almost certainly why the later reading exists at all, a moral built on top of one story and then generalized to the whole law.

And this is the isolation two Gospels report Jesus crossing on purpose. ‘A leper came to him… and Jesus, moved with anger, stretched out his hand and touched him’ (Mark 1:40-41, already on these pages) — the touch this very law makes contagious, given anyway, before the word of healing is even spoken. Matthew 8:2-4 (already on these pages) tells the same scene and then closes it by sending the healed man back to ‘show yourself to the priest’: not exemption from this law, but its ordinary machinery, running exactly as fifty-nine verses of it are written to run.

The clinching evidence: the identical word, growing on wool

The chapter’s last thirteen verses run the whole examine-confine-reexamine procedure a third time — on a garment, on leather, on woven cloth — using the SAME Hebrew phrase, nega tzaraat, that has named a human skin condition for fifty-eight verses. ⚠ This is the strongest evidence inside the text itself against the traditional rendering: no bacterial skin infection, Hansen’s disease included, can grow in a wool coat. Whatever tzaraat names, it names a category broad enough to include a person, a hide, and a length of woven linen — something closer to a spreading, greenish-or-reddish corruption than to any one clinical disease.

Even translators who keep ‘leprosy’ for the human case tend to give this section away. NIV, which already reads ‘a defiling skin disease’ at v2, calls this exact phrase ‘defiling MOULD’ once it reaches fabric — a tacit admission that the same Hebrew noun cannot honestly be forced into one English disease-name across both halves of its own chapter. NVI does the identical thing in Spanish, ‘moho’ here against ‘infección’ at v2. KJV and RV60, by contrast, keep ‘leprosy’/‘lepra’ in a garment for consistency’s sake — which only sharpens the point, since a reader who takes that literally is being told a Hebrew word can infect cloth.

The vocabulary tightens for the worst case: a spreading, unretreating affliction is ‘a MALIGNANT scaly disease’ (vv51–52), burned outright; a stubborn mark that survives one washing and confinement is called pechetet, ‘an erosion’ (v55), from a root meaning a pit or hollow — the fabric itself is being eaten away, which a genuine textile mould does and a human skin disease, by definition, cannot. The chapter that opened on a person’s skin closes, fifty-nine verses later, on the same procedure run on the fibers of a coat — and the seam between the two is exactly where the case against ‘leprosy’ is strongest.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 13 both run 1–59 — verified against the Masoretic text rather than assumed, with no versification shift (unlike the Exodus 21/22 boundary this project has hit before). Six Masoretic paragraph breaks divide it: closed breaks after vv23, 37, 39 and 46, open breaks after vv8, 17, and a final open break closing the whole chapter at v59. The traditional ‘leprosy’ is retired throughout for ‘a scaly disease’ (tzaraat) — the flagship departure of this chapter, argued at length in the note on vv1–8 and clinched by the garment law at the close. Nega, the general word for whatever the priest is examining before a verdict, is kept distinct as ‘the affliction’. Netek (vv29–37) reads ‘a scale’ rather than the King James’s dead ‘scall’. Bohaq (v39), a true hapax legomenon, is printed untranslated. Shti and erev (vv48–59) read ‘warp’ and ‘weft’ — the modern weaving term, in place of the King James’s now-obsolete ‘woof’.

Echoes paid. The joint address to Moses AND Aaron, last seen opening Leviticus 11 (already on these pages), recurs here for the same reason — the priesthood executes this law in person; the tame/tahor system of approach rather than morality, established across Leviticus 11–12, applied now to a bodily affliction instead of an animal or a birth; negaim gedolim, the ‘great blows’ that struck Pharaoh’s household at Genesis 12:17 (already on these pages), the same noun this chapter narrows to a single person’s skin; and shechin, Egypt’s own sixth plague at Exodus 9:9 (already on these pages), reappearing here as an ordinary, non-supernatural sore the priest must tell apart from a fresh disease.

Echoes planted. The healed man’s own offering and full purification ritual are paid off at Leviticus 14, now on these pages, including that chapter’s stranger extension of the identical law to a house’s own walls; Miriam’s own encounter with this affliction, and the later rabbinic tradition tying it to slander, now stand at Numbers 12, on these pages.

⚠ A note on order: Leviticus stands complete on these pages, chapters 1–27.
