The fifth night vision: a golden lampstand fed continually by two olive trees on either side, interpreted for Zerubbabel the governor as a promise that the temple's rebuilding will not depend on human strength — 'not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit' — and that the same hands that laid its foundation will finish it.
The fifth of the eight night visions, same night as chapters 1-3 — 520 BC. Zerubbabel, the vision's other central figure alongside Joshua, is the Persian-appointed governor overseeing the temple's reconstruction, Haggai's own addressee (Haggai 1:1).

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–5 · the FIFTH night vision — a lampstand no one built by hand

⚠ The interpreting angel has to wake Zechariah up (v1) — the fourth vision, Joshua’s courtroom drama, was apparently gripping enough to leave the prophet dazed, or the run of visions is simply exhausting. What he sees is not the tabernacle’s menorah rebuilt from memory: a single lampstand, solid gold, with a gullah — a reservoir bowl — on top of it, seven lamps, and pipes running gold from the bowl to each lamp. ⚠ How many pipes is a genuine, unresolved crux: the Hebrew doubles the numeral, shivah ve-shivah, “seven and seven” — which could mean fourteen pipes total, or seven pipes apiece to each of the seven lamps (forty-nine). The ancient versions do not agree, and this translation keeps the doubled number as given rather than resolving it into either total. ⚠ A small ketiv/qere pair sits inside v2: the consonantal text reads vayomer, “and HE said,” where every other first-person verb in the exchange needs “and I said” — the margin corrects it to va’omer, which this translation follows, as everywhere on this site.

Two olive trees flank the lampstand, one on each side (v3) — and Zechariah, like every reader after him, has no idea what he is looking at. The angel’s “do you not know?” (v5) is not scolding; it is the same rhetorical hinge that opens every one of these visions — the seer needs an interpreter, and the interpretation is the point.

Verses 6–7 · the most famous sentence in the book, and a mountain that stops being one

⚠ “Not by chayil, and not by koach, but by my spirit” is addressed directly to Zerubbabel, and the word choice is not generic. Chayil already carries, wherever this translation has met it before, a strong military edge — STRENGTH, valor, and very often an ARMY specifically. Koach is the more general word for power or capacity. Zerubbabel is a Persian-appointed governor rebuilding a temple in a province with no standing force of its own; the sentence is not abstract piety, it is a precise denial of the ONE resource he manifestly lacks. The promise is not that he will get an army after all — it is that he will not need one.

“Who are you, O great mountain, before Zerubbabel? You will become a plain” (v7) makes the same point in landscape: some large, real obstacle — unnamed, possibly the accumulated opposition of Judah’s neighbors already on record in Ezra 4–5, not yet on these pages — simply stops being a mountain. He then brings out “the top stone” (ha-even ha-rosha, the head or capping stone, likely set at the completion of a building rather than its foundation) to the shout “Grace, grace to it!” — the word doubled for the same reason “holy, holy, holy” is doubled: Hebrew intensifies by repetition, not degree-words.

Verses 8–10 · the day of small things, and the seven eyes again

“The hands of Zerubbabel have laid the foundation of this house, and his HANDS will finish it” (v9) is a promise stated as a fact already half true — the foundation is real, laid years earlier (Ezra 3, not yet on these pages), and the sentence simply extends the same pair of hands to the end. “The day of small things” (v10) names the temptation directly: a rebuilt house that will never match Solomon’s (a grief already voiced at Haggai 2:3, not yet on these pages) is not therefore a failure. ⚠ “They will see the even ha-bedil in Zerubbabel’s hand” — literally a “TIN stone,” tin or lead being dense enough to serve as the weight on a plumb-line. This translation keeps the literal metal rather than jumping straight to the tool it implies (“plummet,” the common English gloss): a builder checking a wall is still true, one more ordinary-craft image inside a vision otherwise full of gold and fire.

“These seven; they are the eyes of Jehovah, ranging through all the earth” is the second appearance of this exact image, one chapter after the first — the seven eyes on the single stone at Zechariah 3:9, engraved there, now identified outright as Jehovah’s own oversight of the whole world, watching a small province finish a small building.

Verses 11–14 · two sons of oil, not two anointed men

⚠ Zechariah asks twice — once about the two olive trees (v11), then more precisely about the two olive branches beside the golden spouts (v12). The word behind “branches” is shibbelei — the SAME word, by root, as “ear of grain” and the famous shibboleth password of Judges 12:6 (not yet on these pages): Hebrew reaches for its grain-word and stretches it, by analogy, onto an olive tree’s fruit-bearing growth. The two branches feed the lampstand’s bowl continuously and self-sustainingly — “that pour the gold out of THEMSELVES” — oil supplied with no human hand shown refilling it.

⚠ The answer (v14) is where this translation departs furthest from the shelf. KJV ASV NIV all print “the two anointed ones” — but the Hebrew says, literally, beney ha-yitzhar, “the two SONS OF OIL.” And yitzhar is not shemen, the word used everywhere else in the Torah for the oil poured on a priest’s or king’s head at an actual anointing (Exodus 29:7, already on these pages); it is the harvest word for freshly pressed oil, the third member of the standing grain-wine-oil triad. “Anointed ones” smuggles a ceremony into a phrase that is really about vitality and abundance — men who ARE, in their persons, what fresh oil represents, standing beside “the Lord of the whole earth,” not a title conferred by ritual. Interpreters overwhelmingly identify the two figures as Zerubbabel the governor and Joshua the high priest — king-line and priest-line, exactly the pair Zechariah’s previous vision (ch3) and Haggai’s closing oracle both single out — though the text here names neither by name.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: the literal “tin stone” for even ha-bedil (v10), rather than jumping straight to “plummet”; and “the two sons of oil” for beney ha-yitzhar (v14), keeping the harvest-oil word distinct from shemen, the actual anointing-oil word, rather than importing “the two anointed ones” and the ceremony it implies.

Echoes paid. The seven eyes (v10) return exactly one chapter after they were engraved on the stone at Zechariah 3:9, now named outright as Jehovah’s own oversight of the whole earth. And the “day of small things” (v10) answers the grief already on record at Haggai 2:3, not yet on these pages, over a house that cannot match Solomon’s.

Echoes planted. “Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit” (v6) — the theological center of these eight visions, named one chapter back in Zechariah 3’s own note as “the word that answers every discouraged builder,” and now finally spoken. The two sons of oil (v14), left unnamed here, are Zerubbabel and Joshua the high priest — the same pair the FOURTH vision just crowned and cleansed, one chapter back. And the eight night visions continue: the flying scroll and the woman in the basket wait one chapter ahead.
