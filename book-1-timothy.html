📖 1 Timothy

Πρὸς Τιμόθεον Α (Pros Timotheon A) — 'To Timothy, 1' — the first of two letters to Timothy, Paul's young lieutenant, left in charge of the congregation at Ephesus.

The first of the three PASTORAL letters — a working manual for holding a congregation together against false teaching, and the first book in the library whose Pauline authorship the majority of scholars dispute. It carries the gospel in a single sentence — 'Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners' — and the church's earliest written job-descriptions for its own officers.

Chapter by chapter

1 Timothy 1The first of the three Pastoral letters — and the first book in the library whose Pauline authorship the majority of scholars doubt, on grounds of vocabulary, church-order and a career that will not fit Acts. ⚠ The debate is laid out on both sides; no vote is cast. What the chapter itself does is set Timothy against a slippery Ephesian error — teachers of “a different doctrine” lost in “myths and endless genealogies” — and answer them not with rival knowledge but with an aim: “the GOAL of the charge is love, out of a pure heart.” Then the law “used lawfully,” and a vice-list built on the Ten Commandments that includes arsenokoitai, a rare word minted from Leviticus and rendered seven different ways by the seven shelf versions — the library sets out the philology and the spread and does not adjudicate the modern question. ⚠ At the centre is the first of the Pastorals’ “faithful sayings”: “Christ Jesus came into the world to save sinners — of whom I am foremost,” present tense, the chief of sinners kept as a standing title and offered as the proof of God’s patience. It breaks into a doxology — “to the King of the ages, immortal, invisible, the only God” — where the King James, following the later text, prints “the only WISE God.” It ends with two men “handed over to Satan” — a discipline meant to reform, not a curse.

About the book

Where it sits
The FIFTEENTH book of the New Testament and the first of the Pastorals (1-2 Timothy, Titus), grouped after the letters to congregations. ⚠ The three were not treated as a single question until the modern period made their common authorship one.
Genre
A LETTER in form, but in substance a manual of CHURCH ORDER addressed to a named delegate: how to silence false teachers, how public prayer should be conducted, what an overseer and a deacon must be, how to handle widows and elders and slaves and the rich. It reads less like an argument than a set of standing instructions, punctuated by sudden bursts of creed and doxology.
Author
Named as PAUL, to 'Timothy, my true child in faith' — but this is the central authorship dispute of the corpus. ⚠ The majority of critical scholars judge the three Pastorals pseudonymous, written in Paul's name a generation after his death: roughly a third of their vocabulary is absent from the other letters, the style is flatter and more formulaic, the church they picture (overseers, deacons, enrolled widows, a fixed body of 'sound teaching') looks later, and the travels they assume cannot be fitted into Acts. The case for Paul: a secretary given a free hand, the new subject of administration, an older man's changed manner, and personal notes hard to invent — with a release from the Acts 28 imprisonment and a further journey to hold it all. The library reports both and does not vote.
Date written
Traditionally c. AD 62–66, on the assumption of a release from the first Roman imprisonment and a journey Acts does not record; on the majority-critical view, c. AD 90–110. The date follows entirely from the authorship question.
Place
Written to EPHESUS, where Timothy has been left (1:3), by a writer who says he is bound for, or already in, MACEDONIA.
Audience
TIMOTHY — son of a Jewish mother and a Greek father, from Lystra, Paul's companion since the second journey and co-sender of several letters — here acting as Paul's delegate over the Ephesian congregation, and young enough that the letter must tell others not to despise his youth (4:12). Behind Timothy stands the congregation, meant to overhear the instructions as commands. Its trouble is a home-grown false teaching — speculative, law-obsessed, and ascetic (4:3 forbids marriage and certain foods) — which the letter combats less by refutation than by insisting on sound teaching, good order and godly conduct.

How it's laid out

1:1–20Charge to Timothy: stop the false teachers; the law used lawfully; Paul as the chief of sinners saved; the good warfare.
2:1–15Public worship: prayer for all people and for kings; 'one God and one mediator'; men and women in the assembly — including the much-argued 2:11-15.
3:1–16Officers: the qualifications of overseers and deacons, closing on a fragment of hymn — 'great is the mystery of godliness'.
4:1–16Against a coming apostasy that forbids marriage and foods; 'train yourself for godliness'; 'let no one despise your youth'.
5:1–6:2Pastoral care by category — widows, elders, slaves — a working rulebook for a mixed community.
6:3–21A last volley at the false teachers and the love of money — 'a root of all kinds of evils' — and a closing charge: 'guard the deposit'.

What it's about

  • SOUND TEACHING — the medical metaphor (hygiainō, 'to be healthy') that is the spine of all three Pastorals: right doctrine as health, error as a spreading disease.
  • CHURCH ORDER — the first written qualifications for overseers and deacons, and rules for widows and elders; a community organising itself to last.
  • GOD OUR SAVIOUR — the title six times across the Pastorals, and the universal reach of 2:4, God 'who wants all people to be saved and to come to a knowledge of the truth'.
  • THE FAITHFUL SAYINGS — five formulas unique to these letters, quoting early Christian confession; the first is 1:15, and the hymn-fragment of 3:16 is of a piece with them.
  • GODLINESS — eusebeia, a word almost absent from the undisputed Paul and everywhere here: religion as ordered, visible reverence.
  • MONEY — the warning that runs to 6:10, 'the love of money is a root of all kinds of evils', and the charge to the rich to be 'rich in good works'.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. Chapter 1's notable readings are two, both small and both places where the King James follows the later Received Text: at 1:4 the earliest text has oikonomia, 'stewardship', where the Received Text's oikodomia gave 'godly edifying'; and at 1:17 the doxology reads 'the only God', where the Received Text added 'wise' — 'the only wise God', probably drawn in from Romans 16:27. ⚠ The letter's largest critical question is not textual but authorial: whether Paul wrote the Pastorals at all. And its most-fought single word is arsenokoitēs at 1:10, minted from Leviticus and rendered across a wide range; the note sets out the philology and does not adjudicate the modern application.

Looking forward

Christ appears here chiefly in confession and formula rather than in sustained argument. He is the one 'who came into the world to save sinners' (1:15), the content of the first faithful saying; 'the man Christ Jesus', the 'one mediator between God and men' who 'gave himself as a ransom for all' (2:5-6); and the subject of the hymn-fragment at 3:16 — 'he was revealed in flesh, vindicated in spirit… taken up in glory'. ⚠ 'God our Saviour' and 'Christ Jesus our Saviour' sit side by side across the letter, and 6:15-16 heaps the doxology's language onto him — 'King of kings and Lord of lords, who alone has immortality'. What is missing is as telling as what is present: little of the cross-and-resurrection exposition that drives Romans or Galatians. Here the faith is a 'deposit' to be guarded, and Christ is its centre stated rather than argued.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP, above all — whether the three Pastorals are Paul's or written in his name a generation later, the single most consequential critical question in the Pauline corpus. The identity and content of the Ephesian false teaching, known only from the rebuttal. The meaning and reach of arsenokoitēs at 1:10. And, from chapter 2 onward, the instructions on women (2:11-15), which have carried enormous later weight, and the picture of church offices. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
