🕰
The Twelve are sent out with power and authority to preach and heal; Herod, having beheaded John, is perplexed by rumors about Jesus. Returning, the apostles are taken aside to Bethsaida, but the crowds follow, and five thousand are fed from five loaves and two fish. Praying alone, Jesus asks the disciples who the crowds and who they themselves say he is; Peter answers, 'the Christ of God,' and the first prediction of the passion follows, with the call to take up the cross daily. About a week later, on a mountain, Jesus is transfigured before Peter, John and James, with Moses and Elijah appearing in glory and a voice from a cloud naming him 'my Son, the Chosen One.' Coming down, he heals a boy the disciples could not; a second prediction of the death goes unperceived; a dispute over who is greatest is settled with a child; and a stranger casting out demons in Jesus's name is left alone rather than stopped. The chapter turns at v51: Jesus 'sets his face to go to Jerusalem,' a Samaritan village refuses him, and three would-be followers are answered with the cost of the road ahead.
c. AD 29, in the Galilean ministry through its close, directly after chapter 8 on Luke's own sequence — corresponding to Mark 6:7-56 and 8:27-9:50's accounts of the same sending, feeding, confession, transfiguration and healing (compressed here across the intervening material Luke omits, 6:45-8:26), and to Matthew 10, 14, 16-18; v51 opens the long central journey to Jerusalem (9:51-19:27) that has no full parallel in Mark's much shorter itinerary.

y los envió a proclamar el reino de Dios y a sanar a los enfermos,

Y en cualquier casa que entren, quédense allí, y de allí salgan.
5
καὶ ὅσοι ἂν μὴ δέχωνται ὑμᾶς, ἐξερχόμενοι ἀπὸ τῆς πόλεως ἐκείνης τὸν κονιορτὸν ἀπὸ τῶν ποδῶν ὑμῶν ἀποτινάσσετε εἰς μαρτύριον ἐπʼ αὐτούς.
And wherever they do not receive you, as you go out from that town, shake off the dust from your feet as a testimony against them.”note
Y dondequiera que no los reciban, al salir de aquel pueblo, sacudan el polvo de sus pies como testimonio contra ellos».

Y saliendo, iban recorriendo las aldeas, una tras otra, anunciando las buenas nuevas y sanando por todas partes.

Ἤκουσεν δὲ Ἡρῴδης ὁ τετραάρχης τὰ γινόμενα πάντα, καὶ διηπόρει διὰ τὸ λέγεσθαι ὑπό τινων ὅτι Ἰωάννης ἠγέρθη ἐκ νεκρῶν,

Herodes el tetrarca oyó de todo lo que sucedía, y estaba sumamente perplejo, porque algunos decían que Juan había resucitado de entre los muertos,
8
ὑπό τινων δὲ ὅτι Ἠλίας ἐφάνη, ἄλλων δὲ ὅτι προφήτης τις τῶν ἀρχαίων ἀνέστη.

y algunos, que Elías había aparecido; y otros, que uno de los profetas antiguos había resucitado.

εἶπεν δὲ ὁ Ἡρῴδης· Ἰωάννην ἐγὼ ἀπεκεφάλισα· τίς δέ ἐστιν οὗτος περὶ οὗ ἀκούω τοιαῦτα; καὶ ἐζήτει ἰδεῖν αὐτόν.

Pero Herodes dijo: «A Juan yo mismo lo decapité; entonces, ¿quién es este de quien oigo tales cosas?». Y procuraba verlo.
10
Καὶ ὑποστρέψαντες οἱ ἀπόστολοι διηγήσαντο αὐτῷ ὅσα ἐποίησαν. καὶ παραλαβὼν αὐτοὺς ὑπεχώρησεν κατʼ ἰδίαν εἰς πόλιν καλουμένην Βηθσαϊδά.
And when the apostles returned, they related to him all that they had done. And taking them along, he withdrew privately to a town called Bethsaida.note
Y cuando los apóstoles regresaron, le contaron todo lo que habían hecho. Y llevándolos consigo, se retiró en privado a un pueblo llamado Betsaida.
11
οἱ δὲ ὄχλοι γνόντες ἠκολούθησαν αὐτῷ. καὶ ἀποδεξάμενος αὐτοὺς ἐλάλει αὐτοῖς περὶ τῆς βασιλείας τοῦ θεοῦ, καὶ τοὺς χρείαν ἔχοντας θεραπείας ἰᾶτο.
But the crowds, learning of it, followed him. And he welcomed them and spoke to them about the kingdom of God, and healed those who had need of healing.note
Pero las multitudes, al saberlo, lo siguieron. Y él las recibió con agrado y les hablaba del reino de Dios, y sanaba a los que tenían necesidad de sanidad.
12
Ἡ δὲ ἡμέρα ἤρξατο κλίνειν· προσελθόντες δὲ οἱ δώδεκα εἶπαν αὐτῷ· Ἀπόλυσον τὸν ὄχλον, ἵνα πορευθέντες εἰς τὰς κύκλῳ κώμας καὶ ἀγροὺς καταλύσωσιν καὶ εὕρωσιν ἐπισιτισμόν, ὅτι ὧδε ἐν ἐρήμῳ τόπῳ ἐσμέν.
Now the day began to decline, and the twelve came up and said to him, “Send the crowd away, so that they may go into the surrounding villages and countryside and find lodging and provisions, because here we are in a deserted place.”note
El día comenzaba a declinar, y los doce se acercaron y le dijeron: «Despide a la multitud, para que vayan a las aldeas y campos de alrededor, y encuentren alojamiento y provisiones, porque aquí estamos en un lugar despoblado».
13
εἶπεν δὲ πρὸς αὐτούς· Δότε αὐτοῖς ὑμεῖς φαγεῖν. οἱ δὲ εἶπαν· Οὐκ εἰσὶν ἡμῖν πλεῖον ἢ ἄρτοι πέντε καὶ ἰχθύες δύο, εἰ μήτι πορευθέντες ἡμεῖς ἀγοράσωμεν εἰς πάντα τὸν λαὸν τοῦτον βρώματα.
But he said to them, “You give them something to eat.” And they said, “We have no more than five loaves and two fish — unless perhaps we ourselves are to go and buy food for all this crowd.”note
Pero él les dijo: «Denles ustedes de comer». Y ellos dijeron: «No tenemos más que cinco panes y dos peces, a no ser que vayamos nosotros mismos y compremos alimento para toda esta gente».
14
ἦσαν γὰρ ὡσεὶ ἄνδρες πεντακισχίλιοι. εἶπεν δὲ πρὸς τοὺς μαθητὰς αὐτοῦ· Κατακλίνατε αὐτοὺς κλισίας ὡσεὶ ἀνὰ πεντήκοντα.
(For there were about five thousand men.) And he said to his disciples, “Have them recline in groups of about fifty each.”note
(Porque eran como cinco mil hombres). Y dijo a sus discípulos: «Hagan que se recuesten en grupos de unos cincuenta cada uno».
15
καὶ ἐποίησαν οὕτως καὶ κατέκλιναν ἅπαντας.
And they did so, and had them all recline.note
Y así lo hicieron, y los hicieron recostar a todos.
16
λαβὼν δὲ τοὺς πέντε ἄρτους καὶ τοὺς δύο ἰχθύας ἀναβλέψας εἰς τὸν οὐρανὸν εὐλόγησεν αὐτοὺς καὶ κατέκλασεν καὶ ἐδίδου τοῖς μαθηταῖς παραθεῖναι τῷ ὄχλῳ.
Then, taking the five loaves and the two fish, he looked up to heaven and blessed them, and broke them, and kept giving them to the disciples to set before the crowd.note
Entonces, tomando los cinco panes y los dos peces, y levantando los ojos al cielo, los bendijo, y los partió, y los iba dando a los discípulos para que los sirvieran a la multitud.
17
καὶ ἔφαγον καὶ ἐχορτάσθησαν πάντες, καὶ ἤρθη τὸ περισσεῦσαν αὐτοῖς κλασμάτων κόφινοι δώδεκα.
And they all ate and were satisfied, and what was left over of the broken pieces was picked up — twelve baskets.note
Y comieron y se saciaron todos, y de los pedazos que les sobraron se recogieron doce canastas.
18

Now it happened, while he was praying alone, that the disciples were with him, and he asked them, saying, “Who do the crowds say that I am?”note
Y sucedió que, mientras él oraba solo, los discípulos estaban con él, y les preguntó, diciendo: «¿Quién dice la gente que soy yo?»

οἱ δὲ ἀποκριθέντες εἶπαν· Ἰωάννην τὸν βαπτιστήν, ἄλλοι δὲ Ἠλίαν, ἄλλοι δὲ ὅτι προφήτης τις τῶν ἀρχαίων ἀνέστη.
And they answered, “John the Baptist; but others, Elijah; and others, that one of the prophets of old has risen again.”note
Y respondiendo, dijeron: «Juan el Bautista; pero otros, Elías; y otros, que uno de los profetas antiguos ha resucitado».

εἶπεν δὲ αὐτοῖς· Ὑμεῖς δὲ τίνα με λέγετε εἶναι; Πέτρος δὲ ἀποκριθεὶς εἶπεν· Τὸν χριστὸν τοῦ θεοῦ.
And he said to them, “But who do you say that I am?” And Peter answered, “The Christ of God.”note
Y él les dijo: «Pero ustedes, ¿quién dicen que soy?» Y Pedro respondió: «El Cristo de Dios».

Ὁ δὲ ἐπιτιμήσας αὐτοῖς παρήγγειλεν μηδενὶ λέγειν τοῦτο,
But he warned them and instructed them to tell this to no one,note
Pero él les advirtió y les mandó que no dijeran esto a nadie,

Y decía a todos: «Si alguien quiere venir en pos de mí, niéguese a sí mismo, y tome su cruz cada día, y sígame.

ὃς γὰρ ἂν θέλῃ τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ σῶσαι, ἀπολέσει αὐτήν· ὃς δʼ ἂν ἀπολέσῃ τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ ἕνεκεν ἐμοῦ, οὗτος σώσει αὐτήν.

Porque el que quiera salvar su vida, la perderá; pero el que pierda su vida por mi causa, ese la salvará.
25
τί γὰρ ὠφελεῖται ἄνθρωπος κερδήσας τὸν κόσμον ὅλον ἑαυτὸν δὲ ἀπολέσας ἢ ζημιωθείς;
For what is a man benefited if he gains the whole world, but loses or forfeits himself?note
Porque ¿de qué le sirve al hombre ganar el mundo entero, si se pierde o se destruye a sí mismo?
26
ὃς γὰρ ἂν ἐπαισχυνθῇ με καὶ τοὺς ἐμοὺς λόγους, τοῦτον ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἐπαισχυνθήσεται, ὅταν ἔλθῃ ἐν τῇ δόξῃ αὐτοῦ καὶ τοῦ πατρὸς καὶ τῶν ἁγίων ἀγγέλων.
For whoever is ashamed of me and my words, of this one the Son of Man will be ashamed, when he comes in his own glory, and the Father’s, and the holy angels’.note
Porque el que se avergüence de mí y de mis palabras, de ese se avergonzará el Hijo del Hombre cuando venga en su gloria, y en la del Padre, y en la de los santos ángeles.
27
λέγω δὲ ὑμῖν ἀληθῶς, εἰσίν τινες τῶν αὐτοῦ ἑστηκότων οἳ οὐ μὴ γεύσωνται θανάτου ἕως ἂν ἴδωσιν τὴν βασιλείαν τοῦ θεοῦ.

Pero les digo de verdad que hay algunos de los que están aquí que no probarán la muerte hasta que vean el reino de Dios».

Y sucedió que, unos ocho días después de estas palabras, tomó consigo a Pedro, a Juan y a Santiago, y subió al monte a orar.

Y mientras oraba, el aspecto de su rostro se hizo diferente, y su ropa se volvió blanca y resplandeciente.
30

Y he aquí que dos hombres conversaban con él, que eran Moisés y Elías,
31
οἳ ὀφθέντες ἐν δόξῃ ἔλεγον τὴν ἔξοδον αὐτοῦ ἣν ἤμελλεν πληροῦν ἐν Ἰερουσαλήμ.

quienes, apareciendo en gloria, hablaban de su partida, que estaba por cumplir en Jerusalén.
32

Pedro y los que estaban con él estaban cargados de sueño; pero, al despertar del todo, vieron su gloria, y a los dos hombres que estaban de pie con él.
33

And as they were parting from him, Peter said to Jesus, “Master, it is good for us to be here; let us make three shelters — one for you, and one for Moses, and one for Elijah” — not knowing what he was saying.note
Y sucedió que, mientras estos se apartaban de él, Pedro dijo a Jesús: «Maestro, bueno es que estemos aquí; hagamos tres enramadas: una para ti, una para Moisés y una para Elías» —sin saber lo que decía.
34
ταῦτα δὲ αὐτοῦ λέγοντος ἐγένετο νεφέλη καὶ ἐπεσκίαζεν αὐτούς· ἐφοβήθησαν δὲ ἐν τῷ εἰσελθεῖν αὐτοὺς εἰς τὴν νεφέλην.
And as he was saying these things, a cloud came and overshadowed them, and they were afraid as they entered the cloud.note
Y mientras decía esto, vino una nube y los cubrió con su sombra; y tuvieron miedo al entrar en la nube.
35

And a voice came out of the cloud, saying, “This is my Son, the Chosen One; listen to him.”note
Y salió una voz de la nube, que decía: «Este es mi Hijo, el Escogido; a él escuchen».
36

Y cuando cesó la voz, Jesús fue hallado solo. Y ellos callaron, y en aquellos días no contaron a nadie nada de lo que habían visto.
37
Ἐγένετο δὲ τῇ ἑξῆς ἡμέρᾳ κατελθόντων αὐτῶν ἀπὸ τοῦ ὄρους συνήντησεν αὐτῷ ὄχλος πολύς.
Now on the next day, when they had come down from the mountain, a great crowd met him.note
Y al día siguiente, cuando bajaron del monte, una gran multitud le salió al encuentro.
38

And look — a man from the crowd cried out, saying, “Teacher, I beg you to look at my son, for he is my only child,note
Y he aquí que un hombre de entre la multitud gritó, diciendo: «Maestro, te ruego que mires a mi hijo, porque es mi hijo único,
39
καὶ ἰδοὺ πνεῦμα λαμβάνει αὐτόν, καὶ ἐξαίφνης κράζει, καὶ σπαράσσει αὐτὸν μετὰ ἀφροῦ καὶ μόγις ἀποχωρεῖ ἀπʼ αὐτοῦ συντρῖβον αὐτόν·
and look — a spirit seizes him, and suddenly he cries out, and it convulses him until he foams, and it scarcely departs from him, bruising him severely.note
y he aquí que un espíritu se apodera de él, y de repente grita, y lo convulsiona con espuma, y a duras penas se aparta de él después de maltratarlo;
40
καὶ ἐδεήθην τῶν μαθητῶν σου ἵνα ἐκβάλωσιν αὐτό, καὶ οὐκ ἠδυνήθησαν.
And I begged your disciples to cast it out, and they could not.”note
y rogué a tus discípulos que lo expulsaran, y no pudieron».
41
ἀποκριθεὶς δὲ ὁ Ἰησοῦς εἶπεν· Ὦ γενεὰ ἄπιστος καὶ διεστραμμένη, ἕως πότε ἔσομαι πρὸς ὑμᾶς καὶ ἀνέξομαι ὑμῶν; προσάγαγε ὧδε τὸν υἱόν σου.
And Jesus answered, “O faithless and twisted generation, how long shall I be with you and put up with you? Bring your son here.”note
Y respondiendo Jesús, dijo: «¡Oh generación incrédula y perversa! ¿Hasta cuándo estaré con ustedes y los soportaré? Trae acá a tu hijo».
42
ἔτι δὲ προσερχομένου αὐτοῦ ἔρρηξεν αὐτὸν τὸ δαιμόνιον καὶ συνεσπάραξεν· ἐπετίμησεν δὲ ὁ Ἰησοῦς τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἀκαθάρτῳ, καὶ ἰάσατο τὸν παῖδα καὶ ἀπέδωκεν αὐτὸν τῷ πατρὶ αὐτοῦ.
While he was still coming, the demon threw him down and convulsed him violently; but Jesus rebuked the unclean spirit, and healed the boy, and gave him back to his father.note
Y cuando aún se acercaba, el demonio lo derribó y lo convulsionó violentamente; pero Jesús reprendió al espíritu inmundo, y sanó al muchacho, y se lo devolvió a su padre.
43
ἐξεπλήσσοντο δὲ πάντες ἐπὶ τῇ μεγαλειότητι τοῦ θεοῦ. Πάντων δὲ θαυμαζόντων ἐπὶ πᾶσιν οἷς ἐποίει εἶπεν πρὸς τοὺς μαθητὰς αὐτοῦ·
And they were all astonished at the greatness of God. But while everyone was marveling at everything he was doing, he said to his disciples,note
Y todos se maravillaban de la grandeza de Dios. Pero mientras todos se asombraban de todo lo que hacía, dijo a sus discípulos:
44

«Guarden bien estas palabras en sus oídos, porque el Hijo del Hombre va a ser entregado en manos de hombres».
45
οἱ δὲ ἠγνόουν τὸ ῥῆμα τοῦτο, καὶ ἦν παρακεκαλυμμένον ἀπʼ αὐτῶν ἵνα μὴ αἴσθωνται αὐτό, καὶ ἐφοβοῦντο ἐρωτῆσαι αὐτὸν περὶ τοῦ ῥήματος τούτου.
But they did not understand this saying, and it was concealed from them so that they could not perceive it, and they were afraid to ask him about this saying.note
Pero ellos no entendían este dicho, y les estaba encubierto para que no lo percibieran, y temían preguntarle sobre este dicho.
46
Εἰσῆλθεν δὲ διαλογισμὸς ἐν αὐτοῖς, τὸ τίς ἂν εἴη μείζων αὐτῶν.
Now an argument arose among them as to which of them might be the greatest.note

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–6 · δύναμιν καὶ ἐξουσίαν… μήτε ῥάβδον μήτε πήραν… εἰς μαρτύριον ἐπʼ αὐτούς dynamin kai exousian… mēte rhabdon mēte pēran… eis martyrion epʼ autous — the identical pair of words a synagogue once used of Jesus, now simply handed away

⭐⭐ “Power and authority” — the same two words, reversed, that once described Jesus himself. Dynamis and exousia are the identical pair the Capernaum synagogue reached for two Gospel-chapters ago, amazed that Jesus commands unclean spirits ‘with authority and power’ (4:36, already on these pages, where both dictionary entries already flag this very echo forward). What astonished onlookers there is simply given away here, over ‘all the demons’ (v1) — not a smaller share of it and not a borrowed use of his name, but the thing itself, handed to twelve men who have not yet earned any reputation of their own.

⚠ Luke’s stricter packing list agrees with Matthew against Mark. No staff, no bag, no bread, no money, no spare tunic (v3) matches Matthew’s parallel sending exactly — ‘no sandals or a staff’ (Matthew 10:10, already on these pages) — against Mark’s own version of this identical commission, which explicitly permits both: ‘nothing… except a staff only… wear sandals’ (Mark 6:8–9, already flagged there as an unresolved three-way disagreement). Two Gospels forbid the very item the third allows, describing the same twelve men sent on the same afternoon. The later Byzantine text reads the plural ‘staffs’ here rather than the earlier singular; the KJV follows it, ‘neither staves’, where the ASV and NWT 1984 both keep the singular, ‘neither staff’.

The shaken-off dust (v5) is a testimony against a town, not a farewell gesture — the identical instruction Matthew gives in the same sending (Matthew 10:14) and Mark gives in his (Mark 6:11, both already on these pages). All three Synoptics agree on this one item exactly as they disagree on the staff.

Verses 7–9 · διηπόρει… τίς δέ ἐστιν οὗτος περὶ οὗ ἀκούω τοιαῦτα diēporei… tis de estin houtos peri hou akouō toiauta — a tetrarch who asks a question in the one Gospel that never shows the reader what he did

⭐⭐ Luke’s Herod is genuinely puzzled; Mark’s and Matthew’s already know the answer. Here Herod hears three competing theories and is left ‘thoroughly perplexed’ (diēporei), ending in a real question, ‘who then is this?’ (v9). Mark’s Herod, hearing the identical three theories, settles on one and states it as fact: ‘John, whom I beheaded — he has been raised’ (Mark 6:16, already on these pages); Matthew’s Herod tells his own attendants the same flat verdict with no other theory even mentioned, ‘This is John the Baptist; he has been raised from the dead’ (Matthew 14:2, already on these pages). Three tetrarchs, three different mental states, over the same rumor.

⚠ The execution this Gospel never narrates. Luke told the reader three chapters ago that Herod had locked John up (3:19–20, already on these pages), but the banquet, the daughter’s dance, the rash oath and the platter — the whole scene Mark spends sixteen verses on (Mark 6:17–29, already on these pages) — never appears here at all. The only confirmation this Gospel ever gives that John is actually dead is this one bare clause in Herod’s own mouth, stated as a fact he already carries and cannot undo.

⭐⭐⭐ “Who is this?” asked by a widening circle. The disciples asked their own version of it in the boat, ‘who then is this, that he commands even the winds and the water?’ (8:25, already on these pages, itself echoing a Pharisee’s dinner guests one chapter before that). Herod asks it here, from the outside, about rumors reaching a court. Nine verses from now Jesus will ask it of the Twelve directly — first what the crowds say, then what they themselves say (vv18–20, below) — the same question, asked by three widening circles, until the third one finally answers it.

Verses 10–17 · πόλιν καλουμένην Βηθσαϊδά… ἄρτοι πέντε καὶ ἰχθύες δύο… κόφινοι δώδεκα polin kaloumenēn Bēthsaida… artoi pente kai ichthyes dyo… kophinoi dōdeka — a town some later manuscripts turn into a wilderness, for the one miracle all four Gospels tell

⚠ “A town called Bethsaida” — or, in the majority text, “a deserted place belonging to” one. The earlier manuscripts this translation follows read simply ‘a town called Bethsaida’ (v10); the later Byzantine text instead reads ‘a deserted place belonging to a town called Bethsaida’ — a reading that looks like a harmonization toward Matthew’s and Mark’s own ‘desolate place’ for this same feeding (Matthew 14:13; Mark 6:32, both already on these pages), since the crowd is about to need somewhere with no shops nearby. This translation keeps the harder, earlier reading: a real town, not a wilderness the later copyists supplied to make the geography match.

⭐⭐⭐ The one miracle, before the resurrection, that all four Gospels tell. Matthew, Mark, Luke and John all narrate this feeding (Matthew 14; Mark 6; John 6, all already on these pages) — and only John names anyone inside it: Philip’s despairing arithmetic about two hundred denarii, Andrew pointing out a boy with barley loaves, the poor man’s grain (John 6:7–9). Luke, like Matthew and Mark, keeps everyone anonymous — ‘the twelve’ raise the problem (v12), and ‘you give them something to eat’ (v13) is aimed at the group, not at any one disciple. The miracle belongs to the crowd, not to a name inside it.

Groups of fifty, plainly counted. Mark tells the identical crowd sitting down in the same Hebrew-flavored doubling this translation has already flagged twice in his own chapter, ‘group by group’ and ‘rows, rows’ (Mark 6:39–40, already on these pages). Luke’s Greek is plainer — a straightforward distributive, ‘about fifty each’ (v14) — the same underlying arrangement without the doubled Semitic idiom.

Verses 18–20 · Τίνα με οἱ ὄχλοι λέγουσιν εἶναι… τίνα με λέγετε εἶναι… Τὸν χριστὸν τοῦ θεοῦ Tina me hoi ochloi legousin einai… tina me legete einai… Ton christon tou theou — three Synoptic confessions, two of them the same length, and a phrase this Gospel will use twice more

⭐⭐ Set during prayer, with no place name at all. Mark places this scene ‘on the road, to the villages of Caesarea Philippi’ (Mark 8:27) and Matthew simply ‘the district of Caesarea Philippi’ (Matthew 16:13, both already on these pages); Luke gives no place at all, framing the question instead around prayer — ‘while he was praying alone’ (v18) — exactly as it will frame the Transfiguration ten verses later (v28–29, below). This Gospel alone already noted Jesus praying at his own baptism (3:21), all night before choosing the Twelve (6:12), and habitually withdrawing to pray (5:16, all already on these pages) — a habit no other Gospel gives him nearly as often, and here it brackets the two moments the whole story has been building toward.

⭐⭐⭐ Three confessions: two of them four Greek words long, and one far longer. Mark’s is ‘You are the Christ’ (Sy ei ho christos), four words, bare — already flagged on these pages as carrying ‘no blessing, no rock, no keys’ (Mark 8:29). Luke’s own answer, ‘the Christ of God’ (ton christon tou theou), is also four words, counted the same way — but grammatically different: an accusative fragment completing the question rather than Mark’s complete sentence with its own verb. Matthew’s is more than double the length of either, ‘the Christ, the Son of the living God’ (Matthew 16:16, already on these pages, ten Greek words), and alone carries the beatitude on Peter and the promise of the keys that follows it.

⭐⭐⭐ The same two-word formula returns twice more in this Gospel — once as glory, once as mockery. Nine verses from now the cloud will name Jesus ‘my Son, the Chosen One’ (v35, below, ho eklelegmenos); at the cross the rulers will jeer, word for word, ‘if this is the Christ of God, the Chosen One’ (23:35, not yet on these pages) — Peter’s confession here, the Father’s own naming on the mountain, and the crowd’s sarcasm at the execution all reach for the identical formula, the last time turned into a taunt.

⚠ No rebuke of Peter follows — this Gospel carries no such scene at all. Both Mark and Matthew tell the very next moment as Peter pulling Jesus aside and being answered, ‘Get behind me, Satan’ (Mark 8:33; Matthew 16:23, both already on these pages). Luke has nothing of it: the confession is followed directly by the prediction (vv21–22, below) with no exchange between Peter and Jesus at all — the same pattern of softening a harsher Marcan scene already flagged at 4:16–30, 6:6–11 and 8:19–21 (all already on these pages).

Verses 21–22 · Δεῖ τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου πολλὰ παθεῖν… τῇ τρίτῃ ἡμέρᾳ ἐγερθῆναι Dei ton huion tou anthrōpou polla pathein… tē tritē hēmera egerthēnai — the first of three identical predictions, told by the same verb of necessity this boy once used of his Father’s house

⭐⭐ “Must” — the same verb, a third time. Dei is the word the twelve-year-old Jesus used of his own obligation, ‘I must be in my Father’s house’ (2:49, already on these pages), and the word the adult Jesus used of his preaching circuit, ‘I must bring the good news of the kingdom of God to the other towns also, because for this I was sent’ (4:43, already on these pages). The identical necessity now governs his death: the Son of Man must suffer, be rejected, be killed, and be raised. Whatever compelled the boy in the temple and the preacher in Galilee compels the cross as well.

“On the third day,” not “after three days.” Mark writes the same prediction as meta treis hēmeras, ‘after three days’, in all three of his passion predictions (Mark 8:31; 9:31; 10:34, already flagged there); Matthew and Luke both write ‘on the third day’ in the corresponding places, a span counted differently even though both describe the same event.

Verses 23–27 · ἀράτω τὸν σταυρὸν αὐτοῦ καθʼ ἡμέραν… ὃς γὰρ ἂν θέλῃ τὴν ψυχὴν αὐτοῦ σῶσαι aratō ton stauron autou kathʼ hēmeran… hos gar an thelē tēn psychēn autou sōsai — one word neither Mark nor Matthew has, added to a saying all three tell alike

⭐⭐⭐ “Daily” — Luke’s own addition to a saying three Gospels otherwise share almost word for word. Neither Mark’s telling of this saying, ‘take up his cross and follow me’ (Mark 8:34), nor Matthew’s, worded identically (Matthew 16:24, both already on these pages), has kathʼ hēmeran, ‘daily’. Luke alone adds it, turning a single decisive act — taking up an instrument of execution once — into a repeated discipline, done again every day. The same single word is the whole distance between a martyrdom and a way of life. The NWT 1984 catches the repetition with an adverb of its own, ‘pick up his torture stake day after day and follow me continually’.

⭐⭐ “Save his life” / “lose it” — the same pairing as Mark, not Matthew’s own variation. Psychē, life or soul, is already flagged on these pages for switching English words mid-argument in its hardest case (Mark 8:35–37); here Luke keeps the same save/lose pair Mark uses (Mark 8:35, already on these pages) straight through both verses, while Matthew’s own version of the identical saying restructures the second half from lose/save to lose/find (Matthew 16:25, already on these pages) — three Gospels telling one saying, two different verb-pairs.

Verses 28–36 · τὸ εἶδος τοῦ προσώπου αὐτοῦ ἕτερον… τὴν ἔξοδον αὐτοῦ… Οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ υἱός μου ὁ ἐκλελεγμένος to eidos tou prosōpou autou heteron… tēn exodon autou… Houtos estin ho huios mou ho eklelegmenos — the plainer of two Synoptic tellings, a hapax word for what is coming, and a voice that has changed since the river

⚠ Luke throws away the word Mark and Matthew both keep. Mark and Matthew both call this metemorphōthē, ‘he was transfigured’ (Mark 9:2; Matthew 17:2, already on these pages, where Mark’s own note already predicted this exact substitution) — a verb that, in the wider Greek-speaking world, belonged to the shape-changing gods of pagan myth. Luke reaches instead for a plainer ‘the appearance of his face became different’ (v29), avoiding the loaded word entirely for a description that could not be mistaken for a myth.

⭐⭐⭐ “His departure” — a Greek word standing in exactly three verses of the whole New Testament. Exodos, searched consonantally without its compound cousin at Matthew 22:9, occurs only here (v31), at Hebrews 11:22 of Israel’s own Exodus from Egypt (‘the exodus of the sons of Israel’, already on these pages), and at 2 Peter 1:15 of Peter’s own approaching death, ‘after my exodos’ (already on these pages) — a letter that names this very mountain two verses later. Three New Testament writers, one rare word, three different departures: a nation’s, Jesus’s, and (on the reading that Peter wrote it) an apostle’s own. This translation keeps the plain sense, ‘departure’, in the verse itself rather than the more loaded ‘exodus’ Hebrews 11 uses of Israel — the echo belongs in the note, not smuggled into the reader’s first impression of the sentence. The ASV and KJV both render it ‘decease’ here, an old word for death that survives today mainly in ‘the deceased’ — and both use the identical English word again at 2 Peter 1:15’s matching Greek, keeping the two verses paired in translation the way this note pairs them in the Greek.

⭐⭐ Set during prayer, again. Neither Mark nor Matthew mentions prayer anywhere in this scene; Luke alone frames it — ‘as he was praying’ (v29) — the same habit already flagged nine verses earlier at Peter’s confession (v18, above) and stretching back to the baptism, the choosing of the Twelve, and the habitual withdrawals this Gospel alone records.

⚠ The voice has changed both person and content since the river. At the Jordan it spoke to Jesus, ‘You are my beloved Son; in you I am well pleased’ (3:22, already on these pages). Here it speaks about him, to the disciples — ‘This is my Son’ (v35) — drops ‘well pleased’ entirely, and adds the one thing the baptism voice never said: an imperative, ‘listen to him’, quoting Deuteronomy’s own promise of a prophet like Moses almost word for word (Deuteronomy 18:15, already on these pages) — aimed at the very moment Moses himself stands beside Jesus and says nothing. “The Chosen One” (ho eklelegmenos) replaces the baptism’s ‘beloved’ and returns twice more in this Gospel, above and at the cross (see v20’s note).

An unnamed mountain. Neither Mark, Matthew nor Luke ever names it. Tradition has long offered Mount Tabor; some modern geographers prefer the much higher Mount Hermon, nearer the district around Caesarea Philippi where the previous scene was set — and the text itself simply does not say.

Verses 37–42 · ὅτι μονογενής μοί ἐστιν… ἰάσατο τὸν παῖδα καὶ ἀπέδωκεν αὐτὸν τῷ πατρὶ αὐτοῦ hoti monogenēs moi estin… iasato ton paida kai apedōken auton tō patri autou — the third and final only child this Gospel promised, and the same noun that has already named two other people

⭐⭐⭐ “Only” — the third and last of three parents this Gospel promised. Luke 7’s closing note named three parents whose monogenēs child this Gospel would give: the widow at Nain’s only son, already told; Jairus’s only daughter (8:42, already on these pages); and this father’s only son, here. All three appear in Luke alone; Mark’s parallel to this very healing has no such word for the boy at all.

⭐⭐ “The boy” — the same noun that has already named a slave and a dying girl. Pais named the centurion’s servant (‘my boy’, 7:7) and, in its feminine form, Jairus’s daughter (‘Child, arise’, 8:54, both already on these pages); here it names this father’s son (v42), completing the same three-scene pattern monogenēs just completed one verse earlier — one Greek noun serving a slave, a dying girl, and a boy with convulsions, with no separate vocabulary needed for any of them.

⚠ Matthew names the disease; Mark and Luke describe only the symptoms. Matthew’s much shorter telling has the father call the boy ‘moonstruck’, selēniazetai — the ordinary folk-diagnosis for epilepsy, blamed on the phases of the moon (Matthew 17:15, already on these pages). Neither Mark’s much longer telling (Mark 9:14–29, already on these pages, including the disciples’ own failed exorcism and the father’s ‘I believe; help my unbelief’) nor Luke’s has any diagnostic word at all — only convulsions, foam, and a departure that leaves the boy ‘bruised’ (v39).

Verses 43–45 · ἐπὶ τῇ μεγαλειότητι τοῦ θεοῦ… μέλλει παραδίδοσθαι εἰς χεῖρας ἀνθρώπων epi tē megaleiotēti tou theou… mellei paradidosthai eis cheiras anthrōpōn — a second prediction dropped into the middle of general astonishment, and understood by no one

The hinge verse. V43 turns, mid-sentence, from a crowd’s astonishment at ‘the greatness of God’ to Jesus’s own flat prediction of betrayal — the single greatest tonal swing inside one Lukan verse so far, with no narrative gap at all between the two.

⚠ Understood by no one, and this time no one dares ask. The first prediction (v22) drew no recorded reaction at all; this one is met with active incomprehension — ‘concealed from them so that they could not perceive it’ (v45) — and fear that stops them from asking a single question. Mark’s telling of this same second prediction adds that they were also simply ‘afraid to ask him’ (Mark 9:32, already on these pages), the identical clause; Luke doubles it with the concealment, so that a hidden meaning and a fear of hearing it both stand in the way.

Verses 46–50 · τὸ τίς ἂν εἴη μείζων αὐτῶν… ὁ γὰρ μικρότερος… οὗτός ἐστιν μέγας to tis an eiē meizōn autōn… ho gar mikroteros… houtos estin megas — a child set beside a prediction of betrayal no one understood, and a stranger the Twelve tried to silence

⚠ An argument about greatness, one verse after a prediction of death no one would ask about. Luke does not soften the timing: the disciples cannot bring themselves to ask Jesus about his own coming betrayal (v45) and immediately begin arguing among themselves about which of them is the greatest (v46). Mark’s parallel makes the same juxtaposition even sharper, with Jesus asking directly what they had been arguing about on the road and the disciples staying silent out of shame (Mark 9:33–34, already on these pages).

A child as the answer, not an illustration. Jesus does not explain what makes a child a model of greatness; he simply stands one beside himself (v47) and identifies receiving the child WITH receiving himself and the one who sent him (v48) — three receptions collapsed into one act, with least and greatest reversed in the same breath. The same paidion gesture recurs in Matthew’s much longer version of this scene (Matthew 18:2–5, already on these pages), which draws out at length what Luke states in a single verse.

⭐ “Not against you is for you” — the positive form of a saying stated negatively elsewhere. John’s complaint (v49) and Jesus’s answer (v50) are word for word the same exchange Mark tells (Mark 9:38–40, already on these pages). Elsewhere this Gospel’s Jesus states the identical principle in reverse, ‘whoever is not with me is against me’ (11:23, not yet on these pages) — two sayings that sound contradictory side by side but answer two different situations: a rival claiming to cast out demons in the same name gets the generous reading; open, active opposition does not.

Verses 51–56 · τὰς ἡμέρας τῆς ἀναλήμψεως αὐτοῦ… τὸ πρόσωπον ἐστήρισεν… οὐκ ἐδέξαντο αὐτόν tas hēmeras tēs analēmpseōs autou… to prosōpon estērisen… ouk edexanto auton — a hapax word for what is coming, the hinge of the whole Gospel, and two verses the later manuscripts could not leave alone

⭐⭐⭐ “Taken up” — a true New Testament hapax, and Luke’s own word twice over. Analēmpsis stands in exactly one verse of the whole New Testament (v51) — searched consonantally, its only relative is the verb analambanō, used of the actual ascension Luke narrates a book later: ‘this Jesus who was taken up from you into heaven’ (Acts 1:11, already on these pages). One author, one root, a noun that names the event here before it happens and a verb that narrates it once it does — the kind of echo only Luke, writing both books, could plant on purpose.

⭐⭐⭐ The hinge of the whole Gospel. ‘He set his face to go to Jerusalem’ (v51) opens the long central section of this Gospel that runs all the way to the entry into Jerusalem nine chapters from now — road, sayings, and encounters with almost no parallel in Mark’s much shorter itinerary. Everything from here through chapter 19 takes place, in Luke’s own framing, on this one road. The ASV and KJV both read ‘he stedfastly set his face’; the NWT 1984 reads ‘he firmly set his face’, and its 2013 revision ‘he resolutely set his face’ — three adverbs reaching for the same determination the bare Greek idiom carries without one.

⚠ A real textual crux: two sentences the earliest manuscripts do not have. The later, majority text adds a clause to James and John’s question in v54 — ‘fire to come down from heaven and consume them, as Elijah also did’ (kept by the KJV: ‘even as Elias did’) — making explicit the allusion to 2 Kings 1, where Elijah calls down fire on two companies of soldiers. The same later text then supplies Jesus’s reply in full at v55–56: ‘You do not know what kind of spirit you are of; for the Son of Man did not come to destroy the souls of men, but to save them.’ Neither clause stands in the earliest witnesses this translation follows, and both read like an old marginal gloss — the Elijah allusion made explicit, then answered by the very sentence this Gospel could plausibly have meant all along — that a later hand pulled into the text itself. This translation prints the shorter reading and reports the longer one here rather than adopting it.

A village that will not receive him, for a reason this Gospel does not spell out. Long-standing hostility between Jews and Samaritans is the likely background — a Samaritan village refusing hospitality to Galilean pilgrims bound for the temple in Jerusalem, the very city Samaritans rejected as the place of worship — but the text itself states only the refusal, not its motive, and the village is never named.

Verses 57–62 · Ἄφες τοὺς νεκροὺς θάψαι τοὺς ἑαυτῶν νεκρούς… οὐδεὶς ἐπιβαλὼν τὴν χεῖρα ἐπʼ ἄροτρον Aphes tous nekrous thapsai tous heautōn nekrous… oudeis epibalōn tēn cheira epʼ arotron — three would-be followers, two sayings Matthew places years earlier, and one that belongs to Luke alone

⭐⭐ Matthew tells two of these three sayings much earlier, before the storm on the lake. The fox-and-nest saying and the ‘let the dead bury their own dead’ exchange both appear in Matthew as well, but eight chapters earlier in his own order, immediately before Jesus crosses the lake into the storm (Matthew 8:18–22, already on these pages) — a scene Luke places three chapters before this one (8:22–25, already on these pages). The two Gospels agree on the sayings and disagree by chapters on when they were said; this translation reports the divergence rather than harmonizing the two timelines into one.

⭐⭐⭐ The third follower, and the plow saying, belong to Luke alone. Neither the man who asks to say farewell to his household first, nor Jesus’s answer about a hand on the plow (vv61–62), has any parallel in Matthew, Mark or John. This translation’s closing image of the whole cost-of-discipleship set is therefore Luke’s own addition to a pair of sayings he shares with Matthew.

⚠ “Let the dead bury their own dead” answers a request the Law itself would honor. Burying a parent was among the most serious filial duties in the Law (compare Genesis 50:5–6’s permission for Joseph, already on these pages); Jesus’s answer does not deny the duty exists, but subordinates it to an urgency that will not wait even for a father’s funeral — the same absolute claim the plow saying makes in agricultural rather than filial terms two verses later.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: power and authority for dynamis and exousia, the identical pair Luke 4:36 already used of Jesus himself (v1); Master for Epistata, extended to its fifth and sixth New Testament appearances (vv33, 49); the boy for pais, its third distinct person in this Gospel (v42); only for monogenēs, its third and final Lukan appearance (v38); life for psychē, kept in the save/lose pairing Mark shares and Matthew does not (vv24–25); departure for exodos, a three-occurrence New Testament word kept plain in the verse and explained in the note (v31); and taken up for analēmpsis, a true hapax answered a book later by its own verb at the ascension (v51).

Echoes paid. Both promises from Luke 7’s closing note are completed here: monogenēs reaches its third and final parent (v38), and pais names its third person, a slave, a dying girl, and now a convulsing boy (v42). The disciples’ “who then is this?” from the storm (8:25) and Herod’s own version of the question (v9) are both answered, at last, by Peter’s confession (v20) — the third and final circle to be asked the same question this Gospel has been widening for three chapters. And the two-word formula of that confession, “the Christ of God,” returns nine verses later as the cloud’s “the Chosen One” (v35).

Echoes planted. “The Christ of God, the Chosen One” will return a third time, as mockery, at the cross (23:35). Epistata has one appearance left in this Gospel, at 17:13. And “taken up” (analēmpsis, v51) is answered by its own verb at the actual ascension, already told in Luke’s second volume (Acts 1:11) — the rare instance of an echo this library can point to as already paid, in a different book, before the chapter that plants it was even translated.
