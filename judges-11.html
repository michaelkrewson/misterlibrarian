Jephthah the Gileadite, the son of a prostitute, is driven out by his half-brothers, gathers empty men in Tob, and is brought back by the elders of Gilead to lead the war against Ammon. He answers Ammon's king with the history of the land from Egypt to the Arnon, makes the vow of v. 31 before battle, and wins from Aroer to Minnith and Abel-cheramim. His only child comes out to meet him, and after two months on the mountains he keeps the vow; the daughters of Israel go each year to commemorate her.
Within the period of the judges, with no date the chapter gives for itself. Its own reckoning is three hundred years for Israel's settlement east of the Jordan (11:26), and it sits between Tola and Jair (10:1-5) and the later judges. The early/late question applies (c. 1200 BC on the early reckoning this project foregrounds, c. 1100 BC on the late).

Jefté envió otra vez mensajeros al rey de los hijos de Amón,

Porque cuando subieron de Egipto, Israel anduvo por el desierto hasta el mar Rojo y llegó a Cades.

Israel envió mensajeros a Sijón, rey de los amorreos, rey de Hesbón; e Israel le dijo: «Te ruego que nos dejes pasar por tu tierra hasta nuestro lugar».
20

Pero Sijón no confió en que Israel pasara por su frontera; reunió a todo su pueblo, acampó en Jahaz y peleó contra Israel.
21

Y Jehová, Dios de Israel, entregó a Sijón y a todo su pueblo en mano de Israel, y los derrotaron; así Israel tomó toda la tierra de los amorreos, habitantes de aquella tierra.
22

They took all the territory of the Amorites, from the Arnon to the Jabbok, and from the wilderness to the Jordan.note
Y poseyeron todo el territorio de los amorreos, desde el Arnón hasta el Jaboc, y desde el desierto hasta el Jordán.
23

Now Jehovah, the God of Israel has driven out the Amorites before his people Israel, and will you take their land?note
Ahora, pues, Jehová, Dios de Israel, ha desposeído a los amorreos delante de su pueblo Israel; ¿y tú los vas a poseer?
24
הֲלֹא אֵת אֲשֶׁר יוֹרִישְׁךָ, כְּמוֹשׁ אֱלֹהֶיךָ--אוֹתוֹ תִירָשׁ; וְאֵת כָּל-אֲשֶׁר הוֹרִישׁ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵינוּ, מִפָּנֵינוּ--אוֹתוֹ נִירָשׁ.
Will you not possess what Chemosh your god gives you to possess? So whoever Jehovah our God has driven out before us, we will possess.note
¿No posees tú lo que te da Kemós tu dios para poseer? Así que a todos los que Jehová nuestro Dios desposeyó delante de nosotros, los poseeremos nosotros.
25
וְעַתָּה, הֲטוֹב טוֹב אַתָּה, מִבָּלָק בֶּן-צִפּוֹר, מֶלֶךְ מוֹאָב:  הֲרוֹב רָב עִם-יִשְׂרָאֵל, אִם-נִלְחֹם נִלְחַם בָּם.

Y ahora, ¿eres tú mejor que Balac hijo de Zipor, rey de Moab? ¿Contendió él alguna vez con Israel, o peleó contra ellos?

Mientras Israel habitó en Hesbón y sus aldeas, y en Aroer y sus aldeas, y en todas las ciudades que están junto al Arnón, trescientos años; ¿por qué no las recuperaron en ese tiempo?
27

Verses 1–3 · Jephthah’s birth and exile, Judges 11:1–3: a mighty man, the son of a prostitute, shut out of his brothers’ inheritance, and gathering men in Tob

★★★ The chapter opens with two descriptions of one man. Jephthah is “a mighty man of valour” and “the son of a prostitute” (v. 1). The first is a warrior’s word; the second is the reason his half-brothers give for shutting him out. Gilead is his father. His mother is not named, and the chapter does not say whether the prostitute was a free woman or a slave.

★★ Gilead’s other sons drive him out. “You shall not inherit in our father’s house” (v. 2). The verb garash, “drive out,” is the one Zebul uses to expel Gaal in Judges 9:41, now on these pages.

★★ He flees to Tob, and “empty men” gather to him. The phrase anashim reqim, “empty men,” is the one Abimelech hires in Judges 9:4, now on these pages. The men who follow Jephthah are the same kind of followers that Abimelech had,.

Verses 4–11 · The war with Ammon and the bargain, Judges 11:4–11: the elders come to fetch Jephthah, and he makes them swear to his headship before Jehovah at Mizpah

★★★ The elders who cast him out come back to him. “Did you not hate me and drive me out of my father’s house? Why have you come to me now, when you are in trouble?” (v. 7). The elders do not deny it: “That is why we have turned back to you now” (v. 8).

★★ The bargain is headship. The elders promise that he “shall be our head over all the inhabitants of Gilead” (v. 8), and Jephthah says that if Jehovah gives Ammon before him he will be their head (v. 9). The word rosh, “head,” is the one the elders use, and it is the title Gideon was offered and refused (compare Judges 8:23, now on these pages).

★★★ The oath is made before Jehovah, at Mizpah. “Jehovah shall be witness between us” (v. 10). Jephthah speaks “all his words before Jehovah in Mizpah” (v. 11). Mizpah of Gilead is the camp where the Israelites gathered in Judges 10:17, now on these pages; the covenant is made where the army is.

Verses 12–28 · The argument with the king of Ammon, Judges 11:12–28: a history of the land from Egypt to the Arnon, three hundred years, and Chemosh

★★★ The king of Ammon claims the land from the Arnon to the Jabbok and the Jordan. “Because Israel took my land when it came up out of Egypt” (v. 13). Jephthah answers with the history: Israel did not take Moab’s land or Ammon’s (v. 15); it asked Edom and Moab for passage and was refused (vv. 17–18); it asked Sihon of the Amorites for passage and was refused, and attacked (vv. 19–22). The story is told in Numbers 21:21–25, already on these pages.

★★★ “Will you not possess what Chemosh your god gives you?” (v. 24). Chemosh is the god of Moab elsewhere in the Bible; here Jephthah calls him the god of the king of Ammon, and the note records that without resolving it. The argument turns on a claim of gift: each people has the land its god gives it, and Jehovah gave Sihon’s land to Israel.

★★ “Three hundred years” (v. 26). Jephthah says Israel has lived in Heshbon, Aroer and the towns along the Arnon for three hundred years, and asks why Ammon did not recover them in that time. All six shelf versions keep the number: the KJV, ASV and NWT 1984 say “three hundred”, the NWT 2013 and TNM 2019 say “300”, and the TNM 1987 says “trescientos años”. The number is the chapter’s own.

★★ The appeal ends with Jehovah as judge. “May Jehovah, the Judge, judge today between the children of Israel and the children of Ammon” (v. 27). The king of Ammon “did not listen” (v. 28), and the next part of the chapter is the war.

Verses 29–33 · The spirit, the vow and the victory, Judges 11:29–33: the spirit of Jehovah comes on Jephthah, and he vows a burnt offering from the doors of his house

★★★ “The spirit of Jehovah came upon Jephthah.” The phrase ruach YHWH “came upon” is the one used for Othniel (Judges 3:10, now on these pages). The spirit comes before the vow, and the vow is the leader’s own act.

★★★ The vow: “whatever comes out of the doors of my house to meet me” (v. 31). The Hebrew ha-yotse, “the one coming out”, is masculine in form, and the English “whatever” keeps open whether a man or a woman is meant. Jephthah says it “shall be Jehovah’s, and I will offer it up as a burnt offering.” The vow is made before the battle, with no name for what will come out. The text does not say what he meant by it, and this note does not decide.

★★ The victory runs from Aroer to Minnith and Abel-cheramim. “From Aroer as far as Minnith, twenty cities, and as far as Abel-cheramim, with a very great slaughter” (v. 33). Aroer is the town on the Arnon named in the argument of v. 26; Minnith and Abel-cheramim are the far end of the campaign.

Verses 34–40 · The daughter, Judges 11:34–40: the vow is kept, two months on the mountains, and a custom in Israel

★★★ His only child comes out to meet him with timbrels and dances. “She was his only child; he had neither son nor daughter besides her” (v. 34). He tears his clothes: “Alas, my daughter! You have brought me very low; you are one of those who trouble me; for I have opened my mouth to Jehovah, and I cannot go back” (v. 35). The KJV and ASV say “cannot go back”. The NWT 2013 says “unable to turn back”. The TNM 2019 says “no puedo volverme atrás”.

★★★ She accepts the vow, and asks for two months. She tells him to do as he said, since Jehovah has avenged him on Ammon (v. 36). She asks to “go up and down on the mountains and weep for my virginity” (v. 37). The verb bakah, “weep,” and the word betulim, “virginity”, are the book’s words for an unmarried girl’s grief.

★★★ At the end of two months he “did to her as he had vowed,” and she “had known no man” (v. 39). The chapter does not say what the vow was carried out as; it says he did what he had vowed. The shelf divides on the last phrase. The KJV keeps the old idiom and says “knew no man”. The NWT 1984 says “never had relations with a man”. The TNM 2019 says “nunca tuvo relaciones sexuales”. The Hebrew yada, “know”, is the word the book uses for sexual knowing (see Genesis 4:1, already on these pages), so the Mister reading keeps the verb and the statement.

★★★ “A custom in Israel”: the daughters of Israel go each year to commemorate her, four days in a year (v. 40). The verb tanot is disputed on the shelf: the KJV “lament”, the ASV “celebrate”, the NWT 1984 “give commendation”, the NWT 2013 “the young women of Israel would go”, the TNM 1987 “dar encomio”, and the TNM 2019 “elogiar”. The Mister reading “commemorate” keeps the uncertainty: the sense is praise, mourning or remembrance, and the text does not settle which.

Echoes paid. The question the Gilead princes asked in Judges 10:18, now on these pages, is answered here by the man they cast out. The argument of vv. 12–28 tells the story of Numbers 21:21–25, already on these pages. The spirit of Jehovah on Jephthah is the same phrase as on Othniel in Judges 3:10.

Echoes planted. Judges 12 (the quarrel with Ephraim, and the Shibboleth) is now on these pages. The chapter does not say what the vow was, and does not comment on the daughter.

Decisions. The verse 31 pronoun is kept neuter: “whatever comes out”. Yada in v. 39 is kept as “had known no man”, with the shelf readings beside it. Tanot in v. 40 is rendered “commemorate”, and the note keeps the dispute open.

⚠ A note on order: Judges stands on these pages at chapters 1–15. Judges 16 and the rest of the book remain open.
