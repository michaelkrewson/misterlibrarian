🕰
An old Joshua is told that very much land -- Philistia, the Lebanon coast -- still remains unconquered, and ordered to allot it to Israel anyway. The chapter then reads Moses' own Transjordan grant to Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh back into the record district by district: the Moabite plateau's cities, the Geshurites and Maacathites left undriven 'to this day', Balaam's death recalled in half a verse, and Levi's exclusion from any territorial share stated twice, at the chapter's opening and its close.
A legal/administrative chapter, not a narrated episode -- it gives no date or duration of its own and reaches back to grants Moses made years earlier (Numbers 32, Deuteronomy 3) rather than advancing the conquest's own timeline. It is the hinge between the conquest narrative (chapters 1-12) and the west-bank allotment by lot that chapter 14 begins. Same contested early/late-date question as the rest of the conquest (c. 1406 BC on the early date this project foregrounds, c. 1230-1210 BC on the late).

Now Joshua was old, advanced in years; and Jehovah said to him: You have grown old, you are advanced in years, and very much of the land still remains to be possessed.note
Y Josué era viejo, entrado en años; y Jehová le dijo: Tú has envejecido, has entrado en años, y queda todavía muchísima tierra por poseer.

Verses 1–7 · Joshua is old, the war is officially over — and Jehovah's first word is that it isn't

⚠ The 2013 revision quietly shrinks v1's quantity, and it is the kind of change that is easy to miss. KJV and ASV both read ‘there remaineth yet very much land to be possessed’; the NWT 1984 keeps the same force, ‘to a very great extent the land yet remains to be taken in possession’. Its own 2013 revision softens it to ‘much of the land remains to be taken in possession’ — dropping the superlative ‘very great extent’ for a plainer ‘much’. Both the TNM 1987 and its own 2019 revision keep the Spanish shelf's force intact on this one, «todavía queda gran parte de la tierra» and «todavía queda mucha tierra» respectively — so the softening shows up only on the English side of this one version's own two editions.

★★★ ‘There remains very much land to be possessed’ lands one chapter after this book said the opposite. Joshua 11:23 (now on these pages) closed the war narrative with ‘the land had rest from war’ — and Joshua 12's own closing note already flagged that Judges would spend a whole book turning that rest into a lease that keeps running out. This chapter does not wait for Judges: in its own first sentence, Jehovah tells an old Joshua that the land still unconquered is not a mopping-up operation but very much — the hill country of Lebanon, the whole Philistine coast, towns this book will never besiege. Rest from WAR and possession of the LAND are not the same claim, and this chapter is the first to say so out loud.

★★ The command to allot unconquered ground uses the same verb the Bible uses for casting a lot. Hapfileha, ‘cause it to fall’ (v6), is the Hiphil of napal, to fall — and that is not a stray metaphor: Esther 3:7 uses the identical verb for Haman literally casting Pur, ‘he caused the lot to fall’, and Nehemiah 11:1 uses it for Jerusalem's own residents drawing lots to fill the city. Jehovah tells Joshua to cause land Israel does not hold to fall to it as an inheritance — the lot-casting language of Joshua 14 and following (now on these pages), already running under a command given before a single actual lot is drawn, over ground no Israelite foot has yet stood on.

★ The Avvim are on this ‘still remaining’ list, and Deuteronomy already said they were gone. Deuteronomy 2:23 (already on these pages) states, without qualification, that ‘the Caphtorim, who came out of Caphtor, destroyed’ the Avvim and settled in their place — a complete displacement, reported as a fact of the past. This verse (v3) lists them anyway, decades later, among the peoples whose land Israel has not yet taken. Readings differ on whether a remnant survived the Caphtorim's conquest after all, whether this is a second enclave the earlier verse never claimed to cover, or whether the two notices simply come from different strands of memory about the same vanished people; this translation states both verses and does not harmonize them.

★ ‘All the regions of the Philistines’ (v2) shares its very root with a name this book has already used for something else. Gelilot, ‘districts, circuits’, is built on galil, the same root that gives ‘Galilee’ (ha-Galil) its name — the identical word-family Joshua 12's own closing note (now on these pages) just flagged in the Septuagint's ‘Galilee’ variant for the king of Goiim. Nothing ties Philistia's ‘circuits’ to the northern district by anything more than a shared root for a stretch of country — but it is the second time in two chapters this book's land-list vocabulary has brushed against that name.

The land north of Philistia is Phoenicia in all but name — Sidon, Byblos, the Lebanon range — and the chapter reuses this book's own war-geography to mark its edge. ‘Baal-gad under Mount Hermon’ (v5) is the identical northern limit Joshua 11:17 and 12:7 (both now on these pages) already gave the whole conquest, extended here one phrase further, to Lebo-hamath — the farthest-north horizon this project first named, and chose a reading for, three books ago at Numbers 13. See that chapter's note, and the Hamath encyclopedia entry, for why this translation keeps the place-name here rather than the more common ‘entrance of Hamath’.

Verses 8–14 · The east bank's grant recapped, the Geshurite border quoted a third time, and Levi's exception finally carried out

★★★ ‘The border of the Geshurites and the Maacathites’ is quoted here exactly as promised — the third and final time. Joshua 12's own note (now on these pages) already counted the phrase in two verses and named this one as the third: Deuteronomy 3:14 (already on these pages), where Jair's conquest first reaches it; Joshua 12:5, which copies it unchanged; and this verse (v11), which copies it a third time, still unchanged. Three uses, one phrase, and in every one of them the two peoples named by it are on the WRONG side of a border that is never actually crossed against them.

★★★ And v13 says outright what the border-phrase only implied: Israel never attacked these two peoples at all. Joshua 12's own closing notes (now on these pages) built a whole running theme out of kings struck but cities never actually held — Jerusalem, Gezer, Taanach, Megiddo, each confirmed unconquered by Judges 1. This verse is the harder case: the Geshurites and Maacathites were never even on a king-list to be struck. ‘Geshur and Maacath have dwelt in the midst of Israel to this day’ — the identical formula Judges will use of the Jebusites in Jerusalem — describes a conquest that never so much as began, named here in the very chapter that is supposed to finish dividing the land.

⚠ The verb at v13 splits the shelf between a failed expulsion and a flat refusal to try. The NWT 1984 reads ‘did not dispossess’; ASV reads ‘drove not out’; KJV reads ‘expelled not’, a slightly more active failure; the NWT 2013 reads ‘did not drive out’. On where the two peoples end up, KJV alone names them twice in full — ‘the Geshurites and the Maachathites dwell among the Israelites’; ASV shortens the second pair to the bare names, ‘Geshur and Maacath dwell in the midst of Israel’; the TNM 1987 does the same in Spanish, «Guesur y Maacat siguen morando en medio de Israel».

★★★ V14 is the payoff this project has been carrying since Numbers 18, and it lands twice in this one chapter. ‘Jehovah is his inheritance’ is a formula this project's own nachalah dictionary entry has tracked since Numbers 18:20 (already on these pages), where it is said to Aaron alone, and widened to the whole tribe at Deuteronomy 18:1-2 (already on these pages). Both of those are promises. This verse, and v33 at the chapter's close, are the promise ACTUALLY CARRIED OUT: the land is being divided, verse by verse, district by district, and Levi is the one tribe with no line in the deed — ‘the offerings of Jehovah… made by fire, are his inheritance’, the same two nouns, chelek and nachalah, that were taken from Aaron and handed back transformed, now written into the actual land registry rather than just spoken as consolation.

★ Og is still ‘the remnant of the Rephaim’, and this book keeps repeating the title rather than the story. Miyyeter ha-Rephaim (v12) is the identical phrase Deuteronomy 3:11 and Joshua 12:4 (both now on these pages) already used of the same king — a recap chapter recapping even its own recaps, Sihon and Og's defeat told a third time in six verses with no new detail added.

Verses 15–23 · Reuben's cities, almost all of which reappear centuries later in a funeral dirge for Moab

★★★ A cluster of Reuben's own towns reappear, by name, in the prophets' later oracles against Moab — because Moab got them back. This chapter gives Reuben Medeba (v9), Beth-baal-meon, Jahaz, Kiriathaim, Sibmah, and Beth-jeshimoth (vv17–20); Isaiah and Jeremiah's dirges for Moab name every one of them again, centuries later, mourned or ransacked (Isaiah 15–16; Jeremiah 48, none yet on these pages) — see each entry for its own citation. A chapter about Israel receiving this plateau is, read against the prophets, also the opening chapter of a story about Israel losing it.

★ Kedemoth names the ground Israel was standing on before it ever won this plateau. ‘The wilderness of Kedemoth’ is where Moses stood when he first sent peace messengers to Sihon (Deuteronomy 2:26, already on these pages) — so the town this chapter allots to Reuben (v18) is the war's own staging camp, now simply a line in the deed.

★★ V21 calls five men ‘princes of Sihon’ that Numbers 31 called ‘kings of Midian’ — the identical five names, under two different titles. Nesikhei Sihon, ‘the princes’ or ‘chieftains’ of Sihon, are Evi, Rekem, Zur, Hur and Reba — the exact same five names Numbers 31:8 (already on these pages) names ‘the kings of Midian’, malkhei Midian, killed in an entirely different campaign. Readings differ on what reconciles the two: that these five ruled Midian as independent kings and ALSO stood as Sihon's vassals, that one title is the earlier political reality and the other a later one, or that the two notices simply preserve two separate traditions about the same five men without settling their exact status. This translation states both titles and does not resolve which, if either, is the more original.

★★★ And the next clause is the death this project has been promising since the men who paid for it. Numbers 31:8's own closing note named this verse by number in advance: Balaam ‘they slew with the sword’, and this verse (v22) repeats the same three words independently, decades later, in an entirely different book — ‘the soothsayer… the sons of Israel killed with the sword among the rest of their slain.’ Ha-qosem, ‘the soothsayer, the diviner’, is the job title this project's own qesem dictionary entry has tracked since the fee Balak's messengers carried in their hands — the Bible's last word on Balaam is also its flattest: no oracle, no refusal, no angel, just a title and a verb.

Verses 24–28 · Gad's cities, a border that may or may not name a second city called Debir, and a valley of leopards and water

★★ ‘The border of Lidbir’ is the identical grammatical puzzle Joshua 12 just raised about a different name — and the shelf resolves it the opposite way. Joshua 12's own note on ‘Lasharon’ (now on these pages) found every shelf witness reading a prefixed lamed as fused into a proper name. Here the Hebrew has the identical construction — li-Dvir, literally ‘to Debir’ — and every version this project has fetched for this chapter (ASV, KJV, both NWT editions, both TNM editions) separates the preposition and reads ‘the border of Debir’, treating it as the ordinary name of a second, northern town rather than a fused place-name ‘Lidbir’. This translation follows that unanimous reading. ⚠ This is almost certainly NOT the same Debir Joshua 10 and 12:13 (both now on these pages) already placed in the southern hill country, near Hebron — two towns sharing one ordinary Hebrew word (‘sanctuary, oracle’) for a name, the same caution this chapter's own Tappuah and Hepher entries already carry forward from Joshua 12. The two are kept separate rather than linked to one entry.

★ And this northern Gilead has its own Lo-debar, a different spelling for what may be the identical neighborhood. 2 Samuel 9:4 and 17:27 (neither yet on these pages) place a town called Lo-debar — ‘no pasture’ — near Mahanaim, home of a second man named Machir (son of Ammiel, no relation to this chapter's Machir son of Manasseh), in the exact district this verse is describing. The consonants are close but not identical — li-Dvir against Lo Devar — and this translation states the possible connection without adopting it.

★ Mahanaim stops being a moment in Jacob's story and becomes a line on a map. ‘Two camps’ is where Jacob met the angels of God just before wrestling at the Jabbok (Genesis 32:2-3, already on these pages); here it is simply the corner where Gad's border (v26) and the half-tribe of Manasseh's (v30) happen to meet — the same geography, generations apart, read first as theophany and now as survey.

★ The valley list closes on a stretch of ground named for leopards and for water that will both, eventually, dry up. Beth-nimrah (v27) almost certainly shares its root with ‘the waters of Nimrim’, which Isaiah and Jeremiah both say will run dry when Moab falls (Isaiah 15:6; Jeremiah 48:34, neither yet on these pages) — the same valley this chapter gives Gad as fertile sheep-country, mourned as a wasteland in the same prophetic breath as Reuben's own lost cities three notes above.

Verses 29–33 · Sixty cities, a number the record cannot keep straight, and Levi's exception closes the deed exactly as it opened it

⚠ The shelf's own unit for v30's count splits old from new, the same seam this project has already met elsewhere. KJV and ASV both keep the old count-word, ‘threescore cities’; the NWT 1984 modernizes it to ‘sixty towns’, and its own 2013 revision keeps the same number but writes it as a numeral, ‘60 towns’. The TNM 1987 and its 2019 revision make the identical move in Spanish, «sesenta pueblos» both times, so the Spanish shelf shows no generational seam here where the English one does.

★★★ ‘Sixty cities’ (v30) is not new in this chapter, and it is not the last word on the number either. The identical phrase, ‘sixty cities’, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Deuteronomy 3:4 (already on these pages), where it first counts ‘all the region of Argob, the kingdom of Og in Bashan’ before any of it has a tribe's name attached; this verse, now crediting the same sixty to Jair's own villages inside that kingdom; and 1 Chronicles 2:23 (not yet on these pages), which uses the SAME number for the SAME villages but adds that ‘Geshur and Aram took Havvoth-jair from them… sixty cities’, centuries later — the number surviving a conquest, a land-grant and a loss without changing. Judges 10:4 (not yet on these pages), by contrast, counts a much later and explicitly different Jair's thirty cities, already flagged as a different man by this project's own Jair encyclopedia entry; and 1 Chronicles 2:22, two verses before the sixty, gives a FOURTH number for what reads like the same clan — Segub's son Jair with ‘three and twenty cities’. Readings differ on whether this traces one shrinking territory, several unrelated counts attached to one name by tradition, or a textual slippage this translation cannot resolve from the evidence alone; what is certain is that Deuteronomy, Joshua and 1 Chronicles all agree on the SAME sixty where Chronicles itself does not, four verses apart.

★ And v31 hands the SAME half-tribe its land twice over, naming Machir and not Jair for the Bashan towns Jair just finished renaming. V30 credits ‘all the villages of Jair’ to Manasseh's border; v31 immediately re-lists Ashtaroth and Edrei, inside that same Bashan, as belonging to ‘the sons of Machir’ — Jair's own father's clan, by the broad genealogical idiom this project's own Jair entry already flagged at Numbers 32:41 (already on these pages). The deed names the conqueror once and the clan he conquered for a second time, in consecutive verses, for the same ground.

★★★ V33 closes the whole land division on the identical sentence v14 opened it with — and this is the second half of the payoff this project has tracked since Numbers 18. ‘Jehovah, the God of Israel, is their inheritance’ (v33) repeats v14's own formula almost word for word, now said of Levi a second time and in the third person rather than the second — the exact widening Deuteronomy 10:9 (already on these pages) already performed from Aaron to the whole tribe. Two mentions of the same exception, one at the start of the deed and one at its close, is this chapter's own way of making sure the omission cannot be read as an oversight: Levi is skipped on purpose, twice, in the one chapter built entirely out of who gets what.

Echoes paid. Joshua 12's promise that ‘the border of the Geshurites and the Maacathites’ would be quoted a third time is paid at v11. Numbers 18:20 and Deuteronomy 18:1-2's promise that Levi's landlessness would be ‘actually carried out’ in this book is paid at vv14 and 33, twice over. Numbers 31:8's own forward note on Balaam's death is paid at v22, in the identical three words, ‘with the sword’. Numbers 32:38's unlinked Sibmah, Baal-meon and Beth-nimrah all receive their first encyclopedia entries here, their rebuilding finally matched to a land grant. And Genesis 14:5's Shaveh Kiriathaim, struck in Abram's own lifetime, is confirmed still on the map, and now Reuben's, at v19.

Echoes planted. Joshua 14 (now on these pages) is where the lot-casting this chapter's own ‘cause it to fall’ (v6) finally happens for real, west of the Jordan. Judges 10:3-4 (not yet on these pages) will count thirty cities for an entirely different Jair, against this chapter's sixty. Judges 11 (not yet on these pages) sends Jephthah home to a Mizpah in this same Gilead that this chapter's own Ramath-mizpeh may or may not be. And Isaiah 15–16 and Jeremiah 48 (neither yet on these pages) are where nearly every town this chapter gives Reuben turns up again, lost.

Decisions. Hebrew and English verse numbers align through the whole chapter, with no versification shift at this book's seam — checked directly rather than assumed from a matching count. ‘Jehovah’ continues this project's standing rendering of the tetragrammaton; nachalah is ‘inheritance’ throughout, matching Numbers 18 and Deuteronomy 10 and 18. At v5 this translation prints the place-name Lebo-hamath rather than the more common ‘entrance of Hamath’, following the reasoned decision already made at Numbers 13 rather than the unexamined default this project's own Numbers 34:8 (already on these pages) left standing — see the Hamath entry for the inconsistency this chapter does not silently inherit. At v26 this translation reads ‘the border of Debir’, separating the prefixed lamed rather than fusing it into a place-name ‘Lidbir’, on the unanimous evidence of every shelf witness fetched for this chapter — the opposite resolution from Joshua 12's ‘the Sharon’, where this project kept the region and let the shelf fuse the name. The Masoretic text marks a petuchah after v14, v23 and v32, and a setumah after v28 and v33 — reproduced as the source marks them. Balaam's title ha-qosem is ‘the soothsayer’, matching this project's own qesem entry; nesikhim at v21 is ‘princes’, kept distinct from the malkhim, ‘kings’, Numbers 31 used of the same five names, since the two Hebrew words are genuinely different and the note states the puzzle rather than silently harmonizing it.

Library. Twenty-two new encyclopedia entries in both languages: the peoples Geshurites and Maacathites (Transjordan); the places Mahanaim, Edrei, Jahaz, Kedemoth, Mephaath, Kiriathaim, Sibmah, Zereth-shahar, Beth-jeshimoth, Ramath-mizpeh, Betonim, Beth-haram, Beth-nimrah, Zaphon, Medeba, Beth-baal-meon, Hamath, the Shihor, Gebal, and Misrephoth-maim. Four existing encyclopedia entries extended, each in both languages: Jair and Ashtaroth (their own forward-citations of this chapter turned into live links), the nachalah dictionary entry (its own forward-citation of this chapter's vv14 and 33 carried out), and the Balaam entry (its promised death, now linked). No new entries for the Geshurite/Maacathite border towns Salcah or for Gilead's own unnamed cities, matching how Deuteronomy 3 and Numbers 32 already left bare names of this kind unlinked.
