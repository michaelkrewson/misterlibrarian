Three unrelated pieces sharing one chapter. The eternal lamp and the weekly bread of the presence, both restating instructions from Exodus almost word for word. Then a narrative interruption: the son of an Israelite woman and an Egyptian father blasphemes God's own name during a camp fight, is held in custody pending a direct ruling from Jehovah, and the ruling arrives as a general law of capital and personal-injury justice — the death penalty for blasphemy, life for life, eye for eye, tooth for tooth, one law for foreigner and native alike — before the man himself is taken outside the camp and stoned.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus. Its lamp and bread laws (vv1-9) restate Exodus 27:20-21 and Exodus 25:30 almost verbatim; its narrative section (vv10-23) is the one piece of case law generated by an actual incident in the wilderness camp, rather than delivered as abstract instruction. Still inside the same Sinai window as the rest of this block (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

Leviticus 24 meaning — the eternal lamp, restated word for word

Verses 2–3 repeat Exodus 27:20–21 (already on these pages) almost verbatim — the same command, the same closing formula, ‘a statute forever throughout your generations.’ This project's own ner tamid entry (already on these pages) already noted that phrase's own history, sealing the priesthood's first standing duty before Aaron and his sons even had garments. Here, restated in Leviticus's own voice, the same lamp still burns.

Bread of the presence — the ritual a promise from Exodus finally keeps

This is the chapter Exodus 25's own note on the golden table (already on these pages) said would come: ‘the fuller weekly-replacement ritual is developed only much later, at Leviticus 24:5–9’ — it arrives here. This project's own lechem panim entry (already on these pages) already named its New Testament afterlife: Mark 2 and Matthew 12 (both already on these pages) both cite David eating this exact bread at Nob (1 Samuel 21, not yet on these pages) to defend his hungry men, and Jesus reaches for the same precedent to defend his own disciples on the Sabbath. A loaf replaced weekly, eaten only by priests in a holy place, becomes, a millennium later, the strongest kind of argument Scripture can make for itself: not a loophole invented, but an irregularity already on the record.

A name too specific to be a parable

Nothing else in this book names a minor character's mother, her father, and her tribe — ‘Shelomith, daughter of Dibri, of the tribe of Dan’ (v11) is a level of specificity this narrative did not need to include if it were only illustrating a principle. The son of a mixed marriage, Israelite mother and Egyptian father, blasphemes during an ordinary camp fight; the text reads like a report of something that actually happened, not a hypothetical built to generate a law.

⚠ ‘Blasphemed’ (v11, vayikov) is a startling verb choice: its root, naqav, ordinarily means ‘to pierce, to bore through’ — and, by extension, ‘to specify precisely, to name outright.’ The man's offense was not vague impiety; he said the Name itself, aloud, as a curse. That is very likely the seed of a much later practice: Jewish tradition's own refusal to pronounce YHVH at all, substituting HaShem, ‘the Name’ — the identical word this verse already uses as a stand-in.

Eye for eye, restated as a court case rather than a rule

Verse 14's detail is easy to read past: those who HEARD the blasphemy lay their own hands on the offender's head before the community stones him — the identical gesture Leviticus 16:21 (already on these pages) uses to load Israel's sins onto the scapegoat's head, here turned back on the man whose own words caused the harm. Guilt, elsewhere transferred outward onto an animal, here returns to its own source.

Vv17–22 restate Exodus 21:24's own ‘eye for eye, tooth for tooth’ (already on these pages) — but this time the abstract law arrives attached to a concrete story, not floating free in a list of case-rulings. ⚠ V19's ‘injury’ (mum) is the identical word this project has already tracked disqualifying a priest from the altar (Leviticus 21, already on these pages) and an animal from the offering (Leviticus 22, already on these pages) — the same noun now doing a third job, as the measure of harm one person inflicts on another. KJV and ASV both keep ‘blemish’ even here, carrying the ritual-disqualification sense over into a case about one person injuring another; NIV shifts to ‘defect’. This translation breaks from all three: ‘injury’ names what mum is actually measuring in this third context — harm inflicted on a person, not a physical mark on him. And v22's ‘one law’ for foreigner and native-born restates, word for word in principle, the standard Exodus 12:49 (already on these pages) already fixed before Israel had even left Egypt.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 24 both run 1–23 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Four Masoretic paragraphs divide it: open after v4, closed after v9, open after v12, open after v23. Naqav ha-Shem, ‘pierced/specified the Name,’ is newly entered — the literal root behind ‘blasphemed,’ and very possibly the seed of HaShem as a later spoken substitute for the divine name itself. ‘Injury’ renders mum in its third distinct legal context this project has now tracked.

Echoes paid. Exodus 27:20–21's eternal lamp (already on these pages), restated here almost verbatim; Exodus 25:30's bread of the presence and this project's own lechem panim entry (already on these pages), finally given its full weekly ritual; Mark 2 and Matthew 12 (both already on these pages), whose own citations of this bread's precedent this chapter now grounds; Leviticus 16:21's hand-laying on the scapegoat (already on these pages), reversed here onto the offender's own head; Exodus 21:24's eye-for-eye formula (already on these pages), now attached to an actual case; Leviticus 21 and 22's own mum (already on these pages), extended to interpersonal injury; and Exodus 12:49's one law for native and foreigner (already on these pages), restated word for word in principle.

Echoes planted. 1 Samuel 21 (not yet on these pages), where David actually eats the bread this chapter institutes; Numbers 15:15–16 (not yet on these pages), which will repeat this chapter's own ‘one law’ formula a third time.

⚠ A note on order: Leviticus stands complete on these pages, chapters 1–27.
