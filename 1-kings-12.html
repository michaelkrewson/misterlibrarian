All Israel comes to Shechem to make Rehoboam king and asks him to lighten the hard service of his father. He forsakes the counsel of the old men for that of the young men and promises scorpions. The northern tribes answer with Sheba’s cry, stone Adoram, and make Jeroboam king; only Judah, with Benjamin, stays with the house of David. Shemaiah stops Rehoboam from going to war. Jeroboam, afraid of Jerusalem, sets up two calves of gold at Bethel and Dan, with priests who are not Levites and a feast in the eighth month.
On Solomon’s death, at the start of Rehoboam’s reign (1 Kings 11:43; 12:1). The chapter gives no year.

20

21

22

Y vino la palabra de Dios a Semaías, varón de Dios, diciendo:

“Say to Rehoboam son of Solomon, king of Judah, and to all the house of Judah and Benjamin, and the rest of the people, saying:note
«Di a Roboam hijo de Salomón, rey de Judá, y a toda la casa de Judá y de Benjamín, y al resto del pueblo, diciendo:
24

25

Y Jeroboam edificó Siquem en la región montañosa de Efraín y habitó en ella; y salió de allí y edificó Penuel.
26

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–5 · 1 Kings 12:1–5 Rehoboam at Shechem — “hard service”, Egypt’s word, and a heavy yoke

Why Shechem? “Rehoboam went to Shechem; for all Israel had come to Shechem to make him king”: David’s line reigns from Jerusalem, and here the son has to travel north to be made king. The verse does not say why, but the place has a history: Joshua gathered all the tribes there and made a covenant (Joshua 24:1, 25, now on these pages), and Joseph’s bones lie there (Joshua 24:32). Shechem had made a king before, Abimelech, with a bad end (Judges 9:1, now on these pages).

Living in Egypt, or back from it? “Jeroboam was living in Egypt”: the letters are וישב, which can read “he lived” or “he returned”. Kings has the first and adds “in Egypt”; the parallel has the second and “from Egypt” (2 Chronicles 10:2). Verse 3 then writes the next verb in two ways, “they came” as written and “he came” as read. The story has a further knot: Jeroboam speaks to Rehoboam at verse 3 and verse 12, but at verse 20 all Israel only then hears “that Jeroboam had returned”. The Greek Bible has no Jeroboam at verse 3, only “the people”. This page prints the Hebrew.

“Hard service”. “Your father made our yoke hard; and you, now lighten the hard service of your father”: apart from this verse, avodah qashah in this form stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, and all three are Egypt: “they made their lives bitter with hard service” (Exodus 1:14), Exodus 6:9, and “put hard service on us” (Deuteronomy 26:6, now on these pages). The tribes are telling Solomon’s son that his father treated them as Pharaoh did. The narrator had said “of the children of Israel Solomon made no slave” (1 Kings 9:22, now on these pages); this is the other side’s account. The word for “yoke” is ol. The ASV and the KJV have “grievous service”, the NWT 1984 “hard service”, and the NIV “the harsh labor”. Note that they offer terms: “and we will serve you”.

Verses 6–11 · 1 Kings 12:6–11 The old men, the young men, and “my little one” — the Hebrew has no word for finger

Two counsels. The old men “had stood before Solomon”; the young men “had grown up with him”. The word for them is yeladim, “boys”, and Rehoboam was forty-one when he began to reign (1 Kings 14:21), so the word says more than their age. The old men’s advice turns on one word, “servant”: “If today you will be a servant to this people and serve them … they will be your servants all the days”. It is the offer of verse 4 handed back. The ASV, the KJV and the NIV keep “a servant”; the Douay has “yield to this people”, and the TLB loses the word, “be good to them”. “He forsook the counsel”: it is the verb Jehovah uses of those who “have forsaken me” in the last chapter (1 Kings 11:33, now on these pages).

“My little one”. “My little one is thicker than my father’s loins”: the Hebrew is qotni, which with this sense stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and in the parallel (2 Chronicles 10:10). There is no word for “finger”. The ASV and the KJV supply “my little finger”, and so do the NIV and the NWT 1984; the Geneva has “My least part”. The young men are plainly being rude, and “loins” is the giveaway. Finger is one reading; some scholars hear a cruder boast, and the text leaves it there. This page prints what the Hebrew prints.

Whips and scorpions. “My father disciplined you with whips, and I will discipline you with scorpions”: the word aqrabbim is the ordinary word for scorpions, as in the wilderness of Deuteronomy 8:15 (now on these pages). Whether it names the creature as a figure or some barbed whip, the verse does not say. The ASV, the KJV, the Douay and the NIV have “scorpions”, the NWT 1984 “scourges”, and the Geneva “rods” and “scourges”.

Verses 12–15 · 1 Kings 12:12–15 “A turning from Jehovah” — a foolish king and a fulfilled word in one sentence

“Hard”. “The king answered the people hard”: qashah, the word of their complaint in verse 4, “hard”. They asked him to lighten what was hard, and he gives them more of it. The ASV and the KJV have “roughly”, and the NWT 1984 and the NIV “harshly”.

A turning from Jehovah. “For it was a turning from Jehovah”: the noun sibbah stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one, and the parallel uses a related form (2 Chronicles 10:15). The narrator holds two things together: Rehoboam chose, and it was from Jehovah. Neither cancels the other. The ASV has “a thing brought about of Jehovah”, the KJV “the cause was from the Lord”, the NWT 1984 “the turn of affairs”, the NIV “this turn of events”, and the Douay reverses the sense, “the Lord was turned away from him”. The word was spoken by Ahijah (1 Kings 11:31, now on these pages).

Verses 16–20 · 1 Kings 12:16–20 “To your tents, O Israel” — or “to your gods”? Adoram stoned, and Jeroboam made king

An old cry. “What portion do we have in David?”: Sheba son of Bichri blew the horn with nearly these words, “We have no portion in David, and no inheritance for us in the son of Jesse” (2 Samuel 20:1, now on these pages). That revolt ended with his head over a wall (2 Samuel 20:22). This one does not end. “Portion” and “inheritance” are chelek and nachalah, the words of the land. The people have turned land language on a dynasty.

Tents, or gods? “To your tents, O Israel!”: the Hebrew is le-ohalekha. The form stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and its parallel (2 Chronicles 10:16). The ASV, the KJV, the Geneva and the NIV have “To your tents”. The NWT 1984 has “To your gods, O Israel”. The two words use the same letters in a different order, and a Jewish tradition is reported to say that scribes moved them, but the text in hand says tents, and the end of the verse has Israel going “to their tents”. “Now see to your own house, David!”: the “house” Jehovah built him (2 Samuel 7:16, now on these pages) is thrown back at him.

Adoram. “King Rehoboam sent Adoram, who was over the forced labor”: of all the men to send. The name is Adoram, as in David’s roll (2 Samuel 20:24), and in Solomon’s he is Adoniram (1 Kings 4:6; 5:28, all now on these pages). The Greek Bible has Adoniram here. “Stoned him with stones”: the man who ran the levy dies at the hands of the levied. The NIV and the NWT 1984 have “Adoram”; the king who threatened scorpions “made haste to get up into the chariot”.

“To this day”, and Judah alone. “Israel has rebelled against the house of David to this day”: the verb is pasha, a word for breaking faith, and “to this day” is the narrator’s, writing from later. The ASV and the KJV have “rebelled”, the NWT 1984 “their revolt”, and the Douay “revolted”. “Except the tribe of Judah alone”: the “one tribe” of the last chapter (1 Kings 11:32, now on these pages). By verse 20 Ahijah’s word has happened.

Verses 21–24 · 1 Kings 12:21–24 Judah, Benjamin and the twelfth piece — Shemaiah stops a war, and a second telling in the Greek

The twelfth piece? “All the house of Judah and the tribe of Benjamin”: the sentence before said “the tribe of Judah alone”, and now there are two. The cloak in the last chapter had twelve pieces, ten for Jeroboam and “one” for David’s line, and one left over (1 Kings 11:30–32, now on these pages). Benjamin may be the answer, and the Greek Bible’s “two sceptres” may be counting the same way, but the Hebrew does not connect them.

“This thing is from me”. “You shall not go up or fight against your brothers”: Shemaiah appears here for the first time in Kings, and the king who would not listen to the people listens to this. The word for what happened is Jehovah’s own, “from me”, as the narrator said in verse 15. “They listened to the word of Jehovah”: the verb of the chapter. Rehoboam did not listen at verse 15; here he does, and the north stays lost. “From me” in this form stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and the parallel (2 Chronicles 11:4). The KJV has “this thing is from me”, the ASV “this thing is of me”, and the NIV “this is my doing”.

A second telling in the Greek. After verse 24 the Greek Bible carries a long passage with no Hebrew behind it in the text printed here. It tells much of the story again and differently. Jeroboam’s mother is Sarira, called a harlot, not the widow Zeruah (1 Kings 11:26, now on these pages); he marries Ano, the sister of Shishak’s wife; and it is Shemaiah, not Ahijah, who tears a new garment into twelve pieces. This page translates the Hebrew and reports the rest.

Verses 25–33 · 1 Kings 12:25–33 Jeroboam’s golden calves at Bethel and Dan — “your gods, O Israel”, and a month devised from his own heart

“In his heart”. “Jeroboam built Shechem … and built Penuel”: both had been destroyed, Shechem by Abimelech (Judges 9:45) and Penuel’s tower by Gideon (Judges 8:17, both now on these pages). Then the chapter goes inside his head: “Jeroboam said in his heart”. He had been offered “a faithful house” if he would listen (1 Kings 11:38, now on these pages). He does not mention it. His fear is that the law of the one place will work: if they go to “the place that Jehovah your God chooses” (Deuteronomy 12:5, now on these pages), their hearts will follow.

He too took counsel. “And the king took counsel”: the verb stands in twelve verses of the Hebrew Bible, three of them in this chapter. Rehoboam took counsel twice, and lost ten tribes. Jeroboam takes counsel once, and does what the next chapters will call his sin. The verse does not say with whom. The NIV has “After seeking advice”, and the Douay “finding out a device”, which is a different thought.

“Your gods, O Israel”. “Look, your gods, O Israel, who brought you up from the land of Egypt”: the last words stand in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: twice at the golden calf, “These are your gods, O Israel, who brought you up out of the land of Egypt” (Exodus 32:4, 8), and here (now on these pages). The narrator lets a reader hear it. The verb is plural in Hebrew, and so in both passages this page prints “gods”. The ASV, the KJV and the NIV have “gods”. The NWT 1984 has “Here is your God”, singular, though the verb it translates is plural. The word for “calf” is egel, the word that may lie behind the “round” top of Solomon’s throne (1 Kings 10:19, now on these pages). Aaron also made an altar and announced “a feast to Jehovah” (Exodus 32:5), and Jeroboam is about to do both.

Bethel, Dan, and “as far as Dan”. Bethel is where Jacob named the place (Genesis 28:19), and Dan had its own carved image and priests (Judges 18:30, both now on these pages). “The people went before the one, as far as Dan”: the sentence seems short of something, and the versions fill it. The KJV and the ASV add “to worship”, the NIV writes a longer sentence with Bethel in it, and the TLB drops Dan from the sentence. The Greek Bible adds that they left the house of the Lord.

Priests, and a month of his own. “Priests from among all the people, who were not of the sons of Levi”: the phrase miqtsot ha-am stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and 1 Kings 13:33 (now on these pages). The ASV has “from among all the people”, the NIV “from all sorts of people”, and the KJV has “of the lowest of the people”, which the Hebrew does not say. The feast “in the eighth month, on the fifteenth day” sits one month after the feast of Leviticus 23:34, the very feast at which Solomon dedicated the house (1 Kings 8:2, both now on these pages). “The month that he had devised from his own heart”: the verb bada stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible in this form; the Hebrew text prints the last word two ways, millevad as written and millibbo as read, and this page prints it as read. Nehemiah uses the same verb of a lie made up (Nehemiah 6:8). The chapter that opened at the place of the covenant closes with a king going up on an altar he made himself.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “hard service” for avodah qashah, as in Exodus 1:14; “my little one” for qotni, with no “finger”; “scorpions” for aqrabbim; “a turning” for sibbah; “tents” for ohalekha; “Adoram” here and “Adoniram” in 1 Kings 4–5, as written; “took counsel” for vayyivvaats, in both kings’ stories; “your gods”, plural, as in Exodus 32:4; “calf” for egel; “a house of high places” for bet bamot; “from among all the people” for miqtsot ha-am; and “devised” for bada.

Echoes paid. Shechem and the covenant, and Joseph’s bones (Joshua 24:1, 25, 32); Abimelech’s kingship there (Judges 9:1, 45); hard service in Egypt (Exodus 1:14; 6:9; Deuteronomy 26:6); “no slave” (1 Kings 9:22); the torn kingdom, the “one tribe” and the twelve pieces (1 Kings 11:30–32); Ahijah’s word, and “they have forsaken me” (1 Kings 11:31, 33); Sheba’s cry and his end (2 Samuel 20:1, 22); the house of David (2 Samuel 7:16); Adoram and Adoniram (2 Samuel 20:24; 1 Kings 4:6; 5:28); the widow Zeruah (1 Kings 11:26); the faithful house (1 Kings 11:38); the one place (Deuteronomy 12:5); Gideon at Penuel (Judges 8:17); Bethel and Dan (Genesis 28:19; Judges 18:30); the golden calf and its feast (Exodus 32:4, 5, 8); the calf’s head of the throne (1 Kings 10:19); and the feast of the seventh month (Leviticus 23:34; 1 Kings 8:2).

Echoes planted. The man of God from Judah at the altar of Bethel, and Josiah named in advance (1 Kings 13, now on these pages); priests from among all the people (1 Kings 13:33, now on these pages); Rehoboam’s age and reign (1 Kings 14:21); the Chronicler’s telling (2 Chronicles 10–11); and Nehemiah’s made-up lie (Nehemiah 6:8). The last three are yet to come in this translation.
