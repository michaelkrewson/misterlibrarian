Early in the morning the chief priests hold a council and hand Jesus over to Pilate, who is surprised by his silence. At the feast Pilate offers to release a prisoner; the crowd, stirred up by the chief priests, chooses the insurrectionist Barabbas over Jesus and calls for crucifixion. Soldiers scourge Jesus, dress him in purple and a thorn crown in mockery, then lead him out; Simon of Cyrene is pressed into carrying the cross to Golgotha, where Jesus is crucified at the third hour between two bandits under the charge 'The King of the Jews.' Darkness covers the land from the sixth to the ninth hour; Jesus cries out in Aramaic and dies, the sanctuary curtain tears in two, and a centurion calls him God's son. Women, including Mary Magdalene, watch from a distance; that evening Joseph of Arimathea, a council member, obtains the body from Pilate and buries it in a rock-cut tomb before the Sabbath.
c. AD 30, Friday of Holy Week, from dawn to the evening before the Sabbath — corresponding to Matthew 27's account of the same day, with the two Gospels' own hour-markers for the darkness and the crucifixion not fully reconciled (see this chapter's own note).

Verses 1–5 · Σὺ λέγεις Sy legeis — before Pilate: a second morning session, and an answer that answers nothing

⭐ A second council meeting, in daylight. Mark 14 already gave the Sanhedrin a night hearing before the high priest (14:53–65); this chapter opens with a separate symboulion, a consultation, held “immediately, early” — Mark’s own favourite adverb, though its first appearance in this Gospel is 1:10, not the very first verse. The Sanhedrin entry already flags that the Mishnah’s rule against a night capital trial is a document written some two centuries later, of uncertain force in the 30s. Whatever the answer, Mark records two sessions, not one: a nighttime interrogation and a morning meeting that Matthew (27:1) and Luke 22:66 (not yet on these pages) both also report — though Matthew is widely held to depend on Mark here rather than confirm it independently, so it is Luke’s version, on the more common account of how the Synoptics relate, that is the genuinely separate witness that something happened at dawn beyond simply marching the prisoner out the door.

“You say so” — the same evasive-affirmative Jesus never explains. Sy legeis answers “Are you the king of the Jews?” the way sy eipas will answer the high priest’s question in Matthew’s trial scene (26:64) and the way John’s Jesus answers Pilate at far greater length (18:34–37, not yet on these pages). It is grammatically an affirmation — “the words are yours, and they stand” — without becoming the plain oath Caiaphas wanted at 14:62’s Egō eimi. KJV and ASV both print ‘Thou sayest’ literally; NWT 1984 ‘You yourself say it’; NIV smooths it to ‘You have said so’. After this one sentence Jesus falls silent, and does not speak again until the cry from the cross at v34.

⚠ Pilate’s amazement opens a frame this chapter closes. Thaumazō, “to marvel,” is a reaction Mark’s own narrator gives to a character only four times in the whole Gospel — and two of the four sit in this chapter: Pilate marvels at the silence here, and marvels again at how quickly Jesus has died, v44. The same verb catches Jesus himself off guard once, at Nazareth’s unbelief (6:6, already on these pages), and a stronger compound of it, exethaumazon, “were utterly amazed,” catches the authorities once more at 12:17 — so this chapter’s pair belongs entirely to the Roman governor, bracketing the whole crucifixion between his two moments of not understanding what he is looking at.

Verses 6–15 · Βαραββᾶς… ἀνέσεισαν Barabbas… aneseisan — the amnesty, the choice, and a verb for a crowd being ‘shaken up’

Two men called by their crime, one by a name. Barabbas is identified twice over in one sentence: “the stasiastai,” the rebels, and “the stasis,” the rebellion. ⭐ Stasiastēs is a true hapax — it occurs nowhere else in the whole New Testament — and it names the very crime Rome executed people for on the cross Jesus is about to occupy. The offer Pilate makes is therefore not a choice between guilt and innocence; it is a choice of which convicted insurrectionist walks free.

⚠ “The crowd went up” is itself disputed. The verb printed at v8, anabas, “having come up,” has a well-attested rival reading, anaboēsas, “having cried out” — two Greek words built on the same root, ana-, deciding whether the crowd climbs toward the governor’s tribunal or simply shouts from where it stands. Either way the request that follows is the same: do as you always do at the feast.

“Stirred up” — a shaking, not a suggestion. Aneseisan (v11) is literally to shake something loose, the same image English keeps in ‘shook the crowd up’; the chief priests do to the people what an earthquake does to loose stone. NWT 1984 ‘stirred up the crowd’; ASV ‘stirred up the multitude’; KJV the same. Once shaken, the crowd supplies its own verdict twice over (vv13, 14), each time a bare imperative — Staurōson, “Crucify” — with no reason given the second time beyond “all the more.”

⭐ Pilate’s own diagnosis, stated once and never revisited. V10’s “he knew it was out of envy” is the narrator’s verdict on the whole proceeding, delivered in a single clause and then dropped — Mark does not have Pilate act on what he knows. Hand over continues here exactly as Mark 14’s closing note promised: the chief priests ‘handed him over’ to Pilate out of envy (v10), and Pilate now ‘hands him over’ to be crucified (v15) — the same verb, paradidōmi, moving the same body through a third pair of hands. See the paradidōmi entry, now extended to this chapter’s three occurrences.

Verses 16–20 · πορφύραν… προσεκύνουν porphyran… prosekynoun — the mock coronation, and the only other time this Gospel uses the word for ‘doing homage’

A courtyard full of Latin. Praitōrion and speira are both Roman loanwords — the governor’s residence and a cohort, up to several hundred men, “the whole” of them called in for the joke. Together with v15’s phragellōsas (scourged — from the Latin flagellum) and this chapter’s own kentyriōn (see the note at v38 below), the scene is dense with the vocabulary of Roman occupation in a Gospel already known for borrowing it more than the others do.

⭐ A wreath, not a diadem — and a verb this Gospel has used only once before. The stephanos entry already distinguishes the victor’s festal wreath from a monarch’s diadēma: no Gospel ever puts a diadēma on Jesus’s head, only a stephanos, the crown handed out at the games, twisted here out of thorns. And the “homage” the soldiers pay him, prosekynoun, is a specific, loaded verb — the one this Gospel keeps for formal obeisance, not the plainer ‘fell at his feet’ or ‘knelt’ it uses of a pleading leper (1:40) or a rich man running up with a question (10:17). Prosekynoun itself is used in only ONE other place in this Gospel’s entire sixteen chapters: the Gerasene demoniac, who “ran and bowed down before him” the moment he saw Jesus from a distance (5:6, already on these pages). A man possessed and terrified, and a cohort of soldiers mocking — the only two times this Gospel reaches for that particular word, and both times the gesture is genuine on one side and forced or contemptuous on the other, never simple reverence.

⚠ Purple here, scarlet in Matthew — and purple again in John. Mark and John both dress him in porphyra, royal purple; Matthew’s soldiers use a chlamys kokkinē, an ordinary scarlet military cloak (27:28) — the ready-to-hand garment rather than a genuine purple one, which real dye made expensive. Whichever the soldiers actually reached for, Mark and John agree on the word that reads as deliberate mockery of KINGSHIP specifically: purple was the color, not just any color. NWT 1984 ‘decked him with purple’; ASV ‘clothe him with purple’; KJV the same. Both Mark and Matthew open the plaiting with the identical participle, plexantes, having plaited — the two accounts share that much wording exactly, even where the cloak’s color parts ways.

“Hail, king of the Jews” borrows a real salute. Chaire is the ordinary greeting shouted to an arriving Caesar or a victorious general; the soldiers stage a whole coronation in miniature — robe, crown, sceptre-reed, acclamation, kneeling homage — performed as cruelty rather than ceremony. Everything the real thing would require is here except the truth of it, which Mark’s own narrator has already stated plainly at 1:1 and lets the reader carry through the joke.

Verses 21–24 · Σίμωνα Κυρηναῖον… Γολγοθᾶν Simōna Kyrēnaion… Golgothan — a stranger named twice over, and a name Mark stops to translate

⭐ Simon is named for his sons, not the other way around. Mark alone gives Simon of Cyrene a family: “the father of Alexander and Rufus” — two names that mean nothing to a first-time reader of this Gospel and everything to whichever community first read it, since naming a passer-by by his otherwise-unmentioned sons only works if the sons are known to the audience. Matthew and Luke both keep Simon; neither keeps the sons. Whether this Rufus is the Rufus Paul greets at Romans 16:13 (not yet on these pages), “the chosen one in the Lord, and his mother and mine,” is an old and unprovable guess — a common name, offered here as a real possibility and nothing firmer.

⭐ The verb that pressed him into service already has a file open. Angareuousin is the Persian-derived word for a soldier’s legal right to commandeer a civilian — the angareuō entry, built at Matthew 5:41’s “go the second mile,” already promised that “the verb returns on Simon of Cyrene.” It does: the word stands in exactly three verses of the whole New Testament, Matthew’s teaching about the mile, and then this same Simon-of-Cyrene moment told twice — here and in Matthew’s own account of it (27:32), the only other verse that reports this particular event. The soldier who once forced a Jewish villager to carry his pack for a mile now forces a bystander to carry a cross.

A name Mark will not leave untranslated. Golgotha is Aramaic for a skull, and Mark glosses it the instant he uses it, the same habit already seen at talitha cumi and ephphatha. See the Golgotha entry for what is and is not known about the site — including that no Gospel, this one included, ever calls it a hill.

⚠ Wine with myrrh, refused — not Matthew’s gall. Mark’s drink is esmyrnismenon oinon, wine drugged with myrrh, a mild narcotic; Matthew’s is wine mixed with cholē, gall, echoing Psalm 69:21 (not yet on these pages) more directly. Some read Mark’s cup as an act of mercy — a custom attributed to pious women, offering a condemned man something to dull the pain — which Jesus refuses, choosing to meet what comes with his mind clear. The library reports the custom as attested tradition rather than proven fact and lets both readings stand.

Casting lots, unquoted. V24’s soldiers divide his garments “casting a lot over them, who should take what” — the action of Psalm 22:18 (not yet on these pages), “they divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots,” without Mark actually quoting it. The psalm supplies the ACTION here and, on the reading many take, the very CRY at v34 below; whether Mark means the reader to hear the whole psalm behind both is a live question the text itself does not settle by naming its source.

Verses 25–27 · ὥρα τρίτη… δύο λῃστάς hōra tritē… dyo lēstas — the third hour, a charge in three words, and two more men named only by their crime

⚠ The third hour here, the sixth at v33 — and John disagrees with both. Mark times the crucifixion itself to roughly 9 a.m., the darkness to noon; John 19:14 (not yet on these pages) has Pilate still sentencing him “about the sixth hour.” Harmonizers have proposed different clock conventions (Roman civil time against the Jewish reckoning this Gospel uses elsewhere) and simple transmission error in one text or the other; the library records the disagreement rather than resolving it.

The charge is the whole case, in three words. Rome posted the crime above the condemned as a public notice, and the words Mark reports — “The king of the Jews” — are the very charge that has run under every exchange with Pilate since v2, printed now as fact by the authority that meant it as mockery. No Gospel’s wording of the inscription matches another’s exactly; Mark’s is the shortest of the four.

⭐ Bandits, again — a word Jesus already used of his own arrest. Lēstas is the word Jesus himself reached for at Gethsemane, asking the arresting party if they had come “with swords and clubs… as against a bandit” (14:48, already on these pages) — treated like a criminal then, crucified between two of them now. It is also the word John’s Gospel applies to Barabbas himself (already noted at his own entry), one word bridging the prisoner who walked free and the two men who did not. The positions themselves, right and left, are the exact ones James and John once asked for “in your glory” (10:37, already on these pages). The request is answered, on a cross, by two men who are not disciples at all.

Verses 29–32 · Οὐὰ… ἵνα ἴδωμεν καὶ πιστεύσωμεν Oua… hina idōmen kai pisteusōmen — three separate groups mock him with one argument, and it is the argument this Gospel keeps testing

⭐ The charge the trial could not make stick, thrown from the road instead. “You who would destroy the sanctuary and build it in three days” echoes the accusation two witnesses brought at the night hearing, whose testimony would not come out isa, matching one another (14:58, already on these pages) — a charge that failed as sworn testimony now succeeds as a taunt, needing no witnesses at all once it is simply shouted, though the wording here drops the “made with hands… not made with hands” contrast that made the original charge so strange.

Three groups, one demand. Passers-by, chief priests with the scribes, and the two men crucified beside him all mock him in the same shape: prove it by coming down. “He saved others; himself he cannot save” states, without meaning to, the Gospel’s own logic in reverse — that saving others is precisely what costs him the power to save himself, not evidence he never had it.

⭐ “That we may see and believe” is a bargain this Gospel has already refused once. The Pharisees at 8:11 (already on these pages) asked for a sign from heaven “to test him”; Jesus refused, flatly, that no sign would be given. Here the same trade is offered again — a visible act in exchange for belief — and Mark, consistent with his own Gospel, does not grant it: no descent, no proof performed on demand, only the events that follow.

Verses 33–37 · Ἐλωῒ ἐλωῒ λεμὰ σαβαχθάνι Elōi elōi lema sabachthani — the one Aramaic cry this translation will not spell the way it spells Matthew’s

⭐ Mark's own spelling is the stable one here, and this translation follows it even where it disagrees with Matthew's own page. Matthew 27’s note already lays out the crux in full: Matthew’s own manuscripts split between Ēli and Elōi, and that chapter’s translation prints Eli because the next verse’s mistaking him for Elijah needs it. The critical apparatus this translation follows for Mark shows no such split at this word — the Greek text used here reads Elōi cleanly, the fuller Aramaic form of the address. So this translation prints “Eloi, Eloi” in this Gospel and “Eli, Eli” in Matthew’s — the SAME saying, rendered two different ways on this site, because the two Gospels’ own manuscripts genuinely preserve two different words, and smoothing them into one would erase a real signal rather than a copying accident.

⚠ Which makes the “calling Elijah” line harder to explain here, not easier. Elōi sounds less like Ēliyahu, Elijah, than Matthew’s Ēli does — and yet Mark reports the identical mishearing at v35. The harder-to-explain reading is often the more likely original one in textual criticism, on the reasoning that a copyist has more reason to smooth away an awkward detail than to introduce one — and “Elōi” sitting beside a joke it fits less naturally than Matthew's “Ēli” is exactly that kind of awkward detail. The library states the puzzle rather than declaring it solved.

⭐ The NWT is not alone in harmonizing the two Gospels to “Eli” — but every formal, essentially-literal translation on this shelf keeps Mark’s own word. KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva, NIV, RV60 and the 1909 Reina-Valera all print “Eloi, Eloi” here; NWT 1984 and its TNM twins read “Eli, Eli” at Mark 15:34 as well as at Matthew 27:46, quietly matching the two accounts — and so, unsurprisingly, does the paraphrase The Living Bible, which is built to read smoothly rather than to preserve this kind of seam. This is the rare case where the usually-exact NWT sides with a paraphrase against its own habit of exactness, and the older, more literal shelf keeps the distinction — worth stating plainly against the general rule that the NWT and ASV are usually the tightest witnesses on this shelf.

No last words at all — only a cry. Mark records Jesus dying with phōnēn megalēn, a loud voice, and nothing articulate in it: exepneusen, “breathed out,” the ordinary word for expiring (the root English keeps in ‘expire’). Unlike Luke, whose Jesus commends his spirit to the Father with his last breath (23:46, not yet on these pages, sharing this same verb), and unlike John's finished word, Mark's Jesus says nothing at the moment of death itself — the Eloi cry stands alone, two verses earlier, unanswered.

Verses 38–39 · ἐσχίσθη… υἱὸς θεοῦ eschisthē… huios theou — the curtain torn, and the confession this Gospel has been waiting an outsider to make

⭐ The sky tore open at the start; the sanctuary tears open at the end — the same verb, twice only. Schizō, to tear or rip, stands in exactly two verses of this Gospel: the heavens are torn open at the baptism (1:10, already on these pages, where that chapter’s own note already tracks the pairing), and the sanctuary curtain tears the same way here. One Gospel, one verb, used nowhere else, for the sky opening over Jesus at his baptism and the sanctuary opening at his death.

Which curtain, Mark does not say — the same silence as Matthew’s. The katapetasma entry, written for Matthew 27:51, already states that neither Gospel specifies the outer curtain (visible from the court, a public sign) or the inner one before the holy of holies (seen once a year, a statement about access) — and that Hebrews 10:20 later reads it as the second. Mark’s wording, “from top to bottom,” is identical to Matthew’s and carries the same interpretive weight: a tear that starts at the top looks like an act done TO the sanctuary, not simply a consequence of anything happening below it.

⭐⭐ Every earlier voice to call him “Son of God” was supernatural; the centurion is the first plain human one. Mark 1’s own opening note already flagged that the title is textually uncertain in the Gospel’s own first sentence — the oldest witnesses split, and this translation prints the shorter reading there — and it named exactly where the title would return “decisively”: “on the lips of a Roman centurion at the cross, 15:39.” This is that verse, and what makes it land is who has said the words before him. The exact phrase “the Son of God” is spoken twice earlier in this Gospel, and both times by unclean spirits, recognizing him despite themselves (3:11; a demoniac's near-equivalent at 5:7, both already on these pages); the voice from heaven calls him “my beloved Son” at the baptism and the transfiguration (1:11; 9:7, already on these pages) without ever pairing the word with “God” in the same breath; the high priest only ASKS it, as an accusation (14:61, already on these pages), and Jesus answers for himself. No ordinary human being in this Gospel has simply stated it as a fact until now — and the one who finally does is a kentyriōn, another Latin loanword, unique to Mark among the Gospels (Matthew and Luke both use the native Greek hekatontarchēs for the same office, including in their own parallels to this exact verse). A Gentile executioner, using a Roman military title Mark alone bothers to transliterate, says plainly what every named disciple in the story never quite has. Whether Mark means it as a genuine confession or as one more line whose irony the reader alone can hear — the centurion has just watched a man die, not risen — the library states the ambiguity and does not resolve it. KJV ‘the Son of God’; NWT 1984 ‘God’s Son’, without the article, which Greek also lacks here.

Verses 40–41 · Μαρία… Σαλώμη Maria… Salōmē — three women named, watching from far off, where every named man has already fled

⭐ Named, where the men of chapter 14 were not. Mark 14’s closing note already counted this: in names, Mark is sparse through the arrest and trial — the woman at Bethany, the swordsman, the slave, the young man in linen, even the high priest, all go unnamed. Here, at the cross, three women get full names in one verse: Mary Magdalene, Mary the mother of James the younger and of Joses, and Salome. Every named male disciple has fled since 14:50; the ones close enough to be named now are three women watching from a distance.

⭐⭐ “James and Joses” — the identical pair of names this Gospel already used of Jesus’s own brothers. Mark 6:3 (already on these pages) names Jesus “the son of Mary and brother of James and Joses and Judas and Simon” — the same two brothers’ names, in the same order, as the two sons this Mary is identified by here. Whether this is the SAME Mary — Jesus’s own mother, named here by her other sons rather than by him — or a different Mary who happens to share both a common name and two sons’ names with Jesus’s family is a real and old dispute, not one this library will settle, and it is tangled up with a second, separate dispute this library will also not settle: whether the “brothers” of 6:3 are Mary’s own later children, or Joseph’s children from an earlier marriage, or cousins — three positions with real advocates across Christian tradition, associated (though not required to travel together) with the names Helvidius, Epiphanius, and Jerome. John’s own list at the cross (19:25, not yet on these pages) names Jesus’s mother AND a separate “Mary the wife of Clopas” standing together, which many take as the same woman named here and read as evidence for a distinct relative — though the identification of “Mary of Clopas” with “the mother of James and Joses” is itself an inference, not something either Gospel states outright. A plainer reading of Mark 6:3 taken entirely on its own would allow that she could be Jesus’s mother herself, watching from a distance and identified here by the sons who were not on trial rather than by the son who was. The library states the readings and the stakes each carries, and votes for none of them.

⚠ “The little/younger” James — a title that only makes sense if there are two. Iakōbou tou mikrou distinguishes THIS James from James the son of Zebedee, already named repeatedly in this Gospel; whether mikros marks his age, his height, or simply his lesser prominence in Mark’s own telling is genuinely unclear, and the shelf divides: KJV and ASV ‘James the less’; NWT 1984 ‘James the Less’ also. This translation keeps “the younger,” the more common resolution, while flagging that the Greek itself does not choose. Mark’s form of the second name here, Iōsētos, is also the shortened one, kept distinct from the full Iōsēph this chapter uses of Joseph of Arimathea two verses later; Matthew’s parallel (27:56) has the mother of “James and Joseph” in full, so the two Gospels’ own forms of the same name genuinely differ, not only this translation’s choice of how to render them.

“Minister” — the same verb, from Galilee to here. Diēkonoun is the verb of service Peter’s mother-in-law took up the moment her fever left her (1:31, already on these pages) and the verb Jesus used to define his own mission, “not to be served but to serve” (10:45, already on these pages). These women have been doing that same work since Galilee, and the text places them still doing it at the one moment nothing can be done.

Verses 42–47 · εὐσχήμων βουλευτής euschēmōn bouleutēs — a member of the very council that condemned him, asking for the body

⭐⭐ Joseph belonged to the very body that condemned him — Mark does not say he was in the room. Mark calls Joseph of Arimathea a bouleutēs, a member of the boulē — the same body named synedrion, council, at 15:1 and throughout 14:55. See the new bouleutēs entry: the word occurs in exactly two verses of the whole New Testament, this one and its parallel at Luke 23:50, and nowhere else. Matthew calls him only “a rich man… who had himself also been made a disciple” (27:57), and John has him a disciple only “secretly, for fear of the Jews” (19:38, not yet on these pages) — three different framings of the same man, none of them contradicting the others so much as choosing what to foreground. What Mark does NOT say is whether Joseph sat among the “all” who “condemned him to be guilty of death” at the night hearing (14:64, already on these pages) — membership in the council is stated, presence and vote at that particular session are not, and taking courage, tolmēsas, to approach Pilate reads as a man defying his own institution whether or not he personally sat in judgment that night.

“Waiting for the kingdom of God” — the same hope, on the lips of a man in power. The phrase echoes what this Gospel has had ordinary people, not councillors, hope for throughout; here it belongs to a man whose institution has just voted the opposite way. Luke’s parallel goes further, calling him a council member who “had not consented to their plan and action” (23:51, not yet on these pages) — the detail Mark leaves out, and precisely the one that would settle whether Joseph was there.

⭐ A borrowed cloth for a borrowed tomb. Joseph buys the linen cloth — the same word, sindōn, the young man left behind naked at the arrest (14:51, already on these pages), now bought new to wrap a body rather than abandoned by one running from soldiers. Mark 14’s closing note planted this exact echo — “a body will be wrapped in a sindōn (15:46)” — and it is now paid. The tomb itself is “cut out of the rock,” new enough that Matthew can call it Joseph’s own (27:60); Mark does not say whose it is, only that it existed and was near enough to reach before the Sabbath began.

Two Marys, watching where a stone was rolled — the note the next chapter opens on. The chapter ends exactly where Mark 14 promised it would: the women who watched from a distance at v40 are now close enough to see the exact tomb, keeping the appointment the next chapter’s spice-buying depends on.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: hand over for paradidōmi in all three verses where it sounds (vv1, 10, 15), continuing straight out of Mark 14; bandits for lēstēs, matching the word Jesus used of his own arrest (14:48) and John used of Barabbas, now applied to the two men crucified either side; Eloi, Eloi at v34, kept distinct from “Eli, Eli” in Matthew’s own chapter, because the two Gospels’ manuscripts genuinely preserve two different words and this translation will not launder that difference away; a crown (stephanos) rather than a diadem, since the Greek itself keeps the two apart; and council member for bouleutēs, naming Joseph’s office rather than softening it to a generic “councillor.” The chapter’s one deliberate gap is left showing rather than smoothed: there is no verse 28, printed with its own explanation rather than quietly renumbered, the same choice already made at 9:44 and 9:46.

Echoes paid. Mark 14’s closing note planted four threads, and all four land in this chapter: “early in the morning,” the fourth night-watch, opens v1; the charge about destroying and rebuilding the sanctuary in three days is thrown at him again at v29, echoing 14:58 minus its “made with hands” contrast; a body is wrapped in a sindōn at v46, the same word the fleeing young man left behind at 14:51; and the women who will come with spices to anoint him (16:1, not yet on these pages) are named and placed at the tomb’s door at vv40–47, in position for the next chapter to open on them. Beyond that chapter’s own promises, two older ones resolve here too: the heavens torn open at the baptism (1:10, whose own chapter note already tracks the pairing) are answered by the sanctuary curtain torn the same way at v38; and Mark 1’s own opening note, naming this exact verse as where “the Son of God” would return “decisively,” is paid at v39, where a human being finally states outright what unclean spirits (3:11, 5:7) and a question under oath (14:61) had only approached.

Echoes planted. The two Marys watch “where he was laid” at the chapter’s very last word, the exact question the next chapter opens by answering. Pilate’s amazement at vv5 and 44 has no third occurrence to pay off — a frame closed within this chapter rather than carried forward. And Joseph of Arimathea, waiting for the kingdom of God from inside the council that opposed it, is left mid-story: Mark says nothing more about him after v46.

Next in this book: chapter 16 — the women find the stone rolled away, a young man in white tells them Jesus has been raised, and this Gospel's own earliest ending breaks off at a sentence about fear.
