Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–7 · Saul’s age in 1 Samuel 13:1 — “a son of a year”, Jonathan strikes the garrison at Geba, Israel a stench to the Philistines, and the people in caves

“Saul was […] years old.” The verse is the formula the book later uses for kings, as in “David was thirty years old when he became king” (2 Samuel 5:4), but where the number of years should stand the Hebrew has ben-shanah, “a son of a year”: Saul was one year old. A number has dropped out, and the bracket marks where. “Two years” for the length of his reign is doubted as well, since the story that follows seems to need more; Paul gives Saul forty years (Acts 13:21, now on these pages), and Josephus says he reigned eighteen years while Samuel lived and twenty-two after his death (Antiquities 6.378, numbered 6.14.9 in Whiston). The main manuscripts of the Greek leave the verse out altogether, and Josephus too goes straight to the three thousand men. The shelf fills or keeps the gap in five different ways. The NIV has “Saul was thirty years old when he became king, and he reigned over Israel forty- two years”; the ASV “Saul was forty years old when he began to reign”; the KJV “Saul reigned one year”; the Douay “Saul was a child of one year when he began to reign”; the NWT 1984 “Saul was [?] years old when he began to reign”.

Jonathan and the garrison. Saul keeps three thousand men, two thousand with himself at Michmash and a thousand “with Jonathan”, who enters the story with no introduction. “And Jonathan struck the garrison of the Philistines that was at Geba”: netsiv, the word of the Philistine garrisons at Gibeah of God (10:5, now on these pages), and here too the Greek takes it as a man’s name, “Nasib the Philistine”. Saul blows the ram’s horn, shofar, “saying: Let the Hebrews hear!”, using the name the Philistines use for Israel (4:9; ivri); the Greek reads “The slaves have revolted”. The ASV has “And Saul blew the trumpet throughout all the land, saying, Let the Hebrews hear”; the NIV “Jonathan attacked the Philistine outpost at Geba”; the NWT 1984 “he had the horn blown throughout all the land”.

A stench to the Philistines. The report that goes round credits the father: “Saul has struck the garrison of the Philistines, and Israel has also become a stench to the Philistines”, niv’ash, from ba’ash. It is Jacob’s complaint after Shechem, “making me a stench to the inhabitants of the land” (Genesis 34:30, now on these pages), and the foremen’s to Moses, “you have made us a stench in the eyes of Pharaoh” (Exodus 5:21, now on these pages). This form, “to become a stench”, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in Samuel. The ASV has “Israel was had in abomination with the Philistines”; the NWT 1984 “now Israel has become foul-smelling among the Phi·lisʹtines”; the NIV “Israel has become obnoxious to the Philistines”.

Like the sand on the seashore. The Philistines bring “thirty thousand chariots and six thousand horsemen, and people like the sand that is on the seashore in multitude”, and camp at Michmash, “east of Beth-aven”, the landmark of the spies’ road to Ai (Joshua 7:2, now on these pages). “Like the sand that is on the seashore” stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: the promise to Abraham (Genesis 22:17, now on these pages), the kings drawn up against Joshua (Joshua 11:4, now on these pages), this army, and Solomon’s wisdom (1 Kings 5:9). Josephus keeps the thirty thousand chariots and adds three hundred thousand foot (Antiquities 6.97, numbered 6.6.1 in Whiston); three thousand chariots is a reading reported from other ancient witnesses. The ASV has “thirty thousand chariots, and six thousand horsemen”; the NIV “with three thousand chariots”; the KJV “eastward from Bethaven”.

Caves, and Hebrews crossing. “The people hid themselves in caves and in thickets and in rocks and in holes and in pits”, as Israel did before Midian, in “the dens that are in the mountains, and the caves, and the strongholds” (Judges 6:2, now on these pages). “And Hebrews crossed the Jordan”: ivrim avru, the name and the verb share their letters, and the Hebrews live up to it. Josephus says most of them fled across the Jordan, to the land of Gad and Reuben (Antiquities 6.99). The ASV has “in caves, and in thickets, and in rocks, and in coverts, and in pits”; the NWT 1984 “in the caves and the hollows and the crags and the vaults and the waterpits”; the KJV “in rocks, and in high places, and in pits”.

Verses 8–14 · Saul’s sacrifice at Gilgal — seven days, “What have you done?”, “I forced myself”, and “a man after his own heart”

Seven days. “And he waited seven days, to the appointed time that Samuel [had set]”: the instruction of 10:8, “Seven days you shall wait, until I come to you and let you know what you shall do”. The Hebrew has no verb after “Samuel”, and the bracket supplies one. In verse 8 the written text and the margin give two forms of the verb “wait”, and in verse 19 the written text has “he said” where the margin reads “they said”. Josephus has Samuel tell Saul to wait and prepare the victims, so that they could sacrifice on the seventh day and then fight (Antiquities 6.100, numbered 6.6.2 in Whiston). The ASV has “according to the set time that Samuel had appointed”; the NWT 1984 “to the appointed time that Samuel [had said]”, with its own bracket.

The burnt offering. “Bring to me the burnt offering and the peace offerings”, the two offerings Samuel had said he would come down to make (10:8), “and he offered up the burnt offering” (olah). “As soon as he had finished … look — Samuel came”, and Saul went out “to bless him”, the ordinary greeting. The chapter does not say whether the fault is that a king offered the sacrifice or that he did not wait; Samuel’s charge is that he did not keep “the commandment”, and readers have taken it both ways. The NWT 1984 has “Saul went out to meet him and bless him”; the ASV “that he might salute him”; the NIV “Saul went out to greet him”.

“What have you done?” Samuel’s first words are God’s question to Cain, “What have you done?” (Genesis 4:10, now on these pages), and the question stands in twenty verses of the Hebrew Bible. Saul gives three reasons: the people scattering, “you did not come within the appointed days”, the Philistines gathering. “I have not entreated the favour of Jehovah”: chilliti penei, literally “softened the face”. “So I forced myself”: va-et’appaq, the verb of Joseph, who “held himself in” before his brothers (Genesis 43:31; 45:1, now on these pages). The ASV has “I have not entreated the favor of Jehovah: I forced myself therefore”; the NWT 1984 “the face of Jehovah I have not softened”; the NIV “So I felt compelled to offer the burnt offering”.

“You have acted foolishly.” “You have acted foolishly”: niskalta, from sakhal, Laban’s word to Jacob, “Now you have acted foolishly” (Genesis 31:28, now on these pages). This form stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible, and one of them is David’s confession after the census (2 Samuel 24:10). The next sentences turn on one verb, tsavah, “command”: Saul did not keep “the commandment … which he commanded you”; the new man will be “commanded” to lead; “you have not kept what Jehovah commanded you”. The ASV has “Thou hast done foolishly”; the NWT 1984 “You have acted foolishly”; the TLB “You fool!”

A man after his own heart. “Your kingdom shall not stand”, where “Jehovah would have established your kingdom over Israel for ever”. “Jehovah has sought out for himself a man after his own heart”: the phrase ish kilvavo stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The man is not named; Paul applies the words to David, joined to a line from Psalm 89 (Acts 13:22, now on these pages). He is to be “leader”, nagid, Saul’s own title (9:16). The Hebrew is past, “has sought”; the Greek has the future, “will seek”. Josephus has Samuel say that Saul and his descendants would otherwise have reigned a very long time (Antiquities 6.104). The ASV has “Jehovah hath sought him a man after his own heart”; the NIV “has sought out a man after his own heart”; the NWT 1984 “Jehovah will certainly find for himself a man agreeable to his heart”, in the future tense.

Verses 15–18 · Six hundred men — Gibeah or Geba, and the raiders in three companies

Six hundred men, Gibeah and Geba. Samuel goes up “from Gilgal to Gibeah of Benjamin”, and Saul musters “about six hundred men” (paqad) out of the three thousand. The Greek is longer here: Samuel goes his own way, and the rest of the people go up after Saul from Gilgal to Gibeah. In verse 16 Saul and Jonathan are at “Geba of Benjamin”. Gibeah (verses 2 and 15) and Geba (verses 3 and 16) are two towns of Benjamin a few miles apart whose names differ by a single letter in Hebrew, and the shelf does not always keep them apart. The Greek adds that the six hundred sat there “and wept”; Josephus brings Saul to “the city of Gabaon” with only six hundred men, most of them without weapons (Antiquities 6.105). The ASV has “abode in Geba of Benjamin”; the KJV “abode in Gibeah of Benjamin”; the NWT 1984 “dwelling in Geʹba of Benjamin”.

The raiders. “And the raiders came out of the camp of the Philistines in three companies”: ha-mashchit, “the destroyer”, the word of the Passover night, “and will not allow the destroyer to come into your houses” (Exodus 12:23, now on these pages), and it stands in nine verses of the Hebrew Bible. “Three companies” is Saul’s own order of battle at Jabesh (11:11, now on these pages). The three roads run north to Ophrah, a town of Benjamin (Joshua 18:23, now on these pages) and not Gideon’s Ophrah; west to Beth-horon; and east over “the valley of Zeboim”, the hyenas, toward the wilderness. The ASV has “the spoilers came out of the camp of the Philistines in three companies”; the NWT 1984 “the force of pillagers would sally forth”; the NIV “Raiding parties went out from the Philistine camp”.

Verses 19–23 · No smith in all Israel — sharpening at the Philistines’, the pim weight, and two swords for an army

No smith. “And no smith was to be found in all the land of Israel”: charash, a worker in hard material, “for the Philistines said, Lest the Hebrews make sword or spear”. The Greek reads “a smith of iron”. Josephus says the Philistines had taken away the Israelites’ weapons, forbidden them to carry or use iron, and made farmers come to them to have a ploughshare or a mattock repaired (Antiquities 6.96, numbered 6.6.1 in Whiston). The ASV has “there was no smith found throughout all the land of Israel”; the NWT 2013 “there was not a metalworker to be found”; the NIV “Not a blacksmith could be found”.

Ploughshare, mattock, axe and hoe. “Each man to sharpen his ploughshare and his mattock and his axe and his hoe”: the first word and the last, macharesheth, have the same letters in Hebrew and are pointed differently, and what each tool was is not certain; the Greek makes the last a sickle. The ASV has “his share, and his coulter, and his axe, and his mattock”; the NWT 1984 “his plowshare or his mattock or his ax or his sickle”; the Douay “his ploughshare, and his spade, and his axe, and his rake”.

The pim. “And the charge for sharpening was a pim”: both words were lost to later readers, and the older versions guessed. The answer came out of the ground, where small stone weights inscribed pym have been found, at about two-thirds of a shekel (reported here; the note on Leviticus 19:36, now on these pages, makes the same point about weights); pim was the price. “A third” is most often taken as a third of a shekel, for the forks and the axes, and others read “three-pronged forks”. The Greek has lost the sense entirely and speaks of a vintage ready to harvest and three shekels a tooth. The KJV has “Yet they had a file for the mattocks”; the NWT 1984 “the price for sharpening proved to be a pim for the plowshares”; the NIV “The price was two-thirds of a shekel”.

Two swords. “On the day of battle neither sword nor spear was found in the hand of any of the people who were with Saul and Jonathan; but they were found with Saul and with Jonathan his son.” Then “the outpost of the Philistines went out to the pass of Michmash”, matsav, the post Jonathan will climb to in the next chapter. The ASV has “And the garrison of the Philistines went out unto the pass of Michmash”; the NWT 1984 “an outpost of the Phi·lisʹtines would sally forth to the ravine pass of Michʹmash”; the TLB “except for Saul’s and Jonathan’s”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “[…] years old”, the gap in 13:1 marked rather than filled; “garrison” for netsiv, as in 10:5, and “outpost” for matsav; “the ram’s horn”; “a stench”, as in Genesis 34:30; “Hebrews”; “to the appointed time that Samuel [had set]”; “What have you done?”, as in Genesis 4:10; “you have acted foolishly”, as in Genesis 31:28; “commanded” kept through verses 13–14; “leader” for nagid; “a man after his own heart”; “the raiders” for ha-mashchit; “three companies”, as in 11:11; and “pim”, left as a name.

Echoes paid. The seven days at Gilgal set in 10:8 are kept and broken; the Philistine garrisons of 10:5 are struck; the leader of 9:16 is promised a successor; Saul’s three companies of 11:11 come back as the enemy’s; and Samuel’s warning that people and king could be swept away (12:25) begins to come true.

Echoes planted. Jonathan and the outpost at the pass of Michmash (1 Samuel 14), and the man after Jehovah’s heart (1 Samuel 16). Neither is translated here yet.
