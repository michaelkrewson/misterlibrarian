🕰
The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. The argument takes Abraham as its test case: what Genesis says he found, the reckoning of Genesis 15:6 and David’s blessing in Psalm 32, the order of reckoning and circumcision, the promise and the law, the faith of a man a hundred years old, and the believers for whom the sentence was also written.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 3; this library dates the letter no more closely than those chapters do. The ages in the story Paul tells are Genesis’s: Abram is seventy-five when he leaves Haran, about eighty-five when the reckoning is placed before Hagar, ninety-nine at his circumcision and a hundred when Isaac is born.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · τί οὖν ἐροῦμεν … εὑρηκέναι … προπάτορα … κατὰ σάρκα … καύχημα … πρὸς θεόν ti oun eroumen … heurēkenai … propatora … kata sarka … kauchēma … pros theon — Romans 4:1–2: what did Abraham “find,” and did he have anything to boast about?

“What, then, shall we say…?” (Romans 4:1). Ti oun eroumen heurēkenai Abraam ton propatora hēmōn kata sarka? The question picks up where Romans 3:27–31 stopped. If boasting has been shut out by “a law of faith,” and law is established and not abolished, the test case is the man any reader of the Law would name first. The formula ti oun eroumen stands in six verses, all in Romans: here, 8:31, and 6:1, 7:7, 9:14 and 9:30 (those four not yet on these pages). Propatōr, “forefather,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament (propatōr).

⚠ “According to the flesh” can go with two different words, and the manuscripts move it. The printed Greek puts kata sarka directly after “our forefather,” which makes it a statement about descent: the forefather in the flesh, as Romans 1:3 says of David’s line. The apparatus shows the verb heurēkenai, “has found,” in different places: Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have it where it is printed here; Westcott–Hort omit it; Robinson–Pierpont put it after hēmōn, so that kata sarka stands right beside “found” and the question becomes what Abraham found by fleshly means. The same apparatus gives propatora, “forefather,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and patera, “father,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The shelf lines up with the split. ASV “hath found according to the flesh” and Geneva “hath found concerning the flesh” put the phrase beside the verb; NIV “our forefather according to the flesh” and Douay “who is our father according to the flesh” put it with Abraham; KJV “as pertaining to the flesh, hath found” sets it in commas just before the verb; NWT 1984 “what shall we say about Abraham” has no verb at all. TLB “humanly speaking, the founder of our Jewish nation” turns the phrase into a description of descent and then adds a question the Greek does not ask. The word “forefather” is printed by ASV, NIV and NWT, and “father” by KJV, Geneva and Douay.

“Something to boast about” (kauchēma, Romans 4:2). Ei gar Abraam ex ergōn edikaiōthē, echei kauchēma; all’ ou pros theon. Chapter 3 asked “Where, then, is the boasting?” (3:27), with the noun kauchēsis, the act. Here the noun is kauchēma, a thing to boast about, a ground for it. It stands in eleven verses of the New Testament and this is the only one in Romans (kauchēma; the perfect verb at 2 Corinthians 7:14 (not yet on these pages) matches the same letters and is not counted). The verb behind both nouns has already been used of the teacher who “boasts in God” (2:17) and “boasts in the law” (2:23). KJV “he hath whereof to glory,” ASV “he hath whereof to glory” and Douay “he hath whereof to glory” use the old verb “glory”; NIV “he had something to boast about” and TLB “he would have something to boast about” use the plain one; NWT 1984 “ground for boasting” keeps the noun; Geneva “wherein to rejoice” moves it toward joy.

“But not before God” (pros theon). The clause has no verb: all’ ou pros theon, “but not toward God.” Paul does not say Abraham had nothing to boast about, only that it would not count where God is the audience; the conditional (“if Abraham was justified by works”) is left standing and answered by the Scripture in verse 3. The verb is edikaiōthē, “was justified,” the verb of Romans 3:28, with “works” here and no “of law”: the argument has left Sinai out and begins before it. KJV “but not before God,” NIV “but not before God” and Douay “but not before God” take the preposition as the audience; ASV “but not toward God” keeps the direction; NWT 1984 “but not with God” and Geneva “but not with God” make it a matter of standing with him. The verb of verse 2 is printed “justified” by the other literal versions and “were declared righteous” by NWT 1984.

Verses 3–5 · ἐπίστευσεν … ἐλογίσθη αὐτῷ εἰς δικαιοσύνην … ὀφείλημα … τὸν δικαιοῦντα τὸν ἀσεβῆ episteusen … elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn … opheilēma … ton dikaiounta ton asebē — Romans 4:3–5: what does “reckoned to him as righteousness” (Genesis 15:6) mean, and who is “the one who justifies the ungodly”?

“And Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” (Romans 4:3). Episteusen de Abraam tō theō kai elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn. The sentence is Genesis 15:6, and the Greek of it is the Greek Old Testament’s own: Rahlfs’s text reads kai episteusen Abram tō theō kai elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn, so Paul’s wording differs only in having de where the Greek Genesis has kai, and in the spelling of the name (Genesis 15 still says Abram). The Hebrew is shorter and stranger: vehe’emin baYHVH; vayachshveha lo, tsedaqah, “and he believed in Jehovah; and he counted it to him, righteousness,” with no word for “as” before tsedaqah and no named subject for “he counted.” (The Genesis page prints “and he counted it to him as righteousness,” supplying the “as” and leaving the subject as unnamed as the Hebrew does; Paul’s passive, “it was reckoned,” names no one either.) The verb form vayachshveha, “and he counted it,” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, Genesis 38:15, where Judah “thought her a prostitute,” and 1 Samuel 1:13, where Eli “thought she was drunk.” In those two a man forms an opinion of a woman from what he sees; here the one forming the opinion is God, and what he is given to see is a man believing a promise. The Greek has eis, “as,” before “righteousness”; the Hebrew of Genesis has no such word, while Psalm 106:31 (not yet on these pages), said of Phinehas, has the same sentence with the preposition: vatechashev lo litsdaqah. Rahlfs’s Greek has the sentence elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn in three places: Genesis 15:6, the Psalm (105:31 in the Greek numbering), and 1 Maccabees 2:52, which asks “Was not Abraham found faithful in testing, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness?” (the word heurethē, “was found,” is the verb of verse 1). The Psalm and 1 Maccabees are not on these pages. KJV “Abraham believed God, and it was counted unto him for righteousness” and ASV “it was reckoned unto him for righteousness” print “for”; NIV “it was credited to him as righteousness” and NWT 1984 “it was counted to him as righteousness” print “as.” NWT 1984 also has “Abraham exercised faith in Jehovah”: the Greek of this verse reads theō, “God,” and the name is the Hebrew’s. TLB “that is why God canceled his sins” says what verses 7–8 say, not what verse 3 does.

⚠ One verb, eleven verses. The Greek verb logizomai, “to reckon, to enter to an account,” stands in eleven verses of this chapter, one use in each: verses 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 22, 23 and 24 (logizomai). It is the verb of Romans 3:28, “we reckon that a person is justified by faith,” and this translation prints “reckon” at all twelve (the dictionary entry logizomai follows the verb). It returns as “I reckon” at Romans 8:18 and in the passive at 8:36, where believers “were reckoned as sheep for slaughter.” The shelf varies. ASV prints “reckon” in all eleven verses. KJV uses three English verbs: “counted” in verses 3 and 5, “reckoned” in verses 4, 9 and 10, and “impute” in verses 6, 8, 11, 22, 23 and 24. Geneva has “counted” in verses 3–5 and “impute” in the other eight, and its own summary of the chapter says Paul “beateth upon this word, Imputation,” ten times; the Greek has it in eleven verses. Douay has “reputed” in nine verses, “reckoned” at verse 4 and “imputed” at verse 8. NIV prints “credited” in eight verses, “credits” or “credit” in two more, and “count against them” at verse 8, so the reader of NIV never sees that verse 8 is the same verb. NWT 1984 has “counted” or “counts” in ten verses and “take into account” at verse 8.

“According to grace” and “according to debt” (verse 4). Tō de ergazomenō ho misthos ou logizetai kata charin alla kata opheilēma. A wage is reckoned, and the wage is a debt: opheilēma, “what is owed,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Matthew 6:12, “forgive us our debts” (opheilēma). The picture is a ledger with two possible entries, a gift or a debt, and the Greek puts them in the same grammar: kata charin, kata opheilēma. Douay “according to grace, but according to debt” is the only version on the shelf that keeps the repeated “according to.” KJV “of grace, but of debt,” Geneva “by favor, but by debt,” NIV “as a gift but as an obligation” and NWT 1984 “as an undeserved kindness, but as a debt” each choose their own pair of prepositions; NWT 1984 prints charis as “undeserved kindness” throughout the chapter.

⚠ “The one who justifies the ungodly” (ton dikaiounta ton asebē, verse 5). The phrase reverses a prohibition. The Hebrew of Exodus 23:7 is ki lo atsdiq rasha, “for I will not justify the wicked” (the page prints “I will not acquit the guilty”), and the Greek Old Testament has ou dikaiōseis ton asebē, with the verb and the noun Paul uses. The same pair, “justify” and “wicked,” stands in the Hebrew of Proverbs 17:15, matsdiq rasha (“he who justifies the wicked and he who condemns the righteous, both are an abomination to Jehovah”), and of Isaiah 5:23, matsdiqei rasha ekev shochad (“who justify the wicked for a bribe”), whose Greek is hoi dikaiountes ton asebē; neither yet on these pages. In each, justifying the wicked is what Jehovah refuses to do or condemns. Paul puts it of God, on purpose, and the way he can is not given here but in 3:26: “so that he himself may be righteous and the one who justifies the one who has faith in Jesus.” The same family gives 1:18 its “ungodliness” (asebeia), and the adjective asebēs returns at Romans 5:6, “Christ died for the ungodly.” KJV “believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly” and ASV “believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly” keep “believe on,” the construction of Acts 16:31; Douay “believeth in him that justifieth the ungodly” and Geneva “believeth in him that justifieth the ungodly” say “in”; NIV “trusts God who justifies the ungodly” replaces the construction with a verb of trust; NWT 1984 “declares the ungodly one righteous” renders “justify” as “declare righteous,” as it does in verse 2; TLB “declares sinners to be good in his sight” widens “the ungodly” to “sinners.”

Verses 6–8 · μακαρισμόν … χωρὶς ἔργων … ἀφέθησαν αἱ ἀνομίαι … ἐπεκαλύφθησαν … οὐ μὴ λογίσηται κύριος makarismon … chōris ergōn … aphethēsan hai anomiai … epekalyphthēsan … ou mē logisētai kyrios — Romans 4:6–8: David’s blessing from Psalm 32, and the man whose sin is not reckoned

“David also speaks of the blessedness…” (Romans 4:6). Kathaper kai Dauid legei ton makarismon tou anthrōpou hō ho theos logizetai dikaiosynēn chōris ergōn. A second witness is called, and it is chosen by its verb. The noun makarismos, “the pronouncing of someone blessed,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, verse 9, and Galatians 4:15 (not yet on these pages) (makarismos). The dictionary entry makarios follows the adjective that the quotation itself uses. The Psalm is quoted for its last verb: Genesis 15:6 says God reckoned righteousness, and Psalm 32:2 (not yet on these pages) says Jehovah does not reckon sin. In Hebrew the two verbs are the one root, chashav (vayachshveha in Genesis, lo yachshov in the Psalm), and in the Greek Old Testament both are logizomai; the chain between a verse from the Law and a verse from the Psalms rests on that word. Chōris ergōn, “apart from works,” is the construction of 3:21, “apart from law,” and 3:28, “apart from works of law.” KJV “describeth the blessedness of the man” and Geneva “declareth the blessedness of the man” keep the noun; ASV “pronounceth blessing upon the man” turns it into a verb phrase; NWT 1984 “speaks of the happiness of the man” and TLB “describing the happiness” choose “happiness.”

“Blessed are those whose lawless acts have been forgiven” (Romans 4:7–8). The quotation is Psalm 32:1–2 (31:1–2 in the Greek numbering; Psalm 32 is not yet on these pages), headed in the Hebrew “Of David. Maskil” with no occasion given. Paul’s words are Rahlfs’s Greek text word for word, the accent on anēr aside: makarioi hōn aphethēsan hai anomiai kai hōn epekalyphthēsan hai hamartiai; makarios anēr hou ou mē logisētai kyrios hamartian. The Greek goes on, oude estin en tō stomati autou dolos, “nor is there guile in his mouth,” where the Hebrew has ve’ein beruchō remiyah, “and in whose spirit there is no guile”; Paul stops before it. The psalm whose heading ties it to David’s sin is another, Psalm 51, which names Nathan and Bathsheba. The Hebrew of the first line is nesui fesha, “whose transgression is lifted,” and the Greek has aphethēsan, “were let go,” the verb of “forgive us our debts” at Matthew 6:12. The second line, kesui chata’ah, “covered of sin,” becomes epekalyphthēsan, “were covered,” and that passive stands in this verse alone in the New Testament (epekalyphthēsan). Verse 7 puts two words for wrongdoing side by side, anomiai, “lawless acts,” and hamartiai, “sins”; the first is the word of the dictionary entry anomia. KJV “whose iniquities are forgiven,” ASV “whose iniquities are forgiven,” Geneva “whose iniquities are forgiven” and Douay “whose iniquities are forgiven” print “iniquities”; NIV “whose transgressions are forgiven” prints “transgressions”; NWT 1984 “whose lawless deeds have been pardoned” is the one version that keeps the a-nomia in the English; TLB “whose sins are forgiven and put out of sight” folds the two lines into one.

⚠ “Whose sin” or “to whom”: one letter in the apparatus. At verse 8 the apparatus gives hou, “whose,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and hō, “to whom,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The shelf follows the split: KJV “to whom the Lord will not impute sin,” ASV “to whom, the Lord will not reckon sin,” Geneva “to whom the Lord imputeth not sin” and Douay “to whom the Lord hath not imputed sin” say “to whom”; NIV “whose sin the Lord will never count against them” and NWT 1984 “whose sin Jehovah will by no means take into account” say “whose.” Either way the man is the same, and verse 6 has already said so from the other side: the person to whom God reckons righteousness is the man to whom he does not reckon sin. The double negative ou mē is the emphatic form of “will not”: NIV “never” and NWT 1984 “by no means” carry it, and the others print a plain “will not.” The Greek reads kyrios, “Lord,” for the Hebrew YHVH, and this translation prints “Jehovah” for it, because the Psalm is naming the God of Israel. In the introduction to the quotation Paul says anthrōpos, “person” (verse 6); the quotation says anēr, “man,” for the Hebrew adam.

Verses 9–12 · ἐν περιτομῇ … ἐν ἀκροβυστίᾳ … σημεῖον … σφραγῖδα … πατέρα … τοῖς στοιχοῦσιν τοῖς ἴχνεσιν en peritomē … en akrobystia … sēmeion … sphragida … patera … tois stoichousin tois ichnesin — Romans 4:9–12: was Abraham reckoned righteous before or after circumcision, and what is a “seal”?

“In circumcision, or in uncircumcision?” (Romans 4:9–10). Pōs oun elogisthē? En peritomē onti ē en akrobystia? Ouk en peritomē all’ en akrobystia. The question of verse 9 is whether David’s blessing falls on “the circumcision” or also on “the uncircumcision,” the two names for Jews and Gentiles that Romans 3:30 ended on, and Paul answers it from the order of events in Genesis. The Greek says “the circumcision, or also the uncircumcision” and does not say “only”; KJV “upon the circumcision only,” Geneva “upon the circumcision only” and NIV “only for the circumcised” add it, and ASV “upon the circumcision, or upon the uncircumcision also” does not. The apparatus gives verse 9’s gar, “for,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and adds hoti after it for Robinson–Pierpont, which is the “we say that” of KJV “we say that faith was reckoned.” Verse 10 puts the question in abstract nouns, “in circumcision, or in uncircumcision,” meaning the state of being circumcised or not, and four versions keep the form: KJV “when he was in circumcision, or in uncircumcision,” ASV “when he was in circumcision, or in uncircumcision,” Douay “When he was in circumcision, or in uncircumcision” and NWT 1984 “When he was in circumcision or in uncircumcision.” NIV “after he was circumcised, or before” turns the state into a sequence, which is what the answer then proves.

⚠ The sequence is in Genesis, and it is longer than a night. The reckoning is Genesis 15:6. Hagar is given to Abram “after Abram had lived ten years in the land of Canaan” (Genesis 16:3), and he left Haran at seventy-five (12:4), so the text puts the reckoning at about eighty-five at the latest; the circumcision comes at ninety-nine (17:24). That is a gap of at least fourteen years by the text’s own ages. The weight of the sign is also in Genesis: an uncircumcised male “shall be cut off from his people; he has broken my covenant” (17:14), so a reader of the Law who hears “not in circumcision” hears something bold.

“A sign, circumcision, a seal” (Romans 4:11). Kai sēmeion elaben peritomēs, sphragida tēs dikaiosynēs tēs pisteōs tēs en tē akrobystia. Genesis 17:11 calls circumcision a sign of a covenant (in the Greek Old Testament en sēmeiō diathēkēs). The Hebrew phrase le’ot berit stands in two verses of Genesis, there and at 9:13, where it is the bow in the cloud. Paul says what the sign is a sign of, and he adds a word the account of circumcision does not have: the Hebrew root for a seal, chotam, stands in two verses of Genesis, 38:18 and 38:25, where it is Judah’s seal left as a pledge, and not in chapter 17. A seal confirms something already in force, and the sentence says what is already in force: the righteousness of the faith that he had in uncircumcision. The genitive peritomēs can be read as apposition, “a sign, namely circumcision,” and this translation prints that; KJV “the sign of circumcision,” ASV “the sign of circumcision,” Douay “the sign of circumcision” and Geneva “the sign of circumcision” keep the genitive; NWT 1984 “a sign, namely, circumcision” and NIV “circumcision as a sign” take the apposition. TLB “The circumcision ceremony was a sign” makes it a ceremony. The result clauses run in an order worth noticing: Abraham is made father first of “all who believe through uncircumcision” (verse 11) and then of the circumcised (verse 12), the reverse of “to the Jew first” at 1:16 and 2:10. The apparatus at verse 11 gives the printed text, without “also” after “reckoned to them,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the NIV, and adds kai for Robinson–Pierpont and Nestle–Aland 28: KJV “imputed unto them also,” Geneva “imputed to them also” and Douay “unto them also it may be reputed” print it, and ASV “reckoned unto them,” NIV “credited to them” and NWT 1984 “counted to them” do not.

⚠ “To those who are not of the circumcision only, but also to those who walk” (Romans 4:12). The Greek repeats the article: tois ouk ek peritomēs monon alla kai tois stoichousin. The second “to those” can name a second group beside the first or restate the first, and the Greek does not say which. This translation keeps both, and so do NWT 1984 “but also to those who walk orderly,” Douay “but to them also that follow the steps” and Geneva “but unto them also that walk”; KJV “but who also walk” and ASV “but who also walk” and NIV “who also follow” make it a single group, which is a decision, not a translation. The verb stoicheō, “to walk in line,” stands in five verses of the New Testament: Acts 21:24, this verse, and Galatians 5:25, Galatians 6:16 and Philippians 3:16 (those three not yet on these pages) (stoicheō). Ichnos, “footstep,” stands in three: here, 2 Corinthians 12:18 and 1 Peter 2:21 (those two not yet on these pages) (ichnos). The apparatus on the last phrase is word order and an article only: Robinson–Pierpont have “the faith, the one in the uncircumcision,” the same phrase as verse 11.

Verses 13–15 · ἐπαγγελία … κληρονόμον … κόσμου … κεκένωται … κατήργηται … ὀργὴν κατεργάζεται … παράβασις epangelia … klēronomon … kosmou … kekenōtai … katērgētai … orgēn katergazetai … parabasis — Romans 4:13–15: how can Abraham be “heir of the world,” and why does “the law work out wrath”?

“Heir of the world” (klēronomon kosmou, Romans 4:13). Ou gar dia nomou hē epangelia tō Abraam ē tō spermati autou, to klēronomon auton einai kosmou, alla dia dikaiosynēs pisteōs. The word “promise” (epangelia) now takes over from “reckon” as the chapter’s carrying word: the noun stands in verses 13, 14, 16 and 20, and the verb in verse 21. The promises as Genesis words them are about a land and a people: to give Abram “this land to possess” (Genesis 15:7), “all the land of Canaan, as an everlasting possession” (17:8), and, through his seed, a blessing for “all the nations of the earth” (22:18). The blessing as Acts 3:25 quotes it, “in your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed,” joins the wording of Genesis 12:3, “families,” to that of 22:18, “in your seed.” “Heir of the world” is not a phrase of any of those verses; it widens the land to the world. The word “heir” itself is the word of Genesis 15:3–4, the question Abram asks just before the reckoning. The Greek kosmou has no article, as at Romans 1:20, “from the creation of the world.” KJV “the heir of the world,” ASV “heir of the world,” NIV “heir of the world” and Geneva “the heir of the world” print the English article; NWT 1984 “heir of a world” reads the missing article as an indefinite one; TLB “the whole earth” makes it the land. The related word klēronomos, “heir,” comes back in the family of God at Romans 8:17.

⚠ “Emptied” and “abolished” (verse 14). Ei gar hoi ek nomou klēronomoi, kekenōtai hē pistis kai katērgētai hē epangelia. The two verbs are perfect passives, and the second is the verb of chapter 3: katargeō, which opened that chapter at 3:3 (will their unfaithfulness “abolish” God’s faithfulness?) and closed it at 3:31 (“Do we then abolish law through the faith?”). In verse 14 the answer to the second question is worked out: if inheriting depended on law, believing would be an empty act and the promise would have nothing left to promise. The same argument runs in Galatians 3:17–18 (not yet on these pages), with the same verb: the law that came later does not “abolish” the promise (katargēsai tēn epangelian), and if the inheritance is out of law it is no longer out of promise. The first verb, kenoō, “to empty,” stands in five verses of the New Testament: here, 1 Corinthians 1:17, “so that the cross of Christ would not be emptied out,” and 1 Corinthians 9:15, 2 Corinthians 9:3 and Philippians 2:7 (those three not yet on these pages) (kenoō). The shelf does not carry the verb of chapter 3 across. ASV prints “make of none effect” at 3:3 and 3:31 and “made of none effect” here, one expression at all three; KJV prints “without effect” at 3:3, “make void” at 3:31 and “made of none effect” here; NWT 1984 prints “without effect” at 3:3 and “abolish” at 3:31. In this verse the pair reads KJV “faith is made void, and the promise made of none effect,” ASV “faith is made void, and the promise is made of none effect” and Geneva “faith is made void, and the promise is made of none effect”; Douay “faith is made void, the promise is made of no effect” is the same; NIV “faith means nothing and the promise is worthless” loses the grammar; NWT 1984 “faith has been made useless and the promise has been abolished” prints the second verb as “abolished,” the English of its own 3:31. TLB “faith is foolish” moves from the argument to a verdict on people.

“The law works out wrath” (verse 15). Ho gar nomos orgēn katergazetai, hou de ouk estin nomos, oude parabasis. The verb katergazomai is the “works out” of Romans 2:9 (“the soul of man who works out evil”) and of 1:27; here its subject is the law and its product is the orgē of 1:18, “God’s wrath is revealed from heaven.” The apparatus at hou de gives de, “but,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and gar, “for,” to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “for where no law is, there is no transgression” and Douay “For where there is no law, neither is there transgression” have the second; ASV “but where there is no law, neither is there transgression” and NWT 1984 “but where there is no law, neither is there any transgression” have the first. NIV “the law brings wrath” and NWT 1984 “the Law produces wrath” give the verb a plain sense; KJV “the law worketh wrath” is the old “works.”

⚠ Transgression needs a line to cross. Parabasis, “transgression,” is a stepping across; the noun stands in seven verses of the New Testament, Romans 2:23, this verse, Romans 5:14, and Galatians 3:19, 1 Timothy 2:14, Hebrews 2:2 and 9:15 (those four not yet on these pages) (parabasis). Verse 15 does not say that there was no sin where there was no law. It says there was no parabasis, no crossing of a commanded line. That distinction is the one Romans 5:13 draws with a ledger word, ellogeō, “to enter to an account,” which stands in two verses of the New Testament: Romans 5:13, where sin is said to be in the world but not entered where there is no law, and Philemon 18, “charge it to my account” (ellogeō). The bookkeeping of the chapter’s first half has not stopped; it has reached the law.

Verses 16–17 · ἐκ πίστεως … κατὰ χάριν … βεβαίαν … παντὶ τῷ σπέρματι … πατὴρ πάντων ἡμῶν … τέθεικά σε … ζῳοποιοῦντος … καλοῦντος τὰ μὴ ὄντα ek pisteōs … kata charin … bebaian … panti tō spermati … patēr pantōn hēmōn … tetheika se … zōopoiountos … kalountos ta mē onta — Romans 4:16–17: why is Abraham “the father of us all,” and what does it mean to “call the things that are not as though they were”?

“For this reason it is of faith, so that it may be according to grace” (Romans 4:16). Dia touto ek pisteōs, hina kata charin, eis to einai bebaian tēn epangelian panti tō spermati. The Greek has no verb in the first clause, only ek pisteōs, “out of faith,” and the reader supplies what it is that is of faith, which verse 13 has named: the promise, the inheritance. The clauses are a chain of purposes: of faith, so that it is according to grace, so that the promise is secure. The grace of the middle link is the charis that verse 4 set against debt, and “secure,” bebaia, is the opposite of the “abolished” of verse 14. KJV “it is of faith, that it might be by grace,” ASV “it is of faith, that it may be according to grace” and Douay “Therefore is it of faith, that according to grace” supply “it is”; NIV “the promise comes by faith” names the subject; NWT 1984 “it was as a result of faith” names the cause. The word for the promise’s security is “firm” in Douay “the promise might be firm,” “sure” in KJV “the promise might be sure” and NWT 1984 “the promise to be sure,” and “guaranteed” in NIV “guaranteed to all Abraham’s offspring.” The promise is secure “to all the seed” in two parts, “that of the law” and “that of the faith of Abraham,” and the second is the wider: Abraham is the father of both. Galatians 3:16 (not yet on these pages) presses the singular of the same word, “seed,” to point at one person, hos estin Christos; Romans 4:16 reads it as a collective, “all the seed.” NIV “not only to those who are of the law but also to those who have the faith of Abraham” turns the neuter “that” into people.

⚠ “A father of many nations I have appointed you” (verse 17). Patera pollōn ethnōn tetheika se. This is Genesis 17:5, and the Greek is the Greek Old Testament’s own, word for word in Rahlfs’s text. The Hebrew of that verse is the explanation of a name: ki av-hamon goyim netatikha, “for I have made you the father of a multitude of nations,” said as Abram becomes Abraham, the name that sounds like av hamon, “father of a multitude.” The Greek quotation says “many” where the Hebrew says “a multitude” and leaves the name’s sound behind. The verb is a perfect, tetheika, “I have set you,” and it is said of nations that were still to be born. KJV “I have made thee a father of many nations,” Geneva “I have made thee a father of many nations,” Douay “I have made thee a father of many nations” and NIV “I have made you a father of many nations” print “made”; NWT 1984 “I have appointed you a father of many nations” is the one that follows the Greek verb. The nations are the ethnē of 3:29, “Is he not also of the nations?”; the quotation is where that question gets its answer.

“In the sight of the God whom he believed” (katenanti hou episteusen theou). The noun is put in the case of the relative: the clause reads literally “in the sight of whom he believed, God.” It is read with “the father of us all”: his fatherhood stands in God’s sight. KJV “before him whom he believed, even God,” ASV “before him whom he believed, even God” and Geneva “before God whom he believed” keep the pointing, and NIV “in the sight of God, in whom he believed” and NWT 1984 “in the sight of the One in whom he had faith, even of God” recast it.

⚠ Two things God does: he makes the dead alive and he calls the things that are not. Tou zōopoiountos tous nekrous kai kalountos ta mē onta hōs onta. The first verb, zōopoieō, “to make alive,” stands in twelve verses of the New Testament, counting the compound syzōopoieō of Ephesians 2:5 and Colossians 2:13 (not yet on these pages): John 5:21, 6:63, this verse, Romans 8:11 and Ephesians 2:5 are on these pages, and the other seven (1 Corinthians 15:22, 36 and 45, 2 Corinthians 3:6, Galatians 3:21, Colossians 2:13 and 1 Peter 3:18) are not yet (zōopoieō). The second phrase has no Old Testament quotation behind it. A verse of the Greek Bible that speaks of what God did with what was not is 2 Maccabees 7:28, where a mother asks her child to look at heaven and earth and everything in them and know that God did not make them out of things that existed (ouk ex ontōn epoiēsen auta ho theos; that book is not on these pages), and the reader of Romans 4 can take Paul’s clause two ways: God summons into existence what is not, or God speaks of what is not as already existing. The Greek hōs onta, “as being,” keeps both. NWT 1984 “calls the things that are not as though they were” and KJV “calleth those things which be not as though they were” keep both; NIV “calls into being things that were not” chooses the first; TLB “speaks of future events with as much certainty as though they were already past” chooses the second and limits it to events. For the first verb, KJV “quickeneth the dead” and Douay “quickeneth the dead” keep the old “quicken”; ASV “giveth life to the dead” and NIV “gives life to the dead” say “life”; NWT 1984 “makes the dead alive” is the English of this translation. The first phrase is taken up again in verse 19, where two bodies are as good as dead, and in verse 24, where God is the one who raised Jesus from the dead.

Verses 18–19 · παρʼ ἐλπίδα ἐπʼ ἐλπίδι … οὕτως ἔσται τὸ σπέρμα σου … κατενόησεν … νενεκρωμένον … ἑκατονταετής … νέκρωσιν τῆς μήτρας par’ elpida ep’ elpidi … houtōs estai to sperma sou … katenoēsen … nenekrōmenon … hekatontaetēs … nekrōsin tēs mētras — Romans 4:18–19: what does “against hope, in hope” mean, and did Abraham look at his own body or not?

“Against hope, believed in hope” (Romans 4:18). Hos par’ elpida ep’ elpidi episteusen eis to genesthai auton patera pollōn ethnōn kata to eirēmenon: Houtōs estai to sperma sou. The Greek sets one noun on both sides of the verb: par’ elpida, “contrary to hope,” and ep’ elpidi, “resting on hope” (Romans 8:24 says that hope that is seen is not hope). The first hope is the human one, the second is the one the promise gave. KJV “Who against hope believed in hope” and Douay “Who against hope believed in hope” keep the order; ASV “in hope believed against hope” reverses it; NIV “Against all hope, Abraham in hope believed” adds “all”; NWT 1984 “beyond hope, yet based on hope he had faith” makes the first a limit; Geneva “above hope, believed under hope” puts the two on a ladder. The quotation, houtōs estai to sperma sou, is Genesis 15:5, and in Rahlfs’s text it is word for word the same Greek. The Hebrew is koh yihyeh zar’ekha, “so shall your seed be,” said as Abram is brought out to count the stars. It is the verse just before the one quoted in verse 3, so the chapter has run the order of Genesis backward: the verdict came first (verse 3), and the promise that was believed comes fifteen verses later. In the same Genesis chapter, a few verses before the reckoning, Abram says “what will you give me? I go on childless” (15:2) and, soon after it, “how am I to know that I shall possess it?” (15:8); Paul’s account mentions neither. The Genesis page prints zera’ at 15:5 as “offspring,” and this chapter prints Paul’s sperma as “seed,” as Romans 1:3 does and as Genesis 22:17–18 does. TLB “Abraham believed God even though such a promise just couldn’t come to pass” paraphrases the first half.

⚠ Two pictures of Abraham in one verse: he looked at his body, or he refused to. Kai mē asthenēsas tē pistei katenoēsen to heautou sōma nenekrōmenon, hekatontaetēs pou hyparchōn, kai tēn nekrōsin tēs mētras Sarras. The apparatus has two splits here. The first is a single small word. The printed Greek has katenoēsen, “he considered,” and the apparatus gives that reading to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and adds ou, “not,” for Robinson–Pierpont, so that the sentence says he did not consider his body. These are opposite pictures: an Abraham who looked squarely at a dead body and did not weaken, and an Abraham who did not look. The shelf divides exactly there. KJV “he considered not his own body,” Geneva “considered not his own body” and Douay “neither did he consider his own body” have the negative; ASV “he considered his own body,” NIV “he faced the fact that his body was as good as dead” and NWT 1984 “he considered his own body” do not. The second split is a word the printed Greek lacks. After sōma, “body,” the apparatus gives the printed text (without ēdē, “now, already”) to Holmes’s SBL text and to Westcott–Hort in their margin, and gives ēdē to Westcott–Hort in their text, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. Five shelf versions print the “now”: KJV “now dead,” Douay “now dead,” Geneva “which was now dead,” ASV “now as good as dead” and NWT 1984 “now already deadened.” This translation prints the Greek it has on the page, without it.

A hundred years, and a dead womb. Hekatontaetēs, “a hundred years old,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament (hekatontaetēs), softened by pou, “about.” Genesis gives the numbers: Abraham says “a hundred years” at 17:17, he is a hundred when Isaac is born (21:5), and Sarah, ninety at 17:17, has passed the time of women (18:11). The perfect participle nenekrōmenon, “as good as dead,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Hebrews 11:12, “and him as good as dead,” and the noun nekrōsis, “deadness,” in two, this one and 2 Corinthians 4:10 (not yet on these pages) (nenekrōmenon; the verb nekroō at Colossians 3:5 (not yet on these pages) shares the letters of nekrōsis and is not counted). Genesis 17:17 has Abraham fall on his face and laugh, and 18:12 has Sarah laugh; Paul’s sentence has neither, and this page does not say how the two fit. KJV “neither yet the deadness of Sarah’s womb” and Geneva “neither the deadness of Sarah’s womb” keep the noun “deadness,” and Douay “nor the dead womb of Sara” turns it into an adjective.

Verses 20–22 · οὐ διεκρίθη τῇ ἀπιστίᾳ … ἐνεδυναμώθη τῇ πίστει … δοὺς δόξαν τῷ θεῷ … πληροφορηθείς … διὸ ἐλογίσθη αὐτῷ εἰς δικαιοσύνην ou diekrithē tē apistia … enedynamōthē tē pistei … dous doxan tō theō … plērophorētheis … dio elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn — Romans 4:20–22: what is “unbelief” here, what does “empowered in faith” mean, and why does verse 22 repeat verse 3?

“He did not doubt through unbelief” (Romans 4:20). Eis de tēn epangelian tou theou ou diekrithē tē apistia alla enedynamōthē tē pistei. The noun apistia is the one that opened the argument of chapter 3: “will their unfaithfulness abolish the faithfulness of God?” (3:3; the dictionary entry is apistia). There it stood against God’s pistis, and this translation printed “unfaithfulness” because the context was whether God keeps faith. Here it stands against Abraham’s pistis and the English that fits is “unbelief”; the Greek reader hears one noun, apistia, and on its other side one noun, pistis, and in both places the question is whether faith survives. The shelf varies. KJV prints “unbelief” at 3:3 and here, and NWT 1984 prints “lack of faith” at both; ASV has “want of faith” at 3:3 and “unbelief” here. The verb diakrinō, “to doubt, to waver,” is the verb of Mark 11:23 and James 1:6 (the dictionary entry is diakrinō). KJV “He staggered not at the promise of God through unbelief” and Douay “he staggered not by distrust” print “staggered”; ASV “he wavered not through unbelief,” NIV “he did not waver through unbelief” and NWT 1984 “he did not waver in a lack of faith” print “wavered”; Geneva “Neither did he doubt of the promise of God through unbelief” prints “doubt.”

⚠ “Empowered in faith” (enedynamōthē tē pistei). The verb is a passive: Abraham was made powerful, he did not summon strength. Endynamoō, “to empower,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament (endynamoō): Acts 9:22, “Saul was being empowered all the more”; this verse; Philippians 4:13, “in the one who empowers me”; 1 Timothy 1:12; and Ephesians 6:10, 2 Timothy 2:1 and 2 Timothy 4:17 (those three not yet on these pages). Hebrews 11:11 says the same thing of Abraham and Sarah with another phrase, “he received power.” The dative tē pistei can be “in faith” or “by faith.” KJV “was strong in faith” and ASV “waxed strong through faith” make Abraham the one who is strong; Douay “was strengthened in faith,” Geneva “was strengthened in the faith” and NIV “was strengthened in his faith” keep the passive; NWT 1984 “became powerful by his faith” keeps the root “power” and TLB “his faith and trust grew ever stronger” makes faith the thing that grows.

“Giving glory to God” (dous doxan tō theō). The phrase “glory to God” in this order stands in five verses of the New Testament, all of them on these pages (doxan tō theō): Luke 17:18, where only the foreigner returns to give glory to God; John 9:24, “Give glory to God,” said to the man born blind; Acts 12:23, where Herod is struck “because he did not give the glory to God”; this verse; and Revelation 11:13. What Abraham does here is what Romans 1:21 said humankind did not do (“they did not glorify him as God”) and what 3:23 says all fall short of. KJV “giving glory to God,” ASV “giving glory to God” and Douay “giving glory to God” keep the participle; NWT 1984 “giving God glory” reorders it.

“Fully convinced that what he has promised he is also able to do” (verse 21). Plērophorētheis, “fully convinced,” is the passive of a verb that means to fill something to the brim; it stands in six verses of the New Testament (plērophoreō, the nouns not counted), and in Luke 1:1 it is said of things “fully accomplished among us.” The Greek verb epēngeltai is a perfect, “he has promised,” so the promise is still standing when it is believed. Douay “whatsoever he has promised, he is able also to perform” keeps the perfect; KJV “what he had promised,” ASV “what he had promised” and NWT 1984 “what he had promised” move it into the past; NIV “God had power to do what he had promised” names God and changes “able” to “had power.”

“Therefore it was reckoned to him as righteousness” (verse 22). The sentence is the sentence of verse 3, elogisthē autō eis dikaiosynēn, and it closes the circle: the chapter opened with the verdict and has now described the faith that earned the verb. The apparatus gives the printed dio to the Holmes edition and to Westcott–Hort in their margin, and dio kai, “therefore also,” to Westcott–Hort in their text, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. ASV “Wherefore also it was reckoned unto him for righteousness” prints the kai as “also”; KJV “And therefore it was imputed to him for righteousness,” Geneva “And therefore it was imputed to him for righteousness” and Douay “And therefore it was reputed to him unto justice” open with “And”; NIV “This is why” and NWT 1984 “Hence” have no word for it.

Verses 23–25 · οὐκ ἐγράφη … διʼ ἡμᾶς … τοῖς πιστεύουσιν ἐπὶ τὸν ἐγείραντα … παρεδόθη διὰ τὰ παραπτώματα … ἠγέρθη διὰ τὴν δικαίωσιν ouk egraphē … di’ hēmas … tois pisteuousin epi ton egeiranta … paredothē dia ta paraptōmata … ēgerthē dia tēn dikaiōsin — Romans 4:23–25: for whom was Abraham’s story written, and what does “raised because of our justification” mean?

“Not written for his sake alone” (Romans 4:23–24). Ouk egraphē de di’ auton monon hoti elogisthē autō, alla kai di’ hēmas hois mellei logizesthai. Paul says what he has been doing with Genesis: the sentence about Abraham was written down so that it could be read of a later person. Scripture is said to be written “for us” in four passages of Paul: here; Romans 15:4 (eis tēn hēmeteran didaskalian egraphē); 1 Corinthians 9:10 (di’ hēmas gar egraphē); and 1 Corinthians 10:11 (those three not yet on these pages). The object of faith has moved. In verse 17 Abraham believed “the God who makes the dead alive”; in verse 24 the believers believe “on the one who raised Jesus our Lord from the dead.” The root “dead” (nekr-) stands four times in three verses of this chapter, in verses 17, 19 and 24, and in verse 19 it is Abraham’s body and Sarah’s womb. With mellei, “is going to be,” the reckoning is not only behind Abraham but ahead of Paul’s readers. KJV “Now it was not written for his sake alone,” ASV “Now it was not written for his sake alone” and NIV “written not for him alone” keep the claim; TLB “wasn’t just for Abraham’s benefit” makes it a matter of benefit. In verse 24 the Greek has a participle, tois pisteuousin, “to those who believe,” with no “if” and no “because”: KJV “if we believe on him that raised up Jesus” and Douay “if we believe in him, that raised up Jesus Christ” make it a condition; NWT 1984 “because we believe on him who raised Jesus” makes it a cause; ASV “who believe on him that raised Jesus our Lord from the dead” and NIV “for us who believe in him who raised Jesus” keep the participle. TLB “when we believe the promises of God” replaces the one who raised Jesus with the promises.

⚠ “Handed over because of our trespasses, raised because of our justification” (verse 25). Hos paredothē dia ta paraptōmata hēmōn kai ēgerthē dia tēn dikaiōsin hēmōn. The two clauses are built on one preposition, dia with the accusative, which in its ordinary use looks back to a cause: “because of.” The first clause fits that without strain (he was handed over because we had trespassed). The second can be read the same way (he was raised because we stood justified, and the raising is the sign that it was so) or as looking forward (he was raised for the sake of justifying us), and the Greek does not choose. This translation prints “because of” at both and lets the reader hold the question. The shelf uses “for” and so says less: KJV “was delivered for our offences, and was raised again for our justification,” ASV “was delivered up for our trespasses, and was raised for our justification,” Douay “was delivered up for our sins, and rose again for our justification” and NIV “was delivered over to death for our sins and was raised to life for our justification” print “for” twice; NWT 1984 “was delivered up for the sake of our trespasses and was raised up for the sake of declaring us righteous” prints “for the sake of” twice and turns “justification” into a verb; TLB “rose again to make us right with God” states the second as a purpose. The noun dikaiōsis, “justification,” the act of declaring righteous, stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Romans 5:18 (dikaiōsis); the dictionary entry is dikaiōsis. Paraptōma, “trespass,” is the word of Ephesians 2:1.

The verb “handed over,” and the Greek Isaiah. Paredothē is the verb of Romans 1:24, 26 and 28, where three times “God gave them over,” and of 8:32, where God “handed him over for us all.” It also stands in the Greek Isaiah, at the end of the Servant poem, close to two more of this chapter’s verbs. Rahlfs’s text of Isaiah 53:11 has dikaiōsai, “to justify,” and 53:12 reads anth’ hōn paredothē eis thanaton hē psychē autou kai en tois anomois elogisthē kai autos hamartias pollōn anēnegken kai dia tas hamartias autōn paredothē: “because his soul was handed over to death, and he was reckoned among the lawless, and he himself bore the sins of many, and because of their sins he was handed over.” Verse 6 has kyrios paredōken auton tais hamartiais hēmōn, “Jehovah handed him over to our sins.” (The Hebrew of these verses is on these pages at Isaiah 53:6, “made the iniquity of us all meet him,” and 53:12, “was numbered.”) The verb “reckoned” of that verse is the one quoted at Luke 22:37, “he was numbered with the lawless.” So two verses of the Greek Isaiah hold the chapter’s governing verbs, logizomai, paradidōmi and dikaioō. Paul does not quote Isaiah here, and the text does not say whether he had it in mind; the overlap of vocabulary is what can be shown.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “reckoned” for logizomai in all eleven verses of the chapter (vv. 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11, 22, 23 and 24), as at Romans 3:28; “justified” for dikaioō (v. 2, v. 5); “works” with no “of law” at v. 2 and v. 6, against “works of law” at 3:20 and 3:28; “abolished” for katargeō (v. 14), as at 3:3, 3:31 and 1 Corinthians 13:8; “emptied” for kenoō (v. 14), as at 1 Corinthians 1:17; “works out” for katergazomai (v. 15), as at 1:27 and 2:9; “transgression” for parabasis (v. 15), as at 2:23; “unbelief” for apistia (v. 20), with the “unfaithfulness” of 3:3 explained in the note; “believe on” for pisteuō epi with the accusative (vv. 5, 24), as at Acts 16:31; “empowered” for endynamoō (v. 20), as at Acts 9:22 and Philippians 4:13; “seed” for sperma (vv. 13, 16, 18), as at 1:3; “handed over” for paradidōmi (v. 25), as at 8:32; and “Jehovah” at v. 8, where the Greek reads kyrios in a quotation naming the God of Israel.

Echoes paid. “We reckon” (3:28) — the verb of every verse from 3 to 24. Boasting (3:27) — v. 2. The circumcised and the uncircumcised (3:30) — vv. 9–12. The unfaithfulness and the faithfulness (3:3) — v. 20. “Abolish” (3:3, 3:31) — the promise abolished, v. 14. “The one who justifies” (3:26) — the one who justifies the ungodly, v. 5. The nations (3:29) — vv. 17–18. “Apart from works” (3:21, 3:28) — v. 6. The Law as witness (3:21) — the chapter calls Genesis 15 and 17 and Psalm 32. The glory of God (1:21, 3:23) — v. 20. Wrath (1:18) — v. 15.

Echoes planted. The verb reckon returns at Romans 8:18 and in the passive at 8:36, and at 6:11 and 9:8 (those two not yet on these pages). Heirs (vv. 13–14) come back at 8:17; “makes alive” (v. 17) at 8:11; “handed over” (v. 25) at 8:32; and the one who justifies (v. 5) at 8:33. Boasting, shut out here, turns round at 5:2–3, and the ledger verb ellogeō and the noun dikaiōsis come back at 5:13 and 5:18. Genesis 15:6 is quoted again at Galatians 3:6 and James 2:23 (those two not yet on these pages); James has the sentence of verse 3 in all its words, including de, where Galatians has kathōs and kai. The seed, the promise and the law are set out again at Galatians 3:16–18 (not yet on these pages).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Where “according to the flesh” belongs in verse 1; whether the repeated “to those” in verse 12 names one group or two; whether “calls the things that are not as though they were” in verse 17 means summoning into existence or speaking of as existing; the two splits in verse 19, whether Abraham “considered” his body or “did not consider” it, and whether the body was “now” dead; and whether “raised because of our justification” in verse 25 looks back or forward.
