📖 Luke

Κατὰ Λουκᾶν (Kata Loukan) — 'According to Luke.' The book names no author; the ascription to LUKE — a physician and companion of Paul — is early and undisputed. It is the first of two volumes: the Gospel and the Acts of the Apostles, both addressed to the same Theophilus, together the longest single contribution to the New Testament by any one hand.

The longest and most literary of the Gospels — a physician's orderly account, and the Gospel of the outsider: women, the poor, the Samaritan, the tax-collector; a Saviour whose coming is sung by a teenage girl and announced first to shepherds.

Chapter by chapter

Luke 1The longest chapter in the Gospels and the songbook of the church — two annunciations set against each other (a priest struck dumb for doubting, a girl in Nazareth blessed for believing), the leap in Elizabeth's womb, and two of the great canticles: Mary's Magnificat ('he has brought down rulers from thrones and lifted up the lowly') and Zechariah's Benedictus ('the dawn from on high').Luke 2⚠ There is no inn in this chapter, and Luke has a word for inn — he uses it eight chapters later, in the good Samaritan. The word here is KATALYMA, a guest room, which is what Luke calls the upper room of the Last Supper. There is no innkeeper either; he arrives with the mediaeval mystery plays. ⚠ One final sigma decides what the angels sang: EUDOKIAS gives peace to the people God has favoured, EUDOKIA gives the King James “good will toward men”, and in the Gospels the word’s owner is always God. Simeon’s song is the vocabulary of a slave being manumitted, his sword is the two-handed one Revelation uses six times, and Anna belongs to a tribe that had been gone seven hundred years. Plus the hardest date in the New Testament: Quirinius governed Syria in AD 6 and Herod died in 4 BC.Luke 3Luke dates John’s first sermon by seven rulers — the fifteenth year of Tiberius, Pilate, three tetrarchs and a high priest with two names — then quotes Isaiah on to “all flesh shall see the salvation of God”, a line the Hebrew does not have. Only Luke has crowds, tax collectors and soldiers asking “What shall we do?”, and the answer “do not shake anyone down”. John is jailed before Jesus is baptized, the Spirit comes “in bodily form”, and a genealogy climbs through Nathan, a Cainan the Hebrew lacks, and Adam, to God.Luke 4Full of the Spirit and led in the wilderness, Jesus is tested three times in words from Deuteronomy, and in Luke the devil explains that the kingdoms of the inhabited world “have been handed over to me”. At Nazareth he reads Isaiah 61 and rolls up the scroll mid-verse, before “the day of vengeance”; the prophet who proclaims Jehovah’s year of welcome is not welcome at home, and the town takes him to the brow of its hill. At Capernaum he rebukes a demon, a fever and a crowd of demons — and the chapter ends in synagogues of Judea, or of Galilee in later copies.Luke 5By the lake of Gennesaret the Simon whose house was already named a chapter early is called at last, after a catch of fish that breaks the nets; a leper is touched and cleansed, a paralyzed man is let down through a roof, and the crowd says, “We have seen incredible things today.” A tax collector named Levi leaves his booth to follow him, and a question about fasting gets two parables about a new garment and new wine — and a closing line found in no other Gospel: “the old is nice.”Luke 6Grain plucked on the sabbath, a withered hand healed in a synagogue watching for a charge, and a whole night in prayer before the Twelve are named — including the payoff of a name planted a chapter early, “Simon, whom he also named Peter.” Coming down to “a level place,” not a mountain, Jesus opens the Sermon on the Plain: four Happy-sayings answered by four Woes (“Happy are you who are poor”, unqualified — not Matthew’s “poor in spirit”), then love for enemies, a Golden Rule dropped mid-argument rather than sealed as a capstone, and the measure poured into your lap “pressed down, shaken together, running over.” The splinter and the beam arrive in wording identical to the other Sermon; a tree is known by its own fruit; and it closes on two builders — one who dug deep for a foundation before a river burst against the house, the other who did not.Luke 7A centurion who never meets Jesus sends word that he is “not worthy” to have him under his roof — and Jesus marvels, the only time in Luke that he does. At the gate of Nain a widow’s only son is given back to her in the very words of Elijah, and for the first time the narrator calls Jesus “the Lord.” John the Baptist asks from prison, twice over, “Are you the one who is to come?”; this generation will neither dance nor weep; and at a Pharisee’s table a woman known as a sinner wets his feet with tears the Greek describes like rain — “her sins, many as they are, have been forgiven, for she loved much,” a “for” that can be read two ways.Luke 8Women who fund the ministry out of their own means, among them Mary Magdalene, introduced by name for the first time; a sower whose seed falls on four soils, interpreted verse by verse for the disciples alone once the crowd is gone; a storm silenced by a rebuke, the disciples asking “who then is this?” — the same question a Pharisee’s dinner guests already asked, one chapter back, about a different kind of authority. Across the lake, a man called Legion is restored to his right mind and sent home to talk, the one person in this Gospel told to spread the news; and on the way back, an only daughter is given back to her father in the same breath a bleeding woman is called “Daughter” and told, in the very words promised last chapter, that her faith has saved her.Luke 9The Twelve are sent out with nothing for the road and given, of all things, Jesus’s own “power and authority”; a crowd of thousands is fed from five loaves and two fish; and Herod, having beheaded John, asks a question this Gospel keeps widening — “who is this?” — until Peter finally answers it, “the Christ of God.” On a mountain Jesus is transfigured while praying, Moses and Elijah beside him talking of the “departure” he is about to fulfill at Jerusalem, and a voice from a cloud names him “my Son, the Chosen One” — a phrase that will return as mockery at the cross. Coming down, a boy is healed where the disciples failed; a second prediction of death goes uncomprehended; a child is set beside an argument about who is greatest; and a Samaritan village that will not receive him opens the verse that turns this Gospel toward Jerusalem for nine chapters: “he set his face to go.” It closes on three would-be followers and a hand that must not look back from the plow.Luke 10Seventy-two are sent out ahead of him in pairs — “lambs in the midst of wolves” — and return rejoicing that even the demons submit, only to be told to rejoice instead that their names are written in heaven; Jesus watches “Satan fall from heaven like lightning” and, in the same breath, thanks his Father for hiding these things from the wise and revealing them to little children. A lawyer, testing him, answers his own question about eternal life with the double command to love God and “your neighbor as yourself” — then asks who his neighbor is, and gets back a story instead of a definition: a priest and a Levite pass by a beaten man on the Jericho road, and a Samaritan, of all people, is the one “moved with compassion.” It closes in a certain village, where one sister serves and another sits at the Lord’s feet, and is told only one thing is necessary.Luke 11A disciple asks to be taught to pray, and gets Luke’s own shorter Lord’s Prayer — no “our,” no “in heaven,” no third petition — followed by a friend roused from bed at midnight by nothing but his own shamelessness (or is it the asker’s?), and “ask, seek, knock” ending on a gift Matthew’s version never names: the Holy Spirit. ⚠ A mute man is healed and the Beelzeboul charge, silent through this whole Gospel until now, gets its own scene — “by the finger of God,” not Matthew’s “Spirit of God” — before an unclean spirit returns with seven worse than itself and a bystander’s blessing on Jesus’s own mother is redirected, not refused. The sign of Jonah is given with no three days and three nights, only preaching and repentance, and the queen of the south rises to condemn a generation with more evidence than she ever had. A Pharisee’s dinner turns into six woes — three to the table’s host, three to a lawyer who complains — closing on the blood of every prophet “from Abel to Zechariah,” and scribes lying in wait to catch something from his mouth.Luke 12A crowd gathered “by the tens of thousands,” trampling each other, hears Jesus turn first to twelve of them — fear God rather than an executioner who “has nothing more” to do, sparrows sold five for two pennies (a better rate than Matthew’s), and the one sin this Gospel will not forgive, given here with no exorcism and no accusation attached to it at all. A man wants an inheritance divided and gets a parable instead: a rich fool plans years of ease on the one night he has none left, twelve first-person verbs in three verses and no other person in the room until God speaks the only other line. “Do not be anxious” — ravens, an unclean bird, fed anyway; lilies outdressing Solomon — closes on a “little flock” handed a kingdom, and a master who comes home and, finding his slaves ready, ties on an apron and serves THEM. ⚠ Peter interrupts to ask if a parable is for the Twelve or for everyone, and gets a household “manager” instead of Matthew’s plain “slave.” Fire, not a sword, for the division a household of five will come to; a baptism still to be gone through; and a saying about reading a red evening sky that this project’s own Matthew 16 note already guessed was borrowed from exactly here.Luke 13Word of Galileans whose blood Pilate mixed with their own sacrifices, and of eighteen killed when a tower falls at Siloam, gets the identical answer twice over: neither proves the dead were the worse sinners, only that everyone else needs to turn around. A fig tree that hasn’t produced in three years is given one more, instead of the axe; a woman bent double for eighteen years is straightened on the Sabbath, over a synagogue ruler’s objection — and a mustard seed and a woman’s yeast, the exact echo the last chapter promised, both become pictures of the kingdom. A narrow door, a locked house, and guests from all four points of the compass reclining with Abraham, Isaac and Jacob answer someone’s question about how many will be saved; Pharisees warn Jesus that Herod wants him dead, and he calls the tetrarch “that fox” without missing a step toward Jerusalem — the city he then addresses by name, twice, grieving over it before he has even arrived.Luke 14A man with dropsy is healed on the Sabbath at a Pharisee ruler’s own table, and the Sabbath argument this Gospel already lost two chapters ago returns sharper — a SON now named beside the ox that falls in a well. Watching the guests scramble for the best couches, Jesus tells them to take the lowest place instead, then tells the host to invite the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind — the exact word for the banquet already promised, nine chapters back, to return here. A great dinner’s invited guests all beg off with excuses, so the master sends his slave first into the city’s streets, then out to the country roads, to fill the house instead; large crowds are told that whoever does not hate father and mother, and carry his own cross, cannot be his disciple, and a builder who never finishes his tower and a king who sues for peace at two-to-one odds both argue that a disciple had better count the cost first. It closes on salt that has lost its taste, fit for neither soil nor manure pile.

About the book

Where it sits
The THIRD Gospel and the LONGEST book in the New Testament; paired with Acts as a single two-volume work that together makes up about a quarter of the NT — more text than Paul. One of the three Synoptics.
Genre
A GOSPEL, and volume one of a two-part history (Luke–Acts) — the most consciously literary of the four. It opens with a classical Greek preface, dates events by emperors and governors, carries the largest vocabulary in the New Testament, and preserves a wealth of material found in no other Gospel (the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, the Rich Man and Lazarus, the road to Emmaus).
Author
By early and near-unanimous tradition, LUKE, 'the beloved physician' (Colossians 4:14) — a Gentile companion of Paul, present in the first-person 'we' sections of Acts (16, 20, 27–28) and named in Philemon 24 and 2 Timothy 4:11. On the majority view the only GENTILE author in the Bible; the medical vocabulary and the close interest in Paul's journeys fit the tradition.
Date written
Commonly placed around AD 80–85, though some argue the 60s–70s. It turns partly on Luke's use of Mark (which he clearly draws on) and on where Acts breaks off — with Paul still alive under house arrest in Rome (c. AD 62), which some read as an early terminus for the whole two-volume work.
Place
Uncertain — proposals include Rome, Achaia, and Antioch. Written for a Greco-Roman readership: Luke smooths Semitic detail, explains Palestine to outsiders, and frames the story inside the wider empire.
Audience
GENTILE Christians and inquirers — 'most excellent Theophilus' and everyone like him. Luke universalises the good news: he traces Jesus' genealogy back not merely to Abraham but to ADAM, 'son of God' (3:38), and ends Acts with the gospel reaching Rome, the heart of the world.

How it's laid out

1:1–4The preface — a formal dedication to Theophilus; the one place a Gospel opens like a Greek history.
1:5–2:52The infancy narrative — two annunciations, the Visitation, the births of John and Jesus, the shepherds, Simeon and Anna, the boy in the temple. Luke's alone, and full of songs.
3:1–4:13John the baptiser, the baptism, the genealogy back to Adam, the temptation.
4:14–9:50The Galilean ministry — healings, parables, the calling and sending of the twelve.
9:51–19:27The long journey to Jerusalem — the heart of Luke, holding most of his unique parables (the Good Samaritan, the Prodigal Son, the Rich Man and Lazarus).
19:28–24:53Jerusalem — the entry, the temple, the Last Supper, the cross, the empty tomb, and the road to Emmaus.

What it's about

  • The OUTSIDER welcomed — women, the poor, tax-collectors, Samaritans, sinners. Luke's Jesus eats with the wrong people and tells stories in which they are the heroes.
  • The GREAT REVERSAL — sounded first in Mary's song (the lowly lifted, the rich sent empty away) and paid out in the Beatitudes-and-woes, in Lazarus at the gate, in the last made first.
  • The HOLY SPIRIT and PRAYER — the Spirit fills Elizabeth, Mary, Zechariah, and Simeon before a single miracle is done; Jesus prays at every hinge and teaches his followers to.
  • JOY and SONG — Luke 1–2 is the songbook of the church (Magnificat, Benedictus, Gloria, Nunc Dimittis); the Gospel begins and ends with people praising God 'with great joy.'
  • SALVATION for ALL — the genealogy to Adam, the Samaritan neighbour, the promise that 'all flesh will see the salvation of God' (3:6).

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. Luke's Greek is the most refined in the Gospels — a classical, balanced periodic preface (1:1–4), then a deliberate shift into Septuagint-flavoured Greek for the infancy story, so the reader hears the Old Testament resume. Its sharpest textual questions cluster later (the sweat 'like blood' in Gethsemane, 22:43–44; some words from the cross), noted where they fall; chapter 1 is textually calm.

Looking forward

Luke's Jesus is the Saviour of the whole world, and above all of those the world discards. He is announced not to kings but to shepherds; his mother is a nobody from Nazareth who sings a revolution; his genealogy runs past Abraham all the way to Adam, making him the second head of the entire human race, not one nation's Messiah only. He is 'Son of the Most High' and 'a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord' — and the one who says his whole mission is 'to seek and to save the lost' (19:10).

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP by Luke the physician is early and rarely contested even critically, helped by the first-person 'we' passages of Acts. The live questions are DATE (the 80s versus a pre-63 date tied to the abrupt ending of Acts), SOURCES (Luke names 'many' predecessors in 1:1; the majority view has him using Mark and a sayings source alongside material unique to him), and a handful of famous later VARIANTS. The two-volume unity of Luke–Acts, addressed to the same Theophilus, is broadly agreed.
