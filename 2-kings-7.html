Elisha says that by tomorrow a seah of fine flour will cost a shekel in the gate of Samaria; the officer on whose hand the king leans says it cannot be, and is told he will see it and not eat. Four men with the scaly disease at the gate go to the camp of Aram at twilight and find it empty, because the Lord had made the camp hear chariots and horses. They eat, hide silver, gold and garments, and tell the gatekeepers; the king suspects an ambush and sends two chariots, which follow a road of thrown-away garments to the Jordan. The people plunder the camp, the prices come true, and the officer is trampled in the gate.
The chapter names no king and gives no date. It follows the siege of 2 Kings 6:24 and does not say how long the siege had lasted.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–2 · 2 Kings 7:1–2 “Hear the word of Jehovah” — a price promised for tomorrow, and the officer who says it cannot be

⭐ “Hear the word of Jehovah”. The last verse of the last chapter ends on “why should I wait for Jehovah any longer?”, and this one begins “And Elisha said”; the chapter division falls between them, and the Hebrew has no pause. The NWT 1984 has “Listen, YOU men”, because the verb is plural. The promise is a price: “a seah of fine flour” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one, verse 16 where it comes true, and verse 18 where the story is told again. The NIV has “a seah of the finest flour”, the ASV and the KJV “a measure of fine flour”, the Douay “a bushel… for a stater”, and the TLB “two gallons of flour… for a dollar”. Josephus has the same two prices and says the seah is a bushel and a half in Italian measure (Antiquities 9.71, 9.85).

“Tomorrow about this time”. “About this time tomorrow”, ke’et machar, stands in eight verses of the Hebrew Bible. Jezebel uses it to threaten Elijah (1 Kings 19:2) and Ben-hadad to threaten Ahab (1 Kings 20:6), both now on these pages; here it carries a price.

⚠ “The officer on whose hand the king leaned”. The Hebrew title is ha-shalish, “the third”, and the Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition has tristatēs. The ASV has “captain”, the KJV “lord”, the Geneva “prince”, the NIV “officer”, the NWT 1984 “adjutant”, and the TLB “officer assisting the king”. Josephus has “the commander of the third part” (Antiquities 9.73). The king “leaned on his hand” as Naaman’s master leaned on Naaman’s (2 Kings 5:18, now on these pages). Radak says only that the words mean the officer the king leaned on.

⭐ “If Jehovah were to make floodgates in the heavens”. “Floodgates in the heavens”, arubbot ba-shamayim, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this verse and verse 19. The nearest wording is the flood’s: “the floodgates of the sky were opened” (Genesis 7:11, now on these pages), and “the floodgates of the heavens” stands in two, Genesis 7:11 and 8:2. The ASV, the KJV, the Geneva and the TLB have “windows”, and the NIV, the NWT 1984 and the Douay “floodgates”. The Greek has katarraktas, “cataracts”. Josephus has the officer say that as God cannot pour barley or flour from heaven in torrents, so this cannot happen. Elisha’s answer is the same in both tellings: “you shall see it with your eyes, but from there you shall not eat.”

Verses 3–8 · 2 Kings 7:3–8 Four men with the scaly disease — “let us fall to the camp of Aram”, a sound that was not there, and two tents

⭐ “Four men with the scaly disease”. The word metsora’im stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, verses 3 and 8, both here. Rashi says “Gehazi and his sons”, citing Sanhedrin 107b, and Radak says the Rabbis say so; the verse says only “four men”. The KJV and the ASV have “leprous men”, the NIV “men with leprosy”, and the TLB and the Douay “lepers”. They are “at the entrance of the gate”, outside the city as Leviticus 13:46 (now on these pages) puts the one who has it: “his dwelling shall be outside the camp.” Josephus says there was a law in Samaria that those with the disease stayed outside (Antiquities 9.74). The verse does not say why they sit there.

“Let us fall to the camp of Aram”. Their reasoning has three cases: the city has famine, staying here is death too, and so, “if they keep us alive, we shall live; and if they put us to death, we shall die.” “Let us fall to”, nippelah el, stands in one verse. The KJV and the ASV have “let us fall unto the host”, the NIV and the TLB “surrender”, the NWT 1984 “invade”, and the Douay “let us run over to”.

⚠ “At twilight”, and “the Lord”. They rise ba-neshef, “at twilight”, and Aram flees at the same time: the word stands in five verses, two of them here (verses 5 and 7). The ASV and the KJV have “twilight”, the NIV “dusk”, the Douay “the evening” and then “in the dark”, and the NWT 1984 “evening darkness”. What Aram heard is the one thing in the chapter the Hebrew gives to “the Lord”, Adonai, in one verse: “the Lord had made the camp of Aram hear the sound of chariots, the sound of horses, the sound of a great force.” Jehovah is named in verses 1, 2, 16 and 19. The ASV prints “the Lord” here and “Jehovah” in those, and the NWT 1984 has “Jehovah himself”. Rashi says it only seemed to them as though they were hearing. Horses and chariots were seen at 2 Kings 6:17 (now on these pages); here they are only heard.

“The kings of the Hittites and the kings of Egypt”. “The kings of the Hittites” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: 1 Kings 10:29, where Solomon’s chariots and horses go out to “all the kings of the Hittites and the kings of Aram” (now on these pages), 2 Chronicles 1:17 (now on these pages), and this one. Josephus has the Arameans say that Joram had hired the king of Egypt and the king of the islands (Antiquities 9.77). The NIV has “the Hittite and Egyptian kings”.

⚠ “Silver and gold and garments”, and “went and hid it”. The men eat, drink, carry out “silver and gold and garments”, hide them, and return to a second tent. Silver, gold and garments are what Naaman set out with (2 Kings 5:5) and what Gehazi took, silver and garments (5:23), both now on these pages. Josephus has the men go in four times where the Hebrew has two tents (Antiquities 9.80). The KJV and the ASV have “went and hid it”, the NWT 1984 “stick them away”, and the TLB “hiding it”.

Verses 9–12 · 2 Kings 7:9–12 “A day of good news” — the gatekeepers, and a king who sees a trap

⭐ “We are not doing right. This day is a day of good news”. “A day of good news”, yom besorah, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The noun is the besorah of Ahimaaz’s run: Joab tells him “you are not a man of tidings today” (2 Samuel 18:20), and the king says of the runner “he comes with good tidings” (18:27), both now on these pages. The Greek Bible in the Vaticanus tradition has hēmera euangelias, “a day of good news”. The KJV, the ASV and the Geneva have “a day of good tidings”, the NIV and the NWT 1984 “a day of good news”, the Douay “a day of good tidings”, and the TLB “wonderful news”. They are “keeping silent”.

⚠ “Guilt will find us”. The Hebrew word is avon, “guilt”. The ASV and the NIV have “punishment will overtake us”, the KJV and the Geneva “some mischief will come upon us”, the Douay “we shall be charged with a crime”, the TLB “some terrible calamity”, and the NWT 1984 “guilt must also catch up with us”. Rashi says “we will be held guilty by the throne”. The verse does not say whom they would be guilty before; they go to the gatekeepers.

“The gatekeepers called, and told it to the king’s house inside”. The news comes in by relay: the four call to the gatekeepers, the gatekeepers call to the palace. The KJV and the ASV have “the porter”, the NIV and the NWT 1984 “gatekeepers”, and the TLB “watchmen”. Josephus has the four shout up to the guards on the wall, and the guards tell the king’s guards (Antiquities 9.81). They say “no man there, and no voice of man”, and “only the horse tied and the donkey tied and the tents as they were”.

⭐ “What Aram has done to us”. The king gets up in the night, and he is not named. He tells his servants what he thinks: “They know that we are hungry, and they have gone out from the camp to hide in the field, saying: When they come out of the city, we shall seize them alive and enter the city.” Josephus has the same suspicion of an ambush (Antiquities 9.82). Ketiv and qere appear three times in this chapter, at verses 12, 13 and 15, and each time the written form has one extra he: bahasadeh for basadeh, “in the field”, here; hahamon for hamon, “the multitude”, and behachpazam for bechofzam, “in their haste”. The ASV and the KJV have “to hide themselves in the field”, the NIV “to hide in the countryside”, the TLB “hidden in the fields”, and the Douay “lie hid in the fields”.

Verses 13–16 · 2 Kings 7:13–16 Five horses, two chariots — “Go and see”, a road of garments, and the word that comes true

⭐ “Five of the horses that remain”. The servant’s plan is a small one: five horses of those left in the city, which are “like all the multitude of Israel that have remained in it” and “like all the multitude of Israel that have been consumed”. The sentence is said twice, once with “remain” and once with “consumed”, so that the loss is the same either way. Rashi says “remain” means those that did not die of hunger, and Radak says the horses are as thin and poor as the multitude. The KJV and the ASV have “five of the horses that remain”, the NIV “five of the horses that are left in the city”, the NWT 1984 “five of the remaining horses”, and the Douay “the five horses that are remaining”. Josephus sends two horsemen to the Jordan (Antiquities 9.83). The Hebrew has them take “two chariots with horses”.

“Go and see”. “Go and see”, lekhu ur’u, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: 2 Kings 6:13, where the king of Aram says it to find Elisha (now on these pages), this verse, where the king of Israel says it to find Aram, and Psalm 66:5. The KJV, the ASV and the Geneva have “Go and see”, the NWT 1984 “Go and see”, and the NIV “Go and find out what has happened”.

⚠ “As far as the Jordan”. They follow the Arameans to the Jordan, and “the whole way was full of garments and vessels that Aram had thrown away in their haste”. The Jordan is where the sons of the prophets cut beams (2 Kings 6:4) and where Naaman dipped (2 Kings 5:14), both now on these pages. The KJV and the ASV have “garments and vessels”, the NIV “clothing and equipment”, the NWT 1984 “garments and utensils”, and the TLB “clothing and equipment”. Josephus has the road full of food and arms (Antiquities 9.84).

⭐ “According to the word of Jehovah”. The people plunder the camp, and the prices come true: a seah of fine flour for a shekel and two seahs of barley for a shekel. The same words stand at the close of the widow’s jar of flour (1 Kings 17:16, with “which he spoke by the hand of Elijah”) and of the twenty loaves (2 Kings 4:44), both now on these pages. The NIV has “as the Lord had said”, the TLB “just as the Lord had said”, and the ASV and the NWT 1984 “according to the word of Jehovah”. Josephus has the same prices and says that the people found gold, silver, flocks and a great store of grain (Antiquities 9.85).

Verses 17–20 · 2 Kings 7:17–20 “Trampled in the gate” — the officer who saw it and did not eat

⚠ “The king appointed the officer… over the gate”. The king puts the officer he leans on in charge of the gate, “and the people trampled him in the gate, and he died, as the man of God had said, who spoke when the king came down to him”. The verse does not say why he was put there. Josephus says it was to hold back the crowd (Antiquities 9.86). “When the king came down to him” names the scene of 2 Kings 6:32–33 (now on these pages), where the messenger is coming down and “he said” is left unnamed; Rashi says “yesterday, when he came to Elisha’s house”. The ASV and the KJV have “when the king came down to him”, the NIV “when the king came down to his house”, and the TLB “when the king had come to arrest him”. The ASV has “trod upon him”, the NIV and the TLB “trampled”, and the NWT 1984 “kept trampling him”.

⭐ The prophecy told twice. Verses 18 and 19 give the exchange of verses 1 and 2 again, almost word for word, and verse 20 ends it: “And so it was to him; for the people trampled him in the gate, and he died.” “Floodgates in the heavens” stands in two verses, here and at verse 2. The officer is told “you shall see it with your eyes, but from there you shall not eat”, and he sees: the verse says “the people trampled him in the gate”. The NWT 1984 has “Here you are seeing it with your own eyes, but from it you will not eat”, the NIV “You will see it with your own eyes… but you will not eat any of it”, and the TLB “You will see it happen, but you won’t be able to buy any of it”.

Where Elisha is. His name stands in this chapter only at verse 1; after that he is “the man of God”, and he is not in the story of the four men, the king’s night or the road. The chapter ends on the gate. Josephus has Adar come back to Damascus, learn that it was God and not an enemy who had frightened his army, and fall ill (Antiquities 9.87).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “a seah of fine flour for a shekel”; “tomorrow about this time”; “the officer on whose hand the king leaned”; “floodgates in the heavens”, as at Genesis 7:11; “the scaly disease”, as at 2 Kings 5; “let us fall to”; “twilight”; “the Lord” for Adonai at verse 6, against “Jehovah” elsewhere; “silver and gold and garments”, as at 5:5; “a day of good news”; “guilt”; “gatekeepers”; “Go and see”, as at 6:13; “vessels”; “according to the word of Jehovah”, as at 4:44; and “trampled”. The written and read Hebrew are recorded at verses 12, 13 and 15.

Echoes paid. “Why should I wait for Jehovah any longer?” (2 Kings 6:33); the king who leans on an officer’s hand (2 Kings 5:18); the flood’s floodgates (Genesis 7:11); the threats “about this time tomorrow” (1 Kings 19:2; 1 Kings 20:6); the scaly disease outside the camp (Leviticus 13:46); silver, gold and garments (2 Kings 5:5, 23); horses and chariots seen (2 Kings 6:17) and heard; the kings of the Hittites (1 Kings 10:29); the messengers of Ahimaaz (2 Samuel 18:20, 27); “Go and see” (2 Kings 6:13); the Jordan (2 Kings 5:14; 2 Kings 6:4); and the closing words over flour and bread (1 Kings 17:16; 2 Kings 4:44).

Echoes planted. Gehazi still “the young man of the man of God”, in the next chapter (2 Kings 8:4); the famine of seven years that Elisha announces there (2 Kings 8:1); and Elisha in Damascus, with Ben-hadad king of Aram sick (2 Kings 8:7). None of these is translated here yet.

Next in this book: 2 Kings 8 — Elisha tells the Shunammite to leave before a famine of seven years; when she comes back and cries to the king, he is talking with Gehazi about all the great things Elisha has done; and Elisha goes to Damascus, where Ben-hadad is sick.
