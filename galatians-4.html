Paul tells the Galatians that the heir under guardians has come of age in Christ, that God sent his Son, born of a woman, and the Spirit crying ‘Abba! Father!’; he recalls how they first received him in his weakness, and reads Hagar and Sarah as two covenants.
Undated by the text: Galatians names no year. ‘The first time’ at 4:13 implies at least two visits to Galatia, but which visits, and so the letter’s date, is argued.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–7 · the heir as a minor, the elements of the world, born of a woman, and “Abba! Father!”

“For as long as the heir is a minor.” Nēpios is an infant, and in law a minor: the son who owns everything and can decide nothing. The picture carries on from the child-minder of 3:24. He is under epitropoi and oikonomoi. Epitropos, a person put in charge, stands in three verses of the New Testament: the vineyard owner’s “steward” at Matthew 20:8, Chuza, “Herod’s steward,” at Luke 8:3, and here, where the one put in charge of a child is a guardian. Oikonomoi are “stewards,” as in “stewards of God’s mysteries” (1 Corinthians 4:1). The KJV reads “under tutors and governors until the time appointed of the father”; the ASV reads “under guardians and stewards”; the NIV reads “subject to guardians and trustees.”

⚠ “The elements of the world” — stoicheia. In ordinary Greek stoicheia are things set in a row: the letters of the alphabet, the elements of matter, the first principles of a subject. Some readers also hear the powers thought to rule the elements and the stars, which would suit the calendar of v10. Stoicheion stands in seven verses of the New Testament: here and v9, Colossians 2:8 and 2:20, Hebrews 5:12, and 2 Peter 3:10 and 3:12. Paul says “we” were enslaved under them, and at v9 he says the same of his Gentile readers’ former gods: the law and the idols are set side by side as the state the child was in. Which sense he means is argued, and this page does not choose (dictionary). The KJV reads “in bondage under the elements of the world”; the ASV reads “under the rudiments of the world”; the NIV reads “under the elemental spiritual forces of the world”; the NWT 1984 reads “the elementary things belonging to the world.”

“The fullness of the time … born of a woman, born under law.” “The fullness of the time” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one: the date the father set in v2. “Born” is genomenon, from ginomai, “come to be,” twice, where vv23 and 29 use gennaō for the births of Abraham’s sons. “Sent out” is exapostellō, here of the Son and at v6 of the Spirit, and this translation prints “sent out” both times. The KJV reads “made of a woman, made under the law”; the ASV reads “born of a woman, born under the law”; the NWT 1984 reads “who came to be out of a woman and who came to be under law.”

“Buy out those under law … adoption.” Exagorazō is the “bought us out” of 3:13. Huiothesia, “adoption,” the legal making of a son, stands in five verses of the New Testament, all in Paul: Romans 8:15, Romans 8:23 and Romans 9:4, where the adoption belongs to Israel; this one; and Ephesians 1:5 (dictionary). The KJV reads “the adoption of sons”; the NIV reads “adoption to sonship.”

⚠ “Abba! Father!” — Aramaic and Greek in one cry. Abba ho patēr: the Aramaic word and the Greek for it, side by side (dictionary). The pair stands in three verses of the New Testament: Jesus in Gethsemane, “Abba — Father — all things are possible for you” (Mark 14:36); Romans 8:15, where “we cry out”; and here, where it is the Spirit who cries. The critical text reads “our hearts” and the Byzantine “your hearts,” which is why the KJV reads “the Spirit of his Son into your hearts.” At v7 the “you” turns singular, each reader by himself: “you are no longer a slave but a son.” The Byzantine text reads “an heir of God through Christ,” and so the KJV: “then an heir of God through Christ”; the ASV, with the critical text, reads “then an heir through God.”

Verses 8–11 · known by God, the weak and poor elements, and days, months, seasons and years

“Or rather to be known by God.” Paul corrects himself in mid-sentence. The Galatians did not find God so much as God knew them, as at 1 Corinthians 8:3, “whoever loves God is known by him,” and 1 Corinthians 13:12, “just as I also was fully known.” Before that they were slaves to “those who by nature are not gods,” the “so-called gods” of 1 Corinthians 8:5. The KJV reads “or rather are known of God”; the TLB reads “or I should say, now that God has found you.”

“The weak and poor elements … all over again.” Ptōcha is “poor,” the word for a beggar; palin anōthen is “again from the start,” and this translation prints “all over again.” Paul’s readers were Gentiles who had never kept the law. To take it on now is, in his words, to turn “back again” to the slavery under the elements that he has just described as theirs before Christ. The KJV reads “the weak and beggarly elements”; the NIV reads “those weak and miserable forces”; the NWT 1984 reads “the weak and beggarly elementary things.”

“Days and months and seasons and years.” Paratēreisthe, “you are carefully observing,” is the verb of the Pharisees who “were watching him closely” on the Sabbath (Luke 14:1, Mark 3:2): a close, anxious watching. The list names no festival. It is usually read as the Jewish calendar of Sabbaths, new moons, feasts and sabbatical years, and sometimes as a calendar of the stars, to go with “the elements”; the verse does not say. At Romans 14:5 Paul is gentler about the same thing: “One person considers one day above another; another considers every day the same.” The KJV reads “Ye observe days, and months, and times, and years”; the NWT 1984 reads “YOU are scrupulously observing”; the TLB reads “on certain days or months or seasons or years.”

“Labored over you in vain.” Kopiaō is hard work to the point of weariness, the “I labored more than all of them” of 1 Corinthians 15:10; eikē, “in vain,” is the word of 3:4. The KJV reads “lest I have bestowed upon you labour in vain”; the NIV reads “I have wasted my efforts on you.”

Verses 12–20 · a weakness of the flesh, an angel of God, eyes dug out, and a mother in labor

“Become as I am, because I also became as you are.” The second clause has no verb in Greek, hoti kagō hōs hymeis, “because I too as you.” This translation supplies “became”: Paul, a Jew, lived as one outside the law among them, as he says at 1 Corinthians 9:21, “to those outside the law I became as one outside the law.” The KJV reads “be as I am; for I am as ye are”; the ASV reads “for I also am become as ye are”; the NIV reads “become like me, for I became like you.”

⚠ “A weakness of the flesh” — and the thorn of 2 Corinthians 12. Di’ astheneian tēs sarkos: with the accusative, dia is “because of,” so an illness was the reason Paul preached in Galatia, not only the condition he preached in. These verses are where the illness reading of the “thorn in the flesh” (2 Corinthians 12:7) finds its support. To proteron is “the first time” or “formerly”; Acts has Paul pass through “the Galatian country” twice (Acts 16:6, Acts 18:23). The critical text reads “your trial in my flesh,” his condition a test for them; the Byzantine reads “my trial,” which is why the KJV reads “my temptation which was in my flesh.” Ekptyō, “spit at,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; ancient writers describe spitting as a way to ward off the evil eye or a demon, which would fit the evil eye of 3:1. Instead they received him “as an angel of God,” where at 2 Corinthians 12:7 the thorn was “an angel of Satan.” The NWT 1984 reads “YOU did not treat with contempt or spit at in disgust”; the ASV reads “ye despised not, nor rejected.”

“You would have dug out your eyes.” Exoryssō, “dig out,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: the friends who “dug through” the roof to lower the paralysed man (Mark 2:4), and this one. Some read the eyes as a clue that Paul’s illness was a disease of the eyes; others read a way of saying they would have given him the dearest thing they had. “Where, then, is your blessedness?” uses makarismos, the “blessedness” of Romans 4:6: their sense of being blessed, or their calling him blessed. The KJV reads “ye would have plucked out your own eyes”; the NIV reads “you would have torn out your eyes”; the ASV reads “Where then is that gratulation of yourselves?”; the NIV reads “Where, then, is your blessing of me now?”

“They are eager for you, not rightly.” Zēloō, three times in two verses, is the warm word of courting; it is the verb of 2 Corinthians 11:2, “I am jealous over you with God’s own jealousy.” The rivals court them in order to “shut you out,” ekkleiō, the verb of Romans 3:27, where boasting “has been shut out.” The KJV reads “They zealously affect you, but not well”; the NWT 1984 reads “They zealously seek YOU, not in a fine way”; the NIV reads “Those people are zealous to win you over, but for no good.”

⚠ “My children, over whom I am in labor again” — Paul as mother. Ōdinō, “to be in labor,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: the woman of Revelation 12:2, “in labor and in agony to give birth”; this one; and v27 of this chapter, Isaiah’s “you who are not in labor” (dictionary). Morphoō, “be formed,” stands in one verse, this one: until Christ takes shape in them. The critical text reads tekna, “children”; Westcott–Hort and the Byzantine text read teknia, “little children,” which is why the KJV reads “My little children, of whom I travail in birth again.” “Change my voice” is change my tone, and “I am at a loss” is the aporeomai of Festus at Acts 25:20. The NWT 1984 reads “I am perplexed over YOU”; the Douay reads “I am ashamed for you,” from the Vulgate’s confundor.

Verses 21–27 · two sons, an allegory, Hagar and Sinai, the present Jerusalem, and the barren woman

“Do you not hear the law?” The law here is the books of Moses, heard read aloud, and the story is Genesis: Hagar bore Abram a son (Genesis 16:15) and Sarah bore Isaac (Genesis 21:2). Paul names neither son yet, nor Sarah; Hagar is named in two verses of the New Testament, vv24 and 25. Paidiskē is “slave-girl,” the word for Hagar in this translation’s Genesis, “an Egyptian slave-girl” (Genesis 16:1) (dictionary). The Douay reads “have you not read the law?” from the Vulgate’s legistis; the NIV reads “are you not aware of what the law says?”

⚠ “Said allegorically.” Allēgoreō stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and it is where English “allegory” comes from: to say one thing and mean another. Whether Paul’s reading here is allegory in the strict sense or the matching of a past story to a present one is argued; the word is his (dictionary). The KJV reads “Which things are an allegory”; the NWT 1984 reads “These things stand as a symbolic drama”; the NIV reads “These things are being taken figuratively”; the Geneva reads “By the which things another thing is meant.”

⚠ “Hagar is Mount Sinai in Arabia.” The editions differ on the joining word, and some witnesses leave “Hagar” out; the Vulgate does, Sina enim mons est in Arabia, and so the Douay: “For Sina is a mountain in Arabia.” Arabia is where Paul went after his call (1:17). Why Hagar should be Sinai the verse does not explain; the suggestions include Hagar’s descendants living in Arabia. “Corresponds” is systoicheō, which stands in one verse, this one: “stands in the same row,” from the root of the stoicheia of vv3 and 9. The ASV reads “this Hagar is mount Sinai in Arabia and answereth to the Jerusalem that now is”; the NWT 1984 reads “this Haʹgar means Siʹnai, a mountain in Arabia.”

⚠ “The present Jerusalem” — and how the allegory has been used. In Christian history this passage was read as a verdict on the Jewish people, with the synagogue as the slave-girl and the church as the free woman, and v30, “Drive out the slave-girl,” was used to justify contempt for Jews. That use is part of the record. It is not the argument of the chapter, which is addressed to Gentile believers who “want to be under law” (v21) and is about whether they should take on circumcision; and the man making it is a Jew who writes at Romans 11:1, “God has not pushed away his people, has he? May it never be!” “She is in slavery with her children” names no people. The TLB writes one in: “and the Jews, who try to follow that system, are her slave children.”

“The Jerusalem above … our mother” — and Isaiah 54:1. The city above is the “new Jerusalem, coming down out of heaven from God” of Revelation 21:2. The Byzantine text adds “of us all,” which is why the KJV reads “which is the mother of us all.” The quotation is Isaiah 54:1, word for word as the Greek Old Testament has it; the Hebrew begins “Sing, O barren.” In Isaiah the words are spoken to a woman left desolate, and the chapters around them are addressed to Zion and Jerusalem. Its “not in labor” is the ōdinō of v19. The KJV reads “Rejoice, thou barren that bearest not”; the NIV reads “Be glad, barren woman, you who never bore a child.”

Verses 28–31 · children of promise, persecution, “Drive out the slave-girl,” and the free woman

“You, brothers, are children of promise, like Isaac.” Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th read “you”; Westcott–Hort and the Byzantine text read “we,” and so the KJV: “Now we, brethren, as Isaac was, are the children of promise”; the NIV reads “Now you, brothers and sisters, like Isaac, are children of promise.” At Romans 9:7 the same story gave Paul “In Isaac shall seed be called for you.”

⚠ “Persecuted” — and what Genesis says. In Genesis 21:9 Sarah saw Hagar’s son “laughing,” metsacheq, the root of Isaac’s own name; the Greek Old Testament reads “playing with Isaac her son.” Genesis does not say that Ishmael persecuted Isaac. Paul reads the scene as hostility, as some later Jewish interpretation also did. “So it is now also” points to the pressure on the Galatians. The KJV reads “persecuted him that was born after the Spirit”; the NWT 1984 reads “began persecuting the one born in the manner of spirit.”

⚠ “Drive out the slave-girl and her son” — Sarah’s words, as Scripture. In Genesis 21:10 these are Sarah’s words to Abraham: “the son of this slave-woman shall not inherit with my son, with Isaac.” Paul quotes them as “what the Scripture says” and writes “with the son of the free woman” for “with my son, with Isaac.” In Genesis God tells Abraham to “listen to her voice” (Genesis 21:12), and then hears the boy and promises Hagar, “I will make him into a great nation” (Genesis 21:18). The KJV reads “Cast out the bondwoman and her son”; the NIV reads “Get rid of the slave woman and her son”; the NWT 1984 reads “Drive out the servant girl and her son.”

“Not children of a slave-girl but of the free woman.” “A slave-girl” has no article; “the free woman” has one. The Vulgate carries the sentence on into the next chapter’s first words, and the Douay ends the verse “by the freedom wherewith Christ has made us free”; in the Greek that is 5:1. The KJV reads “we are not children of the bondwoman, but of the free.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “minor” for nēpios at 4:1 and 4:3; “elements” for stoicheia at 4:3 and 4:9; “sent out” for exapostellō at 4:4 and 4:6; “buy out” at 4:5, as at 3:13; “adoption,” as at Romans 8:15; “Abba! Father!,” as at Romans 8:15; “known by God,” as at 1 Corinthians 8:3; “in vain” for eikē, as at 3:4; “a weakness of the flesh” at 4:13; “dug out” for exoryssō, as at Mark 2:4; “blessedness” for makarismos, as at Romans 4:6; “shut out” for ekkleiō, as at Romans 3:27; “at a loss,” as at Acts 25:20; “in labor” for ōdinō at 4:19 and 4:27; “slave-girl” for paidiskē, as in Genesis 16; and “Drive out” at 4:30, as at Genesis 21:10.

Echoes paid. The child-minder of 3:24 becomes guardians and stewards; buy out and sons from 3:13 and 3:26; Abba from Mark 14:36 and Romans 8:15; known by God from 1 Corinthians 8:3; the weakness of the flesh and the angel promised at 2 Corinthians 12:7; the evil eye of 3:1; Arabia from 1:17; Hagar and Sarah’s sons from Genesis 16:15 and Genesis 21:2; Drive out from Genesis 21:10; in Isaac from Romans 9:7; the new Jerusalem of Revelation 21:2; and the woman in labor of Revelation 12:2.

Echoes planted. Freedom at 5:1, which the Vulgate attaches to 4:31; persecution “now” at 5:11 and 6:12; the elements at Colossians 2:8 and 2:20; the heavenly Jerusalem at Hebrews 12:22; and Isaiah 54:1 in its own chapter.

Next in this book: Galatians 5, already on these pages. “For freedom Christ set us free,” the works of the flesh, and the fruit of the Spirit.
