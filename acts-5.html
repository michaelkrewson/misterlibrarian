Some time after the sharing of goods in chapter 4: a husband and wife sell a piece of property and bring part of the price as though it were the whole, and both die at Peter’s questions; signs and wonders follow, the high priest’s party arrests the apostles, an angel opens the prison by night, and they are back teaching in the temple by daybreak. Brought before the Sanhedrin a second time, they answer that one must obey God rather than people; Gamaliel persuades the council to flog them and let them go.
Undated by the text beyond following chapter 4: the closing verses of chapter 4 are a stretch of time and the story of Ananias and Sapphira is set inside it, with no interval named. The two deaths are three hours apart (v7), the jailbreak is one night (v19) and the hearing the next morning (v21); Gamaliel’s speech names Theudas, whom Josephus places about A.D. 45 or 46, and the library reports that tension rather than dating the chapter by it.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · ἐνοσφίσατο ἀπὸ τῆς τιμῆς enosphisato apo tēs timēs — kept back some of the price — in the verb the Greek Bible last used of Achan, and with the three verbs of 4:37 laid out again

⚠ Verses 1–2 are the last three verses of chapter 4 laid out again, with one more verb. Barnabas ‘sold’ a field, ‘brought’ the money and ‘laid’ it ‘at the feet of the apostles’ (Acts 4:37); Ananias ‘sold’ a piece of property (epōlēsen), ‘brought’ a part (enenkas) and ‘laid’ it (ethēken) in the same words and at the same place. The extra verb is enosphisato, between the sale and the laying: he ‘kept back’ some of the price for himself, the middle voice marking whose benefit it was. Nothing in the Greek says the amount was small, large, or promised; what the verse marks is that the same three actions have a fourth inside them. KJV and ASV ‘kept back part of the price,’ NIV ‘kept back part of the money for himself,’ NWT ‘secretly held back some of the price,’ the Douay ‘by fraud kept back part of the price.’ TLB has no verb of keeping at all: Ananias ‘brought only part of the money, claiming it was the full price.’

Nosphizomai has been on these pages before, in a book about a different theft. It stands in three verses of the New Testament: here (vv2–3) and Titus 2:10 (not yet on these pages), where slaves are told not to ‘pilfer.’ In the Greek Old Testament it is the word for what Achan did — ‘they kept back from the devoted thing,’ enosphisanto apo tou anathematos, in the Greek of Joshua 7:1, the offense that fills Joshua 7. The library reports the echo and does not claim Luke meant it: the verb is the same, the situation (a hidden retention from what had been set apart, found out while the community is still new) is similar, and Luke does not say so. The dictionary entry sets the two uses side by side.

“His wife also being aware of it” (syneiduiēs kai tēs gynaikos) is the verb behind ‘conscience’ — syn, ‘together,’ plus oida, ‘know’ (syneidēsis) — so that what the verse reports is a knowing-together: a conscience shared by two people, and used by them on the same side. KJV and ASV have ‘privy to it,’ NWT ‘his wife also knowing about it,’ NIV ‘with his wife’s full knowledge.’

Two names the text does not pun on, and the library does. Ananias is the Greek form of the Hebrew Ḥananyah, ‘Jehovah has been gracious’ — the name, in the form Hananiah, of the prophet in Jeremiah 28:1 to whom Jeremiah says ‘Jehovah has not sent you’ (28:15) and who ‘died that same year’ (28:17). They are different men, and Luke draws no line between them. Sapphira is probably Aramaic and is usually explained as ‘beautiful’; some connect it to the stone-name sappheiros (Revelation 21:19, already on these pages). The library reports both derivations and decides neither.

Verses 3–4 · ἐπλήρωσεν ὁ Σατανᾶς τὴν καρδίαν σου eplērōsen ho Satanas tēn kardian sou — ‘Satan has filled your heart’ — the first of three fillings in one chapter, and a question about whose property it was

⚠ “Filled” is this chapter’s keyword, and the Greek spends it in two verbs. Eplērōsen here (from plēroō): Satan ‘filled’ Ananias’s heart. At v28 the high priest says, with the same verb, that the apostles have ‘filled’ Jerusalem with their teaching (peplērōkate). At v17 the high priest and his party are ‘filled’ with jealousy (eplēsthēsan zēlou), in the verb (pimplēmi) that the previous chapter used twice of people ‘filled’ with the Holy Spirit (4:8, 4:31). Two verbs, one English word: a heart, a city, a party, a room. This translation keeps ‘filled’ in all of them so the reader can see what the Greek has set side by side. Where Luke’s Gospel said Satan ‘entered into Judas’ (Luke 22:3), Acts says Satan ‘filled’ a heart; and Judas, too, ‘acquired a field’ (Acts 1:18) — the same word for land, chōrion, that Peter uses here at vv3 and 8. KJV ‘filled thine heart,’ NIV ‘so filled your heart,’ the Douay ‘tempted thy heart,’ the NWT ‘emboldened you’ — two renderings that leave the verb of fullness behind.

The lie is told to the Holy Spirit in v3, and to God in v4. Peter’s two sentences are parallel — pseusasthai se to pneuma to hagion, ‘to lie to the Holy Spirit,’ and ouk epseusō anthrōpois alla tō theō, ‘you have not lied to people but to God’ — and the Greek states neither conclusion that readers have drawn from the pair: that the Spirit is God, or that to deceive those who carry God’s Spirit is to deceive God. The traditions divide on exactly that, and this translation prints the two sentences and lets them stand. The NWT prints ‘holy spirit’ in lower case, in line with its teaching that the holy spirit is God’s active force and not a person, and has ‘play false to the holy spirit’ for ‘lie’ in the 1984 edition (NWT 2013 has ‘lie to the holy spirit’); the rest of the shelf reads ‘lie to the Holy Spirit.’

“Did it not remain yours while it remained?” The Greek has the same verb twice (menon soi emenen, ‘remaining, it remained’), and the Douay ‘did it not remain to thee?’ and the ASV ‘did it not remain thine own?’ both keep the doubling, where the NIV ‘Didn’t it belong to you before it was sold?’ and the TLB ‘The property was yours to sell or not’ give the sense and drop the repetition. What the questions say is plain: the property was Ananias’s, the sale was his, and the money after it was in his authority (exousia). The sin Peter names is not keeping some of it. It is the deed ‘put in the heart’ (ethou en tē kardia sou) — the planned pretense that the part was the whole. The verses just before describe owners of fields and houses who sold them and brought the proceeds (4:34–35), and later readers have drawn opposite conclusions about whether any compulsion to give was in view; Peter’s questions say it was not, and the text gives no further verdict.

Verses 5–11 · ἐξέψυξεν … ἐκκλησίαν exepsyxen … ekklēsian — two deaths nobody pronounces, a word that makes its first appearance in Acts, and a pair of people who ‘agreed’

⚠ Neither death comes with a sentence. Peter asks questions in vv3–4; Ananias ‘fell down and breathed his last’ at hearing them; and the verse names no agent. At v9 Peter again says only where the burial party’s feet are. Readers have taken the two deaths as an act of God, as a collapse under exposure, or as a narrated judgment whose mechanism Luke does not describe, and the verses support none of them over the others by an explicit statement. The library sets beside them two other founding-days stories in which a breach is answered at once — Nadab and Abihu, where ‘fire came out from before Jehovah and consumed them’ (Leviticus 10:2), and Achan — and notes that in this chapter there is no fire and no speech of judgment. The verb exepsyxen, ‘breathed out his life,’ stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, v10, and Acts 12:23, where it ends the story of a king who is struck down and ‘eaten by worms.’ KJV ‘gave up the ghost,’ ASV ‘gave up the ghost,’ NIV ‘died,’ TLB ‘fell to the floor, dead!’ and the NWT ‘expired’ in 1984, where NWT 2013 has ‘collapsed and died.’

Feet, three times in nine verses — and twice before. The money is laid ‘at the feet of the apostles’ (v2, and 4:35, 37); the feet of the burial party ‘are at the door’ (v9); and Sapphira falls ‘at his feet’ (v10). The money is surrendered at the apostles’ feet, and the woman falls at Peter’s. The ‘younger men’ (neōteroi, v6) who carry Ananias out become the ‘young men’ (neaniskoi, v10) who carry out Sapphira — the same group in two words — and synestheilan, ‘wrapped him up,’ is the only use of that verb in Acts. No report of it has reached her in the three hours between: the verse says she came in ‘not knowing what had happened.’

“Why was it agreed between you?” The verb synephōnēthē, from syn and phōnē, is the one English borrowed for ‘symphony’: a sounding together. The only earlier use of the verb in Luke’s two volumes is a comparison about patches (Luke 5:36), and Matthew has it in the promise that if two ‘agree’ about anything they ask, it will be done (Matthew 18:19, already on these pages). Here the two who agree are a husband and wife, and what they agree on is a test. Peirasai means to put to the proof; its compound, ekpeirazō, is the verb of Luke 4:12 (‘You shall not put Jehovah your God to the test,’ already on these pages). KJV ‘tempt,’ ASV ‘try,’ NIV ‘conspire to test,’ NWT ‘make a test of.’ TLB has ‘conspiring together to test the Spirit of God’s ability to know what is going on,’ a gloss that adds the reason.

“The Spirit of the Lord” — one object under three names. Peter has said ‘the Holy Spirit’ (v3), then ‘God’ (v4), and now to pneuma kyriou. This translation keeps ‘Lord’ here, because kyrios in Acts can mean the God of Israel or Jesus, and this verse does not say which: the phrase in the Greek of Acts 16:7 reads ‘the Spirit of Jesus.’ The NWT prints ‘the spirit of Jehovah’ in both editions, taking kyrios as God; the KJV, ASV, NIV and Douay print ‘Lord.’ The library notes the choice rather than settling it.

⚠ “Congregation” — the word’s first appearance in Acts. Ekklēsia has not been used in the book until this verse: in chapters 1–4 Luke names the believers in other ways, and the first time he calls them an ekklēsia is in a sentence about fear. The critical Greek printed here has no ekklēsia at 2:47, where the later manuscripts read ‘in the congregation’ (tē ekklēsia). This translation reads ‘congregation’ throughout, with Tyndale; KJV, ASV, NIV, Douay, Geneva and TLB all print ‘church,’ and the NWT alone on this shelf reads ‘congregation.’ See the dictionary entry for why. The first ‘congregation’ in the book of Acts is a congregation that is afraid.

Verses 12–16 · ἐπισκιάσῃ episkiasē — Peter’s shadow, and the verb Luke last used of the power that came on Mary

Signs and wonders, by the hands of the apostles — the answer to a prayer. The congregation in 4:30 asked that ‘signs and wonders happen through the name of your holy servant Jesus,’ and v12 reports them: sēmeia kai terata, ‘signs and wonders,’ the pair of 2:43, now through the apostles’ hands. Hands are a motif of this chapter: they work the signs here (v12), are laid on the apostles by the Sadducees (v18), and are the root of the verb of killing at v30. And they are all ‘with one accord’ (homothymadon) in Solomon’s Portico — the fourth use of that word in Acts, after 1:14, 2:46 and 4:24; the same colonnade where the crowd ran together after the lame man was healed (3:11).

⚠ Who are “the rest” in v13? Tōn de loipōn oudeis etolma kollasthai autois: ‘of the rest, not one dared to join them.’ The Greek gives no noun. The TLB reads the rest as other believers (‘The other believers didn’t dare join them’), the KJV and ASV leave it as open as the Greek (‘of the rest durst no man join himself to them’), the NWT reads ‘not a one of the others,’ and the NIV takes them as outsiders, in a sentence that opens by excluding everyone else. The verse sets against them ‘the people’ (ho laos), who ‘magnified’ the apostles, and v14 sets believers ‘added’ against both: not one would join them, and more and more were added. The Greek has two different verbs: kollasthai, ‘to be glued to,’ the verb of the dust that ‘clings’ to the feet (Luke 10:11) and of the son who ‘attached himself’ to a citizen of the far country (Luke 15:15), and prosetithento, ‘were being added,’ the verb of 2:41 and 2:47. The reader decides who is meant; the library only notes that the people who hold back are not the people who honor.

The verb of the shadow is the verb of Mary. Episkiazō, ‘to overshadow,’ stands in five verses of the New Testament: the power of the Most High that ‘will overshadow’ Mary (Luke 1:35), the cloud on the mountain (Luke 9:34, with Matthew 17:5 and Mark 9:7), and Peter’s shadow here. Only here does a person’s shadow do it. This translation keeps ‘overshadow’ so the echo is visible. The shelf varies: KJV ‘overshadow some of them,’ ASV ‘at the least his shadow might overshadow some one of them,’ NIV ‘at least Peter’s shadow might fall on some of them,’ NWT ‘at least his shadow might fall upon some one of them,’ and the Douay adds a clause that is not in the Greek, ‘and they might be delivered from their infirmities.’ ⚠ The verse states a purpose — they laid the sick out so that his shadow might fall — and v16 reports that those brought from the cities ‘were all healed.’ Luke does not say anyone was healed by the shadow, and the library does not say it for him. The verb for the poor person’s bed, klinarion (‘cot’), stands only here in the New Testament; its companion krabattos, ‘mat,’ is the pallet of Mark 2:4 and John 5:8, both already on these pages. The crowds from ‘the cities around Jerusalem’ bring the sick and ‘those troubled by unclean spirits’: TLB ‘those possessed by demons,’ KJV ‘vexed with unclean spirits.’ Luke’s other, later, healing at a distance is the cloths carried from Paul’s body (Acts 19:12, not yet on these pages), in a word that is also a dictionary entry (soudarion).

Verses 17–21 · ἐπλήσθησαν ζήλου … ῥήματα τῆς ζωῆς ταύτης eplēsthēsan zēlou … rhēmata tēs zōēs tautēs — a party filled with jealousy, and an angel who sends them back to the same place at daybreak

⚠ “Jealousy,” not “zeal.” Zēlos is the word for both, and the verse decides which by its cause: the high priest’s party are ‘filled’ with it because of vv12–16, the crowds and the healings and the honor of the people. The same word is the Gospel’s ‘Zeal for your house will consume me’ (John 2:17, already on these pages), which shows how far the word can turn. The exact phrase eplēsthēsan zēlou, ‘they were filled with jealousy,’ stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Acts 13:45. KJV ‘indignation,’ Geneva ‘full of indignation,’ ASV, NIV and NWT ‘jealousy,’ and the Douay ‘envy.’ The ‘indignation’ of the KJV tradition hides the motive; the verse gives one.

“The party of the Sadducees” — hairesis, and where English got ‘heresy.’ The Greek means a choice, then a school or party that has made one. Latin borrowed it as haeresis, and the Douay translates the Latin: ‘the heresy of the Sadducees.’ In Acts, Luke uses the word of the Sadducees here, of the Pharisees (15:5, and 26:5, not yet on these pages), and, in the mouths of other people, of the Christians themselves (24:5, 24:14 and 28:22, not yet on these pages). NIV ‘the party of the Sadducees,’ KJV and ASV ‘sect.’ This translation reads ‘party’ because it is the plainest English for a body of people defined by a shared position. See the dictionary entry.

“An angel of Jehovah” and “the words of this life.” The Greek reads angelos kyriou, ‘angel of the Lord,’ in the formula the Greek Old Testament uses for Jehovah’s angel, so this translation reads ‘Jehovah’ (the NWT has ‘Jehovah’s angel’; KJV, ASV, NIV and Douay have ‘the Lord’). The angel’s whole message is one command: go to the same place and say the same thing. ‘All the words of this life’ (panta ta rhēmata tēs zōēs tautēs) has an echo in Peter’s own line in John 6:68, ‘you have words (rhēmata) of eternal life.’ Whether ‘this life’ is already a name for the movement, as ‘the Way’ becomes, the Greek does not say; the ASV and TLB capitalize it (‘this Life’), which settles the question by typography, and the NIV adds an adjective that is not in the Greek (‘this new life’).

“The Sanhedrin and all the senate of the sons of Israel.” Gerousia is a word that stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and the phrase tēn gerousian tōn huiōn Israēl, ‘the senate of the sons of Israel,’ is the wording of the Greek of Exodus 3:16, where Moses is told to gather it. Whether it names a larger body added to the Sanhedrin or a second name for the Sanhedrin’s elders, the Greek (kai, ‘and’) allows both. The NIV reads it as apposition, ‘the full assembly of the elders of Israel.’ The KJV and ASV keep ‘all the senate of the children of Israel,’ the Douay has ‘all the ancients of the children of Israel.’

Verses 22–28 · οὐ μετὰ βίας … τῷ ὀνόματι τούτῳ ou meta bias … tō onomati toutō — a locked and empty prison, a captain who arrests without force, and a high priest who will not say the name

The prison, locked with complete security and empty. The report has a precise comedy: the court has convened, it sends for the prisoners, and the officers return to say the prison was ‘locked with complete security, and the guards standing at the doors, but when we opened it we found not a soul inside’ (v23). The doors the angel opened (v19) are closed again by the time the officers arrive, and the verses do not say how. The verb for the authorities’ state of mind is diēporoun, ‘they were perplexed’ — the verb the crowd at Pentecost used (Acts 2:12) and Herod of Jesus (Luke 9:7), and Peter of his vision at Acts 10:17: in each case, people before something they cannot classify. KJV ‘they doubted of them,’ ASV ‘much perplexed,’ NIV ‘at a loss,’ NWT ‘fell into a quandary,’ TLB ‘frantic.’ Hypēretai, ‘officers,’ keeps the word this translation used at John 18:3; the TLB has ‘police,’ the Douay ‘ministers.’

“The captain of the temple” returns, as chapter 4 said he would, and this time he is gentle. He goes himself, brings them ‘not with force’ (ou meta bias), and Luke gives the reason: ‘for they feared the people, lest they be stoned.’ ⚠ It is the third time in two chapters that the people stand between the apostles and the authorities — the council could not punish them ‘because of the people’ (4:21), the people ‘magnified’ them (v13), and now the guard fears being stoned. KJV and ASV ‘without violence,’ NIV ‘did not use force,’ TLB adds a gloss (‘if they roughed up the disciples’) and, in the text fetched for this shelf, prints the whole of v26 again as v27.

⚠ The high priest will not say the name. He says ‘this name’ (tō onomati toutō, v28) and ‘this man’ — as the council had said ‘this name’ at 4:17 — and Peter, in the next breath, supplies it: ‘Jesus’ (v30). The charge is in three parts: parangelia parēngeilamen, ‘with a charge we charged you,’ a doubled form that the Douay keeps (‘Commanding we commanded you’) and that this translation renders as ‘strictly charged’ and flags here; ‘you have filled Jerusalem with your teaching’; and ‘you intend to bring upon us the blood of this man.’ The third is the high priest’s own reading of what the apostles are doing. Pilate’s ‘I am innocent of this blood’ (Matthew 27:24) and the people’s answer, ‘His blood be on us and on our children’ (27:25), both already on these pages, are Matthew’s and not Luke’s, and Acts does not connect them to this sentence.

⚠ A question or a statement? The Greek text printed here has ‘We strictly charged you not to teach on this name’ as a statement. The Nestle-Aland 28 text and the later Byzantine manuscripts add a negative, ou, which turns the sentence into a question: ‘Did we not strictly charge you…?’ The KJV (‘Did not we straitly command you’) and Geneva (‘Did we not straightly command you’) print the question; the ASV, NIV and NWT print the statement. The apparatus names both readings; this translation follows the one printed text and reports the other.

Verses 29–32 · Πειθαρχεῖν δεῖ θεῷ μᾶλλον ἢ ἀνθρώποις Peitharchein dei theō mallon ē anthrōpois — ‘One must obey God rather than people’ — the answer to chapter 4’s ‘we cannot’

⚠ “We cannot” becomes “one must.” The answer to the second hearing is the principle behind the first. At 4:20 Peter and John said ‘we cannot but speak’; here the apostles say peitharchein dei theō mallon ē anthrōpois, ‘one must obey God rather than people.’ Dei is the Greek verb of necessity — ‘it is necessary’ — the same one in ‘by which we must be saved’ (4:12); this translation keeps the impersonal ‘one must’ where the shelf has ‘we must.’ Peitharcheō, ‘to obey one in authority,’ stands in four verses of the New Testament, two of them in this chapter (vv29 and 32), and the others are Acts 27:21 and Titus 3:1 (neither yet on these pages). NIV ‘We must obey God rather than human beings,’ KJV ‘We ought to obey God rather than men,’ ASV ‘We must obey God rather than men,’ and the NWT alone carries the root: ‘We must obey God as ruler rather than men.’ The added ‘as ruler’ follows the verb’s parts (peithō, ‘persuade, obey,’ and archē, ‘rule’) and is not a doctrinal addition; the other versions on the shelf drop the second half.

“Whom you killed, hanging him on a tree.” Diecheirisasthe is built on cheir, ‘hand,’ the hand-word of this chapter (v12, v18), and means to lay hands on to kill; it stands in only one other verse (Acts 26:21, not yet on these pages). ‘Tree’ is xylon, ‘wood,’ the cut thing — the word for the cross in Acts 5:30, 10:39 and 13:29 — and the wording is that of the Greek of Deuteronomy 21:22–23 (kremasantes epi xylou, ‘hanging on a tree’), where a man hanged on a tree is under a curse; Paul makes that connection outright at Galatians 3:13 (not yet on these pages), and Peter here does not. KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva keep ‘tree’; NIV and TLB both print ‘cross,’ which is an interpretation (there is no word for ‘cross’ in the verse), and the NWT has ‘stake.’

“Author and Savior” — and a dative the shelf cannot agree on. Archēgos is the word this translation rendered ‘Author’ of life at 3:15, and it stands in four verses of the New Testament: Acts 3:15 and 5:31 and Hebrews 2:10 and 12:2 (see the dictionary entry). The KJV, ASV, NIV, TLB, Douay and Geneva all read ‘Prince’; the NWT reads ‘Chief Agent.’ The Greek tē dexia autou, literally by or to his right hand, is a dative that can mean place or instrument: the KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva read ‘with his right hand’ (instrument), the NIV ‘to his own right hand’ and the NWT ‘to his right hand’ (place). This translation reads place, as it did at 2:33, where the verse is followed by Psalm 110. And ‘to give repentance to Israel’ makes repentance a gift (metanoia, as at 11:18): NIV ‘that he might bring Israel to repentance,’ TLB ‘an opportunity for repentance.’

The witnesses, and the Spirit given to those who obey. The apostles are witnesses ‘of these things’ (rhēmatōn, ‘words/matters’), and so is the Holy Spirit, ‘whom God gave to those who obey him’ — the same root as the verb of v29. Luke names different conditions for the Spirit’s gift in different places: repentance and baptism at 2:38, asking at Luke 11:13, obeying here; the library reports the three and does not harmonize them. KJV ‘we are his witnesses’ follows a later manuscript reading that adds ‘his’ (the apparatus: autou); the ASV and NIV follow the shorter text.

Verses 33–39 · θεομάχοι theomachoi — Gamaliel’s speech, two rebels the historian dates differently, and the one word in the New Testament for fighting against God

⚠ “Cut to the quick” is a saw. Dieprionto, from diaprizō, is ‘to saw through’ — the wood-cutter’s word, turned to anger. It stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and Acts 7:54, where the audience of Stephen is in the same condition. KJV, ASV and Douay ‘cut to the heart,’ NWT ‘felt deeply cut’ (1984) and ‘infuriated’ (NWT 2013), NIV and TLB ‘furious.’ This translation keeps the idiom, ‘cut to the quick,’ and says here that the Greek verb is a saw.

Gamaliel, and what the name means. A Pharisee, nomodidaskalos, ‘a teacher of the law’ (the word of Luke 5:17), ‘honored by all the people’ — the same people who have been the apostles’ shield. Paul will say he was brought up ‘at the feet of Gamaliel’ (Acts 22:3, not yet on these pages). The name is Hebrew Gamliʾel, ‘God is my reward,’ the name of the chieftain of Manasseh in Numbers 1:10, where the library first noted that a Pharisee of Acts 5 would carry it. ⚠ The verb in his instruction is ekeleusen, ‘ordered,’ and the thing ordered is that ‘the men’ be put outside. The NIV, NWT and Douay read ‘the men,’ as does the critical Greek printed here (anthrōpous); the KJV, Geneva and TLB read ‘the apostles,’ which is the later manuscript reading (apostolous).

⚠ Theudas and Judas: two rebels, and an order the historian reverses. Gamaliel names two earlier movements: Theudas, with about four hundred men, who ‘was killed,’ and Judas the Galilean ‘in the days of the registration’ (apographē, the word of Luke 2:2), who drew people away and ‘perished.’ KJV ‘taxing,’ Geneva ‘tribute,’ ASV ‘enrolment,’ NIV ‘census,’ NWT ‘registration,’ TLB ‘taxation,’ and the Douay spells the first name ‘Theodas.’ The historian Josephus, in Whiston’s translation, tells of a ‘Theudas’ who persuaded a great part of the people to follow him to the Jordan, promising to divide it, and was beheaded under the procurator Fadus (Antiquities 20.97–98); the translator’s own note dates that to about A.D. 45 or 46. He tells, too, of Judas the Gaulonite, ‘of a city whose name was Gamala,’ who with a Pharisee named Sadduc urged a revolt against the taxation (Antiquities 18.1.1, numbered 18.4 in the Greek), and calls Judas the Galilean ‘the author’ of the ‘fourth sect’ of Jewish philosophy (Antiquities 18.1.6, numbered 18.23 in the Greek, where the word is hēgemōn, ‘leader’). So in Josephus Theudas comes after Judas, by about four decades (the translator’s notes date the two to about A.D. 7 and about A.D. 45 or 46), where Gamaliel in Luke names them the other way round, and a Theudas of A.D. 45 would fall after a speech set in the opening years of the congregation. Readers have proposed an earlier rebel of the same name (Whiston’s note allows one), a different man, and a slip in a report of a speech given while the apostles were outside the room (v34). The library reports the three and does not decide between them.

“Fighters against God” is one word. Theomachoi stands in one verse of the New Testament. The related verb stands in the Greek of 2 Maccabees 7:19, where one of the brothers being put to death tells the king not to suppose he will go unpunished, having undertaken theomachein, ‘to fight against God’ (the book is not in the Hebrew Bible; it is in the Douay-Rheims). The Greek of the verse is a conditional: ‘if this plan is from people, it will be overthrown; if from God, you will not be able to overthrow it, lest you even be found fighters against God.’ KJV ‘lest haply ye be found even to fight against God,’ NIV ‘you will only find yourselves fighting against God,’ NWT ‘fighters actually against God.’ ⚠ The chapter’s three contrasts of anthrōpoi and God — ‘not to people, but to God’ (v4), ‘God rather than people’ (v29), ‘from people… from God’ (vv38–39) — are one axis, and the council is the party that chooses by outcome: v39 closes with a verb of persuasion (epeisthēsan), where the apostles at v29 had said peitharchein: they obey God; the council is persuaded by a man.

Verses 40–42 · κατηξιώθησαν ὑπὲρ τοῦ ὀνόματος ἀτιμασθῆναι katēxiōthēsan hyper tou onomatos atimasthēnai — counted worthy of dishonor — one verb, and a reward Jesus had promised

The first beating in the book. Derantes, ‘flogged,’ is from derō, a verb of skin: to flay, to thrash. It is the verb of what the men holding Jesus did (Luke 22:63), and Acts 5:40 is the first use of it in this book (it returns at 16:37; and at 22:19, not yet on these pages). The apostles were arrested at 4:3 and warned at 4:18 and 4:21; this is the first time they are struck. The order is the same one as before: ‘not to speak on the name of Jesus’ (epi tō onomati, as at 4:17–18). KJV ‘beaten,’ NIV and NWT ‘flogged,’ Douay ‘scourged.’

⚠ “Counted worthy to be dishonored” is built from two words that mean the opposite. Katēxiōthēsan (from axios, ‘worthy’) atimasthēnai (from timē, ‘honor’): found worthy of an un-honoring. The verb kataxioō stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, 2 Thessalonians 1:5 (already on these pages), and Luke 20:35, where it is used of ‘those who are counted worthy to attain to that age, and to the resurrection from the dead’ (Luke 20:35, already on these pages). The same verb gives worthiness of the resurrection and of dishonor. And the rejoicing is a payment: Jesus had said ‘Rejoice in that day, and leap for joy’ when people hate and insult you (Luke 6:23, already on these pages), and the apostles go ‘rejoicing.’ KJV ‘counted worthy to suffer shame for his name,’ NIV ‘counted worthy of suffering disgrace for the Name,’ NWT ‘counted worthy to be dishonored in behalf of his name,’ Douay ‘accounted worthy to suffer reproach for the name of Jesus.’

“For the Name” — with nothing after it. Hyper tou onomatos stands without ‘of Jesus’ in the critical Greek printed here; the later manuscripts add ‘of Jesus’ (the apparatus gives the word order), and the Douay prints ‘the name of Jesus.’ ASV and NIV print ‘the Name’ with a capital, as 3 John 1:7 does in the same words (already on these pages: ‘for the sake of the Name they went out’). It is the chapter’s last word about the name that has run through chapters 3, 4 and 5, now with nothing added to it and nothing needed.

“From house to house” — the book’s other venue. Kat’ oikon was how the congregation broke bread at 2:46, and the pairing ‘in the temple and from house to house’ names the two places Acts will keep returning to. NIV and NWT keep ‘from house to house,’ KJV ‘in every house,’ ASV ‘at home,’ and the TLB ‘in their home Bible classes,’ an addition that the Greek does not have. The closing phrase ton christon Iēsoun, ‘the Christ, Jesus,’ is the object of both verbs, ‘teaching and bringing good news’; the critical Greek has the title first and the name after, where the later manuscripts have ‘Jesus the Christ.’ NWT ‘the Christ, Jesus,’ ASV ‘Jesus as the Christ,’ KJV ‘Jesus Christ,’ NIV and TLB ‘that Jesus is the Messiah.’ The ban at 4:18 and the order at v40 are both, by the last verse, disobeyed — ‘they did not stop’ (ouk epauonto) — and the chapter ends in the temple, where the angel had sent them.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “kept back” for nosphizomai at vv2–3, one verb in both verses; “filled” for plēroō at vv3 and 28 and pimplēmi at v17, so the heart, the city and the party are all visibly filled; “breathed his last” for ekpsychō at vv5 and 10; “field” for chōrion at vv3 and 8, as at 1:18; “agreed” for symphōneō and “test” for peirazō at v9; “Lord” for kyrios at vv9 and 14, where the referent is open or Jesus; “congregation” for ekklēsia at v11; “overshadow” for episkiazō at v15, as at Luke 1:35; “party” for hairesis at v17; “Jehovah” at v19, where the Greek reads kyrios in the formula for Jehovah’s angel; “officers” for hypēretai at vv22 and 26, as at John 18:3; “on this name” for epi tō onomati at vv28 and 40, as at 4:17; “one must” for dei at v29, as at 4:12; “Author” for archēgos at v31, as at 3:15; “to his right hand” at v31, as at 2:33; “Sanhedrin” for synedrion, with “senate” for gerousia; “registration” for apographē, as at Luke 2:2; “fighters against God” for theomachoi; “flogged” for derō; “for the Name” at v41; and “the Christ, Jesus” at v42.

Echoes paid. Sold, brought, laid at the apostles’ feet — the three verbs 4:37 gave Barnabas return at vv1–2 with a fourth inside them. The captain of the temple — 4:1 said he would be back at 5:24 and 26, and he is. “We cannot” (4:20) — becomes “one must” at v29. The ban on a name (4:17–18) — repeated by the high priest at v28 and carried out by the apostles, rejoicing, at vv40–42. “Signs and wonders” — the congregation’s prayer at 4:30, answered at v12. Solomon’s Portico — 3:11, now the place where they meet. Homothymadon — a fourth time, at v12. The people as shield — 4:21, then vv13 and 26. Satan and the field — Luke 22:3 and Acts 1:18. Achan’s verb — Joshua 7’s note said nosphizomai would be Ananias’s, and it is (vv2–3). Gamaliel — the name Numbers 1:10 said a Pharisee of Acts 5 would carry. “Rejoice and leap for joy” — Luke 6:23, kept at v41.

Echoes planted. Ekklēsia, whose first appearance in Acts is v11, and which the book will use again at 7:38, at 8:1 and 8:3, and on (9:31 and later, not yet on these pages). Gamaliel, who appears once more at 22:3, where Paul was brought up at his feet. Peter’s shadow, and the cloths carried from Paul’s body at 19:12. The verb exepsyxen, back at 12:23. Hairesis, back at 15:5 and 26:5 for the Pharisees and at 24:5, 14 and 28:22 for the Christians. The saw of v33, back at 7:54. And Ananias, a name that the book will give to two other men: a disciple at Damascus (9:10, and apparently 22:12) and a high priest (23:2 and 24:1).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether Luke means the echo of Achan at vv2–3. Who, or what, causes the deaths at vv5 and 10, and what the verses support. The derivation of ‘Sapphira’ (v1). Whether the shadow healed (vv15–16). Who “the rest” are at v13. Whether “this life” is a name (v20), and whether the senate is a second body (v21). Whether the high priest asks a question or makes a statement at v28 (the Greek texts differ). The order of Theudas and Judas in Gamaliel’s speech, set against Josephus (vv36–37). Whether “the men” or “the apostles” are put outside at v34. Whether the right hand is place or instrument at v31. And the Spirit of the Lord (v9), where kyrios is God or Jesus.
