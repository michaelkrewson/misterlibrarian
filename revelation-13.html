A beast rises from the sea, wearing the dragon's own seven heads and ten horns, its body a composite of the leopard, bear, and lion of Daniel's earlier vision; one of its heads bears a fatal wound that heals, and the whole earth follows it in astonishment, asking 'who is like the beast?' A second beast then rises from the earth, lamb-horned but dragon-voiced, working false signs to make the world worship an image of the first beast and forcing everyone, 'the small and the great, the rich and the poor,' to receive its mark before they may buy or sell — a mark identified, at the chapter's close, by a number: 666.
c. AD 95, continuing the second vision cycle opened in chapter 12 — the two beasts have been read across the centuries as the persecuting Roman state and its supporting imperial cult, and, by later interpreters, variously as other empires or institutions since; the number of the beast has its own long, disputed history of proposed solutions, this book's own dictionary/encyclopedia entries among them.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · a beast from the sea, wearing every empire Daniel ever saw · θηρίον ἀναβαῖνον ἐκ τῆς θαλάσσης thērion anabainon ek tēs thalassēs

⚠ Where the dragon left off. The chapter before this one closed on the dragon standing on the shore, watching the water (12:18, and its own note on a variant that moves that whole clause into this chapter for some Bibles). The shelf splits four ways on exactly where the watching ends and this chapter begins. The KJV opens its own verse 1 with the standing itself, ‘I stood upon the sand of the sea, and saw a beast rise up out of the sea’; the ASV keeps it there too, ‘he stood upon the sand of the sea. And I saw a beast coming up out of the sea’; and the NWT keeps it a third way, ‘it stood still upon the sand of the sea. And I saw a wild beast ascending out of the sea’ — three versions that read the clause three different ways (I / he / it) yet agree on WHERE it belongs. The NIV resolves the pronoun outright, ‘The dragon stood on the shore of the sea. And I saw a beast coming out of the sea’, and still opens chapter 13 with it. But the TLB closes the standing out in chapter 12 and opens this one instead with ‘And now, in my vision, I saw a strange Creature rising up out of the sea’; Geneva opens with ‘And I saw a beast rise out of the sea, having seven heads, and ten horns’; and the Douay-Rheims opens with ‘And I saw a beast coming up out of the sea, having seven heads and ten horns’ — all three exactly where this translation does, with no residue of the sentence before it. Six versions, no consensus on the boundary, and the boundary is a late accident of chapter division, not a claim the Greek itself forces either way.

A body built from Daniel’s whole zoo. The beast’s ten horns and seven heads are the dragon’s own anatomy, one chapter old (12:3), itself borrowed from the fourth and most terrible of Daniel’s four beasts, the one with iron teeth and ten horns (Daniel 7:7). But verse 2 goes further than the dragon did: a leopard’s body, a bear’s feet, a lion’s mouth — Daniel’s third, second, and first beasts (Daniel 7:4–6), named here in the reverse of the order Daniel saw them rise from the sea, one after another, across a lifetime of empires. Whatever historical figure each of Daniel’s four beasts was ever thought to stand for, the literary move here needs no such decoding to register: every animal Daniel needed four separate visions to describe is compressed into one composite monster, still rising, Daniel tells us (7:3), ‘from the great sea’ — the same sea this beast comes up from now.

Names of blasphemy — how many? The apparatus splits this exact chapter three times over a single-word fork between singular and plural (here at v1, and twice more at vv5–6): WH, Treg, NA28 — AND the Byzantine RP reconstruction — all read the plural, ‘names’ (one per head, presumably); only the printed Textus Receptus behind KJV and Geneva reads the singular, ‘the name of blasphemy’, a reading not otherwise attested even among the late Byzantine manuscripts the TR claims to represent. The Douay-Rheims, translating the Latin Vulgate centuries before either English version, already has the plural — ‘names of blasphemy’ — so the singular is a narrower reading than its own textual family usually suggests. This translation follows the wider plural, shared by every edition and translation checked here except the two behind the Textus Receptus.

★ The beast, in full. This is the therion this book has been withholding since its bare, unexplained first mention two chapters ago (11:7) — the promised ‘full portrait’ finally paid off.

Verses 3–4 · a healed wound, and a blasphemous echo of a name · μίαν ἐκ τῶν κεφαλῶν αὐτοῦ ὡς ἐσφαγμένην εἰς θάνατον mian ek tōn kephalōn autou hōs esphagmenēn eis thanaton

⚠ A counterfeit resurrection. ‘Slain’ is esphagmenēn — the identical participle, same root, this book has already used exactly once: the Lamb, ‘standing, as though slain’ (esphagmenon, 5:6), and will use once more of that same Lamb eight verses from here (v8, below). The beast’s healed death-wound is not a second miracle alongside the Lamb’s; it is a forgery of the Lamb’s own death-and-life paradox, wearing the same Greek verb. Many readers have connected the healed wound to a real first-century rumor Roman writers themselves record — that the tyrant Nero, dead by his own hand in AD 68, had not really died, or would return from the east to reclaim the empire (the so-called Nero redivivus legend). The text itself never names him; it only stages the shape of the belief, the whole earth marveling at a death that did not hold.

‘Who is like the beast?’ The chapter just before this one introduced an angel whose very name asks this question and answers it: MICHAEL, Hebrew mi-kha-El, ‘who is like God’, who led heaven’s side to victory over this same dragon one chapter ago (12:7–9). The crowd’s chant here borrows the oldest incomparability formula in the Bible — mi kamokha, ‘who is like you’, sung of Jehovah alone at the sea in Exodus 15:11 (‘Who is like you, O LORD, among the gods?’) — and points it at the beast instead. One chapter after Michael’s own name settled the question in heaven, the earth asks it again as if it had never been answered.

Verses 5–6 · a mouth speaking great things, and a fourth way to count 1,260 days · στόμα λαλοῦν μεγάλα καὶ βλασφημίας stoma laloun megala kai blasphēmias

⚠ Borrowed straight from the little horn. ‘A mouth speaking great things’ is not this book’s own phrase — Theodotion’s Greek Daniel, the version early Christian writers actually read, closes its own 7:8 with the identical four words, stoma laloun megala, for the little horn that grows out of the fourth beast and boasts against heaven (repeated in sense at 7:20, ‘a mouth that spoke great things’; the horn ‘shall speak words against the Most High’ at 7:25). Chapter 12 lent this beast the dragon’s BODY; this chapter lends it Daniel’s little horn’s MOUTH, word for word.

One clock, a fourth unit. ‘Forty-two months’ is the same span this book has already counted twice — as 1,260 days (11:3; 12:6) and as forty-two months once already (11:2) — and will count a fourth way one chapter ago, ‘a time, and times, and half a time’ (12:14). The same three and a half years, now stated for the SECOND time as forty-two months, ticking through the beast’s own reign the way it ticked through the woman’s flight and the witnesses’ prophesying.

His tent, blasphemed. ‘His tent’ is skēnē, the same word this book will use for God’s own dwelling coming down to earth at the very end (‘the tent of God is with humanity’, 21:3, already on these pages) and that John used of the Word who ‘tented’ among us at the Gospel’s opening (John 1:14). ‘Those who dwell in heaven’ repeats, almost verbatim, the dragon’s own victims one chapter back — the very beings a voice from heaven had just told to REJOICE (‘be glad, you heavens, and you who dwell in them’, 12:12) are now the target of the beast’s blasphemy. The single Greek verb behind ‘dwell’ there and here, skēnoō, occurs in only two books of the whole New Testament — John and Revelation, five times total (John 1:14; Revelation 7:15; 12:12; 13:6; 21:3) — so this is not a coincidence of English wording but the same rare verb, aimed first at a blessing and now at an insult.

⚠ Singular or plural, again. ‘Blasphemies’ forks the identical way ‘names’ did in verse 1, twice over in these two verses: WH, Treg, NA28, and the Byzantine RP text all read the plural at both places; only the Textus Receptus behind KJV and Geneva reads singular each time. The Byzantine tradition also expands v5 with an extra clause the earlier text does not have — authority ‘[to make] war’ added directly here, a power the beast is given again, explicitly, two verses later (v7).

Verses 7–8 · every tribe, tongue, people, and nation — reshuffled again · ἐπὶ πᾶσαν φυλὴν καὶ λαὸν καὶ γλῶσσαν καὶ ἔθνος epi pasan phylēn kai laon kai glōssan kai ethnos

The same four words, never in the same order. This exact fourfold sweep of humanity has already named the Lamb’s own purchase — ‘you were slain, and with your blood you purchased for God persons from every tribe and tongue and people and nation’, 5:9 — and the great uncountable crowd before the throne, ‘from every nation and tribes and peoples and tongues’, 7:9. Every time this phrase has recurred so far, its four terms come in a different order; this is the third arrangement in three appearances. The beast is given authority over exactly the totality the Lamb has already been buying people OUT of — two universal claims on the same four-part humanity, laid one on top of the other.

⚠ A slain Lamb, twice in one verse’s reach. ‘The Lamb who was slain’ uses the same esphagmenou already flagged at verse 3 — the word’s third and final appearance in this chapter, framing the beast’s whole false resurrection between two honest mentions of the real one.

⚠ Slain from the founding of the world — slain, or not written? The Greek word order sets ‘from the founding of the world’ directly after ‘slain’, and the shelf splits on whether to keep it there. The KJV keeps it, ‘the Lamb slain from the foundation of the world’; so does the Douay-Rheims, ‘the Lamb, which was slain from the beginning of the world’; the NIV, ‘the Lamb who was slain from the creation of the world’; and the NWT 1984, ‘the Lamb who was slaughtered, from the founding of the world’. But the ASV already moves it, more than a century earlier than either NWT revision — ‘whose name hath not been written from the foundation of the world in the book of life of the Lamb that hath been slain’ — and the NWT 2013 revision moves it the same way, to the FRONT of the sentence, ‘From the founding of the world, not one of their names has been written’. Both attach the phrase to the WRITING instead of the SLAYING, almost certainly to bring this verse into line with its near-twin at Revelation 17:8 (not yet on these pages), whose own word order genuinely does favor that reading. Both readings have real defenders, and this is not a straightforward old-versus-new split — the ASV shows the reordering is at least as old as 1901. This translation keeps the more natural word order, without deciding the theological question either rendering carries — whether the cross was planned before creation, or the names were.

Verses 9–10 · the last ‘let him hear’, and a fate or a warning · εἴ τις ἔχει οὖς ἀκουσάτω ei tis echei ous akousatō

★ The eighth and final bell. ‘Let him hear’ closed all seven of the letters to the churches (Revelation 2–3, already on these pages), always as ‘the one who has an ear, let him hear WHAT THE SPIRIT SAYS to the congregations’. Here the grammar shifts — a bare conditional, ‘IF anyone has an ear’, and no mention of the Spirit or the congregations — and the refrain rings an eighth and LAST time in the whole book; it never returns in chapters 14–22.

⚠ Fate, or a warning to the sword-wielder? This is the single most consequential fork in this chapter’s apparatus. The reading this translation follows — SBLGNT and NA28 alone among the major editions — makes BOTH halves of the verse passive: whoever is destined for captivity goes into captivity; whoever is destined to be killed by the sword is killed by the sword. Read this way the verse is a fate-oracle, echoing Jeremiah’s own fourfold ‘those for death, to death; those for the sword, to the sword… those for captivity, to captivity’ (Jeremiah 15:2, not yet on these pages) — a call to endure whatever is appointed, not to expect rescue. But WH, Treg, AND the Byzantine RP text — essentially the whole prior manuscript tradition, critical and Byzantine alike — read the second half ACTIVE instead, turning fate into a warning: the KJV, ‘he that killeth with the sword must be killed with the sword’; the Douay-Rheims, ‘he that shall kill by the sword, must be killed by the sword’. Read that way the verse turns from a fate accepted into a warning issued — the beast’s own persecutors will meet the sword they use — echoing Jesus’ words to Peter almost verbatim: ‘all who take up the sword will perish by the sword’ (Matthew 26:52). The ASV prints a hybrid of the two halves, ‘if any man is for captivity, into captivity he goeth: if any man shall kill with the sword, with the sword must he be killed’ — fate for captivity, warning for the sword — and the NWT 1984 keeps the same hybrid, ‘if anyone [is meant] for captivity, he goes away into captivity. If anyone will kill with the sword, he must be killed with the sword’. NA28’s late preference for the fully passive reading against its own predecessors is itself the more surprising choice here, not the safer one.

Verses 11–12 · a second beast, lamb-horned and dragon-voiced · εἶχεν κέρατα δύο ὅμοια ἀρνίῳ, καὶ ἐλάλει ὡς δράκων eichen kerata dyo homoia arniō, kai elalei hōs drakōn

⚠ A third false likeness. The chapter has now staged three counterfeits of the true Lamb and its Master in a row — the first beast’s healed death-wound (v3), the crowd’s parody of ‘who is like’ (v4), and now a second beast built to LOOK like the Lamb (two horns, the same word arniōn this book always uses for Christ) while it SOUNDS like the dragon the moment it opens its mouth. This book never gives this second beast a name in this chapter; later chapters call it ‘the false prophet’ (Revelation 16, 19, and 20 — none yet on these pages), a title this chapter has not yet reached for.

A borrowed miracle. Its whole authority is described as ‘the authority of the first beast’, exercised ‘in its presence’ — a subordinate role, propaganda minister rather than tyrant, matching how the beast’s own authority in verse 2 was never its own either, but the dragon’s, handed down one more link in the chain.

Verses 13–15 · fire from heaven, and a speaking image · πῦρ ποιῇ ἐκ τοῦ οὐρανοῦ καταβαίνειν pyr poiē ek tou ouranou katabainein

⚠ Fire, on the wrong side this time. Fire at a prophet’s command is, everywhere else in Scripture, evidence FOR the true God — Elijah calling it down on Carmel, and the two witnesses of this very book, two chapters ago, pouring it from their own mouths to destroy anyone who tried to harm them (11:5). The second beast now performs fire’s other classic use, calling it down FROM heaven rather than breathing it out, in service of a lie — exactly this chapter’s warning made concrete: a miracle is not, by itself, proof of anything.

A speaking statue. Ancient readers needed no allegory to picture this: the imperial cult of the first centuries required public gestures of loyalty — incense burned, an oath sworn — before an actual image of the emperor, and a ‘speaking’ cult statue, worked by hidden mechanisms or a concealed voice, was a documented trick of the wider Greco-Roman religious world, not this book’s own invention. The horror here is not a fantastic special effect; it is an old confidence trick, raised to cosmic stakes.

Verses 16–18 · the mark, and the number of the beast · χάραγμα · ὁ ἀριθμὸς αὐτοῦ ἑξακόσιοι ἑξήκοντα ἕξ charagma · ho arithmos autou hexakosioi hexēkonta hex

★ Charagma. A new word for these pages: not a private tattoo but the ordinary Greek term for an official STAMP — the impress of a die on a coin bearing the emperor’s portrait, and the seal of name and regnal year attached to a contract or a receipt to make it valid. In the first centuries of the empire, a document without the right charagma was worthless for trade; this chapter’s buying-and-selling clause (v17) is not a metaphor invented for the vision but a literal description of how commerce already worked, turned into a test of loyalty. The same word marked a branded slave or a devotee’s cultic tattoo elsewhere in Greco-Roman life — ownership, in every one of its uses.

⚠ 666 — and a rival reading almost as old as the book itself. Among the four editions this chapter’s own apparatus compares, all four agree on the VALUE, six hundred and sixty-six, differing only in grammar: WH and NA28 spell it out with one set of endings, the Byzantine RP with another, and Tregelles prints it as the Greek numeral-letters themselves, chi-xi-stigma (χξϛʹ) — 600 + 60 + 6. But a rival number, 616, is older than any of these printed editions: the church father Irenaeus, writing around AD 180, already knew of ‘some copies’ that read 616 and argued against them, insisting 666 stood ‘in all the most approved and ancient copies’ and had the personal testimony of ‘those who saw John face to face’ behind it. A third-century papyrus discovered at Oxyrhynchus in Egypt (P115, only published in 2005) and the fifth-century Codex Ephraemi both independently confirm that 616 genuinely circulated in antiquity, whatever its merits against 666’s much wider attestation.

Whose number? Irenaeus himself, closest in time to the book’s own composition of anyone who discussed this, offered three candidate names whose Greek letters add to 666 — Euanthas, Lateinos, and Teitan — and declined to choose between them, warning that guessing the identity was less important, and more dangerous, than recognizing the pattern when it arrived. He never proposed Nero. That identification is a modern one, resting on a different alphabet entirely: transliterate ‘Nero Caesar’ into HEBREW letters (used as numbers, the same technique Irenaeus used in Greek) and nrwn qsr totals exactly 666 — a spelling confirmed in a real, contemporary Aramaic document from the Judean desert (Wadi Murabba’at) that transliterates Nero’s name and title the same way. Drop the final letter of ‘Neron’ — the shorter Latin form of the same name — and the same Hebrew count comes to exactly 616, which would make the two numbers not rival guesses but the SAME guess, spelled two defensible ways.

The pebble comes back. ‘Calculate’, psēphisatō, shares its root with the WHITE STONE promised to the overcomer at Pergamum (psēphos, 2:17) — a pebble used, among its other ancient uses, for counting and casting votes. The same root that named an unearned reward eleven chapters ago now names the grim arithmetic this chapter asks of anyone with the wit to attempt it.

Patterns worth carrying forward

What this chapter answers. Chapter 12 showed WHY the earth suffers what it does — a defeated dragon, furious and short on time. This chapter shows HOW: not by the dragon’s own hand, but through two beasts built entirely from parts already on loan — Daniel’s zoo, the dragon’s own body, and, again and again, a counterfeit of the Lamb that only works because the real thing came first. Nothing here is original; that is the chapter’s whole argument.

Where this translation sides with the literal shelf: the wider plural ‘names of blasphemy’ and ‘blasphemies’ against the Textus Receptus’ singular (vv1, 5, 6); the fully passive fate-reading of v10, SBLGNT and NA28 against nearly the whole prior tradition; and ‘from the founding of the world’ kept attached to the Lamb’s slaying (v8), against the NWT 2013’s revised word order.

Where it goes its own way: the chapter opens with no residue of the ‘stood on the sand’ clause at all, agreeing with Geneva, the Douay-Rheims, and the Living Bible against KJV/ASV/NWT/NIV, all four of which open verse 1 with some form of it; and it declines to resolve either the ‘from the founding of the world’ crux or the identity behind 666, reporting Irenaeus’ own three candidates and the modern Nero proposal side by side rather than picking a winner.

Next in this book: Revelation 14, not yet on these pages, where the beast’s mark on the right hand and the forehead (v16) is answered, verse for verse, by the Lamb standing on Mount Zion with 144,000 who carry his name and his Father’s name written on their foreheads instead.
