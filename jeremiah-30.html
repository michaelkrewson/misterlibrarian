Jeremiah 30 — The Mister Translation

1
הַדָּבָר אֲשֶׁר הָיָה אֶל-יִרְמְיָהוּ, מֵאֵת יְהוָה לֵאמֹר.

La palabra que vino a Jeremías de parte de Jehová, diciendo:

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · כְּתָב-לְךָ אֵת כָּל-הַדְּבָרִים … אֶל-סֵפֶר ketav-lekha et kol ha-devarim … el-sefer — write it in a book, spoken to a prophet who almost never gets this instruction

⚠ The book opens by naming itself. Jeremiah has spoken hundreds of oracles by now, almost always aloud, to a crowd or a king. Here, for one of the only times in the book, he is told to write — not just remember, not just deliver, but preserve in a fixed, physical form. The fuller version of this same instruction, dictating an entire scroll to the scribe Baruch, becomes its own chapter later (Jeremiah 36, not yet on these pages) — and there a king burns the result and it has to be written twice. This verse is the first time the book flags its own textuality: what you are reading exists because Jeremiah was told, once, to make sure it would.

These two chapters (30–31) are traditionally called the “Book of Consolation” — the one sustained block of pure hope in a book otherwise built from warnings. Jeremiah 31 is already on these pages; this chapter is its opening half, and Hebrew tradition keeps them as one continuous unit even though this translation, like the chapter divisions themselves, treats them as two.

“Turn the captivity” (shavti et-shevut, v3) is a set legal-sounding idiom for reversing a nation's fortunes, not necessarily limited to exile specifically — it recurs seven more times across these two chapters (vv18; 31:23; and elsewhere), the drumbeat phrase of the whole consolation section.

Verses 4–7 · עֵת-צָרָה הִיא לְיַעֲקֹב et-tsarah hi le-Ya'akov — a time of trouble for Jacob, the same phrase this book will use once more, of everyone

⚠ A grown man doubled over like a woman in labor (v6) is deliberately grotesque. The image is not comparing the coming crisis to childbirth's pain in general; it is asking why MEN are showing a woman's specific posture, hands pressed to the loins — the natural order itself photographed as broken, before a single disaster is even named.

“The time of Jacob's trouble” (tsarah) is a phrase this project will meet again in a much later book: Daniel 12:1's “a time of distress (et tsarah) such as has never been since there was a nation” — already on these pages — uses the identical two-word Hebrew construction, widened from one family to every nation on earth. Whether Daniel is deliberately echoing this verse or the two writers independently reached for the same phrase to describe history's worst point before rescue, this translation reports rather than decides — but the promise attached to both is the same shape: trouble unlike any other, survived.

Verses 8–9 · אֶשְׁבֹּר עֻלּוֹ מֵעַל צַוָּארֶךָ eshbor ullo me'al tsavarekha — I will break his yoke, the same word fought over by two prophets three chapters ago

⚠ This is the yoke of Jeremiah 27–28, and the timing is now Jehovah's own to set. Hananiah claimed the yoke of Babylon was already broken and died within the year for saying so before its time (28:2); Jeremiah wore an iron one and insisted submission was the only path to survival (27:8). Neither man was arguing that the yoke would never break — only about when. This chapter finally supplies the answer the whole dispute was missing: yes, eventually, for real, “in that day” — a day this book does not name, and Hananiah's mistake was naming one.

⚠ “David their king” is one of the most argued single phrases in the book, and the shelf splits on how literally to take it. KJV ASV NIV NWT all print the bare name, “David,” with no gloss. Some modern critical renderings (NET among them) instead read a “Davidic ruler” — a descendant, not the historical king himself, dead some four centuries by this point. Both readings agree on the shape of the hope (a restored throne, of David's own line); they differ on whether the text means that literally or dynastically. This translation keeps the bare name, as the Hebrew has it, and leaves the reading open.

Verses 10–11 · כָלָה … לֹא-אֶעֱשֶׂה כָלָה khalah … lo e'eseh khalah — a full end for everyone else, a real but limited discipline for this people alone

The same word, granted to the nations and withheld from Israel in one sentence. Khalah, “a full end,” annihilation without remainder, is exactly what happens to “all the nations where I have scattered you” — and exactly what does NOT happen to the scattered people themselves. The distinction is not that Israel's punishment is smaller; verse 12's own language calls the wound severe and incurable by any human remedy. The distinction is that one punishment has a floor under it and the other does not.

Verses 12–17 · אָנוּשׁ לְשִׁבְרֵךְ anush le-shivrekh — your injury is incurable, six verses of medical language with no doctor in the room

The metaphor runs a full diagnosis before it runs a cure. No one to plead the case (v13), the lovers — the very allies this nation courted, Egypt among them — who have simply forgotten her (v14), and a direct refusal to pretend the wound is anyone's fault but her own (vv14–15, “I have done these things to you… because your iniquity is great”). Only after six unbroken verses of a wound the text itself calls incurable does v17 supply the impossible cure anyway — not because the diagnosis was wrong, but because the healer is not limited by what medicine can normally do.

“An outcast… Zion, no one seeks her” (v17) is quoted as what OTHERS call her, not what she is; the verse answers a rumor rather than confirming it. The city with no one asking after it is about to be rebuilt on its own ruin-mound (v18) — the same site, not a replacement.

Verses 17–22 · וִהְיִיתֶם לִי לְעָם vi-hyitem li le-am — you will be my people, the covenant formula this book keeps returning to

“My people… my God” (v22) is the identical two-clause covenant formula already sworn to the good figs at 24:7 and to all Israel at 31:32 (Hebrew numbering) — the same promise, now stated a third time across three different chapters of consolation material, each time to a different scope of people.

“Their ruler will come out from their midst” (v21) is deliberately unspecific about a mechanism — a local leader rather than an imposed foreign governor, matched to a king who can “approach” Jehovah directly, which the text itself flags as remarkable: “who is this who would pledge his own heart to approach me?” NWT renders the leader's title “majestic one,” the same word this translation keeps; other versions soften it to “noble” or “prince.”

Verses 23–25 · סַעֲרַת יְהוָה sa'arat YHVH — the tempest of Jehovah, copied word for word from seven chapters earlier

⚠ These two verses are not an echo of Jeremiah 23:19–20 — they are, almost letter for letter, the SAME two verses, planted a second time. “The tempest of Jehovah, wrath, has gone forth… on the head of the wicked it will whirl down… the anger of Jehovah will not turn back until he has done and until he has carried out the purposes of his heart” already stands at 23:19-20, closing the denunciation of the false prophets, with only trivial spelling variation between the two copies. This book does not usually repeat itself whole; when it does, twice inside thirty chapters, the repetition is doing something — the same storm that ended a chapter about lying prophets now ends a chapter about a promised homecoming, the identical weather serving two different chapters as two different kinds of last word.

⚠ Verse 25 carries this translation's Hebrew/English numbering note for the first time in this chapter: in the Hebrew text this is Jeremiah 30's own final verse, but English Bibles count it as the opening verse of chapter 31 — “at that time… I will be the God of all the families of Israel.” Jeremiah 31 on these pages, already shipped, begins at its own verse 1, which is one verse further along than this — so this verse is genuinely new material, not a repeat of anything already on the site, despite carrying a familiar-sounding English chapter number.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “David their king” (v9) kept as the bare name, as the Hebrew has it, with the dynastic reading reported rather than substituted; “a full end” (khalah, v11) kept identical in both halves of the same sentence, granted to the nations and withheld from Israel; and the “tempest of Jehovah” (vv23-24) translated to match 23:19-20's own wording as closely as the two Hebrew copies allow, since the repetition itself is the point.

Echoes paid. The yoke broken here (v8) at last resolves the dispute between 27 and 28 over when, not whether, Babylon's power ends; “my people… my God” (v22) restates the covenant formula already sworn at 24:7 and 31:32; and “I will attend to” (paqad, v20) lands on its favorable sense again, as it did at 27:22 and 29:10.

Echoes planted. The instruction to write it in a book (v2), waiting for its full scene at the dictation to Baruch (Jeremiah 36, not yet on these pages) — and for the burning of that very scroll. And “time of Jacob's trouble” (v7), the same two-word Hebrew phrase Daniel 12:1 will widen from one family to every nation on earth, already on these pages.

This library already carries Jeremiah 29 and 31, both translated out of order; with this chapter the whole run from 18 through 31 is now complete except for the earlier gaps at 2–17.
