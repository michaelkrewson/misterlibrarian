An acted prophecy: Jerusalem engraved on a brick and put under siege, an iron griddle for a wall, the prophet lying on his left side three hundred and ninety days for Israel and forty on his right for Judah, bread of mixed grains by weight and water by measure, baked over cattle dung, and the staff of bread to be broken.
Follows chapter 3 in the early years of the exile of King Jehoiachin. The chapter gives day counts but no date.

Y cuando hayas cumplido estos, te acostarás una segunda vez, sobre tu lado derecho, y llevarás la iniquidad de la casa de Judá cuarenta días; un día por año, un día por año, te lo he dado.

Y mira, he puesto sobre ti cuerdas, para que no puedas volverte de un lado al otro hasta que hayas cumplido los días de tu sitio.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–3 · Ezekiel 4:1–3 a city engraved on a brick — siege works — an iron griddle for a wall

⭐ A brick with a city on it. The mute prophet (3:26, now on these pages) is given something to do instead. The verb for drawing the city is chaqah, ‘to engrave, to cut into’ (chaqah), the verb of Isaiah 49:16 (now on these pages), where a city’s walls are engraved on Jehovah’s palms. Here the city is Jerusalem and the surface is a levenah, a brick. The KJV has “take thee a tile”, the NIV “take a block of clay”, the NWT 2013 “take a brick and put it in front of you. Engrave on it a city—Jerusalem” and the TLB “draw a map of the city of Jerusalem on it”.

⭐ The siege. A siege wall, a ramp, camps and battering rams, all around a brick: the whole apparatus in miniature. The NWT 2013 has “raise up a siege rampart against it” and the KJV “set battering rams against it round about”. The same siege is the one the book later reports from the outside (24:2).

⭐ The griddle. The wall between prophet and city is a machavat, a griddle, the flat pan of the grain offering (Leviticus 2:5, now on these pages) (machavat). The word stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, and the other four are about offerings. The KJV has “take thou unto thee an iron pan”, the NIV “take an iron pan” and the NWT 1984 “an iron griddle”. “It is a sign to the house of Israel” stands in this one verse (ot).

Verses 4–8 · Ezekiel 4:4–8 lying on the left side and the right — a day for a year — cords

⭐ Bearing the iniquity. The prophet lies on his side and bears the avon (avon), the guilt and what it costs, of a house that is not his own. The shelf splits on where the iniquity is put: the KJV has “lay the iniquity of the house of Israel upon it”, the side, while the NIV has “put the sin of the people of Israel upon yourself” and the NWT 2013 “lay the guilt of the house of Israel on yourself”. The Hebrew says alav, ‘on it’.

⭐ A day for a year. Three hundred and ninety days on the left for Israel, forty on the right for Judah. The text gives the numbers as the years of their iniquity and does not say which years. They add up to four hundred and thirty, the figure Exodus 12:40 (now on these pages) gives for the time in Egypt. “A day for a year” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here and Numbers 14:34 (now on these pages), where forty days of spying become forty years in the wilderness, with the same doubled phrase. The KJV has “I have appointed thee each day for a year”, the NIV “a day for each year” and the NWT 1984 “A day for a year, a day for a year”. The scroll writes ‘right’ in verse 6 in a form that the reading tradition changes.

⚠ Cords, and whose hands. In 3:25 (now on these pages) the cords were to be put on him by people the verse does not name; here Jehovah says I have put cords on you. The word “cords” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible; the third (31:10) means thick boughs. The KJV has “I will lay bands upon thee”, the NIV “I will tie you up with ropes” and the TLB “I will paralyze you”. Verse 7 has him set his face against the siege with his arm bared.

Verses 9–11 · Ezekiel 4:9–11 wheat, barley, beans, lentils, millet and spelt — twenty shekels a day — a sixth of a hin

⭐ Siege bread. Six kinds of grain and pulse go into one vessel. The KJV has “wheat, and barley, and beans, and lentiles, and millet, and fitches”, the NIV “millet and spelt” and the NWT 2013 “broad beans”. The last, “spelt”, in this form stands in this one verse.

⭐ By weight and by measure. Twenty shekels of food and a sixth of a hin of water, “from time to time” as the verse says: a ration, not a meal. A shekel is a weight (sheqel) and a hin a liquid measure (hin); the verse does not convert them. The KJV has “twenty shekels a day” and “the sixth part of an hin”, the Douay “twenty staters a day” and the TLB, converting, “eight ounces at a time” and “one quart of water a day”. “By measure” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, verse 16, and Leviticus 19:35 (now on these pages), where it is the honest measure.

Verses 12–15 · Ezekiel 4:12–15 baked over dung — “Ah, Lord Jehovah” — cattle dung instead

⭐ The dung. The bread is to be baked with human excrement, in front of them. The reason is given in verse 13: so shall Israel eat bread unclean among the nations. The KJV has “thou shalt bake it with dung that cometh out of man”, the NIV “using human excrement for fuel” and the Douay “baked under the ashes”.

⭐ The priest objects. Ezekiel is a priest (1:3, now on these pages), and he answers as one: from his youth he has not eaten what died of itself or was torn. The pair “what died of itself or was torn” stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible, and one of them is the priest’s rule at Leviticus 22:8 (now on these pages) (nevelah). The ‘foul meat’ is piggul (piggul), the spoiled offering of Leviticus 7:18 (now on these pages). “Ah, Lord Jehovah!” stands in ten verses of the Hebrew Bible, among them Jeremiah’s answer to his call (Jeremiah 1:6, now on these pages). Peter answers a voice in the same way, that he has never eaten anything common or unclean (Acts 10:14, now on these pages), and Daniel resolves not to defile himself with the king’s food (Daniel 1:8, now on these pages). The KJV has “that which dieth of itself, or is torn in pieces”, the NIV “No impure meat has ever entered my mouth” and the NWT 2013 “Not that, Sovereign Lord Jehovah!”.

⭐ The concession. Cattle dung is allowed in place of human dung. The NIV has “cow dung instead of human excrement”. The scroll writes the word for ‘dung’ of verse 15 in a form that the reading tradition changes. The objection is granted; the rationing of verses 10 and 11 stands.

Verses 16–17 · Ezekiel 4:16–17 the staff of bread broken — bread by weight — wasting away in iniquity

⭐ The staff of bread. What was acted is now said. “The staff of bread” stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: Leviticus 26:26 (now on these pages), where ten women bake in one oven and deliver the bread again by weight; Psalm 105:16; here; Ezekiel 5:16 (now on these pages); and 14:13 (now on these pages) (matteh). The KJV has “I will break the staff of bread in Jerusalem”, the NIV “cut off the food supply in Jerusalem” and the NWT 1984 “breaking the rods around which ring-shaped loaves are suspended”. “With anxiety” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, the other two Ezekiel 12:18 and 12:19 (both now on these pages).

⚠ Wasting away. “Waste away in their iniquity” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here and Leviticus 26:39 (now on these pages), in the same chapter of covenant warnings as the staff of bread. The KJV has “consume away for their iniquity” and the NWT 2013 “waste away because of their error”. The iniquity the prophet bore in verses 4–6 is what the people waste away in.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “iniquity” (avon) kept at verses 4, 5, 6 and 17, so that what the prophet bears is what the people waste away in; “by weight” and “by measure” kept across verses 10, 11 and 16; “siege” kept for the one noun through verses 2, 3, 7 and 8; and “unclean” for the one root at verses 13 and 14.

Echoes paid. The muteness of 3:26 turned into action; the cords of 3:25; the “day for a year” of Numbers 14:34; and the staff of bread and the wasting away of Leviticus 26:26 and 26:39.

Echoes planted. Ezekiel 5 (now on these pages) goes on with the sword that shaves the hair and the same staff of bread (5:16); the siege is reported at 24:1–2; and the priest’s scruple returns in Daniel 1 and Acts 10, both on these pages.

Ezekiel 5, now on these pages: a sharp sword used as a barber’s razor, the hair weighed into thirds, and the judgment on Jerusalem spelled out.
