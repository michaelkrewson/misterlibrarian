The same letter, still from Ephesus. Paul turns from the slogans to the work of the teachers: Apollos has come to Corinth after Paul, and the letter treats the two of them as servants with separate tasks. Acts 18:24–28 tells how Apollos, a learned man from Alexandria, was taught the way of God more accurately and then went to Achaia and helped the believers.
c. AD 54–55, the same letter and date as chapters 1 and 2, written from Ephesus. Acts 18 has Apollos at Corinth after Paul’s eighteen months, and the letter’s own sentence (“I planted, Apollos watered”) places the two of them in the same city within a few years; the fire of the last judgment is not dated and the chapter does not date it.

Verses 1–4 · milk, not solid food, and the party slogans

⚠ “Fleshly people.” The printed Greek has sarkinois, WH, Treg and NA28 agree, which is “made of flesh” in the material sense, the word for a body of flesh. The Received Text reads sarkikois, “fleshly” in the sense of governed by the flesh, which is the word Paul uses in verse 3. The two adjectives are close in sense and the apparatus carries the same pair at Romans 7:14. This translation prints “fleshly” for both, the reading that keeps the sense of verse 3, and the note keeps the distinction in view.

“Infants in Christ” and “milk, not solid food.” Nēpioi, babies, is a word of age, and the picture is a household: babies drink milk and adults eat solid food. The image is a teacher’s complaint, and Hebrews 5:12–14 makes the same complaint about the same diet (Hebrews 5:12–14, not yet on these pages). Peter uses the milk image as a remedy, not a rebuke (1 Peter 2:2, not yet on these pages). Paul is not saying the Corinthians have no food; he is saying they could not take it, and cannot yet.

“Jealousy and strife.” Zēlos and eris are the two sins of a community that is losing its way, and Romans 13:13 lists the same pair among the works of the night. The sentence is built as a question with no answer offered: the Corinthians know what the walk of a human pattern looks like, and Paul asks whether they are walking it.

“I am of Paul”; “I am of Apollos.” The slogans are the ones from 1:12, now answered in the form of a question about humanity. Verse 4 does not name the other two slogans, Cephas and Christ, and the omission is itself a point: the argument is about the pattern of the sayings, not about whose name is the right one.

Verses 5–9 · servants, a planting, and God’s field

“Servants through whom you believed.” Diākonoi, servants, the word that will become the church’s word for a deacon. Apollos is the Alexandrian who “helped them much which had believed through grace” in Corinth (Acts 18:27, KJV), and the chapter’s opening pair has the same history: the one planted, the other watered. The KJV has “ministers,” the NWT “ministers,” the NIV “servants”; the word is the one for table service.

“I planted, Apollos watered, but God kept making it grow.” The imperfect ēuxanen, “kept making grow,” is a continuing action with no end; the two human tasks are finished and the growth is still going on. The sentence gives the human verbs the aorist and the divine one the imperfect, and the grammar says what the sentence means.

⚠ “One, and each will receive his own wage.” The planter and the waterer are one in purpose, and each receives according to his own labor. Reward by work is named twice in this chapter, here and at verse 14, and the same principle is in Romans 2:6. Synergoi, co-workers, is a word Paul has used before for Prisca and Aquila (synergos).

“God’s farm, God’s building.” Georgion, field under cultivation, and oikodomē, building, are the two pictures the chapter will use for the congregation: a crop and a house, one growing and one built. The same house appears in Ephesians 2:20–22, where the building is founded on the apostles and prophets and grows into a temple.

Verses 10–15 · a foundation, the materials, and the fire

“As a wise master builder I laid a foundation.” Architektōn, master builder, is a word for the man who designs and oversees a building, not the one who lays the bricks. Paul claims the role and gives it to grace: “the grace of God given to me.” He also says, in Romans 15:20, that he would not build on another’s foundation, and the claim here is the same claim put in the language of construction.

⚠ “Gold, silver, costly stones, wood, hay, straw.” The apparatus gives the list two ways. The printed text has chrysón and argóron where Treg has chrysion and argyrion, the diminutive forms of coins and metal goods. The list runs from the most valuable to the least, and the materials are the building trade of the first century. Costly stones are the materials of the heavenly city in Revelation 21:19, which is why the list is not simply a scale of worth.

⚠ “The fire itself will test what sort of work each has done.” The printed text has auto, “itself,” before dokimasei, “will test.” The NIV and the Received Text leave it out, and the English reading “the fire will test” shows the same sense without the emphatic pronoun. This translation keeps itself, because the emphasis on the fire is the point.

“He himself will be saved, yet so as through fire.” A burning house, and a builder who walks out of it. The image is Zechariah’s brand snatched from the fire (Zechariah 3:2) and Jude’s rescue of people snatched out of the fire (Jude 23). The Day, hē hēmera, is the day of judgment Paul names in Romans 2:16, and the verb apokalyptetai, “is revealed,” carries the same unveiling as the revelation of the mystery in chapter 2.

Verses 16–17 · you are the temple

“You are God’s temple.” The verb is in the plural, “you (all) are,” and the word is naos, the inner sanctuary, not the outer court. A congregation is the sanctuary where the Spirit lives. Ephesians 2:21 has the same picture of a building growing into a holy temple in the Lord. The apparatus has the verb order differing at this point: Treg, NA28 and the Received Text read oikei en hymin, “dwells in you,” and WH reverses it to en hymin oikei. The sense is unchanged.

“If anyone destroys God’s temple.” Phtheirō means to ruin, to corrupt, to waste away, and the verb is used twice in one sentence: whoever ruins the temple, God will ruin. The punishment matches the offense, and the sanctity of the building explains why. The NWT reads “destroys” and the KJV “defile”; the Greek allows both, and the translation keeps “destroys” so the verb stays the same both times.

Verses 18–23 · become a fool, and boast in no one

“Let him become foolish, so that he may become wise.” The chapter returns to the foolishness of 1:18, where the message of the cross looked like idiocy to the wise. Now the paradox is turned on the Corinthians: the one who thinks himself wise must become a fool to learn the wisdom that is actually wise. The Greek is mōros, and the English word for the same thing is “fool” in the sense of a person who cannot see what he is missing.

⚠ “He catches the wise in their own craftiness.” The sentence is close to Job 5:13 in the Greek Old Testament (not yet on these pages). The word list this library searches did not confirm that verse’s Greek, so the source is stated as close rather than exact. The second quotation, “The Lord knows the reasonings of the wise, that they are futile,” is Psalm 94:11 in the Hebrew numbering, 93:11 in the Greek (not yet on these pages); Paul changes the psalm’s “people” to “wise,” and the change is the whole argument.

“So let no one boast in people.” Kauchasthō, boast, is the verb of the chapter’s first problem and of the dictionary entry. Jeremiah 9:23–24 (not yet on these pages) says the same thing, and the closing quotation in chapter 1 is that passage (1 Corinthians 1:31, already on these pages). The people in the sentence are the teachers whose names have become slogans.

⚠ “Things present or things to come.” The same pair closes Romans 8:38, where nothing can separate us from the love of God. Here the list is a catalogue of what belongs to the Corinthians, and it runs from the people who taught them to the life and death that cannot be taken from them. The last clause, “you are Christ’s, and Christ is God’s,” closes the chapter with an order: belonging runs up, from the community to Christ and from Christ to God, and the next chapter will ask what that order means for judgment (4:3–5).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “fleshly” for both sarkinos and sarkikos, with the distinction in the note; “servants” for diākonoi; “wise master builder” for architektōn; “becomes foolish” for mōros and “boast” for kauchaomai, kept at the same verb in both places; “destroys” for phtheirō both times.

Echoes paid. The party slogans of 1:12; “not in wisdom of speech” (1:17) now answered by the wisdom that the mature hear in 2:6; the boasting of 1:31, closed by the same Jeremiah passage; the foolishness of 1:18 turned on the Corinthians; Romans 8:38’s “present or to come”; and Romans 15:20’s refusal to build on another’s foundation.

Echoes planted. The judgment by fire returns in chapter 4 (4:5) and at the end of the letter; the temple returns when the body is called a temple in chapter 6 (6:19, not yet on these pages); the judge of the servants comes back in 4:3–4. Apollos is named again in 16:12 (not yet on these pages), and the letter ends with the same three names it opened with.
