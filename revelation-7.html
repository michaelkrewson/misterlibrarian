Before the seventh seal opens, four angels hold back the winds of the earth while a fifth seals 144,000 servants of God, twelve thousand from each of the twelve tribes of Israel. John then sees a second group entirely — a multitude no one could count, from every nation, tribe, people, and language, standing before the throne in white robes, identified as those who have 'come out of the great tribulation.'
c. AD 95, an interlude between the sixth seal (chapter 6) and the seventh (chapter 8) — the two groups described, the numbered 144,000 and the uncountable multitude, are read by different interpreters either as the same group described two ways or as two genuinely distinct groups.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–3 · a pause, and a seal, before the seventh seal breaks · σφραγῖδα θεοῦ ζῶντος sphragida theou zōntos

Chapter 6 ended on a question — who is able to stand? — and this chapter opens by delaying the answer further still. The four winds, poised to be released, are held back; the earth, sea, and every tree wait, untouched, while a fifth angel marks a specific group first. Whatever the sixth seal's terror was building toward, this chapter interrupts it on purpose.

The seal itself is never described — only its location, the forehead, and its purpose, protection from what is coming. The image draws on a much older one: Ezekiel's man in linen marking the foreheads of those who grieve over Jerusalem's sin, sparing them from the judgment that follows (Ezekiel 9:4-6). A mark that protects by identifying, not by changing what happens to everyone else.

Verses 4–8 · twelve tribes, and one name missing · ἐκ πάσης φυλῆς υἱῶν Ἰσραήλ ek pasēs phylēs huiōn Israēl

⚠ Dan is not on this list. Every other list of Israel's twelve tribes in the Bible finds some way to reach twelve — usually by omitting Levi (set apart for priestly service, without a land allotment) and splitting Joseph into Ephraim and Manasseh. This list does the opposite: it keeps Levi, and represents Joseph's line with Manasseh named separately and Joseph himself standing in for Ephraim — which reaches twelve only by leaving out Dan entirely, the sole tribe of Jacob's twelve sons never named here. The library reports two old explanations rather than choosing one: a later Jewish and patristic tradition (Irenaeus among the earliest) held that the Antichrist would come from Dan's line, reading Jacob's own blessing over Dan as a serpent ‘by the way’ (Genesis 49:17) as an omen; a separate, older reading points to Dan's own history of idolatry, the golden calf shrine Jeroboam built at Dan itself (1 Kings 12:29). Neither explanation is stated in this text. Only the absence is.

Verses 9–12 · a number no one could count, after a number given exactly · ὄχλος πολύς, ὃν ἀριθμῆσαι αὐτὸν οὐδεὶς ἐδύνατο ochlos polys, hon arithmēsai auton oudeis edynato

The chapter deliberately sets two crowds side by side: a hundred and forty-four thousand, counted exactly, tribe by tribe (vv4-8) — and then a multitude no one could count, ‘from every nation and tribes and peoples and tongues’ (v9). The four-part formula is the same one sung of the redeemed at 5:9, in yet another order — the fourth of seven appearances, none repeating the last one's arrangement.

The palm branches (v9) carried by a crowd standing before God recall the branches of Sukkot, the Feast of Tabernacles (Leviticus 23:40), and the same branches a crowd waved before a different entrance, into Jerusalem, for a different king (John 12:13). Here the procession has already arrived at the throne it was walking toward.

Verses 13–17 · a question John cannot answer, and an old promise, quoted almost word for word · οὗτοί εἰσιν οἱ ἐρχόμενοι ἐκ τῆς θλίψεως τῆς μεγάλης houtoi eisin hoi erchomenoi ek tēs thlipseōs tēs megalēs

⚠ ‘The’ great tribulation, not just ‘a’ great tribulation. KJV alone on this shelf drops the definite article the Greek supplies; ASV, NIV and ESV all keep it, ‘the great tribulation’ — a specific, singular ordeal rather than tribulation as a general category. RV60 and NVI differ from each other on a separate point: RV60 reads han salido, ‘have come out’ — already finished; NVI reads están saliendo, ‘are coming out’ — still under way.

The paradox of v14 is deliberate, not an accident of translation: robes are washed white by being washed in blood. Nothing softens the image in the Greek.

⚠ Verses 16–17 are close to a direct quotation of Isaiah's own promise: ‘they shall not hunger or thirst, neither scorching wind nor sun shall strike them, for he who has pity on them will lead them, and by springs of water will guide them’ (Isaiah 49:10). John changes the shepherd from Jehovah to the Lamb and otherwise keeps the promise nearly intact — the same comfort, given to the same kind of crowd, centuries apart.

⚠ ‘Spread his tabernacle’ or ‘dwell’ or ‘shelter’? Three different choices for one Greek verb, skēnōsei, the same tent-pitching word behind John 1:14's ‘the Word became flesh and tabernacled among us.’ ASV alone keeps the literal image, ‘spread his tabernacle’; KJV reads the plainer ‘dwell among them’; NIV and ESV both abandon the tent image entirely for ‘shelter them with his presence.’ RV60 keeps the literal tent, matching ASV; NVI matches NIV/ESV's shelter-and-presence reading.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation flags rather than smooths: Dan's absence from the tribal list (vv4-8), reported with the library's two leading explanations rather than one chosen answer; and v14's washed-white-in-blood image, kept as the paradox it is in Greek.

Echoes paid. This chapter's pause — four winds held, one question from chapter 6 still unanswered — interrupts the sixth seal's terror on purpose, before the seventh seal chapter 8 will finally open. The ‘nation, tribes, peoples, tongues’ formula (v9), now in a fourth arrangement after 5:9.

Echoes planted. God tabernacling over his people (v15) anticipates the same promise, the same verb, at the New Jerusalem itself — ‘the tabernacle of God is with man’ (21:3). And v17's closing line, God wiping away every tear, is not this book's only time: the identical promise closes the whole vision at 21:4 — the same comfort, offered twice, chapters apart, to different crowds.

⚠ Left open on purpose. Why Dan is missing, and whether the hundred and forty-four thousand and the uncountable multitude are the same group seen two ways or two distinct companies — both old questions, and the library reports the live readings rather than settling either.
