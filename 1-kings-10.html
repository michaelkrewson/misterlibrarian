The queen of Sheba hears the report of Solomon and comes to Jerusalem to test him with riddles; he tells her all her words, she sees his house and his table, and there is no more spirit in her; she blesses Jehovah, gives a hundred and twenty talents of gold, and goes home. The fleet of Hiram brings almug trees from Ophir. Six hundred and sixty-six talents of gold come to Solomon in one year; he makes gold shields and an ivory throne with six steps and twelve lions; the fleet of Tarshish brings gold, silver, ivory, apes and peacocks; all the earth seeks his face; and he buys horses and chariots from Egypt and Kue and sells them on to the kings of the Hittites and of Aram.
The chapter gives no date. It follows the twenty years of building (1 Kings 9:10) and comes before the end of Solomon’s forty years, and the fleet of Tarshish returns ‘once every three years’ (10:22).

16

17

Y el rey hizo un gran trono de marfil, y lo revistió de oro refinado.
19

El trono tenía seis gradas, y la parte alta del trono era redonda por detrás; y había brazos a un lado y al otro junto al lugar del asiento, y dos leones de pie junto a los brazos.
20

Y doce leones estaban de pie allí sobre las seis gradas, a un lado y al otro; no se había hecho nada igual para ningún reino.
21
וְכֹל כְּלֵי מַשְׁקֵה הַמֶּלֶךְ שְׁלֹמֹה, זָהָב, וְכֹל כְּלֵי בֵּית-יַעַר הַלְּבָנוֹן, זָהָב סָגוּר:  אֵין כֶּסֶף, לֹא נֶחְשָׁב בִּימֵי שְׁלֹמֹה--לִמְאוּמָה.
And all the drinking vessels of King Solomon were gold, and all the vessels of the House of the Forest of Lebanon were pure gold; there was no silver, it was not reckoned as anything in the days of Solomon.note
Y todos los vasos de beber del rey Salomón eran de oro, y todos los vasos de la Casa del Bosque del Líbano eran de oro puro; no había plata, no se tenía por nada en los días de Salomón.
22
כִּי אֳנִי תַרְשִׁישׁ לַמֶּלֶךְ בַּיָּם, עִם אֳנִי חִירָם; אַחַת לְשָׁלֹשׁ שָׁנִים תָּבוֹא אֳנִי תַרְשִׁישׁ, נֹשְׂאֵת זָהָב וָכֶסֶף, שֶׁנְהַבִּים וְקֹפִים, וְתֻכִּיִּים.
For the king had a fleet of Tarshish at sea with the fleet of Hiram; once every three years the fleet of Tarshish would come, carrying gold and silver, ivory, and apes and peacocks.note
Porque el rey tenía en el mar una flota de Tarsis con la flota de Hiram; una vez cada tres años venía la flota de Tarsis, trayendo oro y plata, marfil, y monos y pavos reales.
23
וַיִּגְדַּל הַמֶּלֶךְ שְׁלֹמֹה, מִכֹּל מַלְכֵי הָאָרֶץ--לְעֹשֶׁר, וּלְחָכְמָה.
And King Solomon was greater than all the kings of the earth in riches and in wisdom.note
Y el rey Salomón fue mayor que todos los reyes de la tierra en riquezas y en sabiduría.
24
וְכָל-הָאָרֶץ--מְבַקְשִׁים, אֶת-פְּנֵי שְׁלֹמֹה:  לִשְׁמֹעַ, אֶת-חָכְמָתוֹ, אֲשֶׁר-נָתַן אֱלֹהִים, בְּלִבּוֹ.
And all the earth was seeking the face of Solomon, to hear his wisdom that God had put in his heart.note
Y toda la tierra buscaba el rostro de Salomón, para oír su sabiduría que Dios había puesto en su corazón.
25
וְהֵמָּה מְבִאִים אִישׁ מִנְחָתוֹ כְּלֵי כֶסֶף וּכְלֵי זָהָב וּשְׂלָמוֹת, וְנֵשֶׁק וּבְשָׂמִים, סוּסִים, וּפְרָדִים--דְּבַר-שָׁנָה, בְּשָׁנָה.
And they were bringing each man his tribute, articles of silver and articles of gold, and garments and weapons and spices, horses and mules, the due of a year, year by year.note
Y traían cada uno su tributo, objetos de plata y objetos de oro, y vestidos y armas y especias, caballos y mulos, lo de cada año, año tras año.
26
וַיֶּאֱסֹף שְׁלֹמֹה, רֶכֶב וּפָרָשִׁים, וַיְהִי-לוֹ אֶלֶף וְאַרְבַּע-מֵאוֹת רֶכֶב, וּשְׁנֵים-עָשָׂר אֶלֶף פָּרָשִׁים; וַיַּנְחֵם בְּעָרֵי הָרֶכֶב, וְעִם-הַמֶּלֶךְ בִּירוּשָׁלִָם.
And Solomon gathered chariots and horsemen; and he had a thousand four hundred chariots and twelve thousand horsemen, and he stationed them in the chariot cities and with the king in Jerusalem.note
Y reunió Salomón carros y jinetes; y tuvo mil cuatrocientos carros y doce mil jinetes, y los puso en las ciudades de los carros y con el rey en Jerusalén.
27
וַיִּתֵּן הַמֶּלֶךְ אֶת-הַכֶּסֶף בִּירוּשָׁלִַם, כָּאֲבָנִים; וְאֵת הָאֲרָזִים, נָתַן כַּשִּׁקְמִים אֲשֶׁר-בַּשְּׁפֵלָה--לָרֹב.
And the king made silver in Jerusalem as common as stones, and cedars he made as plentiful as the sycamore-figs of the lowland.note
E hizo el rey que la plata en Jerusalén fuera tan común como las piedras, y los cedros tan abundantes como los sicómoros de la Sefela.
28
וּמוֹצָא הַסּוּסִים אֲשֶׁר לִשְׁלֹמֹה, מִמִּצְרָיִם; וּמִקְוֵה--סֹחֲרֵי הַמֶּלֶךְ, יִקְחוּ מִקְוֵה בִּמְחִיר.
And the export of horses that Solomon had was from Egypt, and from Kue: the king’s traders would take them from Kue at a price.note

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · 1 Kings 10 The queen of Sheba’s “hard questions” are riddles — a report, a test, and a train described like an army

Riddles, not “hard questions”. “She came to test him with riddles”: the word is chidot, the word for what Moses does not get from Jehovah, “not in riddles” (Numbers 12:8, now on these pages), and for Samson’s “Let me riddle you a riddle” (Judges 14:12). It names a thing stated so that it has to be worked out. The ASV and the KJV have “hard questions”, and so do the Geneva, the Douay, the NIV and the TLB; the NWT 1984 has “perplexing questions”. This page keeps the word it has used before. The verb “test” is the one of “to test you” (Deuteronomy 8:2, now on these pages).

“For the name of Jehovah”. “Was hearing the report of Solomon, for the name of Jehovah”: the Hebrew sets verb and noun from one root side by side, shomaat et-shema, and then adds two short words, le-shem Yehovah, which do not join easily to what comes before. They are the words of the building: “a house for the name of Jehovah” (1 Kings 5:19), “I have built the house for the name of Jehovah” (1 Kings 8:20, both now on these pages). The versions each decide what they mean. The ASV and the KJV have “concerning the name of”, the NWT 1984 “in connection with the name of Jehovah”, the Douay “in the name of the Lord”, the NIV “his relationship to the Lord”, and the TLB drops the name for “how wonderfully the Lord had blessed Solomon”. Chronicles leaves the phrase out (2 Chronicles 9:1). This page prints the two words as they stand.

Sheba, and a very weighty force. “With a very weighty force”: the phrase be-chayil kaved stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible. Two are this visit, here and in Chronicles (2 Chronicles 9:1), and the other two are the army of Assyria coming up against Jerusalem (2 Kings 18:17; Isaiah 36:2, none of them translated here yet). Chayil is strength of any kind, and an army is one of its senses; “weighty” is what Solomon called his people, “this weighty people of yours” (1 Kings 3:9, now on these pages). The verse then says what the force carries: camels, spices, gold and precious stones. The KJV and the Geneva have “a very great train”, the NWT 1984 “a very impressive train”, the NIV “a very great caravan”, and the Douay “a great train”. She is not named. Sheba is a name in the lists of Genesis, in more than one branch (Genesis 10:7, 10:28; 25:3, now on these pages), and these verses do not say which one is meant or where she ruled; the claims about her kingdom are collected under the Queen of Sheba. Jesus names her by direction, “the queen of the south”, who “came from the ends of the earth to hear the wisdom of Solomon” (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31, now on these pages).

“All her words”. “Solomon told her all her words”: the verb is the one Samson uses of his riddle, “if you can tell it to me” (Judges 14:12), so to “tell” a riddle is to solve it. The narrative gives no riddle and no answer, only the result. The ASV and the KJV have “told her all her questions”, the NWT 1984 “tell her all her matters”, the NIV “answered all her questions”, and the TLB adds a reason the Hebrew does not have, “for the Lord gave him the right answers every time”.

Verses 4–9 · 1 Kings 10:4–9 “No more spirit in her” — what the queen saw, “the half was not told me”, and your men or your wives

From the wisdom to the kitchen. She sees “all the wisdom of Solomon”, and the list that follows is of things that can be looked at: the house, the food, where the servants sit and how the attendants stand, their clothing, the cupbearers. In verse 5 the Hebrew word translated “his cupbearers” can also mean what is drunk, and the NWT 1984 has “his drinks” and the Geneva “his drinking vessels”. The last item is a real fork. The Hebrew here has olato, “his burnt offering”, and the parallel has a different word, aliyyato, “his going up” (2 Chronicles 9:4). The ASV and the KJV print the Chronicles word in Kings, “his ascent by which he went up”; the NWT 1984 has “his burnt sacrifices”, the NIV and the Geneva “burnt offerings”, and the Douay “the holocausts”. This page prints what stands in Kings.

“No more spirit in her”. Od ruach, “spirit any more”, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Rahab’s “no spirit rose up any more in any man” (Joshua 2:11), the kings beyond the Jordan, “there was no spirit in them any more” (Joshua 5:1, both now on these pages), and this verse. Those kings heard what Jehovah had done, and it took the breath out of them in terror. This queen has heard too, and the same words now describe wonder, a difference the context gives and the idiom does not. The NWT 1984 and the TLB keep it, “no more spirit in her”, and so does the Douay, “no longer any spirit in her”; the NIV has “she was overwhelmed” and the Geneva “greatly astonied”.

“The half was not told me”. Her speech goes back over the chapter’s two words. The report, shema, is what brought her in verse 1, and “words”, devarim, is what Solomon told her in verse 3; now “true was the word”, “about your words”, “I did not believe the words”, and at the end “beyond the report that I heard”. Chronicles has “the half of the greatness of your wisdom” (2 Chronicles 9:6). “You have added wisdom and good”: the word is plain tov, “good”, and the shelf gives it a meaning. The NWT 1984 and the TLB have “wisdom and prosperity”, the NIV “wisdom and wealth”, and the Douay “thy wisdom and thy works”. This page keeps “good”, which is the word Solomon asked to discern in 1 Kings 3:9.

Your men, or your wives? “Happy are your men”: the Hebrew is anashekha. The Greek Bible has “Happy are your wives” (hai gynaikes sou), which is what a Hebrew text would say with one letter fewer, nashekha. The Hebrew phrase stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and in Chronicles (2 Chronicles 9:7), both with “men”. The ASV, the KJV, the Geneva and the Douay have “thy men”, the NWT 1984 “your men”, and the NIV and the TLB widen it to “your people”. “Who hear your wisdom”: Solomon asked for “a hearing heart” (1 Kings 3:9).

A foreign queen blesses Jehovah. “May Jehovah your God be blessed”: Hiram said it first, “Blessed be Jehovah this day” (1 Kings 5:21, now on these pages), and each of them says it of the wise son on the throne. “Your God” is her phrase; the verse does not say what she herself worshiped. “Because Jehovah loved Israel forever”: the book of Samuel said “Jehovah loved him” of Solomon at birth (2 Samuel 12:24, now on these pages), and here the love is for Israel. Her last words state what the throne is for, “to do justice and righteousness”: it is what David “was doing” (2 Samuel 8:15), and what all Israel saw the wisdom of God in Solomon for, “to do justice” (1 Kings 3:28, both now on these pages). For “delighted in you” the ASV and the KJV have “delighted in thee”, the NWT 1984 “taken delight in you”, the Douay “whom thou hast pleased”, the Geneva “which loved thee”, and the TLB “who chose you”.

Verses 10–13 · 1 Kings 10:10–13 A hundred and twenty talents, almug wood from Ophir, and “all her desire”

The same hundred and twenty. “A hundred and twenty talents of gold”: the exact amount stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: what Hiram sent the king (1 Kings 9:14, now on these pages), this gift, and its parallel (2 Chronicles 9:9). The word for “spices” is besem, and it frames the visit: the camels carry it, she gives it, and the verse ranks it, “there came no more, in abundance, such spice”. The ASV, the KJV and the NIV have “spices”, the Geneva “sweet odors”, and the NWT 1984 “balsam oil”. Later texts remember Sheba for this cargo: all from Sheba “shall bring gold and incense” (Isaiah 60:6).

A parenthesis, and a tree no one can name. Verses 11–12 step out of the queen’s story to report the fleet of 1 Kings 9:27–28 (now on these pages), and verse 13 steps back in. The Hebrew marks the step only with ve-gam, and also; the NIV, the Douay and the TLB print the two verses in parentheses. The word almuggim stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, these. Chronicles reverses two letters, algummim, in three verses (2 Chronicles 2:7; 9:10–11). The text gives no description of the tree, and the versions show it. The ASV and the KJV transliterate “almug”, the Geneva “Almuggim”, the NWT 1984 and the TLB “algum”; the NIV has “almugwood”; the Douay has “thyine trees”; and the Greek Bible translates a guess at the sense, “hewn timber”. This page keeps the Hebrew name.

Supports, a word found once. “Supports for the house of Jehovah”: the noun misad stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one. The only other place with these letters is an Aramaic verb, “helping” (Ezra 5:2), which fits the sense. The parallel has another word, usually a raised road or path (2 Chronicles 9:11). The NWT 1984 and the NIV have “supports”, the ASV and the KJV “pillars”, and the Douay “rails”. “Lyres and harps for the singers”: this page has kept kinnor as “lyre” and nevel as “harp” (1 Samuel 10:5, now on these pages). The ASV and the KJV have “harps also and psalteries”, the NIV “harps and lyres”, the NWT 1984 “harps and stringed instruments”, and the TLB “harps and harpsichords”, an instrument of a much later age.

“All her desire”, and the hand of the king. “All her desire” is chefets, the word of Solomon’s dealings with Hiram, “all your desire” and “all his desire” (1 Kings 5:22, 24; 9:11, now on these pages). “According to the hand of King Solomon”: the phrase ke-yad ha-melekh stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and two in Esther of a king’s open-handed giving (Esther 1:7; 2:18). The KJV and the NIV have “royal bounty”, the Geneva “kingly liberality”, and the NWT 1984 “the openhandedness of King Solomon”. Then the visit ends in five Hebrew words: “she turned and went to her land, she and her servants”. The verse says nothing of what “all her desire” included. The Ethiopian tradition that she bore Solomon a son rests on other writings, not on this text.

Verses 14–21 · 1 Kings 10:14–21 Six hundred and sixty-six talents — the gold shields, an ivory throne, and a calf’s head or a round top?

666. “Six hundred and sixty-six talents of gold”: the number stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, its parallel (2 Chronicles 9:13), and the sons of Adonikam who came back from exile (Ezra 2:13). At the rough figure of about 34 kilograms for a talent, it comes to some twenty-two tonnes of gold a year. The same number is the number of the beast (Revelation 13:18, now on these pages); neither text refers to the other. The TLB turns the sum into money, “a quarter of a billion dollars”. It had turned the hundred and twenty talents of verse 10 into “$3,500,000”, so a talent of gold there is worth about $29,000 and here about $375,000, nearly thirteen times as much, four verses later.

Traders, merchants, and the mixed peoples. “The men of the traders”: ha-tarim is from the verb of “those who scouted out the land” (Numbers 14:6, now on these pages), people who go about. “All the kings of the mixed peoples”: the Hebrew is pointed ha-erev, the word of the “mixed multitude” that left Egypt (Exodus 12:38) and of “the kings of the mixed peoples who dwell in the wilderness” (Jeremiah 25:24, both now on these pages), where it stands beside “the kings of Arabia”. The same consonants read arav are Arabia, and that is how the parallel is pointed (2 Chronicles 9:14). The ASV follows the pointing here, “the mingled people”; the KJV has “all the kings of Arabia”, the NWT 1984 “the kings of the Arabs”, and the NIV “all the Arabian kings”.

Gold shields. The large shield is tsinnah and the smaller magen, and the larger takes more gold. Zahav shachut, “beaten gold”, stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible, these two and their parallels, and the meaning is worked out from context. The ASV and the KJV have “beaten gold”, the NIV “hammered gold”, the NWT 1984 “alloyed gold”, and the Douay “the purest gold”. “Six hundred of gold”: the Hebrew does not say six hundred of what. The ASV, the KJV and the NIV supply “six hundred shekels”, and the NWT 1984 supplies the same word in brackets. “Three minas”: the NIV and the NWT 1984 have minas, the ASV “three pounds”, the KJV “three pound”, and the Douay “three hundred pounds”; the parallel has “three hundred” of gold (2 Chronicles 9:16). The shields go up in the House of the Forest of Lebanon (1 Kings 7:2, now on these pages). David had brought shields of gold to Jerusalem as plunder (2 Samuel 8:7, now on these pages), and Solomon’s will go the same way, out of the city: “all the shields of gold which Solomon had made”, replaced in bronze (1 Kings 14:26–27, now on these pages).

A calf’s head? “The top of the throne was round behind it”: the Hebrew is rosh agol, and agol is the word for the round sea of the house (1 Kings 7:23, now on these pages). But the vowels are the Masoretes’ addition, and the same three consonants read egel are “calf”, the word of Exodus 32:4 (now on these pages). So the letters can also say “a calf’s head”, and that is what the Greek Bible has: “calves’ heads for the throne behind it” (protomai moschon). All twelve versions of the shelf read for this chapter print round instead. The ASV and the KJV have “round behind”, the NWT 1984 “a round canopy behind it”, the NIV “a rounded top”, and the TLB “a rounded back”. Scholars have asked whether the pointing moved a calf off the throne on purpose; the text cannot settle that, and this page prints the pointing it has. Chronicles has no such clause, and has a “footstool of gold” instead (2 Chronicles 9:18).

Six steps, twelve lions, and silver as nothing. “Refined gold”: the word mufaz stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one, and the parallel has the common word for “pure” (2 Chronicles 9:17). The NWT 1984 has “refined gold”, the NIV “fine gold”, the KJV “the best gold”, and the Douay “the finest gold”. The twelve lions stand “on this side and on that”; the NIV and the TLB spell out the arithmetic, two to a step, which the Hebrew leaves to the reader, and the Douay makes them “twelve little lions”, following the Latin. “Pure gold”, zahav sagur, stands in eight verses of the Hebrew Bible. Six are the furnishings of the house of Jehovah, among them 1 Kings 6:20–21 and 7:49–50 (now on these pages); the other two are this verse and its parallel, the vessels of the king’s hall. “There was no silver, it was not reckoned as anything”: the NWT 1984 has “as nothing at all”, and the NIV softens it to “of little value”.

Verses 22–25 · 1 Kings 10:22–25 Ships of Tarshish — apes and peacocks or baboons, and “all the earth” seeking Solomon’s face

A fleet of Tarshish. “The fleet of Tarshish”: Tarshish is where Jonah bought his passage, the far end of the sea (Jonah 1:3, now on these pages). This fleet, though, sails with Hiram’s, and the last chapter’s fleet left from the Sea of Reeds (1 Kings 9:26, now on these pages), which does not lead to the west. The parallel says “ships going to Tarshish” (2 Chronicles 9:21), and yet later “ships of Tarshish” are built “to go to Ophir for gold” (1 Kings 22:49), so the name may be a kind of ship and not a destination. The verse does not say. The ASV and the NWT 1984 print the name; the KJV spells it “Tharshish”; the NIV has “a fleet of trading ships” and the TLB “merchant fleet”, which settle the question in the translation; and the Douay has the ships go “by sea to Tharsis”.

Ivory, apes and peacocks. Three words of the cargo are rare. Shenhabbim, “ivory”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and in the parallel, against plain shen, “tooth”, for the throne of verse 18; the Douay has “elephants’ teeth”. Tukkiyyim also stands in two verses, the same two, with one spelling each. The ASV, the KJV, the NWT 1984, the Geneva, the Douay and the TLB have “peacocks”, and the NIV has “baboons”. Each has been traced to a foreign word, peacock to one language and monkey to another; these are proposals, and the Hebrew text gives no description of the animal. This page keeps the traditional “peacocks” and says so here. The Greek Bible has no animals in this verse at all: it has stones, “carved and hewn”.

Greater than all the kings. “Greater than all the kings of the earth in riches and in wisdom”: this is what Jehovah promised at Gibeon, “a wise and understanding heart”, and “riches and honor, so that there has not been a man like you among the kings” (1 Kings 3:12–13, now on these pages). The promise is kept in the same two items, in the other order. “That God had put in his heart”: the world comes for what Solomon asked for, and the verse says where it came from.

“Seeking the face”. “All the earth was seeking the face of Solomon”: this is the phrase for going to Jehovah, “David sought the face of Jehovah” (2 Samuel 21:1, now on these pages). Here the object is a king. Chronicles narrows it, “all the kings of the earth” (2 Chronicles 9:23). The NWT 1984 supplies “all the people of the earth”, the NIV has “sought audience with”, and the TLB “Great men from many lands”. “His tribute”: the word is minchah, as in 1 Kings 5:1 (now on these pages). The ASV has “his tribute”, the KJV “his present”, the NIV “a gift”, and the TLB “annual tribute”. “The due of a year, year by year” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and in the parallel.

Verses 26–29 · 1 Kings 10:26–29 Horses from Egypt and Kue — silver like stones, the law of the king in Deuteronomy 17, and a price list

The law of the king. The chapter ends without comment on a set of facts. Deuteronomy gave the king three limits: “he shall not multiply horses for himself, and he shall not make the people go back to Egypt in order to multiply horses”; “he shall not multiply wives for himself”; “silver and gold he shall not multiply for himself greatly” (Deuteronomy 17:16–17, now on these pages). This chapter counts the gold and says what silver was worth in Jerusalem, and it ends with the horses and with Egypt. The narrator states no connection here. The wives are the next verse (1 Kings 11:1, now on these pages).

The numbers. “A thousand four hundred chariots and twelve thousand horsemen”: Chronicles has the same figures (2 Chronicles 1:14, now on these pages). Earlier this book gave “forty thousand stalls of horses” (1 Kings 5:6, now on these pages), and the second Chronicles passage has “four thousand stalls” (2 Chronicles 9:25). The “chariot cities” were on the list of 1 Kings 9:19. “As plentiful as the sycamore-figs of the lowland”: where Kings has only silver, 2 Chronicles 1:15 says “silver and gold”. The NIV has “sycamore-fig trees”, the Douay “sycamores”, and the Geneva “wild fig trees”.

Kue, or linen, or droves. The Hebrew spells miqveh twice in verse 28, and the same letters mean a gathering, the word of the “gathered waters” of Genesis 1:10 (now on these pages). The versions divide three ways. As a place, Kue: the NIV has “from Kue”, the Douay “Coa”, and the TLB updates the map to “southern Turkey”. As a gathering of animals: the ASV has “in droves, each drove at a price”, and the NWT 1984 “the horse drove”. As cloth: the KJV has “linen yarn” and the Geneva “fine linen”. This page prints Kue, as it did at the parallel (2 Chronicles 1:16, now on these pages), because Assyrian records are reported to name a land Que in Cilicia, and the Greek Bible also reads a name here. The word miqveh itself is in the dictionary.

A price list. “Six hundred pieces of silver, and a horse for a hundred and fifty”: the Hebrew says “six hundred of silver”, as verse 16 said “six hundred of gold”, and this page supplies “pieces” as it did at 2 Chronicles 1:17 (now on these pages). A chariot costs four horses. The Douay takes that as the reason, “a chariot of four horses”. The TLB again converts, to “$400” and “$150”, which makes a chariot worth fewer than three horses. “By their hand, they exported them to all the kings of the Hittites and the kings of Aram”: the NIV has “They also exported them”, so Solomon’s traders are also dealers. The Hittites return in the next verse, among the foreign women Solomon loved (1 Kings 11:1, now on these pages).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “riddles” for chidot, as in Numbers 12:8; “for the name of Jehovah”, as in 1 Kings 5:19 and 8:20; “a very weighty force” for chayil kaved, with “weighty” as in 1 Kings 3:9; “no more spirit”, as in Joshua 5:1; “good” for tov in verse 7; “almug” for almuggim; “supports” for misad; “lyres and harps” for kinnorot and nevalim; “all her desire” for chefets, as with Hiram; “the mixed peoples” for ha-erev, as in Jeremiah 25:24; “large shield” for tsinnah and “shield” for magen; “beaten gold” for zahav shachut; “refined gold” for mufaz and “pure gold” for zahav sagur; “fleet” for oni, as in 1 Kings 9:26; “tribute” for minchah, as in 1 Kings 5:1; and “Kue” and “sycamore-figs of the lowland”, as in 2 Chronicles 1:15–16.

Echoes paid. The fleet of Hiram, the gold of Ophir and the navy of Tarshish (1 Kings 9:26–28); the hundred and twenty talents (1 Kings 9:14); no more spirit (Joshua 2:11; 5:1); the hearing heart, the weighty people, and the discerning of good (1 Kings 3:9); riches and wisdom without equal among the kings (1 Kings 3:12–13); the name, for which the house was built (1 Kings 5:19; 8:20); Hiram’s blessing (1 Kings 5:21); all his desire (1 Kings 5:22, 24; 9:11); doing justice and righteousness (2 Samuel 8:15; 1 Kings 3:28); the House of the Forest of Lebanon (1 Kings 7:2); pure gold (1 Kings 6:20–21; 7:49–50); the stalls of horses (1 Kings 5:6) and the chariot cities (1 Kings 9:19); David’s shields of gold (2 Samuel 8:7); the law of the king (Deuteronomy 17:16–17); and the queen of the south (Matthew 12:42; Luke 11:31).

Echoes planted. The Chronicler’s telling of this chapter, with “his going up”, “all the kings of the earth”, the footstool of gold and the four thousand stalls (2 Chronicles 9:1–28); Solomon’s foreign wives, and the Hittites among them (1 Kings 11:1, now on these pages); the calves of gold (1 Kings 12:28); the shields of gold taken away and replaced in bronze (1 Kings 14:26–27, now on these pages); the ships of Tarshish broken on the way to Ophir (1 Kings 22:49); the army of Assyria, with the same weighty force (2 Kings 18:17; Isaiah 36:2); and gold and incense from Sheba (Isaiah 60:6). The others are yet to come in this translation.
