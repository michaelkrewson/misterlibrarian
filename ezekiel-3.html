Ezekiel eats the scroll, is strengthened against a house that will not listen, and is carried to Tel-abib, where he sits seven days stunned. He is made a watchman, goes out to the valley where the glory stands, and is shut up in his house, bound and mute until Jehovah speaks.
Follows chapters 1 and 2 in the fifth year of King Jehoiachin’s exile (1:2). The seven days of verse 15 and 16 are the only dating within the chapter.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–3 · Ezekiel 3:1–3 eat this scroll — sweet as honey

⭐ “Eat what you find.” The hand that held out the scroll of dirges at the end of the last chapter (2:9–10, now on these pages) now has it eaten. The KJV has “eat that thou findest; eat this roll”, the NIV “eat what is before you, eat this scroll” and the Douay “eat all that thou shalt find: eat this book”. The order is eat first, then go and speak.

⭐ Sweet as honey. “Like honey for sweetness” stands in this one verse, and it is said of a scroll written with dirges and moaning and woe. The Psalms say the same of God’s words: sweeter than honey to the palate (Psalm 119:103) and sweeter than honey and the comb (Psalm 19:11, both now on these pages). The little scroll John eats is sweet in his mouth too, and bitter in his stomach (Revelation 10:9–10, now on these pages); here the word for bitter comes later, at verse 14, and it is said of the prophet’s going and not of his stomach. The KJV has “it was in my mouth as honey for sweetness” and the NIV “it tasted as sweet as honey in my mouth”.

Verses 4–9 · Ezekiel 3:4–9 not to a foreign people — strong foreheads — a forehead stronger than flint

⭐ Deep of lip, heavy of tongue. He is not sent across a language barrier. “Deep of lip” (imqei safah) stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here at verses 5 and 6, and Isaiah 33:19. Moses had used the other half of the phrase of himself, kevad peh u-khevad lashon, heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue (Exodus 4:10, now on these pages). The point of verse 6 is the irony: foreigners with a hard speech would have listened. The KJV has “a people of a strange speech and of an hard language”, the NIV “a people of obscure speech and strange language”, the Douay “a people of a profound speech” and the NWT 2013 “an unintelligible language or an unknown tongue”.

⭐ Strong of forehead. The obstacle is not the words. “Strong of forehead” (chizqei metsach) stands in this one verse, with “hard of heart”. The two adjectives of 2:4 (now on these pages) change places: there the faces are qasheh, hard, and the hearts chazaq, strong (printed “stiff of heart”), and here the forehead is strong and the heart hard. The KJV has “impudent and hardhearted”, the ASV “of a hard forehead and of a stiff heart”, the NIV “hardened and obstinate” and the NWT 2013 “hardheaded and hardhearted”.

⭐ Strong against strong. The answer to a strong forehead is a stronger one, and the word that does it is the root of chazaq, ‘to be strong’, which runs through verses 7, 8, 9 and 14. The prophet’s own name, Yechezqel, is built on that root and means that God strengthens (Ezekiel): the commission strengthens the man whose name says it will. Then the stone: shamir (shamir), stronger than flint, the word for the diamond point with which Judah’s sin is engraved at Jeremiah 17:1 (now on these pages). The KJV has “As an adamant harder than flint”, the NIV “like the hardest stone, harder than flint”, the NWT 2013 “your forehead like a diamond, harder than flint” and the TLB “as hard as rock”.

Verses 10–15 · Ezekiel 3:10–15 blessed be the glory of Jehovah — bitter — Tel-abib, seven days stunned

⭐ Heart and ears. Before he goes he is to take the words into his heart and hear them with his ears, and then he goes to the exiles (golah), the sons of his people. The charge repeats the one at 2:7 (now on these pages): whether they hear or refuse.

⚠ “Blessed be the glory” or “as the glory rose.” “Blessed be the glory of Jehovah” stands in this one verse, and the shelf splits on it. The KJV has “Blessed be the glory of the Lord from his place”, the ASV “Blessed be the glory of Jehovah from his place”, the NWT 1984 “Blessed be the glory of Jehovah from his place” and the NWT 2013 “May the glory of Jehovah be praised from his place”; against them the NIV has “as the glory of the Lord rose from the place where it was standing” and the TLB “the glory of the Lord began to move away”. The archive’s Hebrew reads baruch, ‘blessed’, and this translation follows it; the usual explanation of the other reading, a change of one letter to a verb of rising, is reported here and not checked in the archive. The phrase mimmeqomo, ‘from his place’, is the one at Micah 1:3 (now on these pages), where Jehovah comes out of his place. The wings and the wheels are those of chapter 1 (1:15, now on these pages).

⭐ Bitter, and stunned. The KJV has “in bitterness, in the heat of my spirit”, the NIV “in bitterness and in the anger of my spirit” and the NWT 1984 “bitterly in the rage of my spirit”; the text does not say what the anger is at. “Tel-abib” stands in this one verse. Tel is a mound, the word for a ruin-heap, and aviv is the ear of green grain (aviv), as at Exodus 9:31 (now on these pages), and the Douay translates the name: “to the heap of new corn”. The NIV has “Tel Aviv” (Tel-abib). Seven days he sits among them, and the KJV has “remained there astonished among them seven days”, the NWT 2013 “in a dazed condition”. Job’s friends sit seven days too, in silence (Job 2:13). The scroll writes a word in verse 15 that the reading tradition says differently, ‘and where’ for ‘and I sat’.

Verses 16–21 · Ezekiel 3:16–21 a watchman for the house of Israel — four cases — blood required at your hand

⭐ A watchman. The seven days end and the word comes. “I have made you a watchman” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here and Ezekiel 33:7, where the charge returns nearly word for word (33:7–9). The watchman is the one posted to see and call out, as on the roof over the gate in 2 Samuel 18:24, and as Habakkuk says he will stand at his watch (Habakkuk 2:1, both now on these pages) (tsofeh). The KJV has “I have made thee a watchman”, the NIV “I have made you a watchman for the people of Israel” and the NWT 2013 “I have appointed you as a watchman”.

⭐ Four cases. The charge is laid out as a table. A wicked man unwarned dies in his iniquity, and the watchman answers for his blood (verse 18). A wicked man warned who does not turn dies, and the watchman has saved his own life (verse 19). A righteous man who turns away and is not warned dies, and the watchman answers for him (verse 20). A righteous man warned who does not sin lives (verse 21). “His blood I will require at your hand” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: verses 18 and 20 here, and 33:8. The older law of blood is at Genesis 9:5 (now on these pages), with a different verb for ‘require’. The KJV has “his blood will I require at thine hand” and the NIV “I will hold you accountable for their blood”.

⚠ A stumbling block. Verse 20 says that Jehovah puts a stumbling block before the one who turns away, and does not say why. The KJV has “and I lay a stumbling-block before him”, the NIV “and I put a stumbling block before them” and the TLB “and the Lord destroys him”. What the verse does say is what happens to his earlier good: it is not remembered.

Verses 22–24 · Ezekiel 3:22–24 out to the valley — the glory standing there — shut yourself in your house

⭐ The valley. The word is biq’ah (biqah), a broad valley, the word of the valley of dry bones (Ezekiel 37:1, now on these pages). The KJV has “went forth into the plain”, the Geneva “gone forth into the field” and the NWT 1984 “to the valley plain”.

⭐ The glory again. What stood by the Chebar stands in the valley, he falls on his face as at 1:28, and spirit sets him on his feet as at 2:2 (both now on these pages). “And set me on my feet” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, the two places where this happens to him in these opening chapters. This time what follows is not a sending but a confinement: shut yourself in your house.

Verses 25–27 · Ezekiel 3:25–27 cords — mute — let the one who hears hear

⚠ Cords, and whose hands. The verse says “they will put cords on you” and does not name them. The NIV has “they will tie with ropes” and the TLB makes it Jehovah’s own act, “I will paralyze you so you can’t leave”.

⭐ Mute. The watchman who must warn is made unable to speak. “I will make your tongue cling” stands in this one verse; the picture of a tongue stuck to the roof of the mouth is the nursing child’s at Lamentations 4:4 (now on these pages), with the same root and the same word for the palate, and it is the oath of Psalm 137:6. “A man who reproves” stands in this one verse. The KJV has “shalt not be to them a reprover” and the NIV “unable to rebuke them”. The silence is not permanent: Ezekiel 24:27 and 33:22 have his mouth opened.

⭐ Let the one who hears hear. Verse 27 says that when Jehovah speaks with him he will open his mouth. The last words turn the charge of 2:5 and 2:7 (now on these pages) into a command to the hearers. The KJV has “He that heareth, let him hear” and the NIV “Whoever will listen let them listen”. And the reason is the same: they are a rebellious house.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “strong” and its forms kept for the root of chazaq at verses 7, 8, 9 and 14, so that the pun on the prophet’s name can be seen; “spirit” printed without a capital at verses 12, 14 and 24; “warn” kept as one verb through the watchman passage; “require at your hand” identical at verses 18 and 20; and “rebellious house” kept for beit meri at verses 9, 26 and 27.

Echoes paid. The scroll of 2:9–10, eaten; the hardness of 2:4, now met with a stronger forehead; the living creatures, wheels and glory of chapter 1; and the sweetness of God’s words in Psalm 119:103.

Echoes planted. The watchman returns at Ezekiel 33:1–9 and the opening of the mouth at 24:27 and 33:22; the valley is the one where the bones lie in Ezekiel 37; and Revelation 10 takes up the eaten scroll.
