“Do not be afraid, for I have redeemed you”: Jehovah, who created Jacob and formed Israel, promises to be with his people through water and fire, gives Egypt, Cush and Seba as their ransom, and says he will bring their seed from east and west. The nations are put on trial and asked for witnesses; Israel is his witness, he alone is Jehovah and saviour, and no one can rescue from his hand. He has sent to Babylon and will bring its Chaldeans down in their ships, and tells them not to remember the former things, for he is doing a new thing, a way in the wilderness and rivers in the wasteland, for the people formed to recount his praise. Then the tone turns: they have not called on him or brought offerings, they have made him serve with their sins, and he, he, blots out their transgressions for his own sake; he tells them to plead, accuses their first father and their interpreters, and ends by giving Jacob to the ban.
The chapter names no king and no date. Babylon is spoken of as about to fall, and Radak reads it either of Sennacherib’s fall or of Cyrus, while Ibn Ezra reads it of Media and Persia taking Babylon.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · Isaiah 43:1–4 “Do not be afraid, for I have redeemed you” — created and formed, “through the waters”, and Egypt, Cush and Seba as a ransom

⭐ “Do not be afraid”, and “I have called you by your name”. “Do not be afraid” (al tira) stands in 74 verses of the Hebrew Bible, and it is the word that has opened the comfort of the book twice already, at Isaiah 41:10 and 41:14 (both now on these pages). Here it is given a reason that is a price paid: ga’alti-kha, “I have redeemed you”, the verb of the go’el, the kinsman who buys back (dictionary). The KJV, the ASV and the Douay have “I have redeemed thee”, the NIV “I have redeemed you”, the TLB “I have ransomed you”, and the NWT 1984 “I have repurchased you”. “I have called you by your name” (qarati ve-shimkha) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible; the NIV has “I have summoned you by name”. Rashi opens the chapter with “And now, despite all this, thus said the Lord: do not fear”. Radak says some read the whole section of Cyrus, who sent Israel out of Babylon; his father read it of Sennacherib, and he calls that “the right one”, and he adds that it can also be read of the future. Ibn Ezra says: since you confess, now Jehovah will redeem you; and “called by your name” is a great honor.

⭐ “He who created you, Jacob, and he who formed you, Israel”. The two verbs are kept apart: bara, “created”, reserved in the Bible for God (dictionary), and yatsar, “formed”, the potter’s verb (dictionary). They are set on the two names of one people, Jacob created and Israel formed, and the same two verbs return in verse 7 with a third, asah, “made”. The NWT 1984 has “your Creator, O Jacob, and your Former, O Israel”. The Greek Psalter’s Isaiah has “who made you” for the first verb and “who formed you” for the second, ho poiēsas and ho plasas, and ends “you are mine”, emos ei su.

⚠ “When you pass through the waters… through fire”. “When you pass through the waters” (ki ta‘avor ba-mayim) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and it carries the promise a second time: ittekha ani, “I am with you”, here and again at verse 5, where it is “for I am with you”, the formula Paul hears in a Corinthian night (Acts 18:10, now on these pages). Rashi reads the waters as the Sea of Reeds and the rivers as the peoples among whom Israel lived, numerous as a river and unable to prevail; the fire as the day to come when the sun burns the wicked (Malachi 3:19). Radak takes the waters and the rivers as Sennacherib’s army, as the river of Assyria in Isaiah 8:7, and the fire as its burning, and, on the future reading, the peoples through whom Israel will pass out of exile. Ibn Ezra makes the waters and the rivers the princes of Media and Persia when they take Babylon. The KJV and the ASV have “thou shalt not be burned”, and the NWT 1984 “you will not be scorched, neither will the flame itself singe you”.

⭐ “I have given Egypt as your ransom”. “I have given your ransom” (natati kofrekha) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The word is kofer, the price paid for a life (dictionary). The KJV has “I gave Egypt for thy ransom, Ethiopia and Seba for thee”, the ASV “Ethiopia and Seba in thy stead”, the NIV “Cush and Seba in your stead”, the Douay “for thy atonement”, and the TLB adds a name the Hebrew does not have, “to Cyrus in exchange for your freedom”. Rashi says Egypt was the ransom because its firstborn died, and Israel, God’s firstborn son, deserved destruction; Radak reads it of Sennacherib, who turned back from Jerusalem to fight Tirhakah king of Cush (2 Kings 19:9) and whose army fell there; Ibn Ezra, of the kings of Media doing Egypt and Cush harm. The Greek has Soēnēn, “Syene”, for Seba. Verse 4 gives the reason, “since you have been precious in my eyes, you have been honored, and I have loved you”, and the price goes up: “I give humankind in your place, and peoples for your soul”. The KJV and the ASV have “men”, the NIV “people”, and the NWT 1984 “your soul” and “men”; the Greek has “rulers for your head”. “I give humankind in your place” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible.

Verses 5–8 · Isaiah 43:5–8 “From the east I will bring your seed” — north and south told to give up, “created, formed, made”, and a blind people with eyes

⭐ “For I am with you… from the east… from the west”. “Do not be afraid, for I am with you” (al tira ki ittekha ani) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and “for I am with you” (ki ittekha ani) stands in six verses: here, and Jeremiah 1:8, 1:19, 15:20, 30:11 and 46:28 (none of those translated here yet). The seed is gathered from the four directions, zar’ekha, “your seed”. Verse 6 speaks to the winds as to servants: “I will say to the north: Give; and to the south: Do not hold back.” Rashi says the north wind is weak and needs “give”, and the south is strong, and “do not hold back” asks it to blow strongly, as in “Awaken, O north wind, and come, O south wind” (Song of Solomon 4:16). The KJV has “Give up” and “Keep not back”, the NIV “Give them up!” and “Do not hold them back”, and the NWT 1984 “Give up!” and “Do not keep back”. The Greek reads “Boreas” and “Libs”, the north wind and the south-west.

⭐ “Created… formed… made”. “I formed him, yes, I made him” (yetsartiv af ‘asitiv) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and with bara’tiv, “I created him”, it is all three of the verbs of making in one verse. Isaiah 45:7 and 45:18 put the same verbs of creation on Jehovah’s lips about light and the earth. The KJV has “I have created him for my glory, I have formed him; yea, I have made him”, the ASV “whom I have formed, yea, whom I have made”, the NIV “whom I formed and made”, and the NWT 1984 “that I have formed, yes, that I have made”. Radak gives each verb a job: yetsirah is the fashioning of the limbs, ‘asiyah the provision of food and of what the body needs, so that a person should consider how his limbs were made with wisdom and his sustenance arranged from the day of his birth. Ibn Ezra says ‘asitiv means “and I put him right”, and rejects the reading of Rabbi Solomon, the poet, that it means the light of the world, as unconnected with the passage. “Everyone who is called by my name” is, for Radak, all Israel, or the righteous in it, “everyone who is written for life in Jerusalem”; Ibn Ezra insists the phrase is passive, as it stands, “those who are called by my name”, and not “those who call”. The Greek has “everyone who is called by my name; for I prepared him for my glory, and formed him, and made him”.

⚠ “A people blind, though there are eyes”. “A blind people” (‘am ‘iver) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the verb that begins the verse, hotsi, “bring out”, can be an order, “bring out”, or an infinitive, “to bring out”. The KJV and the ASV have “Bring forth the blind people that have eyes”, the NIV “Lead out those who have eyes but are blind”, the NWT 1984 “Bring forth a people blind though eyes themselves exist”, the Geneva “I will bring forth the blind people, and they shall have eyes”, and the TLB “Bring them back to me”. Radak reads the infinitive: I said to bring out of exile a people who are as blind and deaf; Rashi, to bring out of exile those who were exiled because they became like the blind, having eyes and not seeing; Ibn Ezra, that they were so from the start, though they have eyes. The Greek has “and I brought out a blind people”. The blind and the deaf with eyes and ears are a theme of the servant (Isaiah 42:18–20), “Hear, you deaf, and look, you blind”.

Verses 9–13 · Isaiah 43:9–13 “You are my witnesses” and “I am he” — the nations on trial, no god formed before him, and “no one who can rescue from my hand”

⭐ “Let them give their witnesses and be justified”. The scene is a courtroom, and it is the one convened at Isaiah 41:1, “let us come near together for judgment”, and pressed at 41:22, “The former things, what were they? Tell us” (both now on these pages). The nations are gathered; the question is who among them has declared “this”, and made us hear “the former things”, and the test is witnesses (dictionary): let them give their witnesses and be justified, and let them hear and say “It is truth”, emet. The KJV, the ASV and the Geneva have “Let all the nations be gathered together”, the NIV “All the nations gather together”, the Douay “All the nations are assembled together”, and the NWT 1984 “Let the nations all be collected together at one place”. Rashi says that if all the nations gathered, which of them or of their prophets would have told the future, or the first events already past, saying “we foretold them before they came”? The Greek has “rulers” where the Hebrew has “peoples”.

⭐ “You are my witnesses… and my servant whom I have chosen”. “You are my witnesses” (atem ‘edai) stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, verse 12, and Isaiah 44:8. The servant is already known: “you, Israel, my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen” (Isaiah 41:8, now on these pages). Rashi takes “my servant” as Jacob, who will testify to the promise made when he went to Mesopotamia; Radak and Ibn Ezra both take “you” as Israel and “my servant” as the prophet, Radak because the prophet announced Sennacherib’s fall before it happened. The Greek has “Be my witnesses, and I am a witness, says the Lord God, and my servant whom I chose”. The reason is to “know and believe me and understand that I am he”.

⭐ “I am he”, and “before me no god was formed”. “I am he” (ani hu), as God’s own declaration, stands in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible: Deuteronomy 32:39, Isaiah 41:4, 43:10 and 43:13 here, and Isaiah 46:4, 48:12 and 52:6 (those last three yet to come in this translation); the eighth hit in the text is a man’s own confession, not God’s. Ibn Ezra says the words state the oneness above which nothing stands, since everything that exists is not itself. “Before me no god was formed” (lefanai lo notsar El) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the passive notsar bothers the commentators. Radak says it is hard, for the speaker has no beginning, and gives two ways out: before I formed the things formed, no god formed any, and after me none will; or, with his father, notsar is taken as it stands, nothing was formed by any god but by me. Ibn Ezra says some refer “before me” to Sinai, which is not needed, and warns that people blind of heart might think the prophet says God was formed, “God forbid”. The Greek has “before me there was no other God, and after me there shall not be”. The KJV and the ASV have “before me there was no God formed”, the NIV “Before me no god was formed”, the NWT 1984 “Before me there was no God formed”, and the Douay “Before me there was no God formed”.

⭐ “I, I am Jehovah”, “no stranger among you”, and “from today”. “I, I am Jehovah” (anokhi anokhi Yehovah) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the verse after it ends “I am God”. The three verbs of verse 12, “I declared, and I saved, and I made heard”, are the proof a witness can give. “No stranger among you” is zar (dictionary), with no “god” in the Hebrew; the KJV, the ASV and the Geneva have “no strange god among you”, the NIV “not some foreign god”, the Douay “no strange one among you”, and the NWT 1984 “no strange [god]”. Rashi has the stranger as a strange god who would have shown greatness in those days. “And there is a rescuer from my hand” with a negative in front of it (ve-ein miyyadi matsil) stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at Deuteronomy 32:39, where it follows the first “I am he”. Gam miyom, “also from today”, is a puzzle too: the KJV and the Geneva have “before the day was”, the ASV “since the day was”, the NIV “from ancient days”, the NWT 1984 “all the time”, and the TLB “From eternity to eternity”. Rashi and Radak both read it as before there was a day, before the world; Radak adds “since long ago”, as in “from of old I have broken your yoke”. And “who can turn it back”, umi yeshivenah: the KJV has “who shall let it?”, the ASV “who can hinder it?”, the NIV “who can reverse it?”, and the NWT 1984 “who can turn it back?”

Verses 14–17 · Isaiah 43:14–17 “For your sake I have sent to Babylon” — fugitives or bars, “the ships of their shouting”, and a way in the sea

⚠ “I will bring down all of them as fugitives”. “For your sake I have sent to Babylon”: “I have sent to Babylon” (shillachti Bavelah) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the speaker of that “I have sent” is disputed. Radak says the prophecy is of the exile in Babylon, and that Jehovah has sent Cyrus to Babylon to destroy it and bring Israel out; he is surprised that Ibn Ezra reads the verb as if it were shulachti, a passive, “I was sent”. Ibn Ezra says some hear the prophet in it, others, as he does, the word of Jehovah, who came so quickly it is as if he had sent; and he reports a great sage in Spain who said the Glory speaks to the prophet and “the prince of Israel” is the one sent to Babylon to redeem Israel. The next word is harder: bariachim. The ASV has “as fugitives” and the NIV “as fugitives”, the Geneva “they are all fugitives”, the KJV “all their nobles”, the Douay “all their bars”, and the NWT 1984 “cause the bars of the prisons to come down”. Radak reads it as fugitives, as the Babylonians flee when Cyrus comes. Ibn Ezra says it is the bars of Babylon, its gates, and cites “he has destroyed and broken her bars” (Lamentations 2:9, now on these pages). Rashi gives the Targum’s “with oars”.

⭐ “In the ships of their shouting”. Rinnatam is “their shouting”, a ringing cry, joy or lament. The ASV has “the ships of their rejoicing”, the KJV “whose cry is in the ships”, the NIV “in the ships in which they took pride”, and the NWT 1984 “in the ships with whining cries on their part”. Radak reads it as the cry of weeping, as in “Arise, cry out in the night” (Lamentations 2:19, now on these pages); Rashi, as rejoicing, and he notes that it may also be taken of the news of the redemption: the Chaldeans carried off to Media in the very ships in which they rejoiced. Ibn Ezra says the Chaldeans in ships will be brought down out of them. The Greek has “the Chaldeans shall be bound in ships”. The NIV has “your Redeemer” and the KJV “your redeemer”, each with “the Holy One of Israel”, as at Isaiah 41:14 (now on these pages), where the same two titles close the verse; the NWT 1984 has “the Repurchaser of YOU people”.

⭐ “The Creator of Israel, your King”, and a way in the sea. Verse 15 puts the creating, bore Yisra’el, beside “your King”, malkekhem. Verse 16 opens a new introduction, “Thus says Jehovah, who gives a way in the sea and a path in mighty waters”, and verse 17 is the Egyptians: “who brings out chariot and horse, army and might together; they lie down, they will not rise”. Rashi says this is the Sea of Reeds, where he drew the Egyptians out to pursue Israel “with chariots and horses”, and all lay dead on the shore. Radak says that after the sea comes the other miracle, Sennacherib’s army, brought out of Assyria as the fly is whistled for from the end of Egypt’s rivers, and fallen in a night, “and behold they were all dead bodies” (2 Kings 19:35); Ibn Ezra says they are the Chaldeans going out to fight the Persians.

⭐ “Quenched like a wick”. The last image is a lamp going out: da‘aku ke-fishtah kavu, “they are extinguished, quenched like a wick”. Pishtah is the flax of a wick, the dimly burning wick the servant will not quench at Isaiah 42:3. The ASV and the NIV have “a wick”, the KJV and the Geneva “tow”, the Douay “broken as flax”, and the NWT 1984 “Like a flaxen wick they must be put out”. Rashi has the Targum’s “like flax they dimmed, they were quenched”; Radak says it is the flax that, burning, goes out quickly, and so they died in a moment; Ibn Ezra takes da‘aku as “quenched”, and cites “his lamp shall be put out in the blackest darkness” (Proverbs 20:20). The Greek has “as flax quenched”, hōs linon esbesmenon.

Verses 18–21 · Isaiah 43:18–21 “Do not remember the former things… I am doing a new thing” — a way in the wilderness, jackals and the daughters of the ostrich, and a people that recounts

⭐ “Do not remember the former things”. “Do not remember the former things” (al tizkeru ri’shonot) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and it comes straight after verses 16 and 17 have just recalled the sea. Rashi resolves it: the miracles I performed in Egypt, do not remember them from now on, for you shall be engaged in this redemption, to thank and praise. “The former things” are what was challenged in verse 9, “make us hear the former things”. The verb is zakhar, “remember”, never a mere recall in Hebrew (dictionary). Ezra’s opening chapter has the exiles of the return told exactly this (Ezra 1, now on these pages). The KJV has “Remember ye not the former things, neither consider the things of old”, the ASV “Remember ye not the former things”, the NIV “Forget the former things”, and the NWT 1984 “Do not remember the first things”.

⭐ “I am doing a new thing”. “Look — I am doing a new thing” (hineni ‘oseh chadashah) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible; the word is feminine, chadashah, as in “Jehovah has created a new thing in the land” (Jeremiah 31:21, now on these pages), while the plural, “new things”, is Isaiah 42:9. Chadash is “new” (dictionary). “Now it springs forth; do you not know it?”: the KJV and the ASV have “shall ye not know it?”, the NIV “do you not perceive it?”, and the NWT 1984 “YOU people will know it, will YOU not?” Radak says ‘attah, “now”, is close, if Israel return, and that “you know it” means from the Law of Moses, “Jehovah your God will turn your captivity… and gather you from all the peoples” (Deuteronomy 30:3, now on these pages); “a way in the wilderness” he ties to “A voice calls: Clear in the wilderness the way of Jehovah” (Isaiah 40:3, now on these pages). The land is yeshimon, “wasteland”, where the NIV has “wasteland” and the KJV and the ASV “desert”.

⭐ “The jackals and the daughters of the ostrich”. The wild animals honor him because he has given water in the wasteland. “Daughters of the ostrich” (benot ya‘anah) stands in six verses of the Hebrew Bible: Isaiah 13:21, 34:13 and 43:20, Jeremiah 50:39, Micah 1:8 and Job 30:29, none of the others translated here yet. The ASV has “the jackals and the ostriches”, the TLB “the jackals and ostriches too”, the NIV “the jackals and the owls”, the KJV “the dragons and the owls”, the Douay “the dragons and the ostriches”, and the NWT 1984 “the jackals and the ostriches”. The Greek has “sirens and daughters of ostriches”. Ibn Ezra says they are named because they live in dry land, and the good is done for the sake of my people’s honor; Radak, that it is a figure: if the wilderness and the dry land will rejoice, as in “the wilderness and the dry land shall be glad” (Isaiah 35:1), how much more the beasts.

⭐ “That they may recount my praise”. “The people I formed for myself” (‘am zu yatsarti li) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the purpose is tehillati yesapperu, “that they may recount my praise”. The verb is sapar, the one that counts and recounts, which carries “I will recount your name to my brothers” (Psalm 22:23, now on these pages) (dictionary). The NWT 1984 has “that they should recount the praise of me”, the KJV “they shall shew forth my praise”, the ASV “that they might set forth my praise”, and the NIV “that they may proclaim my praise”. The Greek Isaiah has “my chosen race” and “to recount my excellencies”, to genos mou to eklekton and tas aretas mou diēgeisthai, the words Peter turns on his readers, “a chosen race… that you may proclaim the excellencies of the one who called you” (1 Peter 2:9, now on these pages).

Verses 22–24 · Isaiah 43:22–24 “You have made me serve with your sins” — no call, no offerings, and a God wearied by his people

⚠ “For you have wearied of me, Israel”. The tone turns. “Yet you did not call upon me, Jacob; for you have wearied of me, Israel.” The second clause is ambiguous: ki yagata bi is “for you have wearied of me” or “for you have toiled for me”, and the versions split. The KJV and the ASV have “thou hast been weary of me”, the NWT 1984 “you have grown weary of me”, the NIV “you have not wearied yourselves for me”, the Douay “neither hast thou laboured about me”, and the Geneva “thou hast wearied me”. Rashi says it means that you did not call me in your turning after idols, and that you quickly wearied of my worship. Radak gives two readings: you did not even call me in your trouble, still less toil for me; or it is not enough that you did not call me, but you wearied me by serving idols. Ibn Ezra reads it as: not even called until you toiled for me. The verb is yaga’, “weary”, to toil until spent (dictionary), and it comes back twice in verse 24, “you have wearied me”.

⭐ “You have not brought me the sheep of your burnt offerings”. The line says what the covenant people did not do, and the next says what God did not ask. “You have made me serve” (he‘evadtani) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and in verse 23 it is its own opposite, he‘evadtikha, “I have not made you serve with a grain offering”; “you have wearied me” (hoga‘tani) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, against hoga‘tikha in verse 23. The KJV has “I have not caused thee to serve with an offering, nor wearied thee with incense”, the ASV “I have not burdened thee with offerings”, the NIV “I have not burdened you with grain offerings”, the Douay “I have not caused thee to serve with oblations”, and the NWT 1984 “I have not compelled you to serve me with a gift”. Rashi says the offering was a handful, and even that was not made an obligation but a free-will gift. The same sacrifices are a weariness to God at Isaiah 1:14, “they have become a burden on me; I am weary of carrying them” (now on these pages), though with another verb.

⭐ “You have not bought me calamus with silver”. Qaneh is the cane that went into the incense (dictionary), not the verb qanah, “to buy”, that the verse uses with it. The KJV, the ASV and the Douay have “sweet cane”, the NIV “fragrant calamus”, the NWT 1984 “[sweet] cane”, and the TLB “sweet-smelling incense”. Rashi says you did not need to buy it with money, for it grew in your land, and gives Rabbi Abba’s report that cinnamon grew in the Land of Israel; Radak says it did not, and came from a far country, as in “the good cane from a far country” (Jeremiah 6:20); Ibn Ezra says only that it is a spice. “With the fat of your sacrifices you have not satisfied me”, hirvitani: the KJV and the ASV have “filled me”, the NIV “lavished on me”, the Geneva “made me drunk”, and the NWT 1984 “saturated me”.

⭐ “But you have made me serve with your sins”. The reversal is blunt: akh he‘evadtani be-chatotekha, “but you have made me serve with your sins”. The KJV has “thou hast made me to serve with thy sins”, the ASV “thou hast burdened me with thy sins”, the NIV “you have burdened me with your sins”, and the NWT 1984 “you have compelled me to serve because of your sins”. Rashi says it means you have made me an attendant to the nations, the Shechinah returning from Babylon where it had gone to conquer the world under Nebuchadnezzar, so that they would not say he gave his children to an inferior nation. Radak says the sentence reverses things: I did not make you serve, and you made me serve; I bore your burden like a servant who cannot lighten his labor, and by justice I should have consumed you at once, but I bore it, for my own sake. Ibn Ezra says the Torah speaks in the language of human beings: because of your sins I became in the eyes of the nations like a servant with no strength, and so with “you have wearied me”.

Verses 25–28 · Isaiah 43:25–28 “I, I am he who blots out your transgressions” — a case to plead, “your first father sinned”, interpreters, and Jacob given to the ban

⭐ “I, I am he who blots out your transgressions for my own sake”. “I, I am he” (anokhi anokhi hu) stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at Isaiah 51:12, and it is the doubled “I” of verse 11 with “he” added. The verb machah is what is done to writing, to wipe out (dictionary), and it is the verb of David’s plea, “wipe out my rebellions” (Psalm 51:3, now on these pages). “Blots out your transgressions” (mocheh peshaekha) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. “For my own sake” is le-ma‘ani, and at verse 14 the sending to Babylon was le-ma‘ankhem, “for your sake”. Rashi says: neither in your merit nor in the merit of your forefathers; Radak says it is the doubling for emphasis, I am he who forgave the generation of the wilderness and forgive and blot out your sins in every generation, and “for my sake” means that my name not be profaned among you if I consume you. The KJV has “and will not remember thy sins”, the ASV “And I will not remember thy sins”, the NIV “and remembers your sins no more”, and the NWT 1984 “and your sins I shall not remember”.

⭐ “Remind me; let us be judged together; recount it yourself”. The courtroom of verse 9 returns, with Israel now in the dock. Hazkireni is “remind me”, the causative of zakhar, the verb of verse 18, and sapper atah is “recount it yourself”, the intensive stem of sapar, the verb of verse 21, “recount my praise”, and of the counted bones and recounted name of Psalm 22 (now on these pages). The KJV has “declare thou, that thou mayest be justified”, the ASV “set thou forth thy cause”, the NIV “state the case for your innocence”, the NWT 1984 “tell your own account of it”, the Douay “tell if thou hast any thing to justify thyself”, and the Geneva “count thou that thou mayest be justified”. Rashi says “remind me” means the reward I owe you and your forefathers; Radak, that if you say I have forgotten your righteous acts, remind me as one reminds a friend; Ibn Ezra, that if you say you did not sin against me while you were in your land and I exiled you for nothing, remind me.

⭐ “Your first father sinned, and your interpreters transgressed against me”. The first father is disputed. Rashi says Abraham, who said “how am I to know that I shall possess it?” (Genesis 15:8, now on these pages), and of the interpreters that there is none among all the intercessors in whose merit you trust in whom I have not found transgression; Radak says it is Adam, since man is shaped for sin, or on another reading Saul, the first king over all Israel; Ibn Ezra says it is Jeroboam, whom Israel chose as king without Jehovah, and that “father” can also mean a teacher, as “my father, my father, the chariot of Israel” (2 Kings 2:12) and “a father to Pharaoh” (Genesis 45:8), both now on these pages. “Your interpreters” (u-melitsekha) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible; the word is melits, as the interpreter between Joseph and his brothers (Genesis 42:23, now on these pages) (dictionary). The KJV, the ASV, the Geneva and the Douay have “thy teachers”, the NIV “those I sent to teach you”, and the NWT 1984 “your own spokesmen”. Radak says they are your princes and great ones, who should have rebuked the people; Ibn Ezra, ministers who spoke for the king, or Levites who spoke for the priests, or disciples.

⚠ “So I profaned the holy princes… Jacob to the ban”. The chapter ends on judgment. “The holy princes” (sarei qodesh) stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and 1 Chronicles 24:5, and Ibn Ezra says they are the priests. Cherem is the ban, a thing given over to destruction (dictionary): the NWT 1984 has “a man devoted to destruction”, the KJV “to the curse”, the ASV “a curse”, the NIV “to destruction”, and the Douay “to slaughter”. “Reviling” (gidufim) stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The tense of the first verb is also open: the KJV has “I have profaned”, the ASV “I will profane”, and the NWT 1984 “I shall profane”. Radak says the vav is vocalized so that it is not past, in every generation I profane the holy princes and give Jacob to the ban and Israel to reviling for their iniquities, but I do not consume them altogether, and the good perish through the iniquity of the evil.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “Do not be afraid”, as at 41:10 and 41:14; “I have redeemed you” and “your Redeemer”, as at 41:14; “created”, “formed” and “made” for bara, yatsar and asah; “the Holy One of Israel”; “your ransom” for kofer; “humankind” for adam in verse 4; “I am he”, as at Deuteronomy 32:39 and Isaiah 41:4; “my witnesses”; “no one who can rescue from my hand”, as at Deuteronomy 32:39; “savior”; “stranger” for zar; “a new thing”; “wilderness” for midbar and “wasteland” for yeshimon; “recount” for sapar in verses 21 and 26, as in Psalm 22; “made me serve” and “wearied” in verses 23 and 24; “blots out”, as at Psalm 51:3; “interpreters” for melitsim, as at Genesis 42:23; and “the ban” for cherem. The Hebrew of the chapter has no written and read pairs.

Echoes paid. “Do not be afraid” and “worm Jacob” (Isaiah 41:10, 14); the courtroom of the nations (Isaiah 41:1, 22); “my servant, Jacob, whom I have chosen” (Isaiah 41:8); “I am he” (Isaiah 41:4; Deuteronomy 32:39); “a voice calls: clear in the wilderness” (Isaiah 40:3); “I am with you” (Acts 18:10); the exiles told not to remember the former things (Ezra 1); “a chosen race… that you may proclaim” (1 Peter 2:9); “I will recount your name” and the counted bones (Psalm 22:23, 18); “wipe out my rebellions” (Psalm 51:3); “Jehovah has created a new thing” (Jeremiah 31:21); “Jehovah your God will turn your captivity” (Deuteronomy 30:3); the weary sacrifices (Isaiah 1:14); “how am I to know?” (Genesis 15:8); the interpreter between Joseph and his brothers (Genesis 42:23); and “my father, my father” and “a father to Pharaoh” (2 Kings 2:12; Genesis 45:8).

Echoes planted. “You are my witnesses” again (Isaiah 44:8); “I have blotted out, as a thick cloud, your transgressions” (Isaiah 44:22); “I have called you by your name” (Isaiah 45:4); the verbs of creating, forming and making of light and the earth (Isaiah 45:7, 18); “I am he… I will carry” (Isaiah 46:4; 48:12; 52:6); “I, I am he” (Isaiah 51:12); the wick and the new things of Isaiah 42:3 and 42:9, and the blind and the deaf of 42:18–20; the dry land that is glad in Isaiah 35:1; the north wind and the south of Song of Solomon 4:16; Sennacherib’s fall (2 Kings 19:9, 35); the river of Assyria (Isaiah 8:7); and “I am with you” in Jeremiah 1:8 and 46:28. None of these is translated here yet.

⚠ A note on order: Isaiah stands on these pages at chapters 1, 6, 7, 9, 11, 26, 40, 41, 43, 53, 55, 58 and 61. Isaiah 2–5, 8, 10–25, 27–39, 42, 44–52, 54, 56–60 and 62–66 remain open; next in this part of the book is Isaiah 42 — “Look, my servant, whom I uphold”: the first of the servant songs, a bruised reed he will not break, and a new song to Jehovah.
