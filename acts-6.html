‘In these days’ after chapter 5: the congregation is multiplying, and a complaint arises that the Hellenists’ widows are being overlooked in the daily service; the Twelve call the multitude together, seven men are chosen and set before the apostles, and the word of God keeps growing. Stephen, one of the Seven, then does wonders and signs among the people, is disputed by men of the synagogue of the Freedmen, and is brought before the Sanhedrin on the word of false witnesses.
Undated by the text beyond ‘in these days’ (v1) and following chapter 5: no interval is named between the chapters, and the verbs of vv7–8 are imperfect (the word ‘kept growing,’ Stephen ‘was doing’ wonders), so a stretch of time of unstated length lies between the choosing of the Seven (vv1–6) and the arrest (vv9–12). The chapter gives no year, and this library does not supply one.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verse 1 · γογγυσμὸς τῶν Ἑλληνιστῶν πρὸς τοὺς Ἑβραίους gongysmos tōn Hellēnistōn pros tous Hebraious — the Hellenists and the Hebrews of Acts 6:1: a grumbling in the wilderness’s word, and widows overlooked with no culprit named

⚠ Two groups, one word each — and the Greek names no language. Hellēnistēs is built on the verb hellēnizō, ‘to speak Greek, to live as a Greek,’ and Hebraios is ‘Hebrew.’ The noun Hellēnistēs stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Acts 9:29 and Acts 11:20, and at 11:20 the editions divide between ‘Hellenists’ and plain ‘Greeks’ (the apparatus: Tregelles reads Hellēnas). The noun Hebraios stands in three verses too: here, 2 Corinthians 11:22 (‘Are they Hebrews? So am I’) and Philippians 3:5 (‘a Hebrew of Hebrews’), neither yet on these pages. The usual reading takes the first group as Jews whose language was Greek and the second as Jews whose language was Hebrew or Aramaic, and the shelf prints that reading into the verse, where the Greek says neither ‘Jews’ nor ‘speaking’: NIV ‘the Hellenistic Jews… the Hebraic Jews,’ NWT 1984 ‘the Greek-speaking Jews against the Hebrew-speaking Jews,’ TLB ‘Those who spoke only Greek’ (and it adds ‘only’). Older versions keep the Greek and lose the Jewishness: KJV ‘the Grecians against the Hebrews,’ Douay ‘the Greeks against the Hebrews,’ where a modern reader hears the word for people from Greece; ASV ‘the Grecian Jews.’ This translation prints the two Greek words as English words and leaves the explanation here, because the verse gives none. The names of the men chosen at v5 are all Greek, which fits a complaint raised by the Hellenists; the text does not say the Seven were all of that group.

“Grumbling” is the wilderness word. Gongysmos stands in four verses of the New Testament: here; Philippians 2:14 and 1 Peter 4:9 (neither yet on these pages); and John 7:12, ‘much murmuring about him among the crowds.’ In the Greek Old Testament (Brenton’s text) it is the word of Israel’s grumbling over food at Exodus 16 — five times in four verses (7, 8, 9 and 12) — where this translation reads ‘Your grumblings are not against us but against Jehovah’ (16:8). Luke does not say he means the echo. The situation is near enough that the library sets the two side by side: a people being fed, and a complaint about the feeding. What the text does with this one is different: it calls none of the people a grumbler, says nothing of fault, and by the next verse the apostles have acted. See the dictionary entry. KJV, ASV and the Douay ‘murmuring,’ NWT 1984 ‘a murmuring arose,’ the 2013 revision ‘began complaining,’ NIV ‘complained,’ TLB ‘rumblings of discontent.’

Widows, “overlooked” — and no culprit named. Paretheōrounto is the imperfect passive of paratheōreō, ‘to look past, to look beside,’ and it stands in this verse and nowhere else in the New Testament. The imperfect makes it a continuing thing, ‘were being overlooked,’ and the passive names no doer. KJV, ASV and the Douay ‘neglected,’ NIV and NWT ‘overlooked,’ and the TLB adds a motive and an amount the Greek does not state: ‘being discriminated against,’ and ‘not being given as much food… as the widows who spoke Hebrew.’ Chapter 4 reported that ‘neither was there anyone needy among them’ (4:34), and this is the first time in Acts that a member of the community is said to be missed. The word for widow (chēra) is the one the Torah’s triad of the stranger, the orphan and the widow is carried into Greek with. Kathēmerinos, ‘daily,’ stands in this verse only.

The chapter opens and closes on one root. The verb plēthynō, ‘to multiply,’ begins the verse (‘as the disciples were multiplying’), and its noun plēthos, ‘multitude,’ is what the Twelve call together at v2 and what chooses at v5; at v7 the verb returns, ‘was being multiplied.’ Four of the chapter’s first seven verses carry the root. Growth is the cause of the grumbling here, and, at v7, its outcome.

Verses 2–4 · διακονεῖν τραπέζαις … προσκαρτερήσομεν diakonein trapezais … proskarterēsomen — ‘serve tables’ in Acts 6:2: the Twelve, one root for the tables and for the word, and seven men ‘of good testimony’

⚠ One root serves the tables and the word. The Greek has diakonia in ‘the daily service’ of v1, diakonein in ‘to serve tables’ (v2), and diakonia again in ‘the service of the word’ (v4): the same root three times in four verses, applied first to food and then to preaching. The apostles do not set a low word against a high one; they divide two kinds of one work. The noun is also Luke’s word for the Twelve’s own office at 1:17 and 1:25, where this translation prints ‘ministry’; it prints ‘service’ here so that the three occurrences stay visibly one word. Each of the seven English versions on the shelf prints two different English words for the two ends of the one Greek noun: KJV ‘ministration’ at v1 and ‘ministry’ at v4, NIV ‘distribution’ at v1 and ‘ministry of the word’ at v4, NWT ‘distribution’ and ‘ministry of the word,’ TLB ‘distribution’ and ‘preaching, and teaching.’

⚠ The Seven are not called deacons here — or anywhere in Acts. The noun diakonos, the root of ‘deacon,’ does not stand in Acts at all. The verse says what the seven are to do (‘serve tables,’ ‘over this need’) and gives them no title. The title comes from later readers: Irenaeus, in Against Heresies, calls Stephen ‘chosen the first deacon by the apostles’ (3.12.10) and Nicolas ‘one of the seven first ordained to the diaconate by the apostles’ (1.26.3), in the Roberts–Rambaut translation. Whether the Seven are the origin of the office, a single commission, or something else is argued; the library reports it and does not decide.

“Tables” — meals or money? Trapeza is a dining table, and in the New Testament also a banker’s: the money that ought to have been put on one (Luke 19:23, ‘in the bank’ in this translation), and the hand of the betrayer ‘on the table’ (Luke 22:21). ‘Serve tables’ could mean the meal or the funds that were laid ‘at the feet of the apostles’ (4:35). The Greek does not say, and v1’s widows point to food. The KJV, ASV and Douay keep the Greek’s own phrase, ‘serve tables’; the NIV says ‘wait on tables,’ the NWT 1984 puts the food in square brackets (‘distribute [food] to tables’), and the TLB settles it: ‘administering a feeding program.’

“It is not pleasing” — and the saying that ‘pleased’ at v5. Areston, ‘pleasing, acceptable,’ stands in four verses of the New Testament: John 8:29 (‘I always do what pleases him’), here, Acts 12:3 (of what pleased the Jews) and 1 John 3:22 (the last not yet on these pages). The verb of the same root is v5’s ēresen, ‘it pleased’: what is not pleasing at v2 gives way to what pleases the whole multitude at v5. KJV and the Douay ‘It is not reason,’ ASV ‘It is not fit,’ NIV ‘It would not be right,’ NWT 1984 ‘It is not pleasing’ and, in the 2013 revision, ‘not right.’ This translation keeps ‘pleasing’ so the one root can be seen twice. And v2 is the only place in Acts where the group is called hoi dōdeka, ‘the Twelve’: Matthias was numbered with ‘the eleven’ at 1:26, and Peter still stood ‘with the eleven’ at 2:14.

“Of good testimony,” “full of Spirit and wisdom,” and over “this need.” Martyroumenous is the passive of martyreō: men with a witness borne to them, whom others vouch for. In Acts the passive stands of these seven, of Cornelius (10:22), of Timothy (16:2, in the imperfect) and of Ananias of Damascus (22:12, not yet on these pages). The qualifications are a reputation and two gifts; no skill with food or money is named. KJV and Geneva ‘of honest report,’ ASV ‘of good report,’ the Douay ‘of good reputation,’ NWT 1984 ‘certified men’ and 2013 ‘reputable men,’ NIV ‘known to be full of the Spirit and wisdom,’ TLB ‘well thought of by everyone.’ Chreia, ‘need,’ is the word of 2:45 and 4:35, where goods were distributed ‘as anyone had need’; the ‘business’ of the KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva is the need those verses described, and the NIV has ‘responsibility.’ ⚠ Three small readings stand in the apparatus here. At ‘Now, brothers’ the printed Greek has de (Westcott–Hort and Nestle-Aland 28), where Tregelles and the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine text have oun, ‘therefore’: KJV ‘Wherefore,’ ASV ‘therefore.’ At ‘Spirit’ the Byzantine text adds hagiou, ‘holy’: KJV, Douay and Geneva ‘the Holy Ghost,’ ASV and NIV ‘the Spirit.’ And ‘we will appoint’ (katastēsomen, future) is katastēsōmen, ‘we may appoint,’ in the Byzantine text: KJV and ASV ‘we may appoint,’ NIV ‘We will turn this responsibility over to them.’ This translation follows the printed text at all three.

“We will devote ourselves” — the verb of the first believers. Proskartereō, ‘to stay at, to hold fast to,’ is Luke’s verb for the congregation itself: the eleven and the women ‘devoting themselves’ to prayer (1:14), the new believers ‘devoting themselves’ to the teaching, the fellowship, the breaking of the bread and the prayers (2:42), and the daily presence in the temple (2:46). The verb stands in ten verses of the New Testament, six of them in Acts, and here the apostles say they will do again what the whole congregation had been doing, with prayer again first. Luke’s preface called the first witnesses ‘servants of the word’ (Luke 1:2), in another Greek word (hypēretēs) but the same phrase. KJV and the Douay ‘give ourselves continually,’ ASV ‘continue stedfastly,’ NIV ‘give our attention to,’ NWT 1984 ‘devote ourselves,’ TLB ‘spend our time.’

Verse 5 · Στέφανον … Νικόλαον προσήλυτον Stephanon … Nikolaon prosēlyton — the Seven of Acts 6:5: seven Greek names, a proselyte from Antioch, and a man whose name means ‘crown’

⚠ Seven names, and all seven are Greek. Stephanos, Philippos, Prochoros, Nikanōr, Timōn, Parmenas, Nikolaos: no Hebrew or Aramaic name among them, and Prochorus, Nicanor, Timon, Parmenas and Nicolaus each stand in one verse of the New Testament, this one. That fits a complaint raised by the Hellenists, and readers have taken the Seven as chosen from that group. The text does not say so, and it does not say the Seven served only the Hellenists’ widows: it says the multitude chose these men for ‘this need.’ A Greek name is not decisive by itself: two of the Twelve, Philip and Andrew, have them, and it is Philip the Greeks approach at John 12:21. See the Seven.

Stephen — ‘crown’ — first on the list and the only one described. Stephanos is the ordinary Greek word for a wreath, the victor’s laurel (dictionary), and the word of the ‘crown of life’ promised at Revelation 2:10, already on these pages. Luke makes nothing of the meaning, here or when Stephen is killed, and the library does not say he did. He is ‘full of faith and Holy Spirit,’ and the only other person Acts calls full of Holy Spirit and faith, in the reverse order, is Barnabas (11:24) — the Barnabas whose field and money opened chapter 4’s ending. KJV ‘a man full of faith and of the Holy Ghost,’ ASV and NIV ‘a man full of faith and of the Holy Spirit,’ NWT ‘a man full of faith and holy spirit’ (the lower case follows that version’s teaching that the holy spirit is God’s active force and not a person), TLB ‘a man unusually full of faith and the Holy Spirit.’

Philip — the one who is not the apostle. The Philip of this list is the man Acts calls ‘Philip the evangelist, who was one of the seven’ when Paul stays at his house in Caesarea (21:8, not yet on these pages), and whom the book follows in Acts 8. He is not the Philip of John’s Gospel (John 1:43), and not Philip the tetrarch.

Nicolaus — the one proselyte named in the New Testament. Prosēlytos is ‘one who has come over,’ a Gentile who became a Jew. It stands in four verses of the New Testament: Matthew 23:15 (‘to make one convert’ in this translation’s English there), Acts 2:11 (proselytes in the Pentecost crowd), here, and Acts 13:43. Nicolaus is the only individual called one, and the only one of the Seven whose city is given: Antioch (Antioch on the Orontes). KJV, ASV, NWT and the Douay keep ‘a proselyte of Antioch,’ the NIV has ‘a convert to Judaism,’ and the TLB ‘a Gentile convert to the Jewish faith, who had become a Christian,’ a clause the Greek does not have. The KJV, Douay, Geneva and NIV spell the name ‘Nicolas’; the ASV has ‘Nicolaus,’ as this translation does. ⚠ Revelation hates ‘the works of the Nicolaitans’ (2:6) and names ‘the teaching of the Nicolaitans’ (2:15), both already on these pages. The name is formed from Nikolaos, and Irenaeus wrote that ‘the Nicolaitanes are the followers of that Nicolas who was one of the seven first ordained to the diaconate by the apostles’ (Against Heresies 1.26.3, Roberts–Rambaut). Acts says nothing of the kind of this Nicolaus, and Revelation names no founder; the library reports the connection and does not adopt it.

“The saying pleased the whole multitude” — and the multitude chose. The apostles asked the multitude to look out for the men and said they would appoint them (v3); the verb here, exelexanto, is ‘they chose for themselves,’ and its subject is the whole multitude; the next verse leaves the appointing itself without a named subject. It is the verb Luke uses of Jesus choosing the Twelve (Luke 6:13), and here the congregation does it. At 1:23 the congregation ‘put forward’ two men and a lot decided (1:26); here it chooses seven and no lot is cast. KJV and ASV ‘the saying pleased the whole multitude,’ NIV ‘This proposal pleased the whole group,’ NWT 1984 ‘the thing spoken was pleasing to the whole multitude,’ the Douay ‘the saying was liked by all the multitude,’ TLB ‘This sounded reasonable to the whole assembly.’ ‘Saying’ is logos, the ‘word’ of v2, v4 and v7, here a proposal; this translation keeps ‘the saying’ so the other three are not blurred into it.

Verse 6 · προσευξάμενοι ἐπέθηκαν αὐτοῖς τὰς χεῖρας proseuxamenoi epethēkan autois tas cheiras — ‘they laid their hands on them’: who did it, and the Joshua passages it echoes

⚠ Who laid on the hands? The sentence has one grammatical subject all the way through: ‘whom they set before the apostles, and, having prayed, they laid their hands on them.’ The ‘they’ of v5 is the multitude, and the apostles appear in the verse only as the people the Seven were set before. Readers have taken the hands as the apostles’ — NIV ‘who prayed and laid their hands on them,’ NWT 1984 ‘after having prayed, these laid their hands upon them,’ TLB ‘who prayed for them and laid their hands on them in blessing’ — or left it as open as the Greek: KJV ‘when they had prayed, they laid their hands on them,’ ASV ‘they laid their hands upon them,’ the Douay ‘they praying, imposed hands upon them.’ This translation keeps it open. Prayer comes before the hands, and the same two actions in the same order come again when Barnabas and Saul are sent out, after fasting (13:3).

The hands of Joshua. The Greek Old Testament uses this verb and noun for the commissioning of Joshua. In Brenton’s Greek of Numbers 27:18 Moses is told to take Joshua, ‘a man who has a spirit in him,’ and lay his hands on him (epithēseis tas cheiras sou ep’ auton); at 27:23 ‘he laid his hands on him’ (epethēke tas cheiras autou ep’ auton), where Acts has epethēkan. And Deuteronomy ends the story: ‘Joshua son of Nun was full of the spirit of wisdom, for Moses had laid his hands on him’ (Deuteronomy 34:9; the Greek has ‘spirit of understanding,’ pneuma syneseōs, where the Hebrew has ‘wisdom’). In Luke’s chapter the two halves are spread across the verses: the Seven are to be ‘full of Spirit and wisdom’ (v3), and then hands are laid (v6). The Hebrew verb is samakh, ‘to lean, to press’ (dictionary); the Greek verb, epitithēmi, is only ‘to put on.’ Luke does not quote Joshua or say the commissioning is modeled on his, and he does not say anything was conferred at v6: the Spirit has been named twice already, at vv3 and 5, before any hands. The library sets the passages side by side.

Verse 7 · ὁ λόγος τοῦ θεοῦ ηὔξανεν ho logos tou theou ēuxanen — ‘the word of God kept growing’: the first report in Acts where the word itself grows, and priests who obeyed the faith

⚠ The word is the subject. Until now Acts has counted growth in people (2:41, 5:14). Here the thing that grows is the word itself: ho logos tou theou ēuxanen, ‘the word of God kept growing,’ in the imperfect. Auxanō with ‘the word’ as its subject returns at 12:24 and 19:20 (the second not yet on these pages), and 12:24 repeats both verbs of this verse, ēuxanen and eplēthyneto, ‘was being multiplied,’ as the closing summary of its section. The two verbs are the Greek Old Testament’s pair for the first blessing and for Israel in Egypt: auxanesthe kai plēthynesthe, ‘grow and multiply,’ where this translation has the Hebrew ‘be fruitful and multiply’ (Genesis 1:28), and ēuxēthēsan kai eplēthynthēsan at Exodus 1:7, where the sons of Israel ‘were fruitful, and swarmed, and multiplied’ in this translation. (The Greek is Brenton’s text.) Stephen will use the pair of Israel in Egypt in his speech (7:17). KJV and ASV ‘the word of God increased,’ NIV ‘So the word of God spread,’ NWT 1984 ‘the word of God went on growing,’ TLB ‘God’s message was preached in ever-widening circles,’ which turns a word that grows into a message that is carried.

“A great crowd of the priests” — from the order that has already arrested the apostles. The priests came upon Peter and John with the captain of the temple at 4:1, and the high priest and his party laid hands on the apostles at 5:18. Here a great crowd of priests, hiereis, not the chief priests, archiereis, ‘were obeying the faith.’ The verb is hypēkouon, ‘were obeying,’ in the imperfect, and its object is tē pistei, ‘the faith’ — the faith as something one can obey (compare Paul’s ‘obedience of faith,’ Romans 1:5, already on these pages). KJV ‘a great company of the priests were obedient to the faith,’ the Douay ‘a great multitude also of the priests obeyed the faith,’ NIV ‘a large number of priests became obedient to the faith,’ NWT 1984 ‘a great crowd of priests began to be obedient to the faith,’ TLB ‘many of the Jewish priests were converted too.’

“Of the Lord” in one version. The Douay alone on this shelf reads ‘the word of the Lord,’ where the Greek printed here and the other versions read ‘of God’; the apparatus lists no variant at this verse.

Verses 8–10 · πλήρης χάριτος … Λιβερτίνων … οὐκ ἴσχυον ἀντιστῆναι plērēs charitos … Libertinōn … ouk ischyon antistēnai — Stephen ‘full of grace’ or ‘full of faith’ in Acts 6:8, the synagogue of the Freedmen, and a promise from Luke 21 kept

⚠ ‘Full of grace’ — or ‘full of faith’? The printed Greek reads charitos, ‘grace’ (Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle-Aland 28); the Robinson–Pierpont Byzantine text reads pisteōs, ‘faith,’ which is what v5 already called him full of. KJV and Geneva ‘full of faith and power,’ ASV ‘full of grace and power,’ the Douay ‘full of grace and fortitude,’ NIV ‘full of God’s grace and power,’ NWT 1984 ‘full of graciousness and power’ (the 2013 revision ‘divine favor’), and the TLB a third wording, ‘so full of faith and the Holy Spirit’s power.’ Stephen is called plērēs, ‘full,’ at v5, here, and in his last scene at 7:55 (‘full of Holy Spirit’), and the plural of the same adjective is what v3 asked of all seven.

Wonders and signs — in the order the book uses of Moses. Acts uses the pair in two orders. Sēmeia kai terata, ‘signs and wonders,’ stands in four verses of Acts: 4:30, 5:12, 14:3 and 15:12. Terata kai sēmeia, ‘wonders and signs,’ stands in three verses of the whole New Testament: 2:43, of the apostles; here; and 7:36, where Stephen says of Moses that he led the people out ‘doing wonders and signs in the land of Egypt.’ ⚠ The next verses charge Stephen with speaking against Moses; Luke does not draw the line, and Stephen is the first member of the congregation outside the Twelve of whom Luke says he does wonders and signs at all. KJV and Geneva ‘great wonders and miracles,’ ASV, Douay and NIV ‘wonders and signs,’ NWT 1984 ‘portents and signs,’ TLB ‘spectacular miracles.’

The Freedmen — a Latin word in Greek letters. Libertinoi is the Latin libertini, ‘freedmen,’ former slaves who had been set free, spelled out in Greek, and it stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. The Greek does not say whose freedmen, or freed where; the common explanation is Jews, or the descendants of Jews, enslaved abroad and freed, and the text is silent. KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva keep the Latin, ‘Libertines,’ which in English now says something else; the NIV and NWT translate it, ‘Freedmen’; the TLB calls it ‘the Jewish cult of “The Freedmen,”’ adding a word, ‘cult,’ that the Greek does not have: it says synagōgē.

One synagogue, or five? The Greek has one article for the whole list — tēs legomenēs Libertinōn kai Kyrēnaiōn kai Alexandreōn kai tōn apo Kilikias kai Asias — and so allows a single synagogue named for several groups, or several synagogues. The NIV prints the first (‘members of the Synagogue of the Freedmen… Jews of Cyrene and Alexandria as well as the provinces of Cilicia and Asia’); the ASV repeats ‘of the’ before each group (‘and of the Cyrenians, and of the Alexandrians’), which leans toward several. This translation keeps the Greek’s ambiguity. The places are not strangers on these pages: Simon of Cyrene carried the cross (Mark 15:21), and the Pentecost crowd included ‘Egypt and the parts of Libya around Cyrene’ and ‘Asia’ (2:9–10); Cilicia was Paul’s own province (Cilicia). Luke has not named Saul yet; he first appears at 7:58, and whether Saul was among the Cilicians in this argument, as some readers suggest, the text does not say. A Jerusalem synagogue inscription from before A.D. 70 names a priest, Theodotos son of Vettenos — a Latin family name — and a guest-house for visitors from abroad (the Theodotos inscription, as reported; it names no Freedmen, and it cannot be shown to be the building of this verse).

“They were not able to withstand” — a promise from Luke 21, kept. Antistēnai, ‘to stand against,’ is the verb of Luke 21:15: ‘I will give you a mouth and wisdom that none of your opponents will be able to withstand or contradict.’ The verse before it said they would be brought to synagogues and prisons (21:12), and the Spirit’s teaching is promised in Luke 12:12: ‘the Holy Spirit will teach you, in that very hour, what you must say.’ Here the two promises meet in one clause, ‘the wisdom and the Spirit with which he was speaking,’ and Luke 21:15’s verb is used. The infinitive antistēnai stands in four verses of the New Testament (Matthew 5:39, Luke 21:15, this verse and Ephesians 6:13), and in Luke’s two volumes it is these two. Notice what Luke says: they could not withstand; he does not say they were persuaded, and the next verse is what they do instead. ⚠ ‘The wisdom and the Spirit’ is one phrase and can be read as one gift, as the NIV does (‘the wisdom the Spirit gave him’), or as Stephen’s own spirit alongside his wisdom, as the TLB does (‘Stephen’s wisdom and spirit’); this translation prints ‘the Spirit’ because of vv3 and 5 and Luke 12:12. KJV ‘they were not able to resist the wisdom and the spirit by which he spake,’ ASV ‘they were not able to withstand the wisdom and the Spirit by which he spake,’ NWT 1984 ‘they could not hold their own against the wisdom and the spirit with which he was speaking.’

Verses 11–14 · ὑπέβαλον … μάρτυρας ψευδεῖς … καταλύσει τὸν τόπον τοῦτον hypebalon … martyras pseudeis … kataluset ton topon touton — the false witnesses of Acts 6:13: ‘this place,’ Moses, and a charge Luke kept out of Jesus’ own trial

⚠ “Secretly put forward” — one verb, stated once in the New Testament. Hypebalon is the aorist of hypoballō, ‘to throw under, to put up secretly’; it stands in this verse and nowhere else in the New Testament, and the under-prefix is what carries the secrecy. KJV, ASV and the Douay ‘suborned,’ the word English has kept for it; NIV ‘secretly persuaded,’ NWT 1984 ‘secretly induced’ (the 2013 revision ‘secretly persuaded’), and the TLB adds an act the Greek does not name, ‘to lie about him.’ The verbs that follow are all verbs of moving people: synekinēsan, ‘stirred up,’ stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and ‘the people’ it is used of are the people who until now have stood between the apostles and the authorities (4:21, 5:13, 5:26, and ‘favour with the whole people,’ 2:47): this is the first time in Acts that they are moved against a member of the congregation.

⚠ The charge, and where it was heard before. The Greek has martyras pseudeis, ‘false witnesses,’ and a saying about destroying a building. At Jesus’ hearing in Mark, ‘many were giving false testimony against him’ (Mark 14:56) and some ‘stood up’ to say, ‘We heard him saying, “I will destroy this sanctuary made with hands”’ (14:57–58); Matthew has ‘false testimony’ and ‘I am able to destroy the sanctuary of God’ (Matthew 26:59–61). Luke’s own account of Jesus’ hearing has no false witnesses and no sanctuary saying (Luke 22:66–71), and his Gospel has the word ‘give false testimony’ only in the list of commandments (Luke 18:20). The charge is here, in his second volume. Luke does give Jesus the prophecy of the stones ‘thrown down’ (Luke 21:6), in the verb of this verse’s ‘will destroy’ (kataluō): a prediction in the Gospel, an accusation in Acts. The wording also differs: Mark has ‘this sanctuary made with hands’ (naos), and the witnesses here have ‘the place’ (topos) twice, as ‘the holy place’ and ‘this place,’ and add ‘the customs.’ The saying’s own origin is at John 2:19, ‘Destroy this temple,’ where the Gospel adds that ‘he was speaking of the temple of his body’ (2:21). ⚠ Luke calls the witnesses false but does not say which part of what they report is. Stephen’s own speech will say that the Most High does not dwell in things ‘made with hands’ (7:48), the word Mark gave the witnesses. KJV and ASV ‘this Jesus of Nazareth shall destroy this place,’ NIV ‘will destroy this place,’ NWT 1984 ‘will throw down this place,’ and the TLB ‘will destroy the Temple and throw out all of Moses’ laws,’ replacing ‘place’ with ‘the Temple’ and ‘customs’ with ‘laws.’

Moses first, then God — and “the customs.” The first accusation is of ‘blasphemous words against Moses and God’ (v11), and Moses is named first; the second names the holy place and the law (v13); the third names the place again and the customs Moses ‘handed down to us’ (v14). The accusations name Moses (vv11, 14), the holy place (vv13, 14), the law (v13) and the customs (v14); the two things the witnesses say he will ‘destroy’ and ‘change’ are the place and the customs. Ethē, ‘customs,’ is the word of the first demand made of the Gentiles, ‘the custom of Moses’ (Acts 15:1). Paredōken hēmin, ‘handed down to us,’ is the verb and the pronoun of Luke’s own preface, ‘handed them down to us’ (Luke 1:2): Moses’s customs are ‘handed down to us’ in the same verb and pronoun as the account of Jesus in Luke’s preface. KJV ‘the customs which Moses delivered us,’ ASV ‘the customs which Moses delivered unto us,’ NIV ‘the customs Moses handed down to us,’ Douay ‘the traditions which Moses delivered unto us.’ Ho Nazōraios, ‘the Nazarene,’ is the title this translation has kept since 2:22 and 4:10; it stands in seven verses of Acts, one of them (24:5) in the plural, ‘the sect of the Nazarenes.’

Two small readings in the witnesses’ words. At v13 the printed Greek has lalōn rhēmata, ‘speaking words,’ and the Byzantine text has rhēmata blasphēma lalōn, ‘speaking blasphemous words’ — the same two words as v11, which the later reading repeats. The KJV reads ‘to speak blasphemous words against this holy place’; the Geneva ‘to speak blasphemous words against this holy place’ as well; the ASV ‘to speak words against this holy place’; the NIV ‘speaking against this holy place’; the NWT 1984 ‘speaking things against this holy place’; and the Douay ‘to speak words against the holy place.’ The second small reading is that last word: the printed Greek has ‘the holy place’ (tou topou tou hagiou), which is what the Douay prints, and Westcott–Hort and Nestle-Aland 28 add toutou, ‘this,’ as the other five print; the TLB has ‘the Temple.’ ‘Council’ for synedrion at v12 is the KJV, ASV, Douay and TLB (‘Council’); the NIV and NWT keep ‘Sanhedrin,’ as this translation does (Sanhedrin). The verb of v13, ‘set up’ (estēsan), is the verb of v6: the congregation set the Seven before the apostles; the Sanhedrin’s side sets false witnesses before the Sanhedrin — a verb this translation printed as ‘stood them’ at 5:27.

Verse 15 · ἀτενίσαντες … ὡσεὶ πρόσωπον ἀγγέλου atenisantes … hōsei prosōpon angelou — the face of an angel in Acts 6:15: a verb for staring, and a face Moses’s chapter has the words for

⚠ A verb for staring. Atenizō stands in fourteen verses of the New Testament: Luke 4:20 and 22:56, ten verses of Acts, and 2 Corinthians 3:7 and 3:13. It is what every eye in the synagogue at Nazareth did to Jesus when he sat down (Luke 4:20), and what Peter did to the lame man (3:4). Here it is what the whole Sanhedrin does to Stephen, and in his last scene it is what Stephen does at 7:55, when, ‘full of Holy Spirit,’ he stares into heaven. In 2 Corinthians 3:7 (not yet on these pages) it is what the sons of Israel ‘could not’ do to the face of Moses, for its glory, and at 3:13 it is what the veil was for. KJV ‘looking stedfastly on him,’ ASV ‘fastening their eyes on him,’ NIV ‘looked intently at Stephen,’ NWT 1984 ‘gazed at him’ (the 2013 revision ‘stared at him’), the Douay ‘looking on him.’

“Like the face of an angel” — and what the verse does not say. The comparison, hōsei prosōpon angelou, ‘as if the face of an angel,’ stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. It says what the council saw and gives them nothing to say about it. It does not say ‘shone,’ and it does not say Stephen’s face changed: the TLB has ‘Stephen’s face become as radiant as an angel’s,’ adding both a becoming and a radiance. The KJV and ASV ‘saw his face as it had been the face of an angel,’ the Douay ‘as if it had been the face of an angel,’ NIV ‘his face was like the face of an angel,’ NWT 1984 ‘his face was as an angel’s face.’ ⚠ The man was charged at v11 with speaking against Moses, and the library sets beside this verse a face in the Law that people were afraid to come near: ‘the skin of his face shone,’ and Aaron and all the children of Israel ‘were afraid to come near him’ (Exodus 34:29–30). Luke does not make the comparison, and the face of Stephen is called an angel’s, not Moses’s. The previous angel in Acts is the one who opened the prison doors (5:19) and sent the apostles back to the temple.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “grumbling” for gongysmos at v1, as “grumblings” at Exodus 16:8; “overlooked” for paratheōreō; “service” for diakonia and diakoneō at vv1, 2 and 4 (“ministry” at 1:17 and 1:25); “multiplying / multitude / multiplied” for the one root at vv1, 2, 5 and 7; “pleasing” for areston at v2 and “pleased” for ēresen at v5; “of good testimony” for martyroumenous; “need” for chreia at v3, as at 2:45 and 4:35; “devote ourselves” for proskartereō at v4, as at 1:14 and 2:42; “the saying” for logos at v5, against “the word” at vv2, 4 and 7; “proselyte” for prosēlytos; “set” for histēmi at vv6 and 13; “laid their hands” for epitithēmi at v6; “kept growing” and “was being multiplied” for the two imperfects of v7; “wonders and signs” in the Greek’s own order at v8, as at 2:43; “Freedmen” for Libertinoi; “withstand” for antistēnai at v10, as at Luke 21:15; “secretly put forward” for hypoballō; “false witnesses” at v13; “destroy” for kataluō at v14, as at Mark 14:58 and Matthew 26:61; “the Nazarene” for Nazōraios, as at 4:10; “customs” for ethē, as at 15:1; “Sanhedrin” for synedrion; and “staring” for atenizō at v15.

Echoes paid. “As anyone had need” — 2:45 and 4:35, and now “this need” at v3. “Neither was there anyone needy” (4:34) — and the first person in the book who is missed (v1). Devoting themselves to prayer — 1:14 and 2:42, now the apostles’ own word at v4. The Twelve — 1:26, named at last as “the Twelve” at v2. The congregation chooses — 1:23–26 put forward two and cast a lot; v5 chooses seven and casts none. Barnabas’s field (4:36–37) — the other person Acts calls full of Holy Spirit and faith. The wilderness grumbling — Exodus 16:8, at v1. Joshua’s hands — Numbers 27:18, 23 and Deuteronomy 34:9, at vv3 and 6. “Grow and multiply” — Genesis 1:28 and Exodus 1:7, in the Greek, at v7. The promise that none could withstand — Luke 21:15 and 12:12, kept at v10. The false witnesses and the sanctuary saying — Mark 14:56–58 and Matthew 26:59–61, at vv13–14. The people as shield — 4:21, 5:13 and 5:26, until v12. The Pentecost crowd — Cyrene, Egypt, Asia and the proselytes (2:9–11), at v9 and v5. The Nicolaitans — Revelation 2:6 and 15, at v5.

Echoes planted. Stephen, whose name means “crown,” and who is “full” again and stares into heaven at 7:55, and whose speech will say “wonders and signs” of Moses (7:36), “grew and multiplied” of Israel in Egypt (7:17) and “made with hands” of the temple (7:48). Saul, first named at 7:58, with Cilicia already on the stage at v9. Philip, who appears in Acts 8 and is called one of the seven at 21:8. Barnabas, full of Holy Spirit and faith at 11:24. Prayer and hands, together again at 13:3. The word that grows, back at 12:24 and 19:20. Hellēnistēs, back at 9:29 and 11:20. Diakonia, the word of the daily service and the service of the word, back at 11:29 and 12:25 for the relief sent to Judea. Cornelius, Timothy and Ananias of Damascus, each “of good testimony,” at 10:22, 16:2 and 22:12. Proselytes, back at 13:43. Alexandrians, with Apollos, “an Alexandrian by race,” at 18:24. “The sect of the Nazarenes,” at 24:5.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Who the Hellenists and the Hebrews are (v1) — a difference of language, as the usual reading has it, or of culture, which the Greek does not say. Whether “tables” are meals or money (v2). Whether the Seven are deacons, and what office, if any, they begin (vv2–6). Whether the Seven were all Hellenists, and served only the Hellenists’ widows (v5). Whether Nicolaus has anything to do with the Nicolaitans (v5). Who laid on the hands (v6), and what was conferred, if anything. Whether Luke means Joshua (vv3, 6). Whether “the Spirit” is the Holy Spirit or Stephen’s own spirit (v10). One synagogue or five, and who the Freedmen were (v9). Whether Saul was among the Cilicians (v9). Which part of the witnesses’ report was false (vv13–14). Whether the angel’s face recalls Moses’s (v15). And the manuscript questions at vv3, 8 and 13, each followed by the printed text and reported.

Next in this book: chapter 7 — the high priest’s one question, Stephen’s answer, which runs through Israel’s whole history, and what the council does with it.
