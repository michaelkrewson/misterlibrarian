Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–6 · אַל-תִּגְרַע דָּבָר al tigra davar — do not cut back a word, and the sanctuary nobody expected to be a precedent

⚠ The book has gone backwards in time again. The previous chapter was dated to the FOURTH year of Jehoiakim (25:1, 605 BC); this one is dated to the BEGINNING of his reign — 609/608 BC, three or four years earlier. Jeremiah's chapters are filed by theme and catchword rather than by calendar, the same jump already visible between 20 and 21. Reading this book in order is not the same as reading it in sequence.

“Do not cut back a word” (al tigra davar) — from gara, to diminish or subtract. The command is not “say it all” but the sharper negative: preach it without shaving anything off, which presumes there is something here a preacher would want to shave. KJV ASV both keep the arithmetic sense, “diminish not a word”; NIV reads “do not omit a word” and NWT “do not take away a word.” This translation keeps the subtracting force, because the next chapter of his life is the price of having obeyed it.

“Perhaps they will listen” (v3). Ulai — a genuine perhaps, not a rhetorical one. The commission is issued with its own outcome left open, and the reason given is the possibility that God will relent (nacham). This translation renders that root “relent” throughout, the choice already set at 18:8, because English “repent” now implies wrongdoing. The shelf splits three ways: KJV ASV “repent,” NIV “relent,” and NWT the frankest of all, “change my mind.”

⚠ “Like Shiloh” is the whole sermon, and it is an argument from archaeology. Shiloh was Israel's central sanctuary for the entire period of the Judges — the tabernacle and the ark stood there, Hannah prayed there, Samuel grew up there — and it was destroyed. Anyone standing in that temple court knew there had been a previous house of Jehovah, and knew there were ruins where it used to be. The sermon does not argue that God would abandon his own sanctuary in theory; it points north and says it has already happened once. The fuller version of this same sermon stands at Jeremiah 7:1-15, not yet on these pages; this chapter is its trial transcript.

Verse 6 carries a ketiv/qere: the consonants as written give an unusual spelling of “this,” and the vowels supplied read the ordinary form. This translation follows the read form, as it does wherever the two tracks diverge.

Verses 7–10 · וַיֵּשְׁבוּ בְּפֶתַח שַׁעַר-יְהוָה הֶחָדָשׁ va-yeshvu be-fetach sha'ar YHVH he-chadash — and they sat in the gate, which is what a court does

The arrest is unanimous, and that is the point. Priests, prophets, and “all the people” seize him — the professional religious establishment and the ordinary worshippers in the same sentence, with no faction dissenting. “You will surely die” (mot tamut) is the infinitive-plus-finite construction Hebrew uses for absolute certainty, the same shape as Genesis 2:17's warning in Eden.

⚠ The princes come UP from the palace to the temple, and then they SIT. Both details are legal. The Judahite palace stood lower on the ridge than the temple platform, so “came up” is topography, not deference. And sitting in the gate is what a court does — the city gate is the ancient Near East's courtroom, and to “sit” there is to convene as judges (Ruth 4:1-2; Deuteronomy 21:19). The narrative does not say “a trial began.” It says they came up and sat down, and a reader in that culture would know exactly what had just been constituted.

The New Gate appears again at 36:10, where Baruch reads the scroll from a chamber beside it — the same doorway, a few years later, hosting the reading of the very words this trial was about. That chapter is not yet on these pages.

Verses 11–16 · מִשְׁפַּט-מָוֶת mishpat mavet — a sentence of death, said once by the prosecution and once, negated word for word, by the court

⚠ The verdict is the charge with one word added. The priests move for conviction in three Hebrew words: mishpat mavet la-ish ha-zeh — “a sentence of death for this man” (v11). The court's ruling five verses later is the identical phrase with the negative particle in front: ein la-ish ha-zeh mishpat mavet — “there is no sentence of death for this man” (v16). Mishpat is the standard legal noun (a judgment, a case, a sentence), and keeping it as one fixed English phrase in both verses is the whole point: this is a formal motion answered by a formal acquittal in the same terms. The shelf mostly renders it as a judgment about the man's deserving rather than about the case — KJV “worthy to die,” ASV “worthy of death,” NWT “deserves the death penalty” — while NIV's “should be sentenced to death” is closest to the courtroom register. All of them preserve the exact reversal, which matters more than the wording.

Jeremiah's defence is not a defence. He does not deny the charge — he confirms it (v12: Jehovah sent me to prophesy against this house and this city), then repeats the offer that got him arrested (v13), then surrenders: “I am in your hand. Do to me as is good and right in your eyes” (v14). The only thing he argues is that killing him would cost THEM something.

“Innocent blood” (dam naqi, v15) is a legal category, not a sentiment: blood shed without cause creates a debt that attaches to the killers, to the city, and to its inhabitants — Deuteronomy 21:1-9 gives a whole ritual for a corpse whose killer is unknown, precisely to get the guilt off the community. NIV makes that explicit with “the guilt of innocent blood”; KJV and ASV leave it in the Hebrew's own compressed form, which this translation follows.

⚠ And the acquittal turns on a question of fact nobody in the room can settle. The court's stated reason is “he has spoken to us in the name of Jehovah our God” — that is, they rule he is a genuine prophet. But the priests and prophets were arguing the same category in the opposite direction, and Deuteronomy 18:20-22's own test for a false prophet is whether the thing happens, which is no help to a court sitting years before the outcome. The chapter records a capital case decided on an unfalsifiable question, and does not pretend the decision settled it.

Verses 17–19 · מִיכָה הַמּוֹרַשְׁתִּי Mikhah ha-Morashti — the only time one writing prophet is named and quoted by another

⚠ This is the single most remarkable citation in the Hebrew Bible, and it is a legal brief. The elders do not appeal to Jeremiah's character or to mercy. They argue PRECEDENT: a century earlier, Micah the Morashtite said something at least as offensive — “Zion will be plowed as a field” — and Hezekiah did not kill him; the king feared Jehovah instead, and Jehovah relented. The conclusion is drawn as a legal consequence: to do otherwise now would be “doing great evil against our own lives.” It is the only place in the Hebrew Bible where one writing prophet is quoted BY NAME by another, and the quotation is used in court to save a man's life. This translation's Micah 1 page had already flagged this passage from the other end; the verse quoted, Micah 3:12, is not itself on these pages yet.

⚠ The quotation is very nearly exact, and the elders are quoting from memory of a text. Micah 3:12 and this verse agree almost word for word in Hebrew, which tells us something the books themselves never state: that a hundred years after Micah preached, his words existed in a form stable enough for village elders to cite accurately in a capital trial. What the chapter treats as an ordinary courtroom move is also the earliest evidence in the Bible of a prophetic book being read as scripture.

And the precedent cuts a way the elders may not intend. Their case is that Hezekiah's repentance made the prophecy not happen — which concedes, in open court, that a true prophecy of Jerusalem's destruction can be spoken by a real prophet and still be averted. That is exactly the offer Jeremiah has just made twice (vv3, 13). The defence and the sermon are the same argument.

Verse 18 carries a ketiv/qere worth noticing: the consonants as written spell the LONGER form of the prophet's name, Mikhayahu (“Who is like Jehovah?”), while the vowels read the shorter Mikhah that his own book uses. The written and the read preserve both halves of the same name.

Verses 20–24 · אוּרִיָּהוּ בֶּן-שְׁמַעְיָהוּ Uriyahu ben Shema'yahu — the prophet who said the same thing and was extradited for it

⚠ The chapter ends by telling you what usually happened instead. Uriah son of Shemaiah prophesied “according to all the words of Jeremiah” — the same message, the same city, the same reign — and Jehoiakim had him hunted down. He fled to Egypt, and the king sent a named official, Elnathan son of Achbor, to bring him back: a state extradition across an international border to retrieve a preacher. Then Jehoiakim killed him personally with the sword and threw the body into the common graves rather than a family tomb, a deliberate final insult.

Nothing about the story is told to make Jeremiah's escape look inevitable. It is placed here, immediately after the acquittal, to say the opposite: the verdict could easily have gone the other way, and for another man saying the identical thing in the same years, it did. This is the only place in the book where a second prophet's execution is narrated in full, and the narrator spends five verses on a man who has no book and no other mention anywhere in scripture.

⚠ Elnathan son of Achbor turns up again, on the other side. At 36:25 he is one of three officials who urge Jehoiakim NOT to burn Jeremiah's scroll. The man who extradited one prophet to his death later argues for sparing another's words — the book records both facts about him and comments on neither. That chapter is not yet on these pages.

And the last word is a hand. The chapter closes on Ahikam son of Shaphan, whose “hand was with Jeremiah” so that he was not given “into the hand of the people” — the same noun twice, one hand against another. His father Shaphan is the scribe who read the recovered law-book aloud to Josiah (2 Kings 22:8-10); his son Gedaliah will be appointed governor over the survivors after Jerusalem falls, and will be the man Jeremiah is finally released to (39:14; 40:5-6). A single family shows up at the discovery of the law, at the rescue of the prophet, and at the administration of the ruins.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “do not cut back a word” (v2) for al tigra davar, keeping the subtracting sense rather than the softer “omit”; “relent” for nacham at vv3, 13, 19, the fixed rendering set at 18:8 and held against a shelf that splits between “repent” and “change my mind”; “a sentence of death” for mishpat mavet in both v11 and v16, so the acquittal is visibly the charge negated in the same words; and “innocent blood” (v15) left in the Hebrew's compressed legal form.

Echoes paid. Micah of Moresheth (v18) — the citation Micah 1's own note promised from the other end, now standing in its own chapter; nacham, “relent” (vv3, 13, 19) — stated as policy at 18:8-10, and here cited as a case where the policy actually operated, in court, as evidence; “rising up early and sending” (v5) — the same idiom that measured twenty-three unheeded years at 25:3; and Jehoiakim, whose scroll-burning and unpaid palace this book has already judged at 22:13-19, here shown doing the thing those oracles imply he would do.

Echoes planted. The New Gate (v10), waiting for chapter 36, where the scroll is read from a room beside it. Elnathan son of Achbor (v22), waiting for 36:25, where the same man argues against burning that scroll. Ahikam son of Shaphan (v24), waiting for his son Gedaliah, the governor Jeremiah is eventually handed over to. And the fuller temple sermon itself, Jeremiah 7:1-15, which this chapter is the trial for and which the book files sixteen chapters earlier — neither yet on these pages.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether this chapter and Jeremiah 7 are two accounts of one sermon or two sermons on one theme, the text does not say. Whether the acquittal turned on law or on the princes' own politics, it also does not say — it gives their stated reason and leaves the rest. And the trial's central question, whether a man speaking doom is a true prophet, is decided here by a court that cannot possibly know yet, which the chapter records without resolving.

This library already carries Jeremiah 29 and 31, both translated out of order; the sequential thread is still open here. Next in sequence: Jeremiah 27 — Jeremiah walks into the same temple wearing a wooden yoke on his own neck, telling Judah and five allied kings to submit to Babylon, and calling the prophets who promise otherwise liars to their faces.
