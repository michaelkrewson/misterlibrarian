📖 Dictionary

epitithēmi ἐπιτίθημι epitithēmi

TO PUT ON, TO LAY ON — epi, ‘on,’ plus tithēmi, ‘to put’; the Greek verb of laying on hands. ⚠ At Acts 6:6 the Seven are set before the apostles and, ‘having prayed, they laid their hands on them’ (epethēkan autois tas cheiras), the same two actions in the same order as Acts 13:3 (not yet on these pages). In Brenton’s Greek Old Testament it is the verb of Joshua’s commissioning: ‘you shall lay your hands on him’ (Numbers 27:18), ‘he laid his hands on him’ (27:23) and, in the same verb, ‘Moses had laid his hands on him’ (Deuteronomy 34:9), the Hebrew of which is samakh. The verb itself is ordinary and means only ‘to put on’: cloaks on a colt (Matthew 21:7), the crown of thorns on a head (Matthew 27:29; John 19:2), the cross on Simon of Cyrene (Luke 23:26). What the hands conferred at Acts 6:6, if anything, the verse does not say. Shelf at 6:6: KJV ‘they laid their hands on them,’ Douay ‘imposed hands upon them,’ NIV ‘laid their hands on them,’ TLB ‘laid their hands on them in blessing.’ ★ At Acts 8:17–19 the verb stands again, of Peter and John laying hands on the Samaritans and of Simon’s wish to lay hands himself; it is the verb of laying hands also at 9:12, 9:17, 13:3, 19:6 and 28:8 (not yet on these pages). → first discussed at Acts 6:6
