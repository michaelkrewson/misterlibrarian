📖 Dictionary

ektinassō ἐκτινάσσω ektinassō

TO SHAKE OUT, TO SHAKE OFF — ek-, ‘out,’ and tinassō, ‘to shake.’ ⚠ It stands in four verses of the New Testament, and three of them are the same gesture made at a refusal. Matthew 10:14 and Mark 6:11 tell the sent-out disciples, on leaving a house or town (Mark: a place) that will not receive them, to shake off the dust of their feet (Matthew ‘the dust of your feet,’ Mark ‘the dust beneath your feet’); at Acts 13:51 Paul and Barnabas do it against the people who drove them out, and go on to Iconium. The fourth is Acts 18:6, and it is the one that is different: the Jews at Corinth oppose and blaspheme, and Paul, ektinaxamenos ta himatia, ‘having shaken out his garments,’ says ‘Your blood be on your own heads. I am clean. From now on I will go to the nations.’ The Greek has the garments and no dust. Luke has a second verb for the same gesture, apotinassō, at Luke 9:5 (dust, from the feet), and uses it again of Paul and the viper at Acts 28:5. The shelf at 18:6 mostly keeps the garments (‘shook his raiment,’ ‘shook out his garments’); the NIV adds a motive, ‘in protest,’ and the TLB an object, ‘the dust from his robe.’ → first discussed at Acts 18:6
