📖 Dictionary

davaq דָּבַק davaq / yidbequ

TO CLING — to stick fast to something and not be parted from it. ⭐ It is the marriage verb: Genesis 2:24, already on these pages, is where a man leaves his father and mother and CLINGS to his wife, and Ruth 1:14 is where Ruth clings to Naomi when Orpah kisses her and leaves — so Ruth's loyalty to her mother-in-law is written in marriage language, a woman cleaving as a spouse cleaves. ⚠ Numbers 36:7 and 36:9 do something strange with it: in a chapter entirely about who may marry whom, the clinging that gets legislated is a man to a field — the children of Israel shall cling, each one, to the inheritance of the tribe of his fathers. ⚠ Only three of the thirteen versions consulted let a reader see that, because it takes one word used in both places: the ASV (‘cleave’ twice), the TNM 1987 («adherirse» twice) and the RV 1909 («allegarse» twice). The KJV gave English ‘cleave unto his wife’ and then declined to reuse it, printing ‘keep himself to the inheritance’; the NWT 1984 does the reverse, ‘cleave’ at Numbers and ‘stick to’ at Genesis. ⚠ The same three letters also make a hiphil meaning to overtake in a chase (1 Samuel 31:2, of the Philistines catching Saul), so a bare consonantal search returns pursuers as well as spouses. ⭐ Deuteronomy 4:4 is the first place this project has reached where the verb is aimed at God, though as a participle describing a state rather than as a command — 'you who cling to Jehovah your God are alive'. Deuteronomy 10:20 is where it is first COMMANDED: u-vo tidbaq, 'and to him you shall cling'. ⚠ The RV60 is the witness that loses it — where RV 1909 had «á él te allegarás», drawing near and attaching, the 1960 reads «a él seguirás», you shall FOLLOW him, which is done at a distance; ⭐ Deuteronomy 11:22 is the SECOND place it is commanded, one chapter later, and it is an escalation rather than a repetition: at 10:20 clinging was one of four things owed, while at 11:22 it is the last of three infinitives defining what keeping the commandment means, with a whole conditional promise hanging off it. ⚠ The RV60 loses it there in exactly the same way — «siguiéndole» where the RV 1909 had «á él os allegareis», and the same verse also gains a «solo» the Hebrew has not got. ⭐ Deuteronomy 13 is the third place the verb is aimed at God and the first place it is used at BOTH ends of one chapter, pointed opposite ways: 13:5 ends a list of six commands with u-vo tidbaqun, and to him you shall cling, and 13:18 closes the chapter with ve-lo yidbaq be-yadcha me'umah, and nothing of the banned thing shall cling to your hand. Those are its only two occurrences in the chapter, so the frame is the chapter's own. → first discussed at Ruth 1:14
