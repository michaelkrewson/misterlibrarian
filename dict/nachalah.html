📖 Dictionary

nachalah נַחֲלָה nachalah

An INHERITANCE — specifically a heritable landholding, ground that passes down a family and is not supposed to leave it. It is a word about TIME: a nachalah is the piece of the world your descendants will still be standing on. Distinct from chelek, the allotted share, which is about the moment of division rather than the generations after it. ⚠ Numbers 18:20 takes it away from the priests and then hands it back transformed: in their land you shall have no inheritance… I am your portion and your inheritance, the consolation built out of the two nouns just withdrawn. The same arrangement is restated at Deuteronomy 18:1-2 and carried out at Joshua 13:14 and 33 (neither yet on these pages), where the land is actually divided and Levi is actually skipped. It is also the word behind Naboth's refusal to sell his vineyard to a king — the nachalah of my fathers — which is why that story is a legal outrage and not a real-estate dispute. ⚠ Numbers 34:13-15 sets two verbs side by side and the difference is audible: the nine tribes get land Jehovah commanded ‘to GIVE’ them, while Reuben, Gad and half Manasseh ‘have TAKEN’ their nachalah — three times in two verses. Whether the text is passing judgment is genuinely open; that it changes verb is not. The NWT (both editions), the TNM 1987 and both Reina-Valeras keep the taking; the KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims, NIV, NVI and TNM 2019 all flatten it to receiving. ⭐ Numbers 36, already on these pages, is the word’s densest chapter and the one that tests what it is for: nachalah and its verb stand seventeen times in thirteen verses, and the whole argument is whether a heritable landholding can survive a daughter’s marriage. The chapter’s answer is a second law fitted around the first, and its two fencing verbs say what an inheritance must not do and must do — it may not go around from tribe to tribe, and a man must cling to it. ⚠ Deuteronomy 4:20, 38 (already on these pages) points the word in the opposite direction from every other use catalogued here: instead of a family holding LAND, Jehovah takes a whole NATION as his own nachalah — the identical noun this project has tracked describing what Israel receives now describing what Israel itself becomes. ⭐ Deuteronomy 10:9 widens Numbers 18:20 from one man to a whole tribe: the same two nouns, chelek and nachalah, are denied to Levi at large and the same consolation is restated in the third person — Jehovah is his inheritance. What was said to Aaron about himself has become the standing legal position of his tribe. ⭐ Deuteronomy 18:2 keeps the singular that 10:9 used — Jehovah is HIS inheritance — where eleven of the thirteen shelf versions pluralise it, and the two that do not, the NWT 1984 and the TNM 1987, are each abandoned by their own later revision. → first discussed at Num 18:20
