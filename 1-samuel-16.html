Jehovah sends Samuel, still mourning for Saul, to Jesse of Bethlehem with a horn of oil and a heifer to sacrifice. Jesse’s sons pass before him, beginning with Eliab, and Samuel is told “Jehovah sees into the heart”; the youngest, David, is fetched from the flock and anointed among his brothers, and the spirit of Jehovah rushes on him. The spirit of Jehovah turns away from Saul and an evil spirit from Jehovah terrifies him; his servants send for a lyre player, and David comes to court and becomes his armour-bearer.
After Samuel’s parting from Saul at Gilgal (1 Samuel 15:35); the anointing falls on the day of the sacrifice at Bethlehem (1 Samuel 16:5).

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · “How long will you mourn for Saul?” — the horn of oil, a king Jehovah has “seen to”, and the heifer

How long will you mourn. “How long will you mourn for Saul, when I have rejected him from being king over Israel?”: mitabbel, the verb on which the chapter before ended, “for Samuel mourned for Saul” (15:35, now on these pages). “Rejected” is ma’as, the verb of Samuel’s verdict at Gilgal, “he has rejected you from being king” (15:23, now on these pages), and it comes back in verse 7, said of the eldest son of Jesse. The ASV has “How long wilt thou mourn for Saul, seeing I have rejected him from being king over Israel?”; the TLB turns the question into a verdict, “You have mourned long enough for Saul”.

I have seen to a king for myself. “For I have seen to a king for myself among his sons”: ra’iti … li melekh, “I have seen … for me a king”. To see a thing for oneself is the Hebrew way of picking it out and providing it, and it is Abraham’s word on the mountain, “God will see to the lamb for himself” (Genesis 22:8, now on these pages). This chapter is about seeing. Samuel sees Eliab (v6) and is told what the human sees and what Jehovah sees (v7); Saul says “See to a man for me” (v17); and a servant answers “I have seen a son of Jesse” (v18). The Greek keeps the verb, heōraka, “I have seen”. The KJV has “I have provided me a king among his sons”; the NWT 1984 “I have provided among his sons a king for myself”; the NIV “I have chosen one of his sons to be king”.

The horn and the flask. “Fill your horn with oil”: qarnekha, from qeren, horn. Saul was anointed from “the flask of oil”, a pakh (10:1, now on these pages); David is anointed from “the horn of oil” (v13), and “the horn of oil” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at the anointing of Solomon (1 Kings 1:39, now on these pages). The flask comes back for Jehu (2 Kings 9:1, 3). The Talmud draws the line the verses leave unstated: “David and Solomon, who were anointed with oil from a horn, their kingship continued, whereas with regard to Saul and Jehu, who were anointed with oil from a pitcher, their kingship did not continue” (Megillah 14a, in the Koren–Steinsaltz English). The chapter names the vessel and says nothing more about it.

How can I go, and the heifer. “How can I go? Saul will hear, and he will kill me”: the prophet who made Saul king is afraid of him. Jehovah answers with a reason Samuel can give at the gate: “Take a heifer of the herd with you, and say, ‘I have come to sacrifice to Jehovah’”. Nothing he is told to say is false, for he does sacrifice (v5), but it is not the whole errand, and readers have long argued about it. One opinion in the Talmud builds a rule on it: “Rabbi Natan says: It is a mitzva to depart from the truth in order to preserve peace, as it is stated: ‘And Samuel said: How can I go, and Saul will hear and kill me, etc.’” (Yevamot 65b, Koren–Steinsaltz). The text gives the instruction and does not comment on it. The NWT 1984 has “A young cow of the herd you should take with you”; the KJV “Take an heifer with thee”; the Douay “a calf of the herd”.

Verses 4–5 · “Is your coming peace?” — the elders of Bethlehem tremble, and “sanctify yourselves”

The elders came trembling. “The elders of the city came trembling to meet him”: vayyecherdu, the verb of Isaac when he learned whom he had blessed, “Isaac trembled with a very great trembling” (Genesis 27:33), and of the camp at Sinai, “all the people who were in the camp trembled” (Exodus 19:16, both now on these pages). The chapter does not say why Bethlehem is afraid. The Greek has them “astonished” and adds a word to their question, “Is your coming peace, O seer?”. The ASV has “the elders of the city came to meet him trembling”; the KJV “the elders of the town trembled at his coming”; the Geneva “were astonished at his coming”.

Is your coming peace? “Is your coming peace?”: shalom bo’ekha, two words and no question word; the question is in the voice. The verb before them is singular, “and said”, though the elders are many; the Greek has “they said”. Samuel answers with the same word, “Peace”. The NWT 1984 has “Does your coming mean peace?”; the ASV “Comest thou peaceably?”; the TLB “What is wrong?”.

Sanctify yourselves. “Sanctify yourselves, and come with me to the sacrifice”: hitqaddeshu, Joshua’s word on the eve of the Jordan crossing, “Sanctify yourselves, for tomorrow Jehovah will do wonders in your midst” (Joshua 3:5), and again on the night before Achan was found (Joshua 7:13, both now on these pages). Then Samuel himself “sanctified Jesse and his sons”, so the family the choice will fall on is the family he has made ready. The Greek has “sanctify yourselves, and rejoice with me today”. The NIV has “Consecrate yourselves and come to the sacrifice with me”; the TLB “Purify yourselves and come with me to the sacrifice”.

Verses 6–7 · “Jehovah sees into the heart” — Eliab, the height of his stature, and what the human sees

Surely his anointed. “He saw Eliab, and said: ‘Surely his anointed is before Jehovah’”: meshicho, “his mashiach”, the title Samuel had used of Saul when he called “Jehovah and his anointed” to witness (12:3, now on these pages). The Hebrew says only that he “said” it, not to whom; it is the eldest son, and the first one he sees. Josephus says Samuel took Eliab for the king because he was “a tall and handsome man” (Antiquities 6.8.1, in Whiston’s numbering). The ASV has “Surely Jehovah’s anointed is before him”; the NWT 1984 “Surely his anointed one is before Jehovah”.

The height of his stature. “Do not look at his appearance, or at the height of his stature”: Saul had been chosen in a way the eye could check, “from his shoulders upward he was taller than all the people” (9:2; 10:23), and Samuel had shown him to Israel with “Do you see him whom Jehovah has chosen?” (10:24, all now on these pages). “For I have rejected him” is the verb of verse 1 again: the eldest son of Jesse is turned down in the word just used of the king. The KJV has “because I have refused him”; the NWT 1984 “for I have rejected him”.

What meets the eyes, and the heart. “For it is not as the human sees: the human sees what meets the eyes, but Jehovah sees into the heart.” The first clause has no subject in the Hebrew, ki lo asher yir’eh ha-adam, “for not as the human sees”, and the versions supply one: the ASV “for Jehovah seeth not as man seeth”, while the NWT 1984 brackets what it adds, “For not the way man sees [is the way God sees]”, and the Greek writes “God will see”. The rest is a matched pair, la-einayim, “to the eyes”, and la-levav, “to the heart”. The heart is the word of 13:14 (now on these pages), where Jehovah “has sought out for himself a man after his own heart”, kilvavo: the heart he sought is the heart he looks into. The New Testament’s title for God, kardiognōstēs, “knower of hearts”, says the same (Acts 1:24; 15:8, both now on these pages). The ASV has “but Jehovah looketh on the heart”; the NWT 1984 “but as for Jehovah, he sees what the heart is”; the TLB “I look at a man’s thoughts and intentions”.

Verses 8–11 · Seven sons, or eight? — Abinadab, Shammah, and the youngest left with the flock

Neither has Jehovah chosen this one. “Neither has Jehovah chosen this one”, twice; in the Hebrew, “also this one Jehovah has not chosen”. The verb is bachar, the word of 10:24 (now on these pages), “him whom Jehovah has chosen”. The ASV has “Neither hath Jehovah chosen this”; the NWT 1984 “Neither has Jehovah chosen this one”.

Seven, or eight. “Jesse made seven of his sons pass before Samuel”, and then the youngest is fetched, so here Jesse has eight sons, as 1 Samuel 17:12 (now on these pages) says outright, “he had eight sons”. The genealogy in 1 Chronicles 2:13–15 names seven and calls David “the seventh”. Josephus names six brothers, “Eliab was the eldest, Aminadab the second, Shammall the third, Nathaniel the fourth, Rael the fifth, and Asam the sixth”, and then David, seven sons in all (Antiquities 6.8.1). The readings differ, and this chapter names only three of the brothers. In Chronicles the third is called Shimea. The KJV has “Again, Jesse made seven of his sons to pass before Samuel”; the TLB “all seven of his sons presented themselves to Samuel”.

The youngest, with the flock. “Is that the last of the boys?”: hatammu ha-ne’arim, “have the boys come to an end?”. “There is still the youngest left, and look — he is shepherding the flock”: ha-qatan, “the small one”, the word Samuel used of Saul, “small in your own eyes” (15:17, now on these pages). Here it means the youngest, as at 17:14, “David was the youngest” (now on these pages), but it comes straight after a verse about height. Ro’eh ba-tson, “shepherding the flock”, is the work the tribes will name when they make him king: “you shall shepherd my people Israel” (2 Samuel 5:2, now on these pages). The ASV has “There remaineth yet the youngest, and, behold, he is keeping the sheep”; the NWT 1984 “The youngest one has till now been left out”.

We will not sit down. “For we will not sit down until he comes here”: lo nasov, “we will not go round”, usually taken as going round to sit at the meal; the Greek has “we will not recline”. The NWT 1984 has “we shall not sit down to meal until his coming here”; the ASV “we will not sit down till he come hither”.

Verses 12–13 · David anointed — “ruddy, with beautiful eyes”, the horn of oil, and the spirit of Jehovah rushing on him

Ruddy. “Now he was ruddy, with beautiful eyes, and good to look at”: admoni, from adom, red, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible. The first is Esau, “the first came out ruddy” (Genesis 25:25, now on these pages); the other two are David, here and when Goliath looks at him and despises him, “for he was a youth, and ruddy, with a beautiful appearance” (17:42, now on these pages). Whether the redness is hair or skin the Hebrew does not say. Whiston’s Josephus has “of a yellow complexion”. The ASV has “Now he was ruddy, and withal of a beautiful countenance”; the NIV “He was glowing with health”, turning a colour into a state.

Good to look at, after all. The verse after “Do not look at his appearance” describes David’s looks, his beautiful eyes and tov ro’i, “good of appearance”; the narrator does not pretend he was plain. Ro’i is the noun of Hagar’s name for God, “You are El Ro’i” (Genesis 16:13, now on these pages). The Masoretic text leaves an open paragraph break in the middle of this verse, after his looks and before Jehovah speaks. The NWT 1984 has “a young man with beautiful eyes and handsome in appearance”; the KJV “goodly to look to”.

This is he, and his name. “Arise, anoint him, for this is he.” The Hebrew has still not named him. The name David is first written in this book in verse 13, after the oil; before it, the same three letters, דוד, spelled only a cooking pot (2:14) and Saul’s uncle, dod (10:14–16; 14:50, all now on these pages). The Greek names him a verse sooner: “anoint David, for he is good”.

In the midst of his brothers. “Samuel took the horn of oil, and anointed him in the midst of his brothers”: in front of the family, where Saul was anointed alone, after the servant had been sent on ahead (9:27, now on these pages). Josephus makes this anointing private too: Samuel “whispered him in the ear, and acquainted him that God chose him to be their king” (Antiquities 6.8.1). The KJV has “anointed him in the midst of his brethren”; the NIV “anointed him in the presence of his brothers”.

Rushed on David, from that day onward. “And the spirit of Jehovah rushed on David from that day onward”: tsalach, the verb of the spirit on Samson (Judges 14:6, 14:19; 15:14) and on Saul (10:10; 11:6, all now on these pages). On Samson and Saul it came for a moment, a lion, a battle, a band of prophets; only here does the rushing carry “from that day onward”. The Greek has “leaped upon David”. The ASV has “the Spirit of Jehovah came mightily upon David from that day forward”; the NWT 1984 “the spirit of Jehovah began to be operative upon David”.

Verses 14–16 · “An evil spirit from Jehovah” — the spirit turns away from Saul, terror, and a man who can play the lyre

Turned away. “Now the spirit of Jehovah had turned away from Saul”: sarah, the verb with which the episode ends, “and the evil spirit turned away from him” (v23). One verse gives the spirit to David; the next says it has left Saul. The ASV has “Now the Spirit of Jehovah departed from Saul”; the NWT 1984 “And the very spirit of Jehovah departed from Saul”.

An evil spirit from Jehovah. “And an evil spirit from Jehovah terrified him”: ruach ra’ah, and the two words stand side by side in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at Judges 9:23 (now on these pages), where “God sent an evil spirit between Abimelech and the men of Shechem”. Ra’ah covers harm as well as wickedness, and the shelf divides between the two. The ASV has “an evil spirit from Jehovah troubled him”; the NWT 1984 “a bad spirit from Jehovah terrorized him”; the TLB “a tormenting spirit that filled him with depression and fear”. The verse says plainly that the spirit came from Jehovah, and does not explain it; readers have taken it as a demon, as a judgment, and as a sickness of mind.

Terrified, or choked. Bi’atattu, “terrified him”, from bi’et, the verb of Job’s complaint, “you terrify me through visions” (Job 7:14). The Greek has “choked him”, and Josephus takes up the same picture: “some strange and demoniacal disorders came upon him, and brought upon him such suffocations as were ready to choke him”, so that the physicians could think of no remedy but music (Antiquities 6.8.2). The servants use a different name from the narrator: verse 14 says “from Jehovah”, and they say “an evil spirit of God” (vv15–16). The KJV has “an evil spirit from God troubleth thee”; the NIV “an evil spirit from God is tormenting you”.

The lyre. “A man who knows how to play the lyre”: kinnor, the instrument of Jubal, “the father of all who play the lyre and the pipe” (Genesis 4:21), and one of the four the band of prophets carried when Saul met them, where this translation keeps the harp and the lyre apart (10:5, both now on these pages). Menaggen, “playing”, is the verb for striking strings, and the servants add “with his hand”. The Greek transliterates the word, kinyra. The NIV has “someone who can play the lyre”; the KJV “a cunning player on an harp”; the NWT 1984 “a skilled man playing upon the harp”.

Verses 17–23 · David plays for Saul — “Jehovah is with him”, the armour-bearer, and the spirit of God with no “evil”

See to a man for me. “See to a man for me, please, who plays well”: re’u-na li, Saul’s words in the shape of Jehovah’s in verse 1, “I have seen to a king for myself”. The king is looking for the same son of Jesse that Jehovah has already seen, and the servant has seen him too: “Look — I have seen a son of Jesse the Bethlehemite”. Joseph once gave Pharaoh advice in this shape, “let Pharaoh look out a man of discernment and wisdom” (Genesis 41:33, now on these pages). The ASV has “Provide me now a man that can play well”; the NWT 1984 “Provide me, please, a man doing well at playing”.

The servant’s list. “Who knows how to play, and a mighty man of valour, and a man of war, and discerning in speech, and a man of fine form; and Jehovah is with him.” Each piece has been heard before on these pages: gibbor chayil is what Kish was (9:1) and what Boaz was (Ruth 2:1); navon, discerning, is Joseph’s “a man of discernment”, and ish to’ar recalls Joseph “beautiful in form” (Genesis 39:6); and “Jehovah was with him” was said of the boy Samuel (3:19, all now on these pages). Two of the six are a soldier’s titles, and nothing in this book has yet shown the shepherd of verse 11 at war. The ASV has “prudent in speech, and a comely person”; the NWT 1984 “an intelligent speaker and a well-formed man”; the NIV “He speaks well and is a fine-looking man”.

Two ways David comes to Saul. This chapter brings David to court as a musician, loved and made armour-bearer. In the next, Saul watches him go out against the Philistine and asks “Whose son is this youth?”, and Abner cannot tell him (17:55–58, now on these pages). Readers differ: many see two accounts of how David came to Saul set side by side; others harmonize them, with a long interval between, or with Saul asking about the father rather than the son. The Greek as Brenton prints it, from the Vatican manuscript, has no 17:12–31 and no 17:55–58, the verses that give Jesse eight sons and Saul’s question.

A donkey-load of bread. “Jesse took a donkey-load of bread, and a skin of wine, and one kid of the goats”: the Hebrew says chamor lechem, “a donkey of bread”. The Greek read the same consonants, חמר, as chomer, a dry measure, “a gomor of loaves”. The kid is the gift of Judah’s pledge (Genesis 38:17) and of Samson’s visit to his wife (Judges 15:1, both now on these pages). The ASV has “an ass laden with bread”; the NWT 1984 reads a list, “an ass, bread and a skin bottle of wine”.

He loved him greatly. “And he loved him greatly, and he became his armour-bearer”: vaye’ehavehu, “and he loved him”, with no name on either side. Two versions name Saul as the one who loves: the NIV has “Saul liked him very much”, and the TLB “Saul admired and loved him”. The rest keep the Hebrew’s open subject: the ASV has “and he loved him greatly”; the NWT 1984 “he got to loving him very much”. It is the first love in David’s story; Jonathan (18:1), all Israel and Judah (18:16) and Michal (18:20) follow, all three now on these pages. Nose kelim, armour-bearer, is the post of the young man who climbed with Jonathan at Michmash (14:1, now on these pages). “He has found favour in my eyes” is chen, Noah’s word (Genesis 6:8, now on these pages).

The spirit of God, with no “evil”. “Whenever the spirit of God was on Saul”: the Hebrew of this clause is ruach Elohim, with no ra’ah, though the same verse ends with “the evil spirit”. The Greek adds the adjective, “an evil spirit”, and so do all four older English versions on the shelf, the KJV, the ASV, the Geneva and the Douay: the KJV has “when the evil spirit from God was upon Saul”; the Geneva “when the evil spirit of God came upon Saul”. Two versions keep the Hebrew bare: the NIV has “Whenever the spirit from God came on Saul”, and the NWT 1984 “when God’s spirit came to be upon Saul”; its own revision supplies the adjective, the NWT 2013 “Whenever a bad spirit from God came upon Saul”. This translation prints what the Hebrew prints, and the end of the verse says which spirit is meant.

Relief. “And Saul had relief, and it was good for him, and the evil spirit turned away from him”: ve-ravach, “and it was spacious”, “there was breathing room”, written with the same three letters as ruach, spirit, רוח. The verse sets the two side by side: a ruach is on Saul, ravach comes to him, and the evil ruach turns away. “It will be good for you” (v16) comes true in “it was good for him”. The ASV has “so Saul was refreshed, and was well”; the NWT 1984 “there was relief for Saul and it was well with him”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “I have seen to a king for myself” and “see to a man for me” for ra’ah li, as at Genesis 22:8; “rejected” for ma’as, of Saul and of Eliab alike; “the horn of oil”, where 10:1 has “the flask of oil”; “Is your coming peace?”; “sanctify yourselves”, as in Joshua 3:5; “the human” for ha-adam, and “sees into the heart”; “chosen” for bachar, as in 10:24; “ruddy” for admoni, as for Esau; “rushed on” for tsalach, as in 10:10 and 11:6; “turned away” for sar in verses 14 and 23; “an evil spirit” for ruach ra’ah, as in Judges 9:23; “terrified” for bi’et; “the lyre” for kinnor, as in Genesis 4:21 and 10:5; “a mighty man of valour” for gibbor chayil, as in 9:1; “a donkey-load of bread”; “armour-bearer”; “favour” for chen; and “the spirit of God” at verse 23, without the “evil” the Hebrew does not print.

Echoes paid. Samuel’s mourning for Saul (15:35) is answered with “How long?”; “a man after his own heart” (13:14) becomes the heart Jehovah sees; the flask of 10:1 gives way to a horn; the king “taller than all the people” (9:2; 10:23) is set against “do not look at the height of his stature”; Jesse, “the father of David” (Ruth 4:17), receives Samuel; the spirit that rushed on Saul (10:10; 11:6) rushes on David and leaves Saul; and the evil spirit of Judges 9:23 comes again.

Echoes planted. Goliath and the “ruddy” youth (1 Samuel 17:42); “Whose son is this youth?” (17:55–58); Jesse’s eight sons (17:12, all three now on these pages) and the seven of 1 Chronicles 2:13–15; Jonathan’s love (18:1, now on these pages); the evil spirit and the spear (18:10, now on these pages; again at 19:9); David fleeing to Samuel at Ramah (19:18, both now on these pages); the shepherd of Israel (2 Samuel 5:2, now on these pages); and the flask of oil for Jehu (2 Kings 9:1, 3). None of the others is translated here yet.

Next in this book: 1 Samuel 17 — David and Goliath: the champion of Gath in the valley of Elah, forty days of challenge, Jesse’s errand to the camp, Saul’s armour refused, five smooth stones, and “Whose son is this youth?”.
