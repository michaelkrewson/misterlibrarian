Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · “Under the yoke as slaves” — masters, and the verse in the slavery debate

⚠ “Let all who are under the yoke as slaves regard their own masters as worthy of all honor.” These verses were used to defend slavery. In 1841 the Virginia Baptist minister Thornton Stringfellow, in A Brief Examination of Scripture Testimony on the Institution of Slavery, quoted “Let as many servants as are under the yoke count their masters worthy of all honor” and turned the words of verses 3 to 5, “if any man teach otherwise,” against the abolitionists. The passage does not argue that slavery is right. It addresses slaves inside the Roman household as it stood, and it gives its reason: “so that the name of God and the teaching may not be blasphemed,” the concern of Romans 2:24. The same letter lists “slave-traders” among the lawless at 1 Timothy 1:10; Paul tells a slave who can become free to “rather make use of it” at 1 Corinthians 7:21, and sends Onesimus back “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother” at Philemon 1:16. The KJV reads “servants,” the NIV “All who are under the yoke of slavery,” and the NWT 1984 “slaves under a yoke.”

“Masters” and “those who receive the benefit.” Despotēs, “master, owner,” stands in ten verses of the New Testament; at Luke 2:29 and Acts 4:24 it is the name for God, “Master” (dictionary). “The yoke” is the word of “a yoke of slavery” at Galatians 5:1. ⚠ “Those who receive the benefit” is read two ways: the masters, who receive the benefit of the slaves’ good service, as the NWT 1984 reads, “those receiving the benefit of their good service”; or masters who devote themselves to doing good, as the NIV reads, “are devoted to the welfare of their slaves.” Euergesia, “benefit, good deed,” stands in two verses: here and “a good deed done to a sick man” at Acts 4:9. The KJV reads “because they are faithful and beloved, partakers of the benefit.”

Verses 3–5 · “Sound words” and a sick craving — “godliness as a means of gain”

“If anyone teaches a different doctrine and does not agree with sound words.” Heterodidaskaleō, “to teach a different doctrine,” stands in two verses, both in this letter: the charge of 1 Timothy 1:3 and this one, which close the letter as they opened it. “Sound” is the medical word of 1 Timothy 1:10, “the sound teaching”; its opposite comes in verse 4: the teacher is “sick,” nosōn. “Puffed up” is the typhoō of 1 Timothy 3:6 (dictionary). The KJV reads “doting about questions and strifes of words”; the NWT 1984 reads “being mentally diseased over questionings and debates about words.”

“Quarrels about words … constant friction … who suppose that godliness is a means of gain.” Logomachia, “a war of words,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, though its verb returns at 2 Timothy 2:14 (dictionary); diaparatribē, “constant rubbing, friction,” stands in one verse too. Porismos, “a means of gain,” stands in two verses, verses 5 and 6 here, and the writer turns the word: godliness is not a way to make money, but godliness with contentment is itself great gain (dictionary). The Byzantine text adds a command, and so the KJV: “supposing that gain is godliness: from such withdraw thyself.” The ASV reads “supposing that godliness is a way of gain.”

Verses 6–10 · “Godliness with contentment” — “the love of money is a root of all evils”

“But godliness with contentment is great gain.” Autarkeia, “contentment, self-sufficiency,” stands in two verses: here and “complete sufficiency in everything” at 2 Corinthians 9:8; its adjective is Paul’s “I myself have learned, in whatever circumstances I am, to be self-sufficient” at Philippians 4:11 (dictionary). In Greek moral teaching it was the Stoic and Cynic ideal of needing nothing outside oneself; here it rests on God. The KJV and NIV read “godliness with contentment is great gain”; the NWT 1984 reads “godly devotion along with self-sufficiency.”

“For we brought nothing into the world, because neither can we carry anything out.” The thought is Job’s at Job 1:21, “Naked I came out of my mother’s womb, and naked I shall return there.” The Greek joins the two halves with a puzzling “because”; the Byzantine text reads “it is plain that,” and so the KJV: “and it is certain we can carry nothing out.” The ASV keeps the harder text, “for neither can we carry anything out.” “Food and covering” are diatrophē and skepasma, the second either clothing or shelter, and the first stands in one verse of the New Testament. The NWT 1984 reads “having sustenance and covering.”

“Those who want to be rich fall into temptation and a snare.” “Snare” is the word of “the snare of the devil” at 1 Timothy 3:7, and the Vulgate adds “of the devil” here too, and so the Douay: “into the snare of the devil.” Bythizō, “to plunge, to sink,” stands in two verses: the boats so full of fish that they “began to sink” at Luke 5:7, and the people sunk here. Jesus’ “How hard it will be for those who have wealth to enter the kingdom of God!” is at Mark 10:23; “the deceitfulness of riches” is at Mark 4:19. The KJV reads “they that will be rich fall into temptation and a snare”; the NIV reads “Those who want to get rich.”

⚠ “For the love of money is a root of all evils.” The verse is often shortened to “money is the root of all evil,” which drops two of its words. The subject is philargyria, “the love of money,” a noun found in one verse of the New Testament; its adjective is the Pharisees “who loved money” at Luke 16:14 (dictionary). And “root” has no article in Greek: “a root,” one of the roots, though readers also take the Greek as “the root,” and “of all evils” as “of all kinds of evils.” The KJV reads “the love of money is the root of all evil”; the ASV reads “the love of money is a root of all kinds of evil”; the Douay, after the Vulgate’s cupiditas, reads “the desire of money is the root of all evils.” Polycarp, writing to the Philippians early in the second century, opens his fourth chapter with the line: “But the love of money is the root of all evils.” “Reaching for it” is the verb of the man who “aspires” to oversight at 1 Timothy 3:1. Peripeirō, “to pierce through,” stands in one verse of the New Testament.

Verses 11–12 · “Man of God” — “fight the good fight of the faith”

“But you, O man of God, flee these things.” “Man of God” is the title of Moses at Deuteronomy 33:1, “Moses, the man of God,” and of the prophets after him; the address “O man” is Paul’s at Romans 9:20; it returns at 2 Timothy 3:17. The list of virtues ends with praupathia, “gentleness,” found in one verse of the New Testament, where the Byzantine text has the common praotēs (dictionary). The KJV reads “follow after righteousness, godliness, faith, love, patience, meekness”; the NWT 1984 reads “endurance, mildness of temper.”

“Fight the good fight of the faith; take hold of the eternal life.” The Greek is the athlete’s, not the soldier’s: agōnizou ton kalon agōna, “contend in the good contest,” as in “Everyone who competes exercises self-control in all things” at 1 Corinthians 9:25, and as against “the good warfare” of 1 Timothy 1:18, a soldier’s word. “The good confession before many witnesses” is read of Timothy’s baptism, of his ordination with the elders’ hands at 1 Timothy 4:14, or of a trial before authorities. The KJV reads “Fight the good fight of faith” and “hast professed a good profession”; the NWT 1984 reads “Fight the fine fight of the faith.”

Verses 13–16 · “The good confession before Pontius Pilate” — “the King of those who reign,” “unapproachable light”

“Before God, who keeps all things alive, and Christ Jesus, who testified the good confession before Pontius Pilate.” Zōogoneō, “to keep alive,” stands in three verses: “whoever loses it will keep it alive” at Luke 17:33, the infants exposed “so that they would not be kept alive” at Acts 7:19, and this one; the Byzantine text has zōopoieō, “to give life,” and so the KJV, “who quickeneth all things,” where the NWT 1984 reads “who preserves all things alive.” ⚠ Epi Pontiou Pilatou is read as “before Pontius Pilate,” the trial at which Jesus said “for this I have come into the world: to testify to the truth” at John 18:37, or as a date, in Pilate’s time. The Douay, after the Vulgate’s sub, reads “under Pontius Pilate,” the wording the creeds keep. Outside the Gospels Pilate is named at Acts 3:13, Acts 4:27 and Acts 13:28, and among the letters only here.

“Spotless, above reproach, until the appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ.” “Spotless” is aspilos, “unspotted from the world” at James 1:27 and the lamb “without blemish or spot” at 1 Peter 1:19; “above reproach” is the overseer’s word at 1 Timothy 3:2 (dictionary). “Appearing” is epiphaneia, as in “the appearing of his presence” at 2 Thessalonians 2:8 (dictionary). “At the proper times” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 2:6. The ASV reads “without spot, without reproach, until the appearing of our Lord Jesus Christ.”

⚠ “The blessed and only Sovereign, the King of those who reign and Lord of those who rule as lords.” Dynastēs, “sovereign, ruler,” stands in three verses: the “rulers” brought down from their thrones at Luke 1:52, the Ethiopian “high official” at Acts 8:27, and this one, the only place it is God (dictionary). The Greek titles are participles, “of those who reign,” “of those who rule as lords”; the shorter “King of kings and Lord of lords” is the form of Revelation 17:14 and 19:16, where it is the Lamb’s title. Readers take the doxology here as the Father’s, the one who “will show” Christ’s appearing, as at 1 Timothy 1:17, “the only God”; some read it of Christ, as in Revelation. The KJV reads “the blessed and only Potentate, the King of kings, and Lord of lords”; the NWT 1984 reads “the King of those who rule as kings and Lord of those who rule as lords.”

“Who alone has immortality, dwelling in unapproachable light, whom no human has seen or can see.” Athanasia, “immortality,” stands in three verses: “this mortal must put on immortality” at 1 Corinthians 15:53 and 1 Corinthians 15:54, and this one, where God alone has it by nature (dictionary). Aprositos, “unapproachable,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. The unseen God is the God of Exodus 33:20, “man shall not see me and live,” and of John 1:18. The KJV reads “dwelling in the light which no man can approach unto”; the NWT 1984 reads “who dwells in unapproachable light.”

Verses 17–19 · “Those who are rich in the present age” — “the life that is truly life”

“Command those who are rich in the present age not to be haughty, nor to set their hope on the uncertainty of riches.” Hypsēlophroneō, “to be haughty,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does adēlotēs, “uncertainty.” “Set their hope” is the perfect of 1 Timothy 4:10 and 1 Timothy 5:5. The letter does not tell the rich to give up wealth; it tells them where not to put their hope, and the God “who richly provides us with all things to enjoy” is the answer to the teachers of 1 Timothy 4:3. Apolausis, “enjoyment,” stands in two verses: here and Moses choosing ill-treatment “rather than to enjoy sin for a season” at Hebrews 11:25. The Byzantine text reads “the living God,” and so the KJV: “nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy.” The NWT 1984 reads “the present system of things.”

“To do good, to be rich in good works, to be generous, ready to share, storing up for themselves a good foundation.” Eumetadotos, “generous,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does koinōnikos, “ready to share” (dictionary). The storing up is Jesus’ picture of the man “who stores up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God” at Luke 12:21, turned the right way round. “The life that is truly life” uses ontōs, the “truly” of “widows who are truly widows” at 1 Timothy 5:3; the Byzantine text reads “eternal life.” The KJV reads “that they may lay hold on eternal life”; the ASV reads “the life which is life indeed”; the NIV reads “the life that is truly life.”

Verses 20–21 · “Guard the deposit” — “the antitheses of what is falsely called knowledge”

“O Timothy, guard the deposit.” Parathēkē, “a deposit,” something left in another’s keeping, stands in three verses: this one, “he is able to guard my deposit until that day” at 2 Timothy 1:12, and “guard the good deposit” at 2 Timothy 1:14 (dictionary). Kenophōnia, “empty chatter,” stands in two verses: here and 2 Timothy 2:16. The KJV reads “keep that which is committed to thy trust”; the NIV reads “guard what has been entrusted to your care.”

⚠ “The antitheses of what is falsely called knowledge.” Antitheseis means “oppositions, contrary statements”; “falsely called” is pseudōnymos, found in one verse of the New Testament; “knowledge” is gnōsis. Around 180 Irenaeus of Lyon took the phrase for the title of his great work, usually called Against Heresies, whose Greek title is Refutation and Overthrow of Knowledge Falsely So Called. Marcion, in the second century, wrote a book called Antitheses setting the Law against the Gospel, as Tertullian describes it in Against Marcion. Some readers see the verse as aimed at Marcion, which would date the letter in the second century; others take antitheseis as an ordinary word for the arguments of the Ephesian teachers. This page records the readings and does not choose. The KJV reads “oppositions of science falsely so called,” with “science” in its old sense of knowledge; the NWT 1984 reads “the contradictions of the falsely called” knowledge.

“Which some have professed and have missed the mark concerning the faith. Grace be with you all.” Astocheō, “to miss the mark,” stands in three verses: 1 Timothy 1:6, this one, and 2 Timothy 2:18, so the letter ends where it began. The closing “you” is plural, as if the letter were meant to be read to the congregation; the Byzantine text has the singular and an “Amen,” and so the KJV, “Grace be with thee. Amen.” Some later copies add a note that the letter was written from Laodicea, and the Geneva prints it: “The first Epistle to Timothy, written from Laodicea.” The NIV reads “Grace be with you all.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “slaves” and “masters,” the despotēs of Luke 2:29; “a different doctrine,” as at 1:3; “sound” and “sick”; “a means of gain” and “great gain” for one word; “because” in verse 7, as the Greek has it; “a root of all evils,” with no article; “reaching for,” the verb of 3:1; “who keeps all things alive,” the verb of Luke 17:33; “the King of those who reign,” as participles; “set their hope,” as at 4:10; “truly life,” the “truly” of 5:3; “the deposit,” as at 2 Timothy 1:12; “antitheses”; “missed the mark,” as at 1:6; and “you all.”

Echoes paid. The different doctrine of 1 Timothy 1:3; sound teaching from 1 Timothy 1:10; puffed up from 1 Timothy 3:6; the snare of 1 Timothy 3:7; aspiring from 1 Timothy 3:1; above reproach from 1 Timothy 3:2; the laying on of hands of 1 Timothy 4:14; the only God of 1 Timothy 1:17; at the proper times from 1 Timothy 2:6; set their hope from 1 Timothy 4:10; truly from 1 Timothy 5:3; missed the mark from 1 Timothy 1:6; and the deposit of 2 Timothy 1:12 and 2 Timothy 1:14.

Echoes planted. The verb of word-quarrels at 2 Timothy 2:14, empty chatter at 2:16, missing the mark at 2:18, lovers of money at 3:2, and the man of God at 3:17 (the last two yet to come in this translation).

This is the last chapter of 1 Timothy. Next in the Pastoral letters: 2 Timothy 2, already on these pages. Strength in grace, the soldier, the athlete and the farmer, a faithful saying about dying and living with Christ, and the worker who handles the word of truth rightly.
