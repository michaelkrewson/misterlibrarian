Peter goes up to Jerusalem and is challenged by those of the circumcision for going in to uncircumcised men and eating with them; he sets out the roof, the vessel and the falling of the Spirit in order, and they quiet down and glorify God. Those scattered over Stephen reach Phoenicia, Cyprus and Antioch; men of Cyprus and Cyrene speak there to Hellenists; Jerusalem sends Barnabas, who goes to Tarsus for Saul and brings him to Antioch for a year; the disciples are first called Christians. The prophet Agabus signifies a great famine, and the disciples send relief by Barnabas and Saul to the elders in Judea.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year, and its only time-markers are ‘in these days’ (v27) and ‘a whole year’ (v26). It places the famine ‘in the time of Claudius’ (v28) without a year, and says the relief was sent without saying when. Josephus, in Whiston’s translation of the Antiquities (20.5.2), puts a great famine in Judea under the procurators Fadus and Tiberius Alexander; this library reports that and does not tie it to the verse or supply a year.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · οἱ ἐκ περιτομῆς … Εἰσῆλθες πρὸς ἄνδρας ἀκροβυστίαν ἔχοντας καὶ συνέφαγες αὐτοῖς hoi ek peritomēs … Eisēlthes pros andras akrobystian echontas kai synephages autois — Peter called to account, Acts 11:1–3: “those of the circumcision” and the charge that he “went in… and ate with them”

The apostles hear, again. The sentence is built like Acts 8:14, where “the apostles in Jerusalem heard that Samaria had received the word of God.” The verb is dechomai in both (dedektai there, edexanto here), and so is its object, “the word of God.” What differs is the sender. At 8:14 the apostles send Peter and John; at 11:2 the Greek says only anebē Petros, “Peter went up,” and does not name a sender. “The nations” is ta ethnē, the word of 10:45, where the Spirit “had been poured out even on the nations.” KJV, ASV, NIV and TLB print “Gentiles,” NWT 1984 “people of the nations.” Kata tēn Ioudaian spreads the hearers across the land, which NIV keeps (“throughout Judea”) and the KJV and ASV flatten to “in Judaea.”

“Those of the circumcision,” contending. Hoi ek peritomēs, “those of the circumcision,” named the believers who came with Peter to Caesarea (10:45, where the Greek adds “believers”). Here the phrase stands without that noun, and the Greek does not say whether these are a faction or simply the Jerusalem believers who were Jews; both readings are held, and this page does not choose. The words ek peritomēs stand in five verses of the New Testament: Acts 10:45, this verse, and Romans 4:12, Galatians 2:12 and Colossians 4:11 (those three not yet on these pages). The verb is diekrinonto, “were contending,” the middle or passive of the verb the Spirit used to Peter at 10:20 (“hesitating over nothing”); it stands in four verses of Acts, 10:20, this verse, 11:12 and 15:9, and the senses move among hesitating, disputing and distinguishing. KJV, ASV and Douay “contended with him,” NIV “criticized him,” TLB “argued with him,” NWT 1984 “began to contend with him” and NWT 2013 “began to criticize him.” The NIV (“the circumcised believers”) and the TLB (“the Jewish believers”) also call the party believers, a noun this verse does not print.

“You went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them.” The Greek is blunter than the English: pros andras akrobystian echontas, “to men having a foreskin.” Akrobystia stands in seventeen verses of the New Testament, and sixteen of them are in letters that bear Paul’s name (Romans, 1 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians and Colossians); this verse is the one exception. The accusation names no house. KJV and ASV keep it bare (“wentest in to men uncircumcised”), while the NIV (“went into the house of uncircumcised men”) and NWT 1984 (“gone into the house of men”) supply one; the Greek has “the house of the man” at v12, in Peter’s own report. TLB replaces the charge with “You fellowshiped with Gentiles and even ate with them.” “Ate with them” is synephages, from synesthiō, which stands in five verses of the New Testament: the Pharisees’ complaint against Jesus, “This man welcomes sinners and eats with them” (Luke 15:2); the apostles “who ate with him and drank with him” after he rose (Acts 10:41); this verse; and 1 Corinthians 5:11 and Galatians 2:12 (not yet on these pages). Luke 15:2 is the only other place where the verb is an accusation, and the charge there is the same shape as this one. Chapter 10 never says that Peter ate at Cornelius’s house: it has him hungry on the roof (10:10), a voice that tells him to eat (10:13) and a request that he “stay on some days” (10:48). The meal is in the accusation alone.

Verses 4–10 · ἀρξάμενος … ἐξετίθετο αὐτοῖς καθεξῆς … ἐν ἐκστάσει ὅραμα … ἀνεσπάσθη arxamenos … exetheto autois kathexēs … en ekstasei horama … anespasthē — Peter retells the vision in order, Acts 11:4–10: what changes between the roof at Joppa and the room at Jerusalem

“In orderly sequence.” Kathexēs stands in five verses of the New Testament: Luke 1:3, where Luke says he has followed everything closely and means “to write them out for you in orderly sequence, most excellent Theophilus”; 8:1; Acts 3:24; this verse; and Acts 18:23 (not yet on these pages). It is the word Luke uses of his own method, and here he gives it to Peter; the two verses print it the same way. Exetheto, “set it out,” is the verb of a plain exposition; it stands in four verses of Acts: 7:21, where it means a child “exposed,” this verse, and 18:26 and 28:23 (not yet on these pages), where Priscilla and Aquila “set out” the way of God to Apollos and Paul “set out” the kingdom to the Jews of Rome. Arxamenos, “beginning,” is a participle, and the Greek does not say “from the beginning”: KJV “rehearsed the matter from the beginning, and expounded it by order” and NIV “Starting from the beginning, Peter told them the whole story” both read it that way. ASV “began, and expounded the matter unto them in order,” Douay “began and declared to them the matter in order,” Geneva “began, and expounded the thing in order to them,” NWT 1984 “commenced and went on to explain the particulars to them,” TLB “Then Peter told them the whole story.”

What Peter changes in the telling. Set against 10:9–16, the account here drops the housetop, the noon hour and his hunger: it opens “I was in the city of Joppa praying.” It adds three things. The sheet “came right up to me” (kai ēlthen achri emou, v5); he “gazed into it” and “examined it” (atenisas katenooun, v6); and the animals include ta thēria, “the wild beasts,” which the list at 10:12 does not have. KJV, ASV, Geneva, NIV and NWT 1984 print “wild beasts,” Douay “beasts,” and TLB “all sorts of animals, reptiles, and birds which we are not to eat,” a clause neither chapter has. The vision is the same “trance” as 10:10 (ekstasis; en ekstasei stands in this verse and at 22:17, not yet on these pages): the TLB leaves it out, while the KJV, ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 print “trance,” and Douay “an ecstasy of mind.”

“Nothing common or unclean has ever entered my mouth.” At 10:14 Peter said “I have never eaten anything common and unclean”; here it is koinon ē akatharton oudepote eisēlthen eis to stoma mou, with “or” for “and,” and the subject has moved from his eating to the food’s entering. The words “enter into the mouth” are the words of Matthew 15:11, where what goes eis to stoma is what does not make a person common (koinoi), and the verb eiserchomai is the same. The voice’s answer in v9 is word for word the answer of 10:15 (ha ho theos ekatharisen sy mē koinou), with two additions: it comes “out of heaven,” and it “answered.” KJV “nothing common or unclean hath at any time entered into my mouth” and Douay “nothing common or unclean hath ever entered into my mouth” keep the old word; NIV “Nothing impure or unclean has ever entered my mouth,” NWT 1984 “a defiled or unclean thing has never entered into my mouth,” Geneva “nothing polluted or unclean hath at anytime entered into my mouth,” and TLB “For I have never yet eaten anything forbidden by our Jewish laws!” At v9 the voice is KJV “What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common,” ASV “What God hath cleansed, make not thou common,” NIV “Do not call anything impure that God has made clean,” NWT 1984 “You stop calling defiled the things God has cleansed” and TLB “Don’t say it isn’t right when God declares it is!” The shelf varies the two nouns; the Greek keeps one verb, koinoō, “make common,” that Matthew 15:11 has too.

“Pulled up.” At 10:16 the vessel “was taken up” (anelēmphthē), the verb Luke uses of Jesus’s ascension at Acts 1:2, 1:11 and 1:22. Here it is anespasthē, from anaspaō, “to pull up,” a verb that stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Luke 14:5, where a son or an ox that has fallen into a well is pulled out on the sabbath. Nothing in the Greek ties the two uses together, and this page does not. KJV and ASV “drawn up,” Douay “taken up,” NIV “pulled up to heaven again,” NWT 1984 “pulled up again into heaven.”

Verses 11–14 · οἱ ἓξ ἀδελφοὶ οὗτοι … μηδὲν διακρίναντα … ῥήματα πρὸς σὲ ἐν οἷς σωθήσῃ σὺ καὶ πᾶς ὁ οἶκός σου hoi hex adelphoi houtoi … mēden diakrinanta … rhēmata pros se en hois sōthēsē sy kai pas ho oikos sou — the six brothers and the words that save, Acts 11:11–14: “you and all your household”

Three men, six brothers. Chapter 10 sends two household servants and a devout soldier (10:7), three men, and v11 says treis andres. The brothers who went with Peter were “some of the brothers from Joppa” at 10:23; the number six is given only here. Hoi hex adelphoi houtoi is “these six brothers,” and the demonstrative reads as pointing at men standing in the room, which would put them in Jerusalem with Peter; the Greek does not say so. KJV “Moreover these six brethren accompanied me,” NIV “These six brothers also went with me,” TLB “These six brothers here accompanied me.” Cornelius’s own account of the angel at 10:30 had “a man” who “stood before me in bright clothing”; Peter’s report at v13 says “the angel” standing “in his house,” with the article, as 10:3 had “an angel of God.” Ho epikaloumenos Petros, “the one called Peter,” is the participle of 10:5, 10:18 and 10:32.

“Making no distinction.” The Spirit tells Peter to go mēden diakrinanta, and the verb is the one the circumcised party has just used of itself in v2 (diekrinonto). The participle printed here is the active aorist, and the active is the voice of distinguishing: it is what God does at 15:9, where he “made no distinction between us and them.” At 10:20 the middle voice was printed and rendered “hesitating over nothing.” The apparatus of the printed text gives the active diakrinanta to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and to the Robinson–Pierpont text the middle diakrinomenon, the form of 10:20. The shelf splits: KJV “nothing doubting,” ASV “making no distinction,” Douay “nothing doubting,” Geneva “without doubting,” NIV “have no hesitation,” NWT 1984 “not doubting at all” and TLB “not to worry about their being Gentiles.” Each of the seven English versions on the shelf prints the verb of v2 and the verb of v12 with different English words.

“Words by which you will be saved, you and all your household.” The angel’s message at 10:4–6 contains no word of saving; the verb is first said of Cornelius here, in Peter’s report. Rhēmata, “words,” is the noun of 10:22, where the messengers say Cornelius is to “hear words from you” (dictionary). “All your household” is the “with all his household” of 10:2, now said of what is promised. The verb and the pair come round: “you will be saved, you and your household” is the answer given to the jailer at Acts 16:31 (not yet on these pages), and the question of what saves is the one the men from Judea put at 15:1 (“Unless you are circumcised… you cannot be saved”) and Peter answers at 15:11. KJV “Who shall tell thee words, whereby thou and all thy house shall be saved,” ASV “whereby thou shalt be saved, thou and all thy house,” Douay “Who shall speak to thee words, whereby thou shalt be saved, and all thy house,” NIV “He will bring you a message through which you and all your household will be saved,” NWT 1984 “he will speak those things to you by which you and all your household may get saved” and TLB “He will tell you how you and all your household can be saved!”

Verses 15–18 · ἐπέπεσεν τὸ πνεῦμα … ἐμνήσθην … τοῦ ῥήματος τοῦ κυρίου … τὴν ἴσην δωρεάν … κωλῦσαι τὸν θεόν … ἡσύχασαν … τὴν μετάνοιαν εἰς ζωήν epepesen to pneuma … emnēsthēn … tou rhēmatos tou kyriou … tēn isēn dōrean … kōlysai ton theon … hēsychasan … tēn metanoian eis zōēn — “Who was I to hinder God?” Acts 11:15–18: the equal gift, the remembered word and repentance given to the nations

“Fell upon” and “at the beginning.” Epipiptō, “to fall upon,” is said of the Spirit in three verses (as epepesen in the last two): 8:16 (“it had not yet fallen on any of them”), 10:44 and this one. Chapter 10 puts the moment “while Peter was still speaking these words”; Peter’s telling puts it at the start, en de tō arxasthai me lalein, “as I began to speak.” Both can stand, because the Greek of chapter 10 does not say how far into the speech he was. “On us” is the apostles’ own company, and “at the beginning” (en archē) most naturally points to Pentecost; the Greek does not name it. KJV “as on us at the beginning,” NIV “as he had come on us at the beginning,” NWT 1984 “in [the] beginning,” the bracket marking an added article, and TLB “just as I was getting started with my sermon.”

“I remembered the word of the Lord.” Peter remembers a saying in three places in Luke–Acts. The women at the tomb “remembered his words” (rhēmata, Luke 24:8); Peter himself, at the crowing, remembered “the word of the Lord” (Luke 22:61, where the printed text has logos and Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 read rhēma); and here he remembers a rhēma. The saying he cites is Acts 1:5 in all but word order: “John baptized with water, but you will be baptized in the Holy Spirit,” with en pneumati hagiō before the verb there and after it here. The Baptist’s own form of the contrast adds fire (Luke 3:16). KJV “John indeed baptized with water,” ASV “John indeed baptized with water; but ye shall be baptized in the Holy Spirit,” NIV “John baptized with water, but you will be baptized with the Holy Spirit.”

“The equal gift” and “who was I?” Isos, “equal,” is the ordinary word for equal shares (dictionary), and Acts 11:17 is the only verse in Acts that has the adjective. The Greek does not say “the same gift” (NIV and TLB “the same gift,” NWT 1984 “the same free gift,” Douay “the same grace”) but equal in worth, a gift matched to the first. The complaint of the all-day workers at Matthew 20:12, “you have made them equal to us,” uses this adjective; nothing in Acts says Luke had that parable in mind. Dōrea, “gift,” stands in four verses of Acts, 2:38, 8:20, 10:45 and this one, always of what God gives. “As also to us who believed” is the “just as we also did” of 10:47, and Acts 15:8 (“just as he did to us”) says it a third time. The last question changes its object. At 10:47 it was mēti to hydōr dynatai kōlysai tis, “Can anyone forbid the water?”; here it is egō tis ēmēn dynatos kōlysai ton theon, “who was I, that I could hinder God?” with the first-person pronoun set in front of the question, and the object is no longer water but God (dictionary). KJV, ASV and Douay “withstand God,” Geneva “who was I, that I could let God?” (“let” in its old sense of hinder), NIV “stand in God’s way,” NWT 1984 “hinder God” and TLB “who was I to argue?” without an object at all.

“Quieted down.” Hēsychasan stands in five verses of the New Testament: Luke 14:4, where the lawyers and Pharisees fall silent after Jesus asks whether healing on the sabbath is lawful; Luke 23:56, where the women “rested” on the sabbath; this verse; and Acts 21:14 and 1 Thessalonians 4:11 (not yet on these pages). The verb covers silence, calm and rest, which is why it is printed here as “quieted down.” At Acts 21:14 it is the same verb after a refusal to be persuaded, followed by “The Lord’s will be done.” Ara, “so then,” marks a conclusion drawn: NIV “So then,” NWT 1984 “Well, then,” KJV “Then.” KJV and ASV “held their peace,” NIV “had no further objections,” NWT 1984 “acquiesced.” “The repentance to life” (tēn metanoian eis zōēn) stands in this verse alone, and the verb is edōken, “gave”: God gives repentance. Acts 5:31 says it of Israel (“to give repentance to Israel”), and here it is said of the nations (dictionary). KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva “repentance unto life,” NIV “repentance that leads to life,” NWT 1984 “repentance for the purpose of life,” and TLB “the privilege of turning to him and receiving eternal life,” with an adjective the Greek does not print.

Verses 19–21 · οἱ μὲν οὖν διασπαρέντες … Φοινίκης καὶ Κύπρου καὶ Ἀντιοχείας … πρὸς τοὺς Ἑλληνιστάς … χεὶρ κυρίου hoi men oun diasparentes … Phoinikēs kai Kyprou kai Antiocheias … pros tous Hellēnistas … cheir kyriou — the scattered reach Antioch, Acts 11:19–21: Hellenists or Greeks, and “the hand of Jehovah”

The thread of chapter 8, picked up. Hoi men oun diasparentes are the first four words of Acts 8:4, and the verb that follows is the same, diēlthon, “went through” (dictionary). Chapters 9 and 10 intervene, and Luke goes back to the scattering that began at 8:1. The cause is now thlipsis, which this page prints as “distress,” as at 7:10 and 7:11, where Stephen himself uses it of Joseph’s troubles and of the “great distress” that came with a famine over Egypt; chapter 8 called the same event a diōgmos, a “persecution.” KJV, NIV, Douay and TLB print “persecution,” ASV and NWT 1984 “tribulation,” and Geneva “affliction.” Phoenicia stands in three verses of Acts: this one, 15:3 and 21:2 (not yet on these pages). Antioch was named once before, as the home of Nicolaus, “a proselyte of Antioch” (6:5). Cyrenians were named at 6:9, among those who argued with Stephen, and Lucius of Cyrene is in the Antioch congregation at 13:1 (not yet on these pages). “To no one except Jews only” is said of the road to Phoenicia, Cyprus and Antioch; Luke has already shown Philip taking the word to Samaria (8:5) and to an Ethiopian official (8:27), and the verse does not date its limit. KJV “preaching the word to none but unto the Jews only,” ASV “speaking the word to none save only to Jews,” NIV “spreading the word only among Jews,” NWT 1984 “speaking the word to no one except to Jews only.”

Hellenists, or Greeks? The text printed here reads pros tous Hellēnistas, “to the Hellenists,” the word of 6:1 and 9:29 (dictionary), where the people so named are Jews who speak Greek. The verse says they spoke to them also (kai pros), after v19’s “Jews only,” and what follows is a great number turning to the Lord and a report that goes to Jerusalem; that is why some editors print Hellēnas, “Greeks,” a word that would name non-Jews. The apparatus of the printed text gives Hellēnistas to Westcott–Hort, Robinson–Pierpont and Nestle–Aland 28, and Hellēnas to Tregelles and the NIV. This page prints Hellēnistas and does not resolve it. The shelf prints the plainer noun: KJV and Geneva “Grecians,” ASV, Douay, NIV and TLB “Greeks,” and NWT 1984 “Greek-speaking people.”

“The hand of Jehovah.” Cheir kyriou stands in three verses of the New Testament: Luke 1:66 (“the hand of Jehovah was with him,” of the child John), this verse, and Acts 13:11 (not yet on these pages). It is the Greek form of the Hebrew yad YHWH, and this page prints “Jehovah” wherever kyrios in such a formula is the God of Israel (dictionary). Two sentences later the same verse turns to the Lord: epestrepsen epi ton kyrion is the clause of 9:35 (there in the plural), and “Lord” is kept because the previous verse names “the Lord Jesus” and the referent is open. KJV, ASV and Douay “the hand of the Lord was with them,” NIV “The Lord’s hand was with them,” NWT 1984 “the hand of Jehovah was with them,” and TLB “the Lord honored this effort,” which has no hand.

Verses 22–26 · Βαρναβᾶν … τὴν χάριν … ἐχάρη … ἀναζητῆσαι Σαῦλον … χρηματίσαι … Χριστιανούς Barnabān … tēn charin … echarē … anazētēsai Saulon … chrēmatisai … Christianous — Barnabas at Antioch and the first “Christians,” Acts 11:22–26: who gave the name?

Barnabas, the encourager, sees grace. The congregation “sent out” Barnabas (exapesteilan), the verb of 9:30, where the brothers “sent out” Saul to Tarsus. The verb of his work in v23 is parekalei, “was encouraging,” the verb behind paraklēsis, the noun in his nickname, “son of encouragement” (4:36; dictionary). He saw tēn charin, “the grace,” and echarē, “rejoiced,” the same root. The Greek does not say what he saw; NIV adds “what the grace of God had done” and TLB “the wonderful things God was doing,” while NWT 1984 prints its word for the noun, “the undeserved kindness of God.” He urged them prosmenein tō kyriō, “to remain with the Lord,” tē prothesei tēs kardias, “with purpose of heart”; prosmenō comes back with Barnabas present at 13:43 (not yet on these pages), where Paul and Barnabas persuade people “to remain in the grace of God,” and prothesis is the noun that names the loaves of the Presence in Matthew 12:4. KJV “with purpose of heart they would cleave unto the Lord,” Douay “with purpose of heart to continue in the Lord,” NIV “remain true to the Lord with all their hearts,” NWT 1984 “with hearty purpose,” TLB “stay close to the Lord, whatever the cost.” Anēr agathos, “a good man,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Luke 23:50, where Joseph of Arimathea is “a good and righteous man.” Barnabas is “full of Holy Spirit and of faith,” the nouns of Stephen at 6:5 in the other order (“full of faith and Holy Spirit”). “A considerable crowd was added to the Lord” uses the verb and the dative of 5:14 (prosetithento pisteuontes tō kyriō, there printed “believers in the Lord were being added”), and ochlos hikanos, “a considerable crowd,” comes again in v26 as the year’s students.

Searching Tarsus for Saul. Anazētēsai stands in three verses of the New Testament, Luke 2:44, 2:45 and this one; in Luke it is Joseph and Mary looking through the caravan for the boy Jesus, and the pair “search… find” recurs here (heurōn, v26). The Greek gives no reason for the search and no time spent. Tarsus is where the brothers had sent Saul (9:30); the city is named in five verses of Acts, 9:11, 9:30, this one, and 21:39 and 22:3 (not yet on these pages). The whole year is eniauton holon, and the Greek says only that they were gathered en tē ekklēsia, “in the congregation,” and taught. KJV “they assembled themselves with the church,” ASV “they were gathered together with the church,” Douay “they conversed there in the church a whole year,” Geneva “they were conversant with the Church,” NIV “met with the church,” NWT 1984 “gathered together with them in the congregation.”

“Called Christians.” Christianos stands in three verses of the New Testament: this one, Agrippa’s “You are persuading me to make a Christian” at Acts 26:28, and 1 Peter 4:16 (“if as a Christian…”; both not yet on these pages). It is built on Christos, “the Anointed” (dictionary), with an ending taken over from Latin, as in Hērōdianoi, “Herodians” (Matthew 22:16, Mark 3:6, 12:13). The infinitive chrēmatisai has no stated subject, so the verse does not say who gave the name. The verb chrēmatizō stands in nine verses of the New Testament, and its senses divide. It is the verb of a divine warning or instruction at Matthew 2:12, 2:22, Luke 2:26 and Acts 10:22, and in Hebrews 8:5, 11:7 and 12:25; and at Romans 7:3 it is “she will be called an adulteress,” a name earned by an act (Romans 7:3 and Hebrews 8:5 and 12:25 not yet on these pages; Hebrews 11:7 is). Its noun, chrēmatismos, adds Romans 11:4, the divine answer. NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 print “by divine providence called Christians”; the verse has no word for providence, and the phrase carries that version’s reading of the verb as a divine act, as at 10:22. KJV, ASV and NIV print “called Christians,” Douay “named Christians,” Geneva “first called Christians in Antioch,” TLB “were first called” with the name in quotation marks. That the name came from outsiders is a common inference from Acts 26:28 and 1 Peter 4:16; the Greek of 11:26 does not say. Prōtōs, “first,” is an adverb that stands in this verse alone, and the Robinson–Pierpont text reads prōton. The congregation here is ekklēsia, as at v22, printed “congregation” in this translation and in the NWT 1984, and “church” in the rest of the shelf.

Verses 27–30 · προφῆται … Ἅγαβος ἐσήμανεν … λιμὸν … οἰκουμένην … εὐπορεῖτό τις … εἰς διακονίαν … πρὸς τοὺς πρεσβυτέρους prophētai … Hagabos esēmanen … limon … oikoumenēn … euporeito tis … eis diakonian … pros tous presbyterous — Agabus and the famine relief, Acts 11:27–30: each according to his means, and a gift sent to the elders

Agabus, who “signified.” Hagabos stands in two verses, this one and 21:10 (not yet on these pages), and there too he is a prophet who “came down” from Judea, with the verb of v27 (katēlthon, katēlthen). Esēmanen is “signified,” “indicated by a sign”: sēmainō stands in six verses of the New Testament, three of them John’s remark on what a saying of Jesus indicated (12:33, 18:32, 21:19), Acts 25:27, here, and Revelation 1:1 (Acts 25:27 not yet on these pages), where the same form, esēmanen, is what God does through his angel. KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva “signified by the Spirit,” NIV “predicted,” NWT 1984 “proceeded to indicate through the spirit.” The famine is limos, a word Luke has used of famines before: “a famine over all Egypt and Canaan” in Stephen’s speech (7:11, with the same eph’ holēn that stands here) and the “great famine” that “came over all the land” in Elijah’s day in Jesus’s sermon at Nazareth (Luke 4:25). Luke treats the noun as masculine in Luke 4:25 and as feminine at Luke 15:14, and the feminine adjective printed here is megalēn, where the Robinson–Pierpont text has the masculine megan. “The whole inhabited world” is oikoumenē, the word of Luke 2:1 and 4:5. NIV prints “the entire Roman world,” NWT 1984 “the entire inhabited earth,” Douay “the whole world,” and TLB narrows it to “the land of Israel,” which is not what the Greek says. “In the time of Claudius” is Luke’s aside, and the KJV adds a title the Greek does not have (“Claudius Caesar”). The Greek gives no year. Josephus, in Whiston’s translation of the Antiquities (20.5.2), says a great famine fell on Judea under the procurators Fadus and Tiberius Alexander, when Queen Helena bought grain in Egypt and distributed it; that passage is reported here and not tied to Luke’s verse. The emperor’s name comes again at 18:2 (not yet on these pages), and a different Claudius, the tribune Claudius Lysias, writes the letter at 23:26 (not yet on these pages).

“As anyone had means.” Euporeō, “to have means,” stands in this verse alone (dictionary); its noun euporia is the silversmith Demetrius’s “prosperity” at 19:25 (not yet on these pages). The clause is shaped like 4:35: there goods were given out kathoti an tis chreian eichen, “as anyone had need,” and here the gift is sent kathōs euporeito tis, “as anyone had means.” Hōrisan hekastos autōn, “each of them determined,” is the verb of 10:42, and the Greek puts the decision in each person’s hands. KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva “according to his ability,” NIV “as each one was able,” NWT 1984 “according as anyone could afford it,” TLB “each giving as much as he could.” Diakonia is “service,” and the same noun closes the errand at Acts 12:25 (not yet on these pages), where Barnabas and Saul return “having fulfilled the service.” KJV, ASV and Douay “relief,” Geneva “succor,” NIV “help,” NWT 1984 “a relief ministration,” which keeps the noun. Adelphoi are “brothers”: NIV prints “brothers and sisters,” TLB “the Christians in Judea.”

“To the elders.” Presbyteroi in Acts has been the elders of the people, with the rulers and scribes (4:5, 4:8, 4:23, 6:12); this is the first verse where the elders belong to the congregation, and Luke has not said how they came to be. Earlier gifts were laid “at the apostles’ feet” (4:35, 4:37, 5:2); this one goes “to the elders,” and the Greek does not say why. Elders come again at 14:23 (not yet on these pages) and at 15:2, 15:4, 15:6 and 15:22. KJV and ASV “the elders,” Douay “the ancients,” NIV “the elders,” NWT 1984 “the older men,” and TLB “to Barnabas and Paul to take to the elders of the church in Jerusalem,” which changes the name and adds the place.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “nations” for ethnē at v1 and v18, as at 10:45; “contending” for diakrinomai at v2, kept apart from “hesitating” at 10:20 and from “making no distinction” for the active at v12, as at 15:9; “uncircumcised” for akrobystian echontas at v3; “in orderly sequence” for kathexēs at v4, as at Luke 1:3; “trance” for ekstasis at v5, as at 10:10; “vessel” for skeuos at v5, as at 10:11; “wild beasts” for thēria at v6; “slaughter” for thyō at v7, as at 10:13; “common” for koinos at v8 and “make common” for koinoō at v9, as at 10:14–15; “cleansed” for katharizō at v9, as at 10:15; “pulled up” for anaspaō at v10, against “taken up” for analambanō at 10:16; “Look —” for idou at v11, as at 10:17 and 10:19; “the one called Peter” for ho epikaloumenos at v13, as at 10:5, 10:18 and 10:32; “words” for rhēmata at v14, as at 10:22; “fell upon” for epipiptō at v15, as at 10:44; “word” for rhēma at v16; “equal” for isos at v17; “hinder” for kōlyō at v17, against “forbid” at 10:47 and “prevents” at 8:36; “quieted down” for hēsychazō at v18; “scattered” for diaspeirō at v19, as at 8:1 and 8:4; “distress” for thlipsis at v19, as at 7:10 and 7:11; “Hellenists” for Hellēnistai at v20, as at 6:1 and 9:29; “turned” for epistrephō at v21, as at 9:35; “congregation” for ekklēsia at vv22 and 26; “grace” for charis at v23; “encouraging” for parakaleō at v23, the verb of the noun in “son of encouragement” at 4:36; “remain” for prosmenō at v23; “added” for prostithēmi at v24, as at 5:14; “search” for anazēteō at v25; “called” for chrēmatizō at v26, against “divinely instructed” at 10:22; “signified” for sēmainō at v28; “inhabited world” for oikoumenē at v28, as at Luke 2:1 and 4:5; “determined” for horizō at v29, as at 10:42; “service” for diakonia at v29, as at 6:1; “elders” for presbyteros at v30, as at 4:5; “Jehovah” for kyrios at v21 in “the hand of Jehovah,” the formula of Luke 1:66; and “Lord” for kyrios at vv8, 16, 17, 20, 21 (the second), 23 and 24, where the speaker of v8 is Peter addressing the voice, the referent of vv16, 17 and 20 is Jesus, and vv21 (the second), 23 and 24 are open.

Echoes paid. The apostles who “heard” that the word had been received — Acts 8:14, at v1. The accusation of eating with sinners — Luke 15:2, at v3. “In orderly sequence” — Luke 1:3, at v4. The trance and the vessel — Acts 10:10–16, at vv5–10. “Into the mouth” — Matthew 15:11, at v8. The pulling up — Luke 14:5, at v10. The three men, “making no distinction,” and “all your household” — Acts 10:19–20 and 10:2, at vv11–14. “The one called Peter” — Acts 10:5, at v13. The Spirit that “fell upon” — Acts 8:16 and 10:44, at v15. The word remembered — Acts 1:5, Luke 24:8 and 22:61, at v16. The gift and the forbidding — Acts 10:45 and 10:47, at v17. Repentance given — Acts 5:31, at v18. The scattering — Acts 8:1–4, at v19. The Hellenists — Acts 6:1 and 9:29, at v20. The hand of Jehovah — Luke 1:66, at v21. Turning to the Lord — Acts 9:35, at v21. Barnabas, “son of encouragement” — Acts 4:36, at v23. The good man — Luke 23:50, at v24. Full of Holy Spirit and faith — Acts 6:5, at v24. Added to the Lord — Acts 5:14, at v24. The search for one who is found — Luke 2:44–45, at vv25–26. Tarsus — Acts 9:30, at v25. The famine over the whole land — Acts 7:11 and Luke 4:25, at v28. The inhabited world — Luke 2:1, at v28. “As anyone had need” — Acts 4:35, at v29. The elders — Acts 4:5, at v30.

Echoes planted. “The service” fulfilled at 12:25. Prophets and teachers at Antioch, with Barnabas and Lucius of Cyrene, at 13:1. “The hand of Jehovah” upon Elymas, at 13:11. “Remain in the grace of God,” at 13:43. Elders appointed in each congregation, at 14:23, and the elders of the Jerusalem congregation at 15:2, 15:4, 15:6 and 15:22. “Unless you are circumcised, you cannot be saved,” and the answer that God “made no distinction,” at 15:1, 15:9 and 15:11, with the Spirit given “just as to us” at 15:8. “You and your household” saved, at 16:31. Claudius, at 18:2, and the other Claudius, the tribune, at 23:26. Agabus again, at 21:10. “We quieted down” in the same verb at 21:14. “Prosperity” from the same root as “had means,” at 19:25. “Christian” on Agrippa’s lips at 26:28 and in 1 Peter 4:16. Eating with the nations at Galatians 2:12, and the circumcision at Romans 4:12, Galatians 2:12 and Colossians 4:11. The verb of “called” at Romans 7:3 and Hebrews 8:5, 11:7 and 12:25.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Who “those of the circumcision” are (v2). Whether the six brothers stand in the room (v12). The manuscript questions: “making no distinction” or “hesitating” at v12, “Hellenists” or “Greeks” at v20, and the adverb and the adjective marked variable at vv26 and 28, each followed by the printed text and reported. Who gave the disciples the name “Christians,” and what chrēmatizō says about it (v26). Which famine, and when (v28). Why the gift goes “to the elders” (v30).
