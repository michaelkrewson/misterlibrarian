📖 Ephesians

Πρὸς Ἐφεσίους (Pros Ephesious) — 'To the Ephesians' — ⚠ and the title may be wrong. The words 'in Ephesus' at 1:1 are ABSENT from the earliest manuscripts (P46, and the original hands of both Vaticanus and Sinaiticus), leaving a blank where an address should be; Marcion, in the second century, knew this letter as the one to LAODICEA. The likeliest explanation is a circular letter carried round a group of congregations with the destination left open for the courier to fill in.

The calmest and most cosmic thing in the Pauline collection — no crisis, no opponents, not one personal greeting; a letter that begins before the founding of the world and ends with a soldier's armour, and whose subject is the single new humanity God is making out of two.

Chapter by chapter

Ephesians 1The calmest and most cosmic thing Paul wrote — and it may not be addressed where the title says. ⚠ The words “in Ephesus” are MISSING from the earliest manuscripts, leaving a blank where an address should stand; Marcion knew this letter as the one to Laodicea; and although Paul spent three years in Ephesus, longer than anywhere else, the letter contains not one personal greeting and twice says he has only HEARD of his readers’ faith. It reads exactly like a circular carried round a group of congregations with the destination left open. What follows the address is extraordinary: verses 3 to 14 are ONE SENTENCE in Greek — about two hundred words without a full stop, the longest in the New Testament — a man who set out to say “blessed be God” and could not find a place to stop. It runs from before the founding of the world to the gathering up of all things in heaven and earth under one head, and it is not an argument but a doxology that got away from him. Then he prays — not that anything be added, but that “the eyes of your heart” might be opened to see what is already theirs. And when he reaches for a measure of God’s power he does not use a metaphor: he uses an event. How great is it? It is the power that raised Jesus from the dead and seated him above every rule and authority and power and lordship — which, in a city famous for magic and amulets and the great temple of Artemis, was the most practical sentence in the letter.Ephesians 2Chapter 1 ended in doxology; chapter 2 opens with a diagnosis nobody softens. “You were DEAD” — not sick, not struggling, dead in trespasses and sins, following “the ruler of the authority of the air” — and then, four verses in, the hinge of the whole letter: “BUT GOD.” Two words the Greek grammar itself was straining toward, since the opening sentence never finds its own main verb until God supplies one. By grace you have been saved — the phrase interrupts its own sentence twice, muttered almost, as if Paul cannot get three clauses into the argument without saying it again. Then the letter turns to the Gentile readers directly: once called “the uncircumcision” by the so-called “circumcision,” without hope and without God in the world — but now brought near by blood. ⚠ “The dividing wall of the fence” is very likely not an abstraction: the Jerusalem temple's actual inner courts were fenced by a stone barrier, the soreg, posted with warning inscriptions threatening death to any Gentile who crossed it — two of those exact stones have been excavated and sit today in museums in Istanbul and Jerusalem. Paul nearly died over an accusation of breaking that precise rule (Acts 21:28). He tells this church that Christ has broken the wall down and made ONE NEW MAN where there were two — and closes on a building metaphor that will not hold still: “are being built together,” present tense, into a temple that is not finished yet.Ephesians 3“For this reason I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus —” and the sentence breaks off for twelve verses while Paul explains the mystery he was given to steward: that the nations are co-heirs, co-members of one body and co-sharers of the promise. ⚠ He calls himself “less than the least,” a grammatically impossible word made on purpose; the riches of Christ are “untraceable”; the many-coloured wisdom of God is put on show to the powers in the heavenly places. Then the sentence resumes in prayer: to the Father from whom every family is named, for a love whose breadth and length and height and depth surpass knowledge, closing in a doxology to the age of the ages.Ephesians 4The letter turns from doctrine to life: “walk worthily of the calling,” with humility, patience and the bond of peace, and seven times “one”: one body, one Spirit, one hope, one Lord, one faith, one baptism, one God. ⚠ Psalm 68 is quoted with one word changed, “he gave gifts” where the psalm says “received”; the going down “into the lower parts of the earth” is read three ways; and the gifts turn out to be people, apostles, prophets, evangelists, shepherds and teachers, with a famous comma in v12. Then the old man put off and the new man put on, “Be angry and do not sin” from the Greek of Psalm 4, no rotten word, and “do not grieve the Holy Spirit of God.”Ephesians 5“Be imitators of God, as beloved children”: Christ handed himself over “for a fragrant aroma,” and the readers who were once darkness are now light. ⚠ “Wake up, sleeper” is quoted from no known book; “clever jesting” is a word Aristotle is reported to have called a virtue; and “be filled with the Spirit” is set against drunkenness. Then the household: “submitting to one another,” a verse to wives with no verb of its own, how the passage has been used against wives, husbands told to love as Christ who handed himself over, Genesis 2:24 quoted from the Greek, and “This mystery is great,” the Latin’s “sacrament.”Ephesians 6“Children, obey your parents”: Paul quotes “Honor your father and mother” from the Greek and stops at “on the earth,” leaving out the land. ⚠ “Slaves, obey your masters”: how the passage was used to defend slavery, stated plainly, then what it says to masters, “there is no partiality with him.” Then the full armor of God, borrowed from Isaiah, against “the world-rulers of this darkness”; “an ambassador in a chain”; Tychicus, in words shared with Colossians; and a last word, “incorruption.”

About the book

Where it sits
The TENTH book of the New Testament and one of the four 'prison letters' (with Philippians, Colossians and Philemon). Its influence is enormous and quiet: 'by grace you have been saved through faith' (2:8), 'one Lord, one faith, one baptism' (4:5), the household code of chapter 5, and the armour of God (6:10-18).
Genre
A LETTER in form, but closer to a MEDITATION or a circular homily — the only Pauline letter with no local problem behind it. Its Greek is unusually liturgical: long piled-up sentences (1:3-14 is a single two-hundred-word sentence, the longest in the New Testament), heaped synonyms, and the cadences of prayer and blessing rather than argument.
Author
⚠ DISPUTED — the sharpest authorship question among the letters traditionally ascribed to Paul. It names Paul twice (1:1; 3:1) and the ancient church never doubted it. Against that, a substantial body of modern scholarship notes: its Greek style (long periodic sentences, unusual vocabulary) differs from the undisputed letters; roughly a third of it runs closely parallel to COLOSSIANS, sometimes phrase for phrase, but with words shifted in sense; 'the church' means the universal body rather than a local assembly; and the apostles are spoken of as a foundation already laid (2:20), which reads like a later vantage. Others answer that a circular written at leisure in prison would naturally differ in style from a crisis letter dictated at speed. The library reports both and does not vote.
Date written
c. AD 60–62 if Paul wrote it, from the Roman imprisonment ('an ambassador in a chain', 6:20), carried with Colossians and Philemon by Tychicus (6:21). If a disciple wrote it, somewhere in the 80s or 90s.
Place
Rome, on the traditional view; some prefer an earlier imprisonment at Caesarea or Ephesus.
Audience
⚠ Uncertain, and deliberately so on the circular-letter theory: congregations in the Roman province of Asia, of which Ephesus was the chief city. The letter's own evidence points away from a congregation Paul knew personally — he twice says he has only HEARD of their faith (1:15; 3:2), and there are no greetings by name, which is extraordinary in a letter to a city where he had spent three years.

How it's laid out

1:1–2:22What God has done — the great blessing (one sentence), the prayer to SEE it, the dead made alive, and the two made one with the dividing wall broken down.
3:1–21The mystery now disclosed: Gentiles are fellow heirs, in the same body — and a prayer to know the love that surpasses knowledge.
4:1–5:20Therefore walk worthily — one body and one Spirit, the gifts that build it, the old self put off and the new put on, and 'be imitators of God'.
5:21–6:9The household code — wives and husbands (with marriage read as an image of Christ and the congregation), children and parents, slaves and masters.
6:10–24The armour of God, and a closing that names only Tychicus.

What it's about

  • The COSMIC PLAN — to 'gather up all things under one head in Christ' (1:10), the letter's own summary of what God is doing with the universe.
  • ONE NEW HUMANITY out of two — Jew and Gentile, the dividing wall broken down (2:14-16); the letter's practical heart and the content of its 'mystery'.
  • The CHURCH as Christ's BODY and his fullness — here the whole and not a local assembly, with Christ as the head.
  • GRACE, and the impossibility of boasting — 'by grace you have been saved through faith, and this not from yourselves' (2:8-9).
  • The HEAVENLY PLACES (en tois epouraniois) — a phrase found only in this letter, five times: where the blessings are, where Christ is enthroned, and where the powers we struggle against also are.
  • WALKING — the letter's word for ordinary life, used seven times; the second half is entirely about how the cosmic plan changes a Tuesday.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. ⚠ The letter's most significant textual question is in its first verse: 'in Ephesus' is missing from P46 (c. AD 200) and from the original hands of Vaticanus and Sinaiticus, where later correctors supplied it. This translation prints it in BRACKETS rather than silently choosing. Chapter 1 also carries the New Testament's longest sentence (vv3-14), which every English version breaks up, including this one — the note records what is lost by doing so.

Looking forward

Ephesians has the highest and widest view of Christ in the collection. He is the one in whom we were chosen 'before the founding of the world' (1:4); the one in whom God's plan is 'to gather up all things under one head' (1:10); raised, seated at the right hand 'far above every rule and authority and power and lordship' (1:21); the head of the body, and its fullness (1:23); the peace who 'made both one and broke down the dividing wall' (2:14); the husband whose love for the congregation is the pattern for a marriage (5:25). Chapter 1 is almost entirely about what God did IN him — the preposition 'in Christ' or its equivalent occurs about a dozen times in eleven verses.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP, above all, and its relation to COLOSSIANS (which letter used which, or whether one author wrote both). The ADDRESS — Ephesus, Laodicea, or a blank. Whether 'predestined' in 1:5 and 1:11 concerns individuals or the corporate purpose. Whether the congregation as Christ's 'fullness' (1:23) means he fills it or it completes him. And the HOUSEHOLD CODE of 5:21-6:9, especially 'wives, submit' read against 'submit to one another' in 5:21, and the letter's failure to condemn slavery outright — questions the library states plainly rather than smoothing over. No votes cast.
