Samuel, grown old, makes his sons Joel and Abijah judges at Beersheba; they take bribes and bend judgment. The elders of Israel come to Ramah and ask for a king to judge them like all the nations. Jehovah tells Samuel that the people have rejected not him but Jehovah as king, and to warn them of the king's due: he will take their sons, daughters, fields and servants and tithe their harvest and flocks. The people refuse to listen, and Jehovah tells Samuel to make a king reign for them.
Samuel's old age (1 Samuel 8:1), at the end of the period of the Judges.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · Samuel’s sons Joel and Abijah — judges at Beersheba who “bent toward gain, and took a bribe, and bent judgment”

Sons as judges. “When Samuel was old, he made his sons judges over Israel”: a judge hands the office to his sons, where Gideon had refused, “I will not rule over you, and my son will not rule over you” (Judges 8:23, now on these pages). They sit at Beersheba, the southern end of the land in the phrase “from Dan to Beersheba” (Judges 20:1, now on these pages). The ASV has “they were judges in Beer-sheba”; the NIV “they served at Beersheba”. Josephus puts one son at Bethel and the other at Beersheba (Antiquities 6.32, numbered 6.3.2 in Whiston). Chronicles lists “the firstborn Vashni, and Abijah”, where vashni reads as “and the second” with the first name missing, and later in the same chapter names “Joel son of Samuel” (1 Chronicles 6:13 and 6:18 in the Hebrew, 6:28 and 6:33 in English Bibles).

“Bent judgment.” “His sons did not walk in his ways”, as Eli’s sons “did not know Jehovah” (1 Samuel 2:12, now on these pages): the book’s second house whose sons fail. “They bent toward gain, and took a bribe, and bent judgment”: the same verb, natah, twice, and the sentence breaks the law clause by clause, “You shall not bend judgment; you shall not regard a face; and you shall not take a bribe” (Deuteronomy 16:19, now on these pages). Gain is betsa, the thing the first judges of Israel were to be men “who hate” (Exodus 18:21, now on these pages); bribe is shochad; judgment is mishpat, a word the chapter will use again. The ASV has “turned aside after lucre, and took bribes, and perverted justice”; the NWT 1984 “they were inclined to follow unjust profit and would accept a bribe and pervert judgment”. Josephus makes the sons a lesson that children do not always take after their parents (Antiquities 6.33).

Verses 4–9 · “Set for us a king, like all the nations” — the elders at Ramah, “evil in the eyes of Samuel”, “they have rejected me”, and “the due of the king”

“Like all the nations.” The elders come to Ramah: “Now set for us a king to judge us, like all the nations.” The words are those of the king law, “Let me set a king over myself, like all the nations that are around me” (Deuteronomy 17:14, now on these pages); “like all the nations” stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: that law, this verse, verse 20, and Moab’s taunt in Ezekiel 25:8. They ask for a king “to judge us”, to do the work Samuel has done, after a book that ends “In those days there was no king in Israel” (Judges 21:25, now on these pages). The ASV has “now make us a king to judge us like all the nations”; the NIV “such as all the other nations have”.

“Evil in the eyes of Samuel.” “The thing was evil in the eyes of Samuel”: the idiom of Judges, “The sons of Israel did what was evil in the eyes of Jehovah” (Judges 2:11, now on these pages), here about Samuel’s eyes. He does not answer the elders; he prays. The NWT 1984 has “the thing was bad in the eyes of Samuel”; the ASV “But the thing displeased Samuel”. Josephus says Samuel was grieved by his inborn love of justice and his hatred of kings, and lay awake the whole night without food (Antiquities 6.36–37, numbered 6.3.3 in Whiston).

“They have rejected me.” “It is not you they have rejected, but me they have rejected from being king over them”: the verb is ma’as, as in the wilderness, “you have rejected Jehovah who is among you” (Numbers 11:20, now on these pages), and the King is the one of the song at the sea, “Jehovah will reign forever and ever” (Exodus 15:18, now on these pages). “Forsaking me and serving other gods” is Jehovah’s charge in Judges, “you have forsaken me and served other gods” (Judges 10:13, now on these pages). The ASV has “they have rejected me, that I should not be king over them”; the NWT 1984 “it is I whom they have rejected from being king over them”.

The due of the king. “You shall solemnly warn them, and tell them the due of the king”: mishpat ha-melekh, and the phrase stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and verse 11. It is the mishpat of the sons’ bent “judgment” in verse 3, and of “the priests’ due from the people” that Eli’s sons took with a fork out of the pot (1 Samuel 2:13, now on these pages): first the priests’ due, now the king’s. The ASV has “shalt show them the manner of the king”; the NWT 1984 “the rightful due of the king”; the Douay “the right of the king”; the NIV “will claim as his rights”.

Verses 10–18 · The king’s due — “he will take” your sons, daughters, fields and servants, “he will tithe”, and “you yourselves will be his servants”

The people who asked. Samuel speaks to “the people who were asking him for a king”: the verb is sha’al, the verb of Hannah’s “he is lent to Jehovah” (1 Samuel 1:28, now on these pages), and the root of the name Sha’ul, Saul, “asked”, the king the next chapter brings (1 Samuel 9:2, now on these pages).

“He will take.” The king’s due is a list of takings: “he will take” your sons (v. 11), your daughters (v. 13), your best fields, vineyards and olive groves (v. 14), your servants, young men and donkeys (v. 16). The sons go to his chariots and horsemen, where the king law said “he shall not multiply horses for himself” (Deuteronomy 17:16, now on these pages), and become “rulers of thousands and rulers of fifties”, the ranks of Jethro’s judges (Exodus 18:21). “To plow his plowing and to reap his reaping” keeps the Hebrew’s paired nouns and verbs. The daughters become “perfumers”, the trade of the holy oil, “the work of a perfumer” (Exodus 30:25, both now on these pages), and cooks and bakers. The ASV has “to be perfumers, and to be cooks, and to be bakers”; the KJV “to be confectionaries”.

“He will tithe.” Twice the king “will tithe”, ya‘sor, the verb of the tithe the law gives to Jehovah: “You shall surely tithe all the yield of your seed” (Deuteronomy 14:22, now on these pages; ma‘aser). This tithe goes “to his officials”, sarisim, the word for court officers and eunuchs. The list ends “and you yourselves will be his servants”, as the Egyptians said in the famine, “we and our ground will be servants to Pharaoh” (Genesis 47:19, now on these pages). In verse 16 the Greek has “your best cattle” where the Hebrew has “your best young men”, two words that look alike in Hebrew (baqar, bachur). The NWT 1984 follows the Greek, “YOUR best herds”; the ASV has “your goodliest young men”. Josephus says the daughters will serve like slave-girls who fear the lash (Antiquities 6.41, numbered 6.3.5 in Whiston).

No answer. “You will cry out on that day because of your king … and Jehovah will not answer you on that day.” The cry is za‘aq, the cry of the Judges cycle, “The sons of Israel cried to Jehovah, and Jehovah raised up a deliverer” (Judges 3:9), and the reverse of the chapter before, where “Samuel cried out to Jehovah for Israel, and Jehovah answered him” (1 Samuel 7:9, both now on these pages). The Greek ends the verse “because you chose for yourselves a king”. The Douay has that line too, “because you desired unto yourselves a king”; the ASV has “and Jehovah will not answer you in that day”.

Verses 19–22 · “No — a king there shall be over us” — a king to “go out before us and fight our battles”, and “make a king reign for them”

“No.” “The people refused to listen to the voice of Samuel”: the verb is ma’en, Edom’s refusal of passage (Numbers 20:21, now on these pages). “No — a king there shall be over us”: the Hebrew puts “king” first. The ASV has “Nay: but we will have a king over us”; the NWT 1984 “No, but a king is what will come to be over us”.

“Fight our battles.” “We too will be like all the nations”, the second time in the chapter, “and our king will judge us, and go out before us, and fight our battles.” Moses had asked for a leader “who will go out before them” (Numbers 27:17), and Joshua had said “Jehovah your God is the one who fights for you” (Joshua 23:10, both now on these pages). The KJV has “and go out before us, and fight our battles”; the NIV “to go out before us and fight our battles”.

“Make a king reign for them.” Samuel “spoke them in the ears of Jehovah”, and for the third time the answer is “Listen to their voice” (vv. 7, 9, 22), now with “make a king reign for them”, the causative of malakh, to reign. Then Samuel sends the men home, “each man to his city”, with no king named. Josephus has Samuel promise to call them back when he learns from God whom he is giving them (Antiquities 6.44, numbered 6.3.6 in Whiston). The ASV has “Hearken unto their voice, and make them a king”; the NWT 1984 “you must cause a king to reign for them”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “bent toward gain … bent judgment” for the doubled natah, as in Deuteronomy 16:19; “like all the nations”, as in Deuteronomy 17:14; “evil in the eyes of”; “rejected” for ma’as; “the due of the king” for mishpat ha-melekh, as “the priests’ due” in 2:13; “asking” for sha’al; “he will take”, four times; “he will tithe”, as in Deuteronomy 14:22; “rulers of thousands and rulers of fifties”, as in Exodus 18:21; and “make a king reign”.

Echoes paid. The king law of Deuteronomy 17 is quoted by the elders; the sons of a judge fail as Eli’s sons failed (2:12); the “due” Eli’s sons took (2:13) becomes the king’s due; and the cry that Jehovah answered at Mizpah (7:9) is the cry he will not answer.

Echoes planted. Saul, “asked”, at 1 Samuel 9:2, now on these pages, and Samuel’s own word on the king at 1 Samuel 12, also now on these pages.

Next in this book: 1 Samuel 9 — Kish of Benjamin loses his donkeys, and his son Saul, taller than all the people, goes looking for them; a servant suggests asking the seer in the town, and Jehovah tells Samuel, “Tomorrow at this time I will send you a man.”
