The other half of the diagnostic law: not how to detect scaly disease, but how a person clears of it re-enters the community — two birds and living water outside the camp, seven days, then an eighth-day rite that touches blood and oil to the same ear, thumb and big toe Aaron's own ordination touched. A poor person's cheaper version follows, and then the identical law extended a third time, to the stones and plaster of a house.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus. Its house-plague law (v34) is explicitly future-dated to the settlement — 'when you come into the land of Canaan' — the one piece of this two-chapter unit not given for the wilderness camp at all. Still inside the same Sinai window as Leviticus 13 (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

Outside the camp — and the word this project already promised: living water

The chapter never once uses a noun for the person at its center — not ‘the leper’, only ‘the one with scaly disease’, ‘the one to be cleansed’. Hebrew has such a noun, m’tzora, and v2 uses it exactly once, as a label for the LAW itself (‘this shall be the law of the m’tzora’) rather than to address the person directly through the rest of the chapter, which keeps returning to participles — the one being cleansed, the afflicted person. This translation follows that lead and never prints ‘leper’ as an English noun, for the same reason it retired ‘leprosy’ one chapter back.

And the priest is the one who moves. The healed person cannot yet enter; the boundary of the camp is absolute until a verdict is given, so the examination happens at the edge, not inside it. Two living birds, cedar wood, scarlet thread, and hyssop are brought; one bird is killed in an earthen bowl over living water — not ‘running’ or ‘fresh’ water, which is what nearly every English and Spanish version prints, but the literal Hebrew mayim CHAYIM, water that is alive. ⚠ This project’s own note on John 4 already named this verse before it existed on these pages — flagging that Greek hydōr zōn, what Jesus offers the woman at the well, is ‘the same idiom Hebrew has in mayim chayim, the “living water” required for certain purifications’. This is that purification, named by name: a healed person, cleared for return, cleansed with water the text itself calls living. KJV, ASV and NWT all read ‘running water’; NIV reads ‘fresh water’; on the Spanish shelf RV60 has ‘aguas corrientes’ and NVI ‘agua de manantial’ — six versions, two languages, and not one keeps the word ‘alive’ that is actually there.

The second bird is dipped alive in the first bird’s blood and the living water together, sprinkled seven times, and released ‘over the open field’ — nothing further is said about it, and the text does not explain the image, though the shape (one creature dies, a second visibly carries something away and is let go) is one this book will use again on a larger scale. Only after this does the process turn inward: washed clothes, a bath, and then — on the seventh day — total, deliberate baldness, head, beard and eyebrows shaved bare. ⚠ That is worth setting directly against the chapter just before this one: Leviticus 13:40–41 (already on these pages) declared ordinary, involuntary baldness harmless, no examination needed. Here baldness is not tolerated. It is required — not a symptom to be cleared but the ritual itself, undergone on purpose.

The eighth day — the same three points Aaron’s own ordination touched

Seven days after re-entering the camp, on the eighth, comes the rite this chapter has been building toward: blood and oil applied to the tip of the right ear, the thumb of the right hand, and the big toe of the right foot. That is not a generic gesture. It is, point for point and in the same order, Aaron’s own ordination — Leviticus 8:23–24 (already on these pages), where the identical three places on Aaron’s own body receive the identical blood, before he can serve as priest at all. This project’s own note on that verse called it consecrating ‘hearing, doing, and walking’ — the body’s working parts, made ready one at a time. Here the ceremony that ordained a man into the priesthood is run again on an ordinary Israelite returning from outside the camp, with no comment at all on the parallel. The text simply repeats the gesture and expects the reader who has been paying attention to notice.

⚠ Oil follows the blood, in the same three places, from the priest’s own left palm — poured, sprinkled seven times ‘before Jehovah’, dabbed with a finger, and finally poured out whole over the head. No other purification in this book uses oil this way, on a person rather than an object or an altar; the closest parallel is again Aaron’s own anointing (Leviticus 8:12, already on these pages), oil poured on the head alone, with nothing dabbed to ear, hand or foot. The healed person receives BOTH halves of what only a priest and a priest-in-training separately received before — not because he is being made a priest, the text never says that, but because re-entry into a holy people is treated, physically, with the same vocabulary as entry into holy service.

Only after the ear-thumb-toe rite does the ordinary sacrificial machinery run: sin-offering, then burnt-offering, then grain-offering, ‘and the priest shall make atonement for him’ — the same closing formula this book has used since Leviticus 4 (already on these pages), applied here to a person whose only fault was having been sick.

‘Such as his means suffice for’ — the concession this book keeps making

The full rite costs three animals: two lambs and a ewe-lamb. The poor version costs one lamb and two birds — and the phrase ‘such as his means suffice for’ (or its equivalent) is repeated four times in twelve verses, once for nearly every substitution the poor person is allowed to make. What does NOT get cut is the ear-thumb-toe rite itself: v25 runs it in full, blood from the single guilt-offering lamb touched to the identical three points, oil applied the identical way. The concession is entirely in the animals’ number and price, never in the ceremony’s content — a poorer household is not given a smaller welcome back.

⚠ This is the identical concession Leviticus 12:8 (already on these pages) already made for a woman who could not afford a lamb after childbirth — there, two birds standing in for one lamb; here, birds standing in again, this time alongside a guilt-offering lamb that cannot itself be substituted (a guilt-offering, unlike a sin-offering, has no bird-tier anywhere in this book). Two different laws, two chapters apart, reach for the same solution to the same problem: need is met without being priced.

A house that doesn’t exist yet — the same law, a third time

‘When you come into the land of Canaan’ — v34 is the only line in this whole two-chapter law that is not spoken for the wilderness camp at all. Nobody at Sinai owns a house with a plastered stone wall; the nation lives in tents. This is legislation written in advance for a settlement that has not happened yet, extending the identical diagnostic vocabulary — affliction, examine, confine seven days, spreading, malignant — from skin (Leviticus 13, already on these pages) to fabric (Leviticus 13 again) to architecture, the third domain in as many chapters governed by one method.

⚠ And the text states outright, without hedging, that Jehovah is the one who puts the affliction in the house (‘I put a scaly-disease affliction in a house’, v34) — a claim of direct agency this book never makes for a person’s skin or a garment’s mold, both simply described as occurring. Later Jewish tradition (the Mishnah’s tractate Nega’im) took the hint literally enough to treat house-tzaraat as a special, didactic sign rather than ordinary mildew — some rabbis went so far as to hold it had never actually occurred and never would, existing in the text purely ‘to be expounded and receive reward’. The chapter itself does not go that far; it gives the priest a perfectly ordinary quarantine-scrape-replaster-and-reassess procedure, with a demolition clause if the mold returns a third time.

One practical detail is easy to miss and worth naming: before the priest ever steps inside to examine a suspected house (v36), the owner empties it. Furniture, dishes, whatever the household owns comes out FIRST — not to hide anything, but so that an eventual unclean verdict on the house does not retroactively contaminate every object that happened to be sitting in it. The law protects the family’s property from its own diagnosis.

The colophon that closes two chapters at once

Four short verses list, by name, every category the last two chapters have covered: the affliction of scaly disease, the scale, scaly disease in a garment, scaly disease in a house, the swelling, the scab, the bright spot. This is not Leviticus 14 summarizing itself. It is summarizing Leviticus 13 AND 14 together (already on these pages), in the order both chapters actually introduced their terms — the single formal colophon for what is, by its own reckoning, one law in two parts.

⚠ And it states its own purpose in one infinitive this project has tracked since the first week of Genesis: ‘to teach’ — or more literally, to CAUSE TO DISTINGUISH — ‘the day it is unclean, and the day it is clean’. Lehavdil, the same verb that first separated light from darkness (Genesis 1:4, already on these pages), was handed to Aaron as the priesthood’s entire job description on the worst day of his life (Leviticus 10:10, already on these pages), and closed Leviticus 11 (already on these pages) as that chapter’s own stated purpose. It closes this one too. A hundred and sixteen verses of skin, fabric and architecture, in the end, all exist to teach one act: telling clean from unclean, the verb creation itself started with.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 14 both run 1–57 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Two Masoretic paragraph breaks divide it: closed after v20, open after v32, with a final open break closing the chapter at v57. ‘The one with scaly disease’ replaces ‘the leper’ throughout, continuing Leviticus 13’s own retirement of that noun. Mayim chayim (vv5, 6, 50, 51, 52) is kept literal as ‘living water’ rather than ‘running’ or ‘fresh’ water — the flagship departure of this chapter, paying off a connection this project’s own John 4 note had already drawn before this chapter existed. ‘Guilt-offering’ is kept for asham, matching Leviticus 5’s established rendering; a log of oil is transliterated rather than converted to a modern volume, since the word belongs to this chapter alone.

Echoes paid. Aaron’s own ordination blood — ear, thumb, big toe — from Leviticus 8:23–24 (already on these pages), run again here on a returning Israelite; the hyssop dipped in blood at the Passover doorposts, Exodus 12:22 (already on these pages); the ‘such as his means suffice for’ concession first made for a poor mother at Leviticus 12:8 (already on these pages); and lehavdil, ‘to distinguish’, tracked since Genesis 1:4 and Aaron’s charge at Leviticus 10:10 (both already on these pages), closing this two-chapter law exactly as it closed Leviticus 11.

Echoes planted. This chapter’s own living water (vv5–6) is the purification this project’s John 4:10 note (already on these pages) named in advance; the released living bird (v7, v53) sets up the more famous goat sent to Azazel in the wilderness at Leviticus 16, now on these pages.

⚠ A note on order: Leviticus stands complete on these pages, chapters 1–27.
