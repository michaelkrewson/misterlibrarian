Bitcoin vs. Humanity

Every person alive, set against a currency that cannot print more of itself. The world’s population keeps growing; Bitcoin’s supply is capped at 21 million coins and most of it is already mined — so every birth divides a fixed pie a little thinner. Here is what that looks like in the actual numbers.

“It might make sense just to get some in case it catches on. If enough people think the same way, that becomes a self fulfilling prophecy.”— Satoshi Nakamoto, January 2009, days after the first bitcoin transaction

Even that "issued" figure overstates what can ever be spent: block 501,726, mined December 30, 2017, has a coinbase transaction that claims 0 BTC instead of the 12.5 BTC subsidy then in effect — an entirely empty block, its reward destroyed by whatever mining bug produced it. That satoshi will never exist. The 21 million cap this page computes from the halving schedule is Bitcoin’s theoretical ceiling; the real one is smaller still, and no one can ever put it back.

How these numbers are made

Four different kinds of number sit on this page, and they are not equally true.

  • Computed, and exact. Bitcoin issued and the 21 million cap — the published halving schedule, summed in whole satoshis from the live block height, exactly like the Bitcoin Board and its own working.
  • Polled, and live. That block height itself, refreshed from mempool.space about once a minute while this page is open.
  • Extrapolated, continuously. World population. No public source publishes a live headcount — there is no census running in real time — so this is a straight line drawn from the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024 (medium variant) net growth rate, ticking forward in your browser every second. It is a model, not a measurement, refreshed by hand roughly once a year from the next UN revision.
  • Fixed, and refreshed by hand. Global household wealth and the world's count of USD millionaires, both from the UBS Global Wealth Report 2025 (year-end 2024) — there is no live feed for either, so these are updated roughly once a year as new editions of that report appear.

The chart's historical points use the theoretical ten-minutes-a-block schedule rather than real block timestamps — real blocks land a little faster than that on average, which is why the live point sits a hair off the theoretical line.
