📖 Philippians

Πρὸς Φιλιππησίους (Pros Philippēsious) — 'To the Philippians' — the congregation in a Roman COLONY in Macedonia, named for Philip II of Macedon and refounded by Rome with army veterans after the battle of 42 BC fought on its plain. Their civic pride is why Paul, uniquely, tells them to exercise their CITIZENSHIP worthily of the gospel.

A thank-you note for money, written in chains, that sounds the word JOY sixteen times and contains one of the oldest Christian hymns — the warmest thing Paul wrote, to the one congregation that never gave him trouble.

Chapter by chapter

Philippians 1A thank-you note for money, written in chains, by a man awaiting a verdict that could go either way — and it is the warmest thing Paul wrote. He does not even call himself an apostle: just “Paul and Timothy, slaves of Christ Jesus,” level with his young colleague, because nothing in this letter needs defending. The Philippians were the one congregation that never gave him trouble; they had been sending him money since the first day, and “your partnership in the gospel” is very nearly a commercial receipt. The word to watch is JOY — it sounds four times in this chapter and sixteen in four short chapters, from a prison cell. Then two of the most disarming paragraphs in the New Testament. First: there are people in Rome preaching Christ specifically to spite him, out of “envy and rivalry,” hoping to make his imprisonment worse — and his response is “what of it? … whether in pretense or in truth, Christ is being announced, and in this I rejoice.” Bad motives he shrugs at; a different gospel he had cursed twice over in Galatians. Second: asked to choose between execution and release, he cannot. “To me, to live is Christ and to die is gain” — ten Greek words with no verb in them — and then, honestly, “which I will choose, I do not know.” ⚠ He decides to expect release on the grounds that other people still need him. And he tells a proud Roman colony to “live out your CITIZENSHIP worthily of the gospel,” a word every version flattens to conduct.

About the book

Where it sits
The ELEVENTH book of the New Testament and one of the four 'prison letters'. It is quarried above all for the hymn of 2:6-11 ('he emptied himself, taking a slave's form'), which is very likely a pre-existing Christian poem Paul is quoting — and therefore one of the oldest surviving statements of belief about Christ, older than any Gospel.
Genre
A LETTER, and the closest thing in the collection to a genuine personal letter of FRIENDSHIP — it follows the conventions ancient handbooks laid down for letters between friends and partners: thanks for gifts received, news of the sender's circumstances, reassurance, and the language of longing and shared feeling. It is also a THANK-YOU LETTER: the Philippians had sent Paul money more than once, and chapter 4 finally acknowledges it in the vocabulary of a commercial receipt.
Author
PAUL, uncontested, with TIMOTHY as co-sender — and, notably, with NO apostolic title: 'Paul and Timothy, slaves of Christ Jesus.' In every other letter to a congregation he leads with 'apostle'. Nothing here needed defending.
Date written
c. AD 60–62 on the traditional reading, from the Roman imprisonment — the clues being 'the whole praetorium' (1:13) and 'those of Caesar's household' (4:22). ⚠ Because praetorium can mean any governor's headquarters, an earlier imprisonment at Ephesus or Caesarea has also been argued, which would move the letter into the mid-50s.
Place
Rome, on the traditional view.
Audience
The congregation at PHILIPPI — the first Paul founded on European soil (Acts 16), summoned there by a night vision. Its beginnings were vivid: Lydia the purple-cloth dealer as first convert, an exorcism, a beating, a jail, an earthquake, and magistrates who came in person to apologise on learning they had flogged Roman citizens. The relationship stayed warm for a decade: they sent money repeatedly, and sent Epaphroditus with more, who nearly died on the errand.

How it's laid out

1:1–11Thanksgiving and prayer — joy, partnership, and a prayer that their love grow in DISCERNMENT.
1:12–26Paul's circumstances: chains that advanced the gospel, rivals who preach Christ to spite him, and a man genuinely undecided between living and dying.
1:27–2:18Citizenship worthy of the gospel; humility; and the HYMN — 'he emptied himself… therefore God highly exalted him'.
2:19–30Timothy and Epaphroditus, who 'came near to death' carrying their gift.
3:1–4:1Against those who trust the flesh — Paul's own credentials counted as loss, 'the surpassing worth of knowing Christ', and 'our commonwealth is in the heavens'.
4:2–23Euodia and Syntyche; 'rejoice always'; 'I have learned to be content'; and at last the receipt for the money.

What it's about

  • JOY — chara and its verb about sixteen times in four chapters, more densely than anywhere else in Paul, and written from prison awaiting a capital verdict.
  • PARTNERSHIP (koinōnia) — with real money behind the word; the letter is a receipt as much as a meditation.
  • HUMILITY, and the mind of Christ — the hymn of chapter 2 given as the pattern for settling a congregation's squabbles.
  • CITIZENSHIP — a Roman colony told that its true commonwealth is in the heavens (1:27; 3:20).
  • CONTENTMENT and INDIFFERENCE TO OUTCOME — 'whether through life or through death'; 'I have learned to be content in whatever circumstances'.
  • SUFFERING AS GIFT — 'to you it was given… not only to believe in him but also to suffer for him' (1:29), which becomes 'the fellowship of his sufferings' in chapter 3.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. The text is secure and chapter 1 is textually calm apart from the order of vv16-17, which the Byzantine tradition reverses (and the KJV with it, so its verse 16 is our 17). The letter's real critical questions are literary rather than textual: whether 2:6-11 is a quoted hymn (widely held), and whether the sharp break at 3:1 marks the joining of two or three separate notes — the sudden shift of tone at 'finally, my brothers' being the usual evidence.

Looking forward

Philippians holds the highest Christology in Paul and states it as a poem rather than an argument. The hymn of 2:6-11 has him existing 'in the form of God', not clutching at equality, EMPTYING himself (ekenōsen — the word behind 'kenosis'), taking a slave's form, obedient to the point of death, 'death on a cross' — and then exalted and given the name at which every knee bows, which in Isaiah 45:23 is the name of Jehovah. Chapter 1 supplies the personal side of the same thing: 'to live is Christ and to die is gain', and 'Christ will be magnified in my body, whether through life or through death'. Chapter 3 adds the cost: everything Paul had is counted loss 'because of the surpassing worth of knowing Christ Jesus my Lord'.

Where the debates are

Where the letter was written FROM (Rome, Ephesus or Caesarea), and therefore when. Whether it is ONE letter or two or three joined at the seam of 3:1. Whether 2:6-11 is a quoted hymn and, if so, whether harpagmon at 2:6 means Christ did not SEIZE equality he lacked or did not CLING to equality he had — one of the most consequential single words in the New Testament. Who Euodia and Syntyche were quarrelling about, and who the unnamed 'true yokefellow' of 4:3 is. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
