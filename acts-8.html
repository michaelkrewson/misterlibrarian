‘On that day’ after Stephen’s stoning: a great persecution arises against the congregation in Jerusalem, and everyone but the apostles is scattered through Judea and Samaria; Saul, who was approving the killing, ravages the congregation house by house. Philip, one of the Seven, goes down to Samaria and proclaims the Christ; a magician named Simon believes and is baptized; Peter and John come down from Jerusalem, the Spirit is received, and Simon offers money for the authority to give it. Philip is then sent south on the road to Gaza, meets an Ethiopian eunuch reading Isaiah, baptizes him, and is found at Azotus and on his way to Caesarea.
Tied to chapter 7 by ‘on that day’ (v1) and otherwise undated: the chapter names no year and gives no interval between Philip’s preaching in Samaria, the journey of Peter and John from Jerusalem and back, and the meeting on the road to Gaza. Acts 9:1, which follows, has Saul ‘still breathing threat and murder’ against the disciples (Acts 9). The chapter gives no date for the eunuch’s queen, whose title, Candace, is not a name, and no date for Simon beyond this scene. This library does not supply a year.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · συνευδοκῶν … ἐλυμαίνετο syneudokōn … elymaineto — Saul in Acts 8:1–3: “approving his killing,” ravaging the congregation house by house, and everyone scattered but the apostles

“Approving.” Synedokōn is the verb of syn, “with,” and eudokeō, “to be pleased”: to be pleased along with. It stands in six verses of the New Testament: here, Luke 11:48 (“you are witnesses, and you approve of the deeds of your fathers; for they killed them”), Romans 1:32, Acts 22:20, where Paul says he was “standing by and approving” at Stephen’s death, and 1 Corinthians 7:12–13 (not yet on these pages), where it is a spouse who “consents” to stay. The noun in “his killing,” anairesis, stands in this verse alone in the New Testament; at 22:20 the same event is told with the verb, “of those killing him.” The sentence gives Saul no act, only a stance, and Luke has just placed him at the garments (7:58). KJV and ASV “consenting unto his death,” Geneva “consented to his death,” NIV “approved of their killing him,” NWT 1984 “approving of the murder of him” (the 2013 revision “approved of his murder”), TLB “in complete agreement with the killing of Stephen.” The Douay prints this clause at the end of its own Acts 7:59, and begins its chapter 8 at “And at that time.”

“Congregation,” and the scattering. Ekklēsia is read “congregation” throughout (dictionary); in Acts the word has stood at 5:11 and 7:38, and here it is the third use and the first for Jerusalem’s congregation under attack, and it comes again at v3. The verb “scattered,” diesparēsan, is diaspeirō, built on speirō, “to sow”; it stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, v4, and Acts 11:19, which restarts the story in the same words — Hoi men oun diasparentes diēlthon, “So then, those who had been scattered went through” — “from the affliction that came over Stephen.” Everyone but the apostles is scattered through “the regions of Judea and Samaria,” the two places that Acts 1:8 had named between Jerusalem and “the end of the earth.” The apostles are in Jerusalem again at v14. KJV and ASV “scattered abroad,” Douay “dispersed,” NIV and NWT 1984 “scattered,” TLB “fled.” KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva and NIV print “church” for the Greek ekklēsia; the NWT 1984 prints “congregation.”

“Devout men” and “great lamentation.” Eulabēs, “devout,” is Luke’s adjective alone in the New Testament, in four verses: Simeon at Luke 2:25, the Jews gathered at Pentecost (Acts 2:5, “devout men from every nation”), these men, and Ananias of Damascus at Acts 22:12. The text does not say whether the men who carried Stephen away were believers or Jews of the older piety, and this translation leaves it open. The verb synekomisan is a verb of this verse alone in the New Testament, “carried away, gathered up”; burial is not named in the Greek but is what the mourning implies. KJV “carried Stephen to his burial,” NWT 1984 “carried Stephen to the burial,” ASV “buried Stephen,” NIV “buried Stephen,” Douay “took order for Stephen’s funeral.” Kopetos, “lamentation,” likewise stands in this verse alone in the New Testament; in the Greek of Genesis 50:10 (Brenton’s text) the same two words, kopeton megan, are the great mourning for Jacob, in the Hebrew “a great and very heavy lamentation.” KJV and ASV “great lamentation,” NWT 1984 “a great lamentation,” NIV “mourned deeply.”

“Ravaging” — and “house after house.” Elymaineto is lymainomai, which stands in this verse alone in the New Testament; in the Greek of Psalm 80:13 (79:14 there) it is what “the boar out of the wood” does to the vine, elymēnato autēn sys ek drymou (Brenton’s text; the Psalm is not yet on these pages). The imperfect says it went on. “House after house,” kata tous oikous, is the plural of the phrase that described the congregation itself: “breaking bread from house to house” (kat’ oikon, Acts 2:46) and “every day in the temple and from house to house they did not stop teaching” (Acts 5:42). The pair “men and women” (andras te kai gynaikas) comes again in Saul’s own accounts, at Acts 9:2 and 22:4, and in this chapter at v12, where Philip baptizes “both men and women.” KJV “made havock of the church” and Douay “made havock,” ASV “laid waste the church,” NIV “began to destroy the church,” NWT 1984 “began to deal outrageously with the congregation” (the 2013 revision “began to ravage”), TLB “like a wild man, going everywhere to devastate the believers.” KJV “haling men and women,” ASV “dragging men and women.”

Verses 4–8 · εὐαγγελιζόμενοι … ἐκήρυσσεν euangelizomenoi … ekēryssen — bringing the good news (euangelizomai) versus proclaiming (kēryssō) in Acts 8:4–5: the scattered, Philip in Samaria, and why the Samaritans were not the audience anyone expected

Two verbs for speaking. The scattered “went through the land euangelizomenoi” (v4), and Philip “was ekēryssen-ing” the Christ (v5). Euangelizomai, “to bring good news” (dictionary), is the verb this chapter keeps: it stands in five of its verses — vv4, 12, 25, 35 and 40 — of the sixteen verses of Acts that have it (5:42 is the earlier one). Kēryssō, “to proclaim as a herald,” stands in one verse of this chapter, v5, and in nine verses of Acts altogether (the others are 9:20, 10:37, 10:42, 13:24, 15:21, 19:13, 20:25 and 28:31). The object is different each time: “the word” (v4), “the Christ” (v5), “the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ” (v12), and, with the eunuch, “Jesus” (v35). The verbs differ, and so does what each is said to carry; the Greek does not rank the scattered believers’ good news below Philip’s heralding. KJV “went every where preaching the word,” ASV “went about preaching the word,” Douay “went about preaching the word of God,” NIV “preached the word wherever they went,” NWT 1984 “went through the land declaring the good news of the word,” TLB “went everywhere preaching the Good News about Jesus.” At v5 KJV and Douay “preached Christ,” ASV “proclaimed unto them the Christ,” NIV “proclaimed the Messiah,” NWT 1984 “began to preach the Christ.” “The Christ” is the Greek ton christon, kept as at Acts 5:42; “Messiah” is the Hebrew-derived word for the same title.

Philip, and the city of Samaria. Philip is the second of the Seven named after Stephen at Acts 6:5; the encyclopedia entry is Philip the evangelist. Nothing in chapters 6 or 8 says he was sent to Samaria; he “went down” (katelthōn). The Greek has the article, tēn polin tēs Samareias, “the city of Samaria”: NIV “a city in Samaria”; KJV, ASV and NWT 1984 keep the article. The printed text is a critical edition’s choice; the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions in the archive omit the article (“a city of Samaria”) and Tischendorf’s has it. Whether the city is Samaria itself or an unnamed city of the district Luke does not say, and the name “Samaria” in v9 is the whole “nation of Samaria.” See Samaria.

The Samaritans, in the Gospels that are on these pages. The relation was not easy, and the pages show how. A Samaritan village “did not receive him, because his face was set toward Jerusalem,” and James and John asked whether to tell fire to come down on it (Luke 9:52–54). Jesus’ instruction to the Twelve was, “do not enter a city of the Samaritans” (Matthew 10:5). The woman at the well says, “Jews have no dealings with Samaritans” (John 4:9), and says that “our fathers worshiped on this mountain, and you say that the place where one must worship is in Jerusalem” (John 4:20), and “many of the Samaritans believed in him” (4:39). The one who comes upon the wounded man is “a certain Samaritan” (Luke 10:33); the one leper of ten who returns is a Samaritan, “this foreigner” (Luke 17:16–18). So when the crowds in the city “paid attention with one accord” to a Jewish preacher, the scene answers all of that. “With one accord,” homothymadon, is the word of the congregation at Acts 1:14, 2:46, 4:24 and 5:12, and of the mob at 7:57, and here it describes the Samaritan crowds. And Acts 1:8’s “Judea and Samaria” is now being walked.

Signs, spirits, joy. The unclean spirits “cry with a loud voice” as they leave, the same loud cry (phōnē megalē) as the man who runs to Jesus at Mark 5:7. A grammatical seam is in the printed Greek of v7: it reads polloi, “many,” as the subject of “were coming out,” so that the people with the spirits would be the ones coming out; the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions read pollōn, “from many,” and a singular verb. The sense is the spirits leaving the many, which is what this translation prints, and all seven shelf versions have it so: KJV “came out of many,” Geneva “came out of many,” Douay “went out,” TLB “cast out,” ASV “from many” … “they came out,” NIV “impure spirits came out of many,” NWT 1984 “these would cry out with a loud voice and come out.” “Signs”: ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 “signs”; KJV, Douay and TLB “miracles.” KJV “great joy,” ASV “much joy,” NWT 1984 “a great deal of joy,” TLB “much joy”; the Greek of the printed text is polylē chara, “much joy,” and the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions have chara megalē, “great joy.”

Verses 9–13 · μαγεύων … ἡ Δύναμις τοῦ θεοῦ ἡ καλουμένη Μεγάλη mageuōn … hē Dynamis tou theou hē kaloumenē Megalē — Simon the mageuōn in Acts 8:9–13: “the Power of God called Great,” the verb of amazement, and a man who believes and is baptized

The magic-worker. Mageuōn, “practicing magic,” and its noun mageia in v11 stand in these two verses alone in the New Testament. Related is magos, the word of Matthew 2’s Magi (Matthew 2:1) and of the “magician, a false prophet” at Acts 13:6 and 13:8, where the name Elymas is explained as “the magos.” Luke gives Simon no description beyond the verb, the nation he amazed, and his claim; he does not say how the magic worked. KJV “used sorcery, and bewitched the people,” ASV “used sorcery, and amazed the people,” NIV “had practiced sorcery in the city and amazed all the people,” NWT 1984 “practicing magical arts and amazing the nation of” Samaria, Douay “had been a magician in that city, seducing the people,” TLB “formerly been a sorcerer.” This translation keeps “magic” because that is the word, and keeps “sorcery” for pharmakeia (dictionary), a different word.

Amazed, amazing, amazed. The verb of astonishment, existēmi, is the thread of the passage: Simon existanōn the nation (v9), had exestakenai them with his magic (v11), and then Simon himself existato, was amazed, at the signs and great powers Philip’s preaching accompanied (v13). The first two are done by him to others, the third happens to him. The verb is the amazement of the Pentecost crowd at Acts 2:7 and 2:12, and of the Jewish believers who “were amazed” at Acts 10:45. ASV and NIV “amazed” at v9, KJV “bewitched.” At v13 KJV “wondered,” ASV “was amazed,” NIV “astonished,” NWT 1984 “was amazed,” Douay “being astonished, wondered.”

“Someone great” and “the Power called Great.” Simon says he is tina heauton megan, “someone great” (v9); the people, “from small to great” (apo mikrou heōs megalou), say Houtos estin hē Dynamis tou theou hē kaloumenē Megalē (v10): “This man is the Power of God that is called Great.” “Great” is this chapter’s word: a great persecution (v1), a great lamentation (v2), a loud voice (v7), someone great (v9), the Great Power (v10), and at v13 the “great powers” that Simon sees — dynameis megalas, the very two words of his title, now in the plural, and in Philip’s hands. Dynamis is “power” (dictionary); “the Power” is also a name for God that the Gospels have on Jesus’ lips: he will be “seated at the right hand of the power of God” (Luke 22:69), and “seated at the right hand of the Power” (Mark 14:62). Whether the Samaritans meant a divine intermediary, an angelic being, or a title of honor, the text does not say. A later Christian tradition makes this Simon the source of heresies; that is not in Acts, and this library has not checked those later sources. At v13 the printed Greek has dynameis megalas; the Byzantine edition reads “powers and signs happening,” without “great.” The printed Greek at v10 has hē kaloumenē, “that is called”; the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions in the archive read simply hē megalē, “the great Power of God.” KJV “the great power of God,” Geneva “that great power of God,” ASV “that power of God which is called Great,” Douay “the power of God, which is called great,” NIV “the Great Power of God,” NWT 1984 “the Power of God, which can be called Great” (the 2013 revision “which is called Great”). TLB has the crowd speak of him “as the Messiah,” which the Greek does not say.

“He also believed.” The narrator says episteusen — Simon “believed” and was baptized, in the same sentence-shape as the crowd of v12 and with “also,” kai autos. What kind of belief is not said, and the story goes on to v21; readings differ, and this translation does not settle it. He “kept devoting himself” to Philip: proskarterōn, the verb of the believers at Acts 1:14, 2:42 and 2:46 (dictionary). “Paid attention,” proseichon, is what the crowds do to Philip (v6) and, twice, to Simon (vv10–11). KJV “continued with Philip,” ASV “continued with Philip,” NIV “followed Philip everywhere,” NWT 1984 “was in constant attendance upon Philip,” Douay “adhered to Philip.”

Verses 14–17 · οὐδέπω … ἐπιπεπτωκός … ἐπετίθεσαν τὰς χεῖρας oudepō … epipeptōkos … epetithesan tas cheiras — Laying on of hands and the delayed Holy Spirit in Acts 8:14–17: “not yet fallen,” Peter and John, and the readings that differ

What the Greek says. The apostles in Jerusalem hear that Samaria “has received the word of God” — dedektai, dechomai, the verb of welcome — and send Peter and John, who pray “that they might receive Holy Spirit” (the verb is a different one, lambanō, which stands again at vv17 and 19). The reason is v16: oudepō gar ēn ep’ oudeni autōn epipeptōkos, “for it had not yet fallen on any of them.” The verb epipiptō with the Spirit stands in three verses of Acts: here, in Acts 10:44 (“the Holy Spirit fell upon all who were hearing the word”), and in 11:15, where Peter says “the Holy Spirit fell on them, just as also on us at the beginning.” The baptism is “into the name of the Lord Jesus” (eis to onoma), where Acts 2:38 reads “on the name of Jesus Christ” (epi tō onomati) and Acts 10:48 “in the name” (en). KJV “fallen upon none of them,” ASV “fallen upon none of them,” NIV “had not yet come on any of them,” NWT 1984 “had not yet fallen upon any one of them,” TLB “had not come upon any of them.” The Greek of v17 has the imperfect twice, “they were laying … and they were receiving,” a repeated action; KJV and ASV “Then laid they their hands on them, and they received,” NIV supplies the subject, “Peter and John placed their hands.” The verb “lay on” is epitithēmi (dictionary), the verb of Acts 6:6, where hands were laid on the Seven; in Acts the phrase “laid hands on” with this verb also stands at 9:12, 9:17, 13:3, 19:6 and 28:8. The NWT 1984 prints “holy spirit” in lower case and without an article here and at vv15, 17 and 19, a doctrinal rendering: the New World Translation’s publishers understand the spirit as God’s active force, and the Greek pneuma hagion, which has no article at vv15, 17 and 19, does not itself decide the matter; the KJV prints “Holy Ghost.”

The readings. The passage is cited in more than one way, and this translation prints what is there and does not choose. In traditions that practice confirmation as a rite separate from baptism, v17 is read as the apostles’ laying on of hands completing what the Samaritans’ baptism began; in traditions that look for the Spirit as a distinct experience after conversion, the Samaritans are an example of believers baptized who had not yet received; others read the delay as unique to this moment, a sign that Samaria’s reception had to be confirmed from Jerusalem, so that, as at Acts 1:8, the witness might be seen to run from one center; and still others read the Samaritans’ faith as incomplete until now, since the narrator says only that they believed Philip (v12). Acts itself does not give one order. At Acts 2:38 the promise follows baptism; at 10:44–48 the Spirit falls before baptism and water follows. At 19:1–7 men baptized into John’s baptism are baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus, and when Paul lays hands on them the Spirit comes. Here the sequence is faith, baptism, hands, Spirit, with a gap that the text says only is “not yet.” Who is meant by the “hands” is not narrowed either: Philip, who baptizes, is not said to have laid hands on them, and the apostles are not said to have baptized.

Verses 18–19 · προσήνεγκεν αὐτοῖς χρήματα … τὴν ἐξουσίαν ταύτην prosēnenken autois chrēmata … tēn exousian tautēn — Simony in Acts 8:18–19: Simon offers money for “this authority,” and the word that took his name

What he asked for. Simon “saw that through the laying on of the apostles’ hands the Spirit was given” — the printed Greek has to pneuma, “the Spirit,” and the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions add to hagion, “the Holy.” He offers chrēmata, “money, funds,” and asks for exousia, “authority,” the power to give the Spirit by laying on his own hands: “so that anyone on whom I lay hands may receive Holy Spirit.” What he asks to buy is the ability to confer, not the Spirit for himself. The noun chrēma in the sense of money stands in four verses of Acts: 4:37, where Barnabas “brought the money and laid it at the apostles’ feet,” here at vv18 and 20, and Acts 24:26, where Felix hopes Paul will give him money. KJV and ASV “power,” Douay “power,” TLB “power,” NIV “ability,” NWT 1984 “authority.” NIV “offered them money,” TLB “offered money to buy this power.”

“Simony.” The English word simony, for buying or selling a sacred office or spiritual thing, is named for this Simon. Reference works report that the word was spread by Gregory I, pope 590–604, who called such trade the “simoniac heresy,” and that church legislation against paying for spiritual things is traced back as far as the Council of Elvira, about 305; this library has not read those sources itself. Dante’s Inferno, Canto 19, is reported to put popes among the simonists. None of that is in Acts. What Acts has is the offer here and Peter’s answer in v20, that the gift of God is not to be acquired with money; the later word is built on the offer alone.

Verses 20–24 · τὸ ἀργύριόν σου σὺν σοὶ εἴη εἰς ἀπώλειαν … χολὴν πικρίας to argyrion sou syn soi eiē eis apōleian … cholēn pikrias — “Your silver go with you to destruction” in Acts 8:20–24: Peter’s answer to Simon, “portion nor lot,” “gall of bitterness,” and the “Lord” Simon is told to ask

The curse, in a wish. To argyrion sou syn soi eiē eis apōleian is a wish in the optative: “May your silver be with you to destruction.” Peter has no silver of his own: Acts 3:6 has “Silver and gold I do not have.” The phrase eis apōleian, “to destruction,” stands in five verses of the New Testament: here, Romans 9:22, Hebrews 10:39 and Revelation 17:8 and 17:11 (none yet on these pages). The wish names the silver, with Simon alongside it (“with you”), and v22 then offers a way out. The charge is the verb enomisas, “you thought, supposed,” and the infinitive ktasthai, “to acquire”: the same verb as Judas, who “acquired a field with the wages of his wickedness” (Acts 1:18). The “gift,” dōrea, is the word of “the gift of the Holy Spirit” at Acts 2:38 and of the Gentiles’ at Acts 10:45, and at 11:17; the context here makes it the Spirit. ASV, NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 “silver”; KJV, Geneva, NIV and TLB “money.” The Douay reads “Keep thy money to thyself, to perish with thee,” a different shape from the wish. KJV “the gift of God may be purchased with money,” ASV “obtain the gift of God with money,” NIV “buy the gift of God with money,” NWT 1984 “you thought through money to get possession of the free gift of God” (the 2013 revision: “you thought you could acquire the free gift of God with money”).

“Portion nor lot,” and a heart not straight. Ouk estin soi meris oude klēros, “you have neither portion nor lot,” is the Greek of the Levites’ standing in Deuteronomy: “he has no portion or inheritance with you” (Deuteronomy 12:12, 14:27, 14:29; the Greek is ouk estin autō meris oude klēros). That is a share in what belongs to God’s people, here “in this word” (en tō logō toutō), which KJV, ASV, Douay and NWT 1984 print “matter” and NIV “ministry.” “Not straight before God” is ouk estin euthēia enanti tou theou; in the Greek of Psalm 77:37 (78:37 in the Hebrew; not yet on these pages) the line is “their heart was not straight with him,” hē kardia autōn ouk euthēia met’ autou (Brenton’s text), and in the Hebrew lo nakhon immo, “not steadfast with him.” The Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions read enōpion for enanti, with the same meaning. KJV and ASV “not right,” NWT 1984 “not straight.” NIV “your heart is not right before God” and, a few words earlier, “no part or share.”

“Gall of bitterness” and “bond of wickedness.” The phrase eis cholēn pikrias kai syndesmon adikias is two Old Testament lines welded. The first is Deuteronomy 29:17 in the Hebrew (29:18 in English): “a root bearing gall and wormwood” (Deuteronomy 29:17, shoresh poreh rosh vela’anah), which the Greek has as rhiza anō phyousa en cholē kai pikria, “a root springing up in gall and bitterness” (Brenton’s text); Acts compresses the pair into a genitive, cholē pikrias. The root of Deuteronomy is a man who hears the curse and blesses himself in his heart; Hebrews 12:15 (not yet on these pages) takes the same line as “a root of bitterness springing up.” The second is the Greek of Isaiah 58:6, lue panta syndesmon adikias, “loose every bond of wickedness” (Brenton’s text; Isaiah 58 is not yet on these pages). The noun adikia is the word of Judas’ “wages of wickedness” at Acts 1:18, which is why it is “wickedness” here; kakia in v22 is “evil.” Cholē stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Matthew 27:34 (dictionary). The preposition is eis, “into,” used where en, “in,” would be expected, and it can carry motion: you are “in” it or “turning into” it. KJV, ASV and Geneva “in the gall of bitterness, and in the bond of iniquity,” Douay “in the bonds of iniquity,” NIV “full of bitterness and captive to sin,” NWT 1984 “a poisonous gall and a bond of unrighteousness” (the 1984 edition; the 2013 revision “a bitter poison and a slave of unrighteousness”), TLB “jealousy and sin in your heart,” which is not the Greek.

“The Lord” — Jehovah or Jesus? Peter tells Simon to ask tou kyriou (v22), Simon asks him to ask ton kyrion (v24), and in v25 the apostles speak “the word of the Lord.” This translation prints “Lord” at all three, because kyrios in Acts can mean the God of Israel or Jesus, and nothing in these verses says which: v16 has just said “the Lord Jesus,” and Stephen has just prayed to “Lord Jesus” (Acts 7:59). The NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah” at all three, taking kyrios as God; KJV, Geneva and Douay have “God” at v22 where ASV, NIV and the printed Greek have “Lord,” and the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions read theou, “God,” at v22 as well (and, in the Byzantine edition, at v24). “Of the Lord” at v25 is kept as at Acts 15:35. Simon’s answer asks for protection and does not repent aloud: “so that nothing of what you have said may come upon me.” Where the story goes from here, in Acts, is not told. KJV “Pray ye to the Lord for me,” ASV “Pray ye for me to the Lord,” NIV “Pray to the Lord for me,” NWT 1984 “Make supplication for me to Jehovah,” TLB “Pray for me.”

Verses 25–26 · κατὰ μεσημβρίαν … αὕτη ἐστὶν ἔρημος kata mesēmbrian … hautē estin erēmos — Acts 8:25–26: an angel of Jehovah, “toward the south” or “at noon,” the road to Gaza, and what “This is desert” refers to

Two directions in one preposition phrase. Kata mesēmbrian is “toward the south” or “at midday”: mesēmbria is both the noon hour and the quarter of the sky where the sun stands at noon. KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and NWT 1984 take “south”; TLB adds a time, “arriving around noon,” and drops the direction. The word is the one the Greek Old Testament uses of Ashdod in Zephaniah 2:4, where it reads “Azotus shall be cast out at noon” (Azōtos mesēmbrias ekriphēsetai, Brenton’s text) beside “Gaza shall be plundered”; in the Hebrew, ‘azzah ‘azuvah, “Gaza shall be forsaken,” and “Ashdod, at noon they shall drive it out” (Zephaniah 2:4; the chapter is not yet on these pages). Whether Luke meant this verse to sound in the ear is not said; the two cities named in it are the two this chapter names, Gaza here and Azotus at v40.

“This is desert.” Autē estin erēmos is a feminine pronoun, and both words it could refer to are feminine: tēn hodon, “the road,” and Gazan, “Gaza.” The Greek does not choose, and this translation does not. The shelf divides by punctuation: KJV “unto Gaza, which is desert” (the clause attaches to Gaza), ASV “the same is desert,” Douay “this is desert,” Geneva “which is waste,” NIV “the road—the desert road—that goes down,” NWT 1984 “(This is a desert road.),” TLB “through the Gaza Desert.” The two readings have each been argued: that Luke calls the road empty, which suits a story of a man met alone on it; or that the city of Gaza was “desert,” a place laid waste. A reference work reports that the Hasmonean king Alexander Jannaeus overthrew Gaza about 96 BC and that it was resettled and rebuilt after Pompey in 63 BC, which is before Luke wrote; this library has not checked the ancient sources for that. Which road from Jerusalem to Gaza is meant is not said either. See Gaza.

“An angel of Jehovah.” The Greek is angelos kyriou, the form the Greek Old Testament uses for Jehovah’s angel, as at Acts 5:19, so this translation reads “Jehovah.” The NWT 1984 has “Jehovah’s angel” (both editions); KJV “the angel of the Lord”; ASV, Douay and NIV “an angel of the Lord.” The apostles’ return in v25 is told with two participles, diamarturamenoi and lalēsantes, “having solemnly testified and spoken,” and two imperfects, and on the way they “were bringing the good news” to many Samaritan villages: the verb of v4 is now Peter’s and John’s. KJV “preached the gospel in many villages of the Samaritans,” Geneva “towns,” Douay “to many countries of the Samaritans,” NIV “in many Samaritan villages,” NWT 1984 “declaring the good news to many villages of the” Samaritans.

Verses 27–29 · Αἰθίοψ εὐνοῦχος δυνάστης Κανδάκης … τῆς γάζης Aithiops eunouchos dynastēs Kandakēs … tēs gazēs — The Ethiopian eunuch in Acts 8:27–29: Candace as a title, dynastēs, the treasure (gaza) beside Gaza, and a man reading Isaiah in a chariot

“Ethiopian.” Aithiops is the Greek Old Testament’s name for Cush, the kingdom south of Egypt: where the Hebrew of Psalm 68:32 has Kush tarits yadav le-Elohim, “Cush shall hasten to stretch out its hands to God,” the Greek has Aithiopia prophthasei cheira autēs tō Theō (Psalm 67:32 there, Brenton’s text; the Psalm is not yet on these pages), and in Zephaniah 3:10 the Hebrew “beyond the rivers of Cush” is the Greek “from the ends of the rivers of Ethiopia” (neither yet on these pages). See Cush. The encyclopedia places Cush in ancient Nubia, in modern Sudan.

“Candace” is a title. The Greek has Kandakēs basilissēs Aithiopōn, “of Candace, queen of the Ethiopians,” and the NIV prints the word as “Kandake” and adds a gloss in parentheses, “queen of the Ethiopians.” A reference work reports that kandake was a Meroitic term for a queen or queen mother of the Kingdom of Kush, that Greek and Roman writers mistook it for a personal name, and that Strabo, Pliny the Elder and Cassius Dio use it; this library has not read those authors, and the point is reported, not verified. In the New Testament it stands here alone. KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva and NWT 1984 print “Candace” as a name; TLB “Candace the queen.” See Candace.

“Eunuch” and “high official.” Eunouchos in the Greek Old Testament is also a court official, whether or not castrated (dictionary); here it stands with dynastēs, which stands in three verses of the New Testament: Mary’s “he has brought down rulers from thrones” (Luke 1:52), here, and 1 Timothy 6:15 (not yet on these pages), where it names God as “the blessed and only Sovereign.” It means a man of power. Whether he was a eunuch in the body and so barred from the assembly by Deuteronomy 23:2 (which bars the man “wounded by crushing” and the man “whose member is cut off” from “the assembly of Jehovah”), or an official of the court by title, the Greek does not say; Isaiah 56:3–5 (not yet on these pages) has Jehovah tell “the eunuchs who keep my Sabbaths” that they will have in his house “a hand and a name” (yad vashem) “better than sons and daughters,” the passage to which the Deuteronomy page already points. Nor does Luke say whether he was a Jew, a proselyte, a God-fearer or none; the text says only that he “had come to Jerusalem to worship” (proskynēsōn) and has a scroll of Isaiah. KJV, ASV and Douay “of great authority,” NWT 1984 “a man in power,” NIV “an important official,” Geneva “chief Governor,” TLB “the Treasurer of Ethiopia.”

The treasure, and Gaza. The man was epi pasēs tēs gazēs autēs, “over all her treasure.” Gaza as a common noun, “treasure,” is a loanword usually said to be Persian; it stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and its compound gazophylakion, “treasury,” stands in four verses (Mark 12:41, 43, Luke 21:1, John 8:20; dictionary). In the Greek the city of the previous verse, Gazan, and the treasure of this one, gazēs, share one stem and one spelling, apart from the case ending. Whether Luke noticed it the text does not say. KJV and ASV “treasure,” NIV “treasury,” NWT 1984 “treasure,” Douay “treasures.” He was “sitting in his chariot and reading the prophet Isaiah”; the imperfect aneginōsken is a continuing action, aloud, as v30 shows; NWT 1984 “reading aloud,” NIV “reading the Book of Isaiah.” “Go near and join yourself” is proselthe kai kollēthēti, and the speaker is “the Spirit” without a further name. KJV and ASV “Go near, and join thyself to this chariot,” NWT 1984 “Approach and join yourself to this chariot,” NIV “Go to that chariot and stay near it.”

Verses 30–31 · Ἆρά γε γινώσκεις ἃ ἀναγινώσκεις; … ὁδηγήσει Ara ge ginōskeis ha anaginōskeis? … hodēgēsei — “Do you understand what you are reading?” in Acts 8:30–31: a Greek wordplay on knowing and reading, and hodēgēsei, “guide,” the road verb

Knowing and reading. Philip’s question puts two forms of one root side by side: ginōskeis, “you know,” and anaginōskeis, “you read” — ana-ginōskō, “to know again, to recognize,” the ordinary Greek verb for reading. English can print the pair only by choosing: “Do you really know what you are reading?” keeps “know,” the fixed rendering of ginōskō, and the second verb is the reading. Ara ge is a pair of particles that introduces the question, “really? indeed?” KJV, ASV and Geneva “Understandest thou what thou readest?,” NIV “Do you understand what you are reading?,” Douay “Thinkest thou that thou understandest what thou readest?,” NWT 1984 “Do you actually know what you are reading?,” TLB “Do you understand it?” The Greek has ginōskō, not syniēmi, “understand,” the verb Luke uses of the apostles’ understanding at Luke 24:45, where Jesus “opened their minds to understand the Scriptures.”

“Unless someone guides me.” The answer, Pōs gar an dunaimēn ean mē tis hodēgēsei me, is built on hodos, the road he is traveling on (v26, v36, v39): hodēgeō is “to lead on the road,” and the eunuch asks to be road-led. The verb stands in five verses of the New Testament: John 16:13, where the Spirit of truth “will guide you into all the truth” (the same form, hodēgēsei), here, Matthew 15:14 and Luke 6:39 (a blind man guiding a blind man) and Revelation 7:17 (the Lamb “will guide them to springs of the water of life”). Whether Luke has the Spirit’s promise in mind he does not say; the Spirit has just sent Philip to the chariot (v29). The printed Greek has hodēgēsei, the future; the Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions have the subjunctive hodēgēsē, the same sense. The passage he is reading, Isaiah 53 in its Greek form, is taken up in the next note. At Luke 24:27 Jesus, “beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, interpreted to them, in all the Scriptures, the things concerning himself”; Philip’s “beginning from this Scripture” at v35 uses the same participle, arxamenos. KJV “except some man should guide me,” ASV “except some one shall guide me,” Geneva “except I had a guide,” Douay “unless some man shew me,” NIV “unless someone explains it to me,” NWT 1984 “unless someone guided me,” TLB “when there is no one to instruct me.” He then “urged” Philip (parekalesen) to come up and sit with him; KJV “desired,” ASV “besought,” NWT 1984 “urged.”

Verses 32–35 · Ὡς πρόβατον ἐπὶ σφαγὴν ἤχθη … ἐν τῇ ταπεινώσει Hōs probaton epi sphagēn ēchthē … en tē tapeinōsei — Isaiah 53:7–8 in Acts 8:32–35: “like a sheep to slaughter,” the Greek wording, what the quotation leaves out, and “about whom does the prophet say this?”

The Greek, not the Hebrew. The passage Luke names, hē periochē tēs graphēs, “the passage of the Scripture,” is the Greek translation of Isaiah 53:7–8. Set against the Greek Old Testament (Brenton’s text), v32 differs in small ways: Acts has the aorist participle and the pronoun, tou keirantos auton, “the one who sheared him,” where the Greek Old Testament has tou keirontos, the present participle with no pronoun, and Acts ends to stoma autou, “his mouth,” where the Greek Old Testament has to stoma. Acts omits the clause the Greek Old Testament puts first, Kai autos dia to kekakōsthai ouk anoigei to stoma autou, “and he, because of his affliction, does not open his mouth.” At v33 Acts and the Greek Old Testament read alike through “his life is taken from the earth,” and there Acts stops: the Greek continues, “because of the iniquities of my people he was led to death.” The Hebrew differs more. On this site’s own Isaiah 53 the verse reads “He was pressed, and he himself was afflicted, and he does not open his mouth. Like a lamb to the slaughter he is led, and like a ewe before her shearers is dumb,” “from restraint and from judgement he was taken, and as for his generation, who gives it a thought? For he was cut off from the land of the living — from my people’s transgression, a stroke to them.” Where the Hebrew has me’otser, “from restraint,” the Greek has en tē tapeinōsei, “in his humiliation,” and where the Hebrew has the plural “shearers” and the verb of a lamb “led,” the Greek has one shearer and the aorist ēchthē, “was led.” Where the Hebrew has mi yesoche’ach, “who gives it a thought?,” the Greek has tis diēgēsetai, “who will recount?” See the note on that page for the individual and corporate readings of the Servant, which this library sets side by side and does not decide.

The words of the passage. Here tapeinōsis, “humiliation,” comes first and then “his judgment was taken away.” “Judgment,” krisis, can be the verdict or the justice owed him, as NIV “deprived of justice” has it. “Mute,” aphōnos, is “without voice”: Douay “without voice.” KJV “He was led as a sheep to the slaughter; and like a lamb dumb before his shearer,” ASV “as a lamb before his shearer is dumb,” NIV “as a lamb before its shearer is silent,” NWT 1984 “a lamb that is voiceless before its shearer.” At v33 KJV “In his humiliation his judgment was taken away: and who shall declare his generation?,” NIV “In his humiliation he was deprived of justice. Who can speak of his descendants?,” NWT 1984 “During his humiliation the judgment was taken away from him. Who will tell the details of his generation?,” Geneva “In his humility his judgment hath been exalted,” Douay “In humility his judgment was taken away,” TLB “justice was denied him; and who can express the wickedness of the people of his generation?” The Byzantine and Textus Receptus editions add autou, “his,” after “humiliation.” “Generation,” genea, can mean contemporaries, descendants or a lifespan; NIV “descendants” chooses one.

“About whom?” The eunuch’s question, peri tinos ho prophētēs legei touto peri heautou ē peri heterou tinos, is the question this passage has been read two ways to answer; the Servant’s identity is not given in the quoted lines. Philip “opened his mouth,” the Greek idiom for beginning to speak, “and, beginning from this Scripture, brought him the good news of Jesus”: euēngelisato autō ton Iēsoun, “Jesus” as the thing proclaimed. What Philip said is not given. KJV “preached unto him Jesus,” NIV “told him the good news about Jesus,” NWT 1984 “declared to him the good news about Jesus,” TLB “used many others to tell him about Jesus,” which adds to the Greek.

Verses 36–38 · τί κωλύει με βαπτισθῆναι; … [verse 37] ti kōlyei me baptisthēnai? … [verse 37] — The Ethiopian eunuch’s baptism in Acts 8:36–38 and the missing verse 37: “If you believe with all your heart,” and why the critical Greek text leaves it out

“What prevents me?” Ti kōlyei me baptisthēnai, “What prevents me from being baptized?,” has an answering question in Peter’s mouth at Acts 10:47, mēti to hydōr dynatai kōlysai tis tou mē baptisthēnai toutous, “Can anyone forbid the water, so that these should not be baptized?” Whether the question has the exclusion of Deuteronomy 23:2 in mind the text does not say. They “came to some water” (epi ti hydōr), which says nothing about its size or kind: KJV and ASV “a certain water,” NIV “some water,” NWT 1984 “a certain body of water,” TLB “a small body of water.” “Both went down into the water” and “came up out of the water” (v39) are said of both men, Philip and the eunuch; the verbs are of motion, and the Greek does not say what was done in the water beyond “he baptized him.” KJV “what doth hinder me to be baptized?,” ASV “what doth hinder me to be baptized?,” Geneva “what doth let me to be baptized?,” Douay “what doth hinder me from being baptized?,” NIV “What can stand in the way of my being baptized?,” NWT 1984 “what prevents me from getting baptized?,” TLB “Why can’t I be baptized?” Ἰδοὺ at the start of the eunuch’s words is the same “look” as the narrator’s at v27.

⚠ The verse that is not here. The verse numbered 37 in the Textus Receptus line of Bibles reads, in the Greek, Eipe de ho Philippos, Ei pisteueis ex holēs tēs kardias, exestin. Apokritheis de eipe, Pisteuō ton huion tou theou einai ton Iēsoun Christon: “And Philip said, If you believe with all your heart, you may. And he answered and said, I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God.” The critical text printed here does not have it, and goes from the eunuch’s question in v36 to “And he ordered the chariot to stop” in v38, subject unstated. The Greek editions in the archive divide: the Textus Receptus prints the verse; the “Byzantine” edition prints it in brackets; the Tischendorf, text-critical and Family 35 editions leave it out. A reference work reports that the verse is absent from most Greek manuscripts and present in some, among them the sixth- or seventh-century codex E, that the Old Latin and Vulgate have it, and that Irenaeus (about 180) and Cyprian (about 250) cite it; the reading is read as a later addition that supplied a confession where the story had none, and the nineteenth-century commentator J. A. Alexander is reported to have suggested that it was omitted by scribes who disliked the delay of baptism. This library has not checked those manuscripts or those writers, and the report is given as a report. The shelf divides, and the dividing line is not early against late: the KJV, Geneva, Douay and TLB print the verse, as does the ASV text as fetched, while the NIV and the NWT 1984 leave it out (the NWT 1984, in both editions, prints the number with a dash and no text). This translation follows the earliest manuscripts and leaves the gap exactly where they leave it, so that no verse is renumbered, rather than printing words that the earliest witnesses do not carry; the question and the answer, if there was one, are given here so that a reader can see what is missing.

The story with the verse, and without it. With it, the scene has a small hinge in the middle: the eunuch asks what prevents him, Philip names one condition, “with all your heart,” and the eunuch answers in a single line of confession, “I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God,” before the chariot stops. Without it, nothing is said between the question and the water. Philip does not answer in words; the answer is the stopping of the chariot and the going down, and what the eunuch believes has to be read back from v35, where Philip “told him the good news about Jesus.” The text reads whole either way. A common explanation of how such a line enters a text, given here as a report and not as something this library has verified, is that baptismal practice asked for a confession before the water, that a scribe who knew the practice wrote the words in the margin of a copy, and that a later copyist took the marginal line for part of the text. The opposite explanation, that the line was original and was cut, is the one the suggestion of J. A. Alexander above belongs to. A reader who opens an older Bible at Acts 8 and finds a verse 37 that this page does not have is therefore looking at one of the oldest and best-known disagreements between printed Bibles, not at an omission by accident, and the choice here is the earliest manuscripts’ reading, with the other reading set out in full above.

The baptism. The subject of “he ordered the chariot to stop” is not stated; the nearest person is the eunuch, who has just spoken, and Philip is the one who “baptized him.” Compare Acts 2:41, where those who welcomed the word “were baptized”: here the baptizer is named, and in v12 the Samaritans “were being baptized” with no baptizer. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “commanded the chariot to stand still”; NIV “gave orders to stop the chariot,” NWT 1984 “commanded the chariot to halt,” TLB “He stopped the chariot.”

Verses 39–40 · πνεῦμα κυρίου ἥρπασεν τὸν Φίλιππον … Ἄζωτον … Καισάρειαν pneuma kyriou hērpasen ton Philippon … Azōton … Kaisareian — Philip “snatched away” in Acts 8:39–40: harpazō, the Spirit of Jehovah, Azotus (Ashdod) and Caesarea

“Snatched away.” Hērpasen is harpazō, “to seize, to snatch,” a verb of speed and force. In the New Testament it is the verb of Paul “snatched up” to the third heaven and to paradise (harpagenta, hērpagē, 2 Corinthians 12:2 and 12:4, not yet on these pages), of the living “snatched up in the clouds” to meet the Lord (harpagēsometha, 1 Thessalonians 4:17, not yet on these pages), and of the child of the woman “snatched up to God and to his throne” (Revelation 12:5). In the Gospels it is the verb of a strong man’s goods taken, of the crowd that would “seize” Jesus to make him king (John 6:15), and of the wolf that “snatches” the sheep (John 10:12), and at Acts 23:10 the soldiers “seize” Paul out of the council. The word is violent in its sense; the Greek does not say that Philip was moved by anything but the Spirit, and does not say how far. KJV, ASV and Geneva “caught away,” Douay “took away,” NIV “suddenly took Philip away,” NWT 1984 “quickly led Philip away” (the 2013 revision the same), TLB “caught away.”

“The Spirit of Jehovah.” The Greek is pneuma kyriou, and this translation reads “Jehovah.” Here the Greek is the formula of the Old Testament for the Spirit that lifts and carries a person away: Obadiah says to Elijah, ruach Yhwh yisa’acha, “the Spirit of Jehovah will carry you where I do not know” (1 Kings 18:12; in the Greek, pneuma Kyriou arei se, Brenton’s text), and Elisha’s young men fear that “the Spirit of Jehovah has taken him up and cast him on some mountain or into some valley” (2 Kings 2:16; in the Hebrew nesa’o ruach Yhwh); neither chapter is yet on these pages. The Greek verb there is airō, “to lift,” not harpazō; the Hebrew is “carry.” At Acts 5:9 the same two Greek words, to pneuma kyriou, are kept as “the Spirit of the Lord,” because that verse has no such formula and its referent is open (kyrios in Acts can be Jehovah or Jesus; Acts 16:7 has “the Spirit of Jesus”); here the carrying-away is the OT scene, and the same Spirit has just spoken at v29. The NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah’s spirit” at both verses; KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NIV “the Spirit of the Lord.” “The eunuch saw him no more”; “for he went on his way rejoicing,” with the verb of the road and the joy of v8; the eunuch goes ten hodon autou, “his own road.”

Azotus and Caesarea. Heurethē eis Azōton is “was found at Azotus”: the verb is the passive of “find” and the preposition is eis, “into, to,” so that Luke says where he turned up and not how he came there. Azotus is the Greek form of Ashdod (Ashdod), the Philistine city of the Zephaniah line above; the name stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. KJV and ASV “was found at Azotus,” Douay “was found in Azotus,” NWT 1984 “was found to be in Ashdod,” NIV “appeared at Azotus,” TLB “found himself at Azotus.” “Passing through” he “was bringing the good news to all the cities” — the fifth euangelizō of the chapter, in the imperfect — “until he came to Caesarea”: Caesarea, on the coast. The name stands in fifteen verses of Acts, the first here: 8:40, 9:30, 10:1, 10:24, 11:11, 12:19, 18:22, 21:8, 21:16, 23:23, 23:33, 25:1, 25:4, 25:6 and 25:13 (the Caesarea of Matthew 16:13 is Philippi, another place). At Acts 21:8 Paul stays there “in the house of Philip the evangelist, who was one of the Seven,” and none of the other five of the Seven named at Acts 6:5 is named again in Acts. See Philip the evangelist.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “congregation” for ekklēsia at vv1 and 3; “approving” for synedokeō, as at Luke 11:48; “scattered” for diaspeirō at vv1 and 4; “bringing the good news” for euangelizomai at vv4, 12, 25, 35 and 40, against “proclaiming” for kēryssō at v5; “with one accord” for homothymadon at v6, as at 1:14, 2:46, 4:24, 5:12 and 7:57; “paid attention” for prosechō at vv6, 10 and 11; “amazing / amazed” for existēmi at vv9, 11 and 13; “magic” for mageia, kept apart from “sorcery” for pharmakeia; “devoting himself” for proskarterō at v13, as at 1:14 and 2:42; “great” for megas at vv1, 2, 7, 9, 10 and 13; “fallen on” for epipiptō at v16; “laid hands” for epitithēmi at vv17 and 19, as at 6:6; “acquire” for ktaomai at v20, as at 1:18; “wickedness” for adikia at v23, as at 1:18, and “evil” for kakia at v22; “portion nor lot” for meris oude klēros, as the Deuteronomy pages have “portion or inheritance”; “make petition” for deomai at vv22 and 24; “desert” for erēmos at v26; “treasure” for gaza at v27; “know” for ginōskō at v30; “guide” for hodēgeō at v31; “snatched away” for harpazō at v39; and “Jehovah” for kyrios at vv26 and 39, where the referent is God, against “Lord” at vv16, 22, 24 and 25, where Jesus or God may be meant.

Echoes paid. Saul at the garments — Acts 7:58, at v1. “Judea and Samaria” — Acts 1:8, at vv1 and 5. “House to house” — Acts 2:46 and 5:42, at v3. Philip of the Seven — Acts 6:5, at v5. “One accord” — Acts 7:57, at v6. Hands laid — Acts 6:6, at v17. The money laid at the apostles’ feet — Acts 4:37, at v18. “Silver and gold I do not have” — Acts 3:6, at v20. Judas’ field — Acts 1:18, at vv20 and 23. The Samaritans of the Gospels — Luke 9:52, John 4:9, Luke 10:33 and 17:16, at vv5–25. The Servant — Isaiah 53:7–8, at vv32–33. The eunuch — Deuteronomy 23:2, at v27. The Spirit who guides — John 16:13, at v31. The Spirit who carries away — 1 Kings 18:12 and 2 Kings 2:16, at v39.

Echoes planted. “Those who had been scattered went through”, repeated in the same words at Acts 11:19. Saul, who tells his own account of the “men and women” at 9:2 and 22:4, and his own “standing by and approving” at 22:20. Simon, the magician, and Elymas the magos at 13:6–8. The Spirit falling at 10:44 and 11:15. The hands of Ananias at 9:12 and 9:17, and of Paul at 19:6. Peter’s question about the water at 10:47. Philip, at Caesarea in 21:8, and the four daughters who prophesy at 21:9. Caesarea, the setting of 10:1 and of Paul’s later imprisonment. The Gentile who hears in chapter 10.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether the men who carried Stephen away were believers (v2). What kind of belief Simon had (v13), and what “the Power called Great” meant to the Samaritans (v10). Why the Spirit was withheld from baptized Samaritans until the apostles came, and what that implies for baptism and for the laying on of hands (vv14–17), where the traditions differ. Whether “the Lord” is Jehovah or Jesus at vv22, 24 and 25. Whether “This is desert” is the road or the city, and whether the direction is south or noon (v26). Whether the eunuch was a eunuch in the body, and whether he was a Jew, a proselyte or neither (v27). What Philip said about Isaiah 53 (v35). The manuscript questions: the missing verse 37, the article at “the city” (v5), the grammar of v7, “called Great” (v10), “Lord” or “God” at vv22 and 24, and the plural “powers” at v13, each followed by the printed text and reported.
