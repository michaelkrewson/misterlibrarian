Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · “For all people, for kings” — four words for prayer, and “a calm and quiet life”

“Petitions, prayers, intercessions and thanksgivings be made for all people.” Four nouns for prayer: deēsis, a request (dictionary); proseuchē, prayer; enteuxis, an appeal on someone’s behalf, a word found only in this letter; and eucharistia, thanks. “For all people” is hyper pantōn anthrōpōn. The KJV reads “supplications, prayers, intercessions, and giving of thanks, be made for all men”; the NIV reads “petitions, prayers, intercession and thanksgiving be made for all people”; the NWT 1984 reads “be made concerning all sorts of men,” a narrowing the Greek does not make.

“For kings and all who are in high positions, so that we may lead a calm and quiet life.” The instruction is the prophet’s to the exiles, “seek the shalom of the city to which I have exiled you, and pray for it to Jehovah,” at Jeremiah 29:7; Paul’s “Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities” is at Romans 13:1. Ēremos, “calm,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); hēsychios, “quiet,” is the root of “work quietly” at 2 Thessalonians 3:12 and returns in verses 11 and 12 (dictionary). “Godliness” is eusebeia, a favorite word of this letter (dictionary). The KJV reads “a quiet and peaceable life in all godliness and honesty,” where “honesty” has its old sense of decency; the ASV reads “in all godliness and gravity”; the Douay reads “in all piety and chastity.”

Verses 3–7 · “Wants all people to be saved”; “one mediator”; “a ransom for all”

“God our Savior, who wants all people to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth.” “God our Savior” is the title of 1 Timothy 1:1. ⚠ Readers take the wish three ways. Many read God’s desire for every person, though not every person is saved. Augustine, in Enchiridion 103, offered among other readings that “all men” may mean every class of men, of every rank and condition. Some read a promise that all will in the end be saved. This page sets out the three and does not choose. The Greek is pantas anthrōpous, the “all people” of verse 1; the NWT 1984 reads “whose will is that all sorts of men should be saved,” the KJV “Who will have all men to be saved.” “Knowledge” is epignōsis (dictionary).

“For there is one God, and one mediator between God and humans, himself human, Christ Jesus.” “One God” is the confession of Deuteronomy 6:4, “Jehovah our God, Jehovah is one,” as Paul uses it at 1 Corinthians 8:6 and Romans 3:30. Mesitēs, “mediator,” stands in six verses: Galatians 3:19 and Galatians 3:20, this one, and Hebrews 8:6, 9:15 and 12:24 (yet to come in this translation; dictionary). “Humans” and “himself human” are the same word, anthrōpos: the mediator stands on the human side. The KJV reads “the man Christ Jesus,” the ASV “himself man, Christ Jesus,” and the NWT 1984 “a man, Christ Jesus.” ⚠ The verse has been central to an old disagreement. The Augsburg Confession (1530), in Article 21, rests its case against calling on the saints on Christ as the one mediator; the Second Vatican Council’s Lumen Gentium 60 quotes this verse and says that Mary’s role “in no way obscures or diminishes this unique mediation of Christ.” This page records both and does not choose.

“Who gave himself as a ransom for all.” Antilytron stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); the plain lytron is Jesus’ own saying, “to give his life a ransom in exchange for many,” at Mark 10:45 and Matthew 20:28 (dictionary). Here the prefix anti-, “in exchange,” is built into the word, and “many” has become “all.” ⚠ The NWT 1984 reads “a corresponding ransom for all,” and the Watchtower’s teaching builds on that wording: a perfect man, Jesus, corresponding to the perfect Adam. The Greek prefix means “in exchange”; the correspondence is the doctrine’s. The KJV reads “a ransom for all, to be testified in due time”; the Douay reads “a redemption for all.” “The testimony at its proper times” is compressed in Greek; the NIV reads “This has now been witnessed to at the proper time.”

“A herald and an apostle — I am telling the truth, I am not lying — a teacher of the nations.” The same three titles stand at 2 Timothy 1:11, “a herald and an apostle and a teacher.” The oath is the one of Romans 9:1, “I am telling the truth in Christ — I am not lying”; the Byzantine text adds “in Christ” here too, and so the KJV: “I speak the truth in Christ, and lie not.” “Teacher of the nations” matches “an apostle of nations” at Romans 11:13.

Verse 8 · “Lifting up holy hands” — the men, in every place

“So I want the men to pray in every place, lifting up holy hands without anger or argument.” “The men” is andras, males, not the anthrōpoi, “people,” of verses 1 to 5. “In every place” is the phrase of 1 Corinthians 1:2, “all those who in every place call on the name of our Lord.” Lifted hands are the posture of prayer at Psalm 141:2, “the lifting up of my hands as the evening sacrifice”. “Holy” is hosios (dictionary): the NWT 1984 reads “lifting up loyal hands,” the Douay and Geneva “pure hands,” and the KJV “holy hands.” “Argument” is dialogismos; the KJV reads “without wrath and doubting,” the ASV “without wrath and disputing.”

Verses 9–10 · “Not with braided hair and gold or pearls” — dress, modesty, and good works

“That women adorn themselves in respectable clothing, with modesty and soundness of mind.” “Adorn” and “respectable” are one root, kosmeō and kosmios, the order and arrangement of the word “cosmetic.” Katastolē, “clothing,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. Sōphrosynē, “soundness of mind,” stands in three verses: Paul’s “words of truth and of a sound mind” to Festus at Acts 26:25, this one and verse 15 (dictionary). The KJV reads “with shamefacedness and sobriety,” and the ASV “with shamefastness and sobriety,” the older form of the word; the NWT 1984 reads “with modesty and soundness of mind.”

“Not with braided hair and gold or pearls or costly garments.” Plegma, “braided hair,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); 1 Peter 3:3 makes the same list with other words. ⚠ Readers take the verse as a standing rule about dress and hair, or as aimed at display of wealth, gold and pearls and costly clothes, in a congregation of rich and poor. This page sets out both and does not choose. The KJV reads “not with broided hair, or gold, or pearls, or costly array”; the NIV reads “not with elaborate hairstyles or gold or pearls or expensive clothes.”

“As is fitting for women who profess reverence for God, with good works.” Theosebeia, “reverence for God,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); its adjective is the “devout” of John 9:31. The adornment is the works. The KJV reads “But (which becometh women professing godliness) with good works”; the NWT 1984 reads “women professing to reverence God.”

Verses 11–12 · “Let a woman learn in quietness” and “I do not permit a woman to teach” — authentein, and how the verses have been read and used

“Let a woman learn in quietness, in all submission.” The verb is a command that women learn, the place of a disciple, as Mary “sat down at the Lord’s feet and was listening to his word” at Luke 10:39 and Paul was “brought up… at the feet of Gamaliel” at Acts 22:3. “Quietness” is hēsychia, not sigē, silence: the noun stands in four verses: the crowd that grew quiet at Acts 22:2, “work quietly” at 2 Thessalonians 3:12, and verses 11 and 12 here (dictionary); its adjective is the “quiet life” asked for everyone in verse 2. The KJV and NWT 1984 read “in silence,” the ASV “in quietness.” “Submission” is hypotagē (dictionary); the verse does not say to whom.

“I do not permit a woman to teach or to have dominion over a man; she is to be in quietness.” Authenteō stands in one verse of the New Testament, and its meaning is disputed (dictionary). ⚠ The shelf shows the range: the KJV and Geneva read “to usurp authority over the man”; the Douay reads “to use authority over the man,” after the Vulgate’s dominari in virum; the ASV reads “to have dominion over a man”; the NWT 1984 reads “to exercise authority over a man”; the NIV reads “to assume authority over a man,” a 2011 change from its 1984 wording, have authority, that was criticized from both sides. Readers take it as the ordinary exercise of authority, which the verse withholds from women in the congregation, or as a domineering or seized authority, which the verse forbids as a misuse. This translation keeps the ASV’s “have dominion” and leaves the question here. “Teach” and “have dominion” may be two things or one; “I do not permit” is present tense, read as a standing rule or as the writer’s present practice.

For all congregations, or for this one? ⚠ Some readers take verses 11 and 12 as a rule for every congregation at every time. Others read them against the situation this letter addresses: teachers of “a different doctrine” at Ephesus (1 Timothy 1:3–7), and women newly taught. They point to women who taught and spoke elsewhere in these books: Priscilla, with Aquila, setting out “the way of God more accurately” to Apollos at Acts 18:26; the four daughters of Philip “who prophesied” at Acts 21:9; Phoebe at Romans 16:1; Junia, “outstanding among the apostles,” at Romans 16:7; and women who pray and prophesy in the gathering at 1 Corinthians 11:5. On the other side stands 1 Corinthians 14:34, “The women are to keep silent in the congregations.” The authorship of the letter is itself disputed, as the notes on its first chapter set out. This page sets out the readings and does not choose.

How the verses have been used. ⚠ These are among the verses most often cited to keep women from teaching and from pastoral office. The Southern Baptist Convention’s Baptist Faith and Message (2000) says that “the office of pastor is limited to men as qualified by Scripture,” citing 1 Timothy 2:9–14; many other churches ordain women. The verses that follow have also been read as a judgment on women as such, which the next note takes up. This page records the uses and the readings and does not choose between the churches.

Verses 13–15 · “Adam was formed first”; “the woman was deceived”; “saved through the childbearing”

“For Adam was formed first, then Eve.” “Formed” is plassō, the verb of the Greek of Genesis 2:7, “Jehovah God formed the human from the dust of the ground” (dictionary); the woman is built from his side at Genesis 2:22. Readers take the order of creation as the ground of a lasting order between men and women, or as the first step of an illustration whose point is the deception that follows. The KJV reads “For Adam was first formed, then Eve”; the NWT 1984 reads “For Adam was formed first, then Eve.”

“And Adam was not deceived, but the woman, thoroughly deceived, came to be in transgression.” The woman’s own words are at Genesis 3:13, “The serpent deceived me, and I ate”; “thoroughly deceived” is the compound exapataō, where the Byzantine text has the simple verb (dictionary). “Transgression” is parabasis (dictionary). ⚠ The verse has been read as a judgment on women as such: John Chrysostom, in his ninth homily on this letter, writes, “The woman taught once, and ruined all,” and others have drawn from it the idea that women are more easily deceived. Elsewhere Paul puts sin’s entry on Adam, “through one man sin entered into the world,” at Romans 5:12, and turns Eve’s deception into a warning to a whole congregation, men and women, at 2 Corinthians 11:3: “as the serpent deceived Eve by his cunning, your minds may be corrupted.” The KJV reads “but the woman being deceived was in the transgression”; the NIV reads “it was the woman who was deceived and became a sinner.”

“But she will be saved through the childbearing, if they remain in faith and love and sanctification.” Teknogonia, “childbearing,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, with the article (dictionary). ⚠ Readers take it four ways: saved through the birth of the Child, the offspring promised at Genesis 3:15, itself read more than one way; kept safe through childbirth, as the NWT 1984 reads, “she will be kept safe through childbearing”; saved in the course of a woman’s ordinary life, on the conditions that follow; or saved even through the pain promised at Genesis 3:16. The subject also shifts from “she” to “they”: women generally, the woman and her husband, or her children. The Vulgate makes both singular, and so the Douay: “if she continue in faith.” This page sets out the readings and does not choose. “Sanctification” is hagiasmos, as at 1 Thessalonians 4:3 (dictionary). The KJV reads “she shall be saved in childbearing”; the NIV reads “women will be saved through childbearing”; the TLB adds Genesis to the verse, “So God sent pain and suffering to women when their children are born.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “all people” in verses 1 and 4, not “all sorts of men”; “quiet” and “quietness” for one root in verses 2, 11 and 12, as at 2 Thessalonians 3:12, not “silence”; “God our Savior,” as at 1:1; “humans” and “himself human” for one word; “ransom,” as at Mark 10:45; “herald,” as at 2 Timothy 1:11; “the men” for andras in verse 8; “holy hands”; “soundness of mind,” as at Acts 26:25; “have dominion,” as in the ASV, with the dispute in the note; “formed,” as at Genesis 2:7; “thoroughly deceived” for the compound; “the childbearing,” with the article; and “she” and “they” as the Greek has them.

Echoes paid. The quiet life promised at 2 Thessalonians 3:12; God our Savior from 1 Timothy 1:1; the false teachers of Ephesus from 1 Timothy 1:3; the Christ who “came into the world to save sinners” at 1 Timothy 1:15, now the ransom for all; the mediator of Galatians 3:20; the ransom of Mark 10:45; sanctification from 1 Thessalonians 4:3; and Eve deceived, from 2 Corinthians 11:3.

Echoes planted. The overseer who manages his household well, and “women likewise,” at 3:1–13; “the Savior of all people” again at 4:10; and the verb of childbearing, of the younger widows, at 5:14 (all yet to come in this translation).

Next in this book: 1 Timothy 3, already on these pages. The overseer and the servants, “women likewise,” and “great is the mystery of godliness.”
