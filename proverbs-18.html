Proverbs of Solomon on the man who cuts himself off, the fool's mouth and the whisperer's morsels, the strong tower of Jehovah's name beside the rich man's imagined wall, answering before hearing, the gift, the lot, the wronged brother, the tongue that holds death and life, finding a wife, and a friend who clings closer than a brother.
Part of the collection headed “The proverbs of Solomon” (10:1–22:16), attributed to Solomon, son of David (reigned c. 970–931 BC); the book keeps no calendar of its own.

1
לְתַאֲוָה, יְבַקֵּשׁ נִפְרָד;    בְּכָל-תּוּשִׁיָּה, יִתְגַּלָּע.
He who separates himself seeks a craving;

El que se aparta busca lo que codicia;
contra toda sabiduría práctica se enciende.
2
לֹא-יַחְפֹּץ כְּסִיל, בִּתְבוּנָה:    כִּי, אִם-בְּהִתְגַּלּוֹת לִבּוֹ.
A fool takes no delight in discernment,
only in laying his heart bare.note
El necio no se complace en el discernimiento,
sino solo en dejar al descubierto su corazón.
3
בְּבוֹא-רָשָׁע, בָּא גַם-בּוּז;    וְעִם-קָלוֹן חֶרְפָּה.
When a wicked man comes, contempt comes too,
and with disgrace, reproach.note
Cuando viene el malvado, viene también el desprecio,
y con la deshonra, el oprobio.

מַיִם עֲמֻקִּים, דִּבְרֵי פִי-אִישׁ;    נַחַל נֹבֵעַ, מְקוֹר חָכְמָה.
Deep waters are the words of a man’s mouth —
a stream pouring out, a fountain of wisdom.note
Aguas profundas son las palabras de la boca del hombre:
arroyo que mana, fuente de sabiduría.
5
שְׂאֵת פְּנֵי-רָשָׁע לֹא-טוֹב--    לְהַטּוֹת צַדִּיק, בַּמִּשְׁפָּט.
To lift the face of the wicked is not good,
to turn aside the righteous in judgment.note
Levantar el rostro del malvado no es bueno,
para desviar al justo en el juicio.
6
שִׂפְתֵי כְסִיל, יָבֹאוּ בְרִיב;    וּפִיו, לְמַהֲלֻמוֹת יִקְרָא.
A fool’s lips walk into a dispute,
and his mouth calls for blows.note
Los labios del necio entran en disputa,
y su boca llama a los golpes.
7
פִּי-כְסִיל, מְחִתָּה-לוֹ;    וּשְׂפָתָיו, מוֹקֵשׁ נַפְשׁוֹ.
A fool’s mouth is his ruin,
and his lips a snare for his soul.note
La boca del necio es su ruina,
y sus labios, una trampa para su alma.
8
דִּבְרֵי נִרְגָּן, כְּמִתְלַהֲמִים;    וְהֵם, יָרְדוּ חַדְרֵי-בָטֶן.
The words of a whisperer are like morsels gulped down,
and they go down into the inner rooms of the belly.note
Las palabras del murmurador son como bocados que se tragan con avidez,
y bajan a las cámaras del vientre.
9
גַּם, מִתְרַפֶּה בִמְלַאכְתּוֹ--    אָח הוּא, לְבַעַל מַשְׁחִית.
Even one who lets himself go slack in his work
is a brother to a master of destruction.note
También el que se afloja en su trabajo
es hermano del señor de la destrucción.
10
מִגְדַּל-עֹז, שֵׁם יְהוָה;    בּוֹ-יָרוּץ צַדִּיק וְנִשְׂגָּב.
A strong tower is the name of Jehovah;
the righteous runs into it and is set on high.note
Torre fuerte es el nombre de Jehová;
a ella corre el justo, y es puesto en alto.
11
הוֹן עָשִׁיר, קִרְיַת עֻזּוֹ;    וּכְחוֹמָה נִשְׂגָּבָה, בְּמַשְׂכִּתוֹ.
The wealth of the rich is his strong city,

Los bienes del rico son su ciudad fuerte,
y como muro puesto en alto, en su imaginación.
12
לִפְנֵי-שֶׁבֶר, יִגְבַּהּ לֶב-אִישׁ;    וְלִפְנֵי כָבוֹד עֲנָוָה.
Before a crash, a man’s heart is lofty,

Antes del quebranto se enaltece el corazón del hombre,
y antes de la honra, la humildad.
13
מֵשִׁיב דָּבָר, בְּטֶרֶם יִשְׁמָע--    אִוֶּלֶת הִיא-לוֹ, וּכְלִמָּה.
Whoever answers a matter before he has heard —
it is folly to him, and humiliation.note
El que responde a un asunto antes de haber oído,
necedad le es, y humillación.
14
רוּחַ-אִישׁ, יְכַלְכֵּל מַחֲלֵהוּ;    וְרוּחַ נְכֵאָה, מִי יִשָּׂאֶנָּה.
A man’s spirit will sustain him in his sickness,
but a crushed spirit — who can carry it?note
El espíritu del hombre lo sustentará en su enfermedad,
pero el espíritu abatido, ¿quién lo podrá llevar?
15
לֵב נָבוֹן, יִקְנֶה-דָּעַת;    וְאֹזֶן חֲכָמִים, תְּבַקֶּשׁ-דָּעַת.
The heart of one who understands acquires knowledge,
and the ear of the wise seeks knowledge.note
El corazón del que entiende adquiere conocimiento,
y el oído de los sabios busca conocimiento.
16
מַתָּן אָדָם, יַרְחִיב לוֹ;    וְלִפְנֵי גְדֹלִים יַנְחֶנּוּ.
A man’s gift makes room for him
and leads him before great men.note
El regalo del hombre le abre espacio
y lo lleva delante de los grandes.
17
צַדִּיק הָרִאשׁוֹן בְּרִיבוֹ;    יבא- וּבָא- רֵעֵהוּ, וַחֲקָרוֹ.
The first in his dispute is in the right;
then his neighbor comes and searches him out.note
Justo es el primero en su disputa;
luego viene su prójimo y lo escudriña.
18
מִדְיָנִים, יַשְׁבִּית הַגּוֹרָל;    וּבֵין עֲצוּמִים יַפְרִיד.
The lot puts an end to contentions
and separates the mighty.note
La suerte pone fin a las contiendas
y separa a los poderosos.
19
אָח--נִפְשָׁע מִקִּרְיַת-עֹז;    ומדונים וּמִדְיָנִים, כִּבְרִיחַ אַרְמוֹן.
A brother wronged is harder than a strong city,
and contentions are like the bar of a citadel.note
El hermano agraviado es más duro que una ciudad fuerte,
y las contiendas son como el cerrojo de una ciudadela.
20
מִפְּרִי פִי-אִישׁ, תִּשְׂבַּע בִּטְנוֹ;    תְּבוּאַת שְׂפָתָיו יִשְׂבָּע.
From the fruit of a man’s mouth his belly is satisfied;
with the produce of his lips he is satisfied.note
Del fruto de la boca del hombre se sacia su vientre;
del producto de sus labios se sacia.
21
מָוֶת וְחַיִּים, בְּיַד-לָשׁוֹן;    וְאֹהֲבֶיהָ, יֹאכַל פִּרְיָהּ.
Death and life are in the hand of the tongue,
and those who love it will eat its fruit.note
La muerte y la vida están en mano de la lengua,
y los que la aman comerán su fruto.
22
מָצָא אִשָּׁה, מָצָא טוֹב;    וַיָּפֶק רָצוֹן, מֵיְהוָה.
He who has found a wife has found good,
and draws out goodwill from Jehovah.note
El que ha hallado esposa ha hallado el bien,
y obtiene benevolencia de Jehová.
23
תַּחֲנוּנִים יְדַבֶּר-רָשׁ;    וְעָשִׁיר, יַעֲנֶה עַזּוֹת.
The poor speaks pleas for mercy,
but the rich answers with strong words.note
El pobre habla con súplicas,
pero el rico responde con palabras fuertes.
24
אִישׁ רֵעִים, לְהִתְרֹעֵעַ;    וְיֵשׁ אֹהֵב, דָּבֵק מֵאָח.
A man of many companions is headed for ruin,
but there is a friend who clings closer than a brother.note
El hombre de muchos compañeros va camino de la ruina,
pero hay amigo que se aferra más que un hermano.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–2 · “He that separateth himself seeketh his own desire” — the man who cuts himself off, a craving, and a heart laid bare

He who separates himself. “He who separates himself seeks a craving; against all practical wisdom he flares up”: nifrad is the one who has split himself off from others, from parad, to separate, and ta’avah is a craving, the word of the rabble who “craved a craving” in the wilderness (Numbers 11:4, now on these pages). Tushiyyah is “practical wisdom”, as in “guard practical wisdom and discretion” (Proverbs 3:21). Yitgalla, “he flares up”, is a verb Proverbs keeps for a quarrel breaking out: it stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in Proverbs, here and twice more (Proverbs 17:14 and 20:3). The ASV has “He that separateth himself seeketh his own desire, And rageth against all sound wisdom”; the NWT 1984 “against all practical wisdom he will break forth”. The KJV reads the verse the other way round, as praise of a scholar who withdraws to study: “Through desire a man, having separated himself, seeketh and intermeddleth with all wisdom”. The Geneva reads it the same way: “and occupy himself in all wisdom”.

A craving, or a pretext? The Greek has a different first line: “a man who wishes to separate from friends seeks pretexts”, prophaseis. That is what the Hebrew would say with one letter changed, le-to’anah, “for a pretext”, in place of le-ta’avah, “for a craving”, and to’anah is the “occasion” Samson was seeking against the Philistines (Judges 14:4, now on these pages). The Douay, made from the Latin, follows that line: “He that hath a mind to depart from a friend seeketh occasions”. Readings differ; this translation prints the Hebrew as it stands.

A heart laid bare. “A fool takes no delight in discernment, only in laying his heart bare”: the fool is the kesil, the dull, complacent fool of 3:35, and tevunah is “discernment”, as in “the human who draws out discernment” (3:13). Be-hitgallot libbo is “in his heart’s uncovering itself”, from galah, to uncover, and it sounds like v1’s yitgalla: one man flares up, the other lays himself bare, and neither is listening. The ASV has “But only that his heart may reveal itself”; the NWT 1984 “except that his heart should uncover itself”; the NIV turns the heart into talk, “delight in airing their own opinions”.

Verses 3–5 · “The words of a man’s mouth are as deep waters” — contempt that comes in, deep waters and a fountain, and lifting the face of the wicked

Contempt comes in. “When a wicked man comes, contempt comes too, and with disgrace, reproach”: the verb comes twice, be-vo … ba, as if contempt walked in at his heels. Qalon, “disgrace”, is what fools carry off in Proverbs 3:35, and cherpah is “reproach”. The same consonants can be read rasha, “a wicked man”, or resha, “wickedness”; the printed vowels give the man, and the NIV takes the other reading, “When wickedness comes, so does contempt”. The ASV has “When the wicked cometh, there cometh also contempt”. The Douay has “when he is come into the depth of sins”, which follows the Greek, eis bathos kakōn, “into the depth of evils”.

Deep waters and a fountain of wisdom. “Deep waters are the words of a man’s mouth — a stream pouring out, a fountain of wisdom”: two lines with no “and” or “but” between them, and readings differ on how they relate. Deep water is water that has to be drawn up, so the first line may mean that a man’s words hide what is in him, with the second line as the contrast; or both lines may describe one thing, wise speech that is deep and fresh at once. Mayim amuqqim, “deep waters”, opens two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and a saying about counsel in a man’s heart (Proverbs 20:5). The NIV writes in the contrast, “but the fountain of wisdom is a rushing stream”; the ASV leaves the lines side by side, “The wellspring of wisdom is as a flowing brook”; the NWT 1984 has “a torrent bubbling forth”. Nove’a, “pouring out”, is the verb Wisdom herself uses, “I will pour out my spirit to you” (Proverbs 1:23). The Greek ends the verse with “a fountain of life”, pēgē zōēs.

To lift the face. “To lift the face of the wicked is not good, to turn aside the righteous in judgment”: se’et penei, “to lift the face” of someone, is Hebrew for partiality, receiving one party’s face with favor; it is what Jehovah does not do, “who does not lift the face and does not take a bribe” (Deuteronomy 10:17, now on these pages). See panim. The ASV has “To respect the person of the wicked is not good”; the NWT 1984 “The showing of partiality to the wicked one is not good”; the KJV “to overthrow the righteous in judgment”.

Verses 6–8 · “The words of a talebearer are as wounds” — a fool’s mouth, a snare for the soul, and morsels gulped down

A fool’s mouth. “A fool’s lips walk into a dispute, and his mouth calls for blows”: riv is a dispute, the same word v17 uses for a case at law, and mahalumot are blows, the beating that is waiting for “the backs of fools” (Proverbs 19:29). Then: “A fool’s mouth is his ruin, and his lips a snare for his soul”. Moqesh nafsho, “a snare for his soul”, is the snare a reader is warned to keep away from in Proverbs 22:25, “take a snare for your soul”; here the fool did not need a bad companion to set one, because he carries it in his own mouth. The KJV has “and his mouth calleth for strokes”; the ASV “And his mouth calleth for stripes”; the Douay “his mouth provoketh quarrels”; the NWT 1984 “his lips are a snare for his soul”.

The whisperer. “The words of a whisperer are like morsels gulped down, and they go down into the inner rooms of the belly”: nirgan comes from ragan, to mutter or grumble, the verb of “you grumbled in your tents” (Deuteronomy 1:27, now on these pages). The noun stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in Proverbs. The NWT 1984 calls him “the slanderer”, the NIV “a gossip”, the ASV “a whisperer”.

Morsels or wounds? Mitlahamim is a word whose sense is uncertain. It stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and its double, which repeats the whole verse letter for letter (Proverbs 26:22), so there is no third passage to test it on. The second line, which has the words going down into the belly, suits food, and most of the shelf reads it so: the ASV has “as dainty morsels”; the NWT 1984 “like things to be swallowed greedily”. The KJV has a different picture, “The words of a talebearer are as wounds”. Chadrei vaten, “the inner rooms of the belly”, uses cheder, the inner room of a house: gossip is swallowed and then lives in the back rooms. The Greek has an entirely different saying in this place, “fear casts down the lazy, and the souls of the effeminate will go hungry”, and the Douay prints both, “The words of the double tongued are as if they were harmless” and then “Fear casteth down the slothful”.

Verse 9 · “He also that is slothful in his work is brother to him that is a great waster” — slack hands and a master of destruction

Slack. “Even one who lets himself go slack in his work is a brother to a master of destruction”: mitrappeh is from rafah, to go slack, the picture of a hand loosening its grip. He breaks nothing; he only lets go, and the proverb puts him in the same family as the man who smashes.

A master of destruction. Ba’al mashchit is built like “master of dreams”, what Joseph’s brothers call him (Genesis 37:19, now on these pages), and “master of anger” (Proverbs 22:24): a man named for the thing he owns. Mashchit is the Destroyer of the Passover night. This is the first of three brothers in the chapter: a brother to destruction here, a brother wronged in v19, and a friend closer than a brother in v24. The KJV has “is brother to him that is a great waster”; the ASV “Is brother to him that is a destroyer”; the NWT 1984 “he is a brother to the one causing ruin”; the TLB “A lazy man is brother to the saboteur”.

Verses 10–12 · “The name of Jehovah is a strong tower” — a strong tower and a strong city, a wall high only in the imagination, and a heart lofty before a crash

A strong tower. “A strong tower is the name of Jehovah; the righteous runs into it and is set on high”: migdal oz, “a strong tower”. The phrase stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, in a psalm that calls God “a strong tower before the enemy” (Psalm 61:4; 61:3 in English Bibles), and at Thebez, where “there was a strong tower in the middle of the city, and all the men and women of the city fled there” (Judges 9:51, now on these pages). Nisgav, “set on high”, is high out of reach, the word of “it is high — I cannot reach it” (Psalm 139:6). The ASV has “The name of Jehovah is a strong tower; The righteous runneth into it, and is safe”; the NWT 1984 “and is given protection”; the Geneva “and is exalted”.

A strong city, in his imagination. The next verse answers it phrase for phrase: “The wealth of the rich is his strong city, and like a wall set on high — in his imagination”. Qiryat uzzo, “his strong city”, repeats the oz of the tower, and nisgavah, “set on high”, repeats the righteous man’s word; only the last word changes, be-maskito, “in his imagination”. The righteous is high in fact; the rich man is high in his own head. Maskit is elsewhere a figured stone, an image made to be gazed at (Numbers 33:52, now on these pages); here the image is inside him. The first line also opens another proverb, which ends differently (Proverbs 10:15). The KJV has “and as an high wall in his own conceit”; the ASV “in his own imagination”; the NIV “they imagine it a wall too high to scale”; the Douay has no imagination in it, “as a strong wall compassing him about”.

Before a crash. “Before a crash, a man’s heart is lofty, and before honor, humility”: shever is a breaking, a crash, and yigbah, “is lofty”, is one more height in a run of three verses, the tower, the wall and now the heart. Both halves have twins: “before a crash” opens the saying about pride (Proverbs 16:18, now on these pages), and “before honor, humility” ends a saying about the fear of Jehovah (Proverbs 15:33). Anavah is from the root of anav, humble. The NWT 1984 has “Before a crash the heart of a man is lofty”; the KJV “Before destruction the heart of man is haughty”; the ASV “And before honor goeth humility”.

Verses 13–15 · “He that answereth a matter before he heareth it” — answering before hearing, a spirit that sustains, and an ear that seeks

Answering before hearing. “Whoever answers a matter before he has heard — it is folly to him, and humiliation”: meshiv davar is “bringing back a word”, the verb of “so that you bring back sayings of truth to those who send you” (Proverbs 22:21). Ivvelet is “folly”, as in 22:15, and kelimmah is “humiliation”. The KJV has “He that answereth a matter before he heareth it, it is folly and shame unto him”; the NWT 1984 “that is foolishness on his part and a humiliation”; the TLB “to decide before knowing the facts!”

A spirit that sustains. “A man’s spirit will sustain him in his sickness, but a crushed spirit — who can carry it?”: yekhalkel is the verb Joseph uses when he promises to keep his family alive through the famine, “And I will provide for you there” (Genesis 45:11, now on these pages). The body can be carried by the spirit; the question is who carries the spirit. Ruach nekh’ah, “a crushed spirit”, is a phrase of this book: it stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in Proverbs. The ASV has “But a broken spirit who can bear?”; the KJV “but a wounded spirit who can bear?”; the NWT 1984 “as for a stricken spirit, who can bear it?”; the Douay “but a spirit that is easily angered, who can bear?”

An ear that seeks. “The heart of one who understands acquires knowledge, and the ear of the wise seeks knowledge”: navon is the participle of bin, “the understanding” of Proverbs 1:5; qanah, “acquires”, is the ordinary verb for buying; and yevaqqesh, “seeks”, is the verb of v1. The man who separates himself seeks a craving; the wise ear seeks da’at, knowledge, which the verse names twice. The ASV has “The heart of the prudent getteth knowledge”; the NWT 1984 “the ear of wise ones seeks to find knowledge”; the Geneva “and the ear of the wise seeketh learning”.

Verses 16–18 · “A man’s gift maketh room for him” — a gift that opens doors, the other side of the case, and the lot

A gift that makes room. “A man’s gift makes room for him and leads him before great men”: mattan is a plain gift, the word Shechem uses when he offers “a very great bride-price and gift” (Genesis 34:12, now on these pages). It is not shochad, the bribe that buys a verdict, and the proverb neither praises nor condemns it; it reports how doors open. Yarchiv lo is “makes wide for him”. The ASV has “A man’s gift maketh room for him”; the NWT 1984 “will make a large opening for him”; the TLB “A gift does wonders”.

The other side of the case. “The first in his dispute is in the right; then his neighbor comes and searches him out”: the Hebrew sets the verdict first, tsaddiq, “in the right”, with no “seems”. The first speaker is right only until the second arrives, and chaqar, “searches out”, is the verb of “Jehovah, you have searched me” (Psalm 139:1). The written text has yavo, “he comes”; the text as read has u-va, “and comes”; the sense is the same. The NWT 1984 keeps the bare adjective, “The one first in his legal case is righteous”; the ASV supplies a verb, “He that pleadeth his cause first seemeth just”; the NIV “until someone comes forward and cross-examines”. The Douay has “The just is first accuser of himself”, which follows the Greek, dikaios heautou katēgoros.

The lot. “The lot puts an end to contentions and separates the mighty”: goral is the lot, and midyanim, “contentions”, is from the family of madon, the “contention” that goes out with the scoffer (Proverbs 22:10). Yafrid, “separates”, is the verb of v1: there a man separates himself and flares up; here the lot separates the fighters and the fight ends. The ASV has “The lot causeth contentions to cease, And parteth between the mighty”; the NWT 1984 “it separates even the mighty from one another”; the TLB has “A coin toss ends arguments”, a coin where the Hebrew has a lot.

Verse 19 · “A brother offended is harder to be won than a strong city” — a brother wronged, the bar of a citadel, and a brother helped

A brother wronged. “A brother wronged is harder than a strong city”: the Hebrew first line is only ach nifsha mi-qiryat oz, “a brother — wronged — than a strong city”, with no adjective at all; “harder” is supplied from the “than”. Nifsha is a passive of pasha, the verb behind pesha, revolt: a brother who has been rebelled against, betrayed by his own side. The form stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The strong city is v11’s: the rich man fortifies himself with money, the wronged brother with his grievance. The ASV has “A brother offended is harder to be won than a strong city”; the NWT 1984 leaves the gap open, “A brother who is transgressed against is more than a strong town”; the NIV “more unyielding than a fortified city”.

The bar of a citadel. “And contentions are like the bar of a citadel”: the written text has medonim and the text as read has midyanim, two spellings of v18’s “contentions”. The beriach is the bar laid across a gate, the bar Samson carried off with the gate of Gaza, “bar and all” (Judges 16:3, now on these pages), and armon is a citadel. The KJV has “like the bars of a castle”; the NWT 1984 “like the bar of a dwelling tower”.

A brother helped. The Greek turns the warning into a promise: “a brother helped by a brother is like a strong and high city”, boēthoumenos. The Greek’s “helped” fits a Hebrew word one letter away from nifsha: nosha, “saved”. The Douay follows the same line: “A brother that is helped by his brother, is like a strong city”. Readings differ; this translation prints the Hebrew.

Verses 20–21 · “Death and life are in the power of the tongue” — eating your own words, and a tongue with a hand

The fruit of the mouth. “From the fruit of a man’s mouth his belly is satisfied; with the produce of his lips he is satisfied”: the verb sava, “to be satisfied”, comes twice, and the translation keeps both. Tevu’ah, “produce”, is the harvest word of “from the first of all your produce” (Proverbs 3:9). In v8 other people swallow a whisperer’s words; here a man eats his own. The ASV has “A man’s belly shall be filled with the fruit of his mouth”; the NWT 1984 “he will be satisfied even with the produce of his lips”; the TLB narrows it to advice, “Ability to give wise advice satisfies like a good meal!”

In the hand of the tongue. “Death and life are in the hand of the tongue, and those who love it will eat its fruit”: be-yad lashon is literally “in the hand of the tongue”. Yad, hand, is the ordinary Hebrew for power and control, and the translation keeps the hand because the picture is the point: a tongue holding death in one hand and life in the other. The Greek keeps it too, en cheiri glōssēs. “Those who love it” are those who love the tongue, who like to talk; they eat what it grows, and the line does not say whether that is death or life. The KJV, the ASV, the Geneva, the Douay and the NWT 1984 all print “Death and life are in the power of the tongue”; the NIV has “The tongue has the power of life and death”.

Verse 22 · “Whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing” — found and found, goodwill drawn out from Jehovah, and the word “good” the Hebrew leaves off

Found, found. “He who has found a wife has found good, and draws out goodwill from Jehovah”: the first line is four Hebrew words, matsa ishah matsa tov, “has found a wife, has found good”. Tov is the word of “It is not good for the human to be alone” (Genesis 2:18, now on these pages). Va-yafeq, “and draws out”, is the verb of “the human who draws out discernment” (Proverbs 3:13), and ratson is goodwill, acceptance, kept distinct from chen, favor. The whole second line stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and in Wisdom’s own speech, “whoever finds me finds life, and draws out goodwill from Jehovah” (Proverbs 8:35). The verse sets finding a wife beside finding Wisdom, and the book ends by asking the question the other way round: “A woman of valour — who can find?” (Proverbs 31:10).

The missing “good”. The Hebrew does not call the wife good; the goodness is in the finding. The NWT 1984 supplies the adjective and brackets it, “Has one found a [good] wife?”; the ASV has “Whoso findeth a wife findeth a good thing, And obtaineth favor of Jehovah”. The Greek supplies “good” too, gynaika agathēn, and then adds a whole saying the Hebrew does not have: “whoever puts away a good wife puts away good things, and he who keeps an adulteress is foolish and ungodly”. The Douay translates both: “He that hath found a good wife, hath found a good thing” and “He that driveth away a good wife, driveth away a good thing”.

Verses 23–24 · “There is a friend that sticketh closer than a brother” — pleas for mercy, strong words, companions who break you, and a friend who clings

Pleas and strong words. “The poor speaks pleas for mercy, but the rich answers with strong words”: tachanunim are “pleas for mercy”, the word of “I will lead them with pleas for mercy” (Jeremiah 31:8, now on these pages); the poor man talks to the rich as if he were praying. The rich answers azzot, “strong things”, from oz, strength. The plural stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. It is the fourth oz in the chapter, after the strong tower of v10, the strong city of v11 and the strong city of v19, and the only one aimed at another person. The NWT 1984 keeps the strength, “one that is rich answers in a strong way”; the ASV has “But the rich answereth roughly”; the NIV “but the rich answer harshly”. The Greek has neither this verse nor the next.

Companions who break you. “A man of many companions is headed for ruin”: ish re’im le-hitro’ea, “a man of companions is for hitro’ea”, and the last word can come from two roots spelled alike. One is ra’a, to break or shatter: this form of it stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and in Isaiah’s earth that is “utterly broken” (Isaiah 24:19). The other is ra’ah, to keep company, the verb of “Do not befriend a master of anger” (Proverbs 22:24). Read the first way, a crowd of companions can break a man; read the second, a man with companions must keep up the friendship. The NIV has “One who has unreliable friends soon comes to ruin”; the NWT 1984 “There exist companions disposed to break one another to pieces”; the ASV “He that maketh many friends doeth it to his own destruction”. The KJV takes the other root, “A man that hath friends must shew himself friendly”, and the Geneva before it, “ought to show himself friendly”. This translation reads the breaking, which the “but” of the next line sets against a friend who holds.

A friend who clings. “But there is a friend who clings closer than a brother”: the friend is ohev, “one who loves”, from ahav, a different word from the many re’im. Davek, “who clings”, is the verb of the man who “clings to his wife” (Genesis 2:24) and of Ruth, who “clung to” Naomi (Ruth 1:14). This is the chapter’s third brother: v9 had a brother to destruction, v19 a brother wronged, and v24 a friend who holds on closer than either. The ASV has “But there is a friend that sticketh closer than a brother”; the NWT 1984 “a friend sticking closer than a brother”; the Douay “A man amiable in society, shall be more friendly than a brother”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “a craving” for ta’avah, as in Numbers 11:4; “practical wisdom” for tushiyyah and “discernment” for tevunah, as in chapter 3; “flares up” for yitgalla; “to lift the face” for partiality, as in Deuteronomy 10:17; “dispute” for riv and “contentions” for midyanim, as 22:10 has “contention”; “a snare for his soul”, as in 22:25; “a whisperer” for nirgan, from the verb of Deuteronomy 1:27; “master of destruction”, as “master of dreams” and “master of anger”; “set on high” for nisgav in vv10–11 alike; “sustain” for kilkel, the verb of Joseph’s provision; “draws out” for hefiq, as in 3:13, and “goodwill” for ratson, kept apart from chen, favor; “pleas for mercy”, as in Jeremiah 31:8; “strong” for every oz, so that the tower, the cities and the rich man’s words stay one family; and “clings” for davek, as in Genesis 2:24.

Echoes paid. The craving of Numbers 11:4; Samson’s occasion in Judges 14:4; practical wisdom from 3:21 and discernment from 3:13; Wisdom pouring out her spirit in 1:23; the face Jehovah does not lift in Deuteronomy 10:17; the snare for the soul of 22:25; the grumbling in the tents of Deuteronomy 1:27; the master of dreams of Genesis 37:19 and the master of anger of 22:24; the strong tower of Thebez in Judges 9:51; the height out of reach of Psalm 139:6; the figured stone of Numbers 33:52; the bringing back of 22:21; Joseph’s provision in Genesis 45:11; Shechem’s gift in Genesis 34:12; the searching of Psalm 139:1; the contention of 22:10; the bar of Gaza’s gate in Judges 16:3; the produce of 3:9; the pleas of Jeremiah 31:8; the befriending of 22:24; the “not good” of Genesis 2:18; the clinging of Genesis 2:24 and Ruth 1:14; and the woman of valour of 31:10.

Echoes planted. The deep waters of counsel (Proverbs 20:5); the whisperer’s saying repeated (Proverbs 26:22); humility before honor (Proverbs 15:33); the rich man’s strong city again (Proverbs 10:15); Wisdom’s goodwill from Jehovah (Proverbs 8:35); the quarrel that flares up (Proverbs 17:14 and 20:3); the crushed spirit that dries the bones (Proverbs 17:22); the strong tower of Psalm 61; and the broken earth of Isaiah 24:19. None is translated here yet.
