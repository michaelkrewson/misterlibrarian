To the elders from 'a fellow elder': 'shepherd the flock of God,' not for shameful gain and not lording it, and the chief shepherd's unfading crown; Proverbs 3:34 again, 'God opposes the proud'; anxiety cast on God; the adversary 'like a roaring lion'; 'the God of all grace' who will mend and ground; and the letter sent 'through Silvanus,' with greetings from 'she who is in Babylon' and from Mark.
c. AD 62–64 on the traditional reading, later on others (see chapter 1).

Translator’s Notes

Verse 1 · “A fellow elder” — witness of the sufferings, sharer in the glory

“So I exhort the elders among you, as a fellow elder and a witness of the sufferings of Christ, and also a sharer in the glory that is about to be revealed.” “Elders” is presbyteroi, the “elders of the congregation” of James 5:14 and of Ephesus at Acts 20:17 (dictionary). Sympresbyteros, “fellow elder,” stands in one verse (dictionary): the writer who opened as “an apostle of Jesus Christ” (1 Peter 1:1) speaks to the elders as one of them. “The sufferings of Christ” are those of 1 Peter 1:11 and 4:13; “the glory that is about to be revealed” is Paul’s phrase at Romans 8:18, “the glory about to be revealed to us.” Westcott and Hort, Tregelles and the 27th Nestle-Aland edition read oun, “so”; the 28th edition and the Byzantine text read tous, “the elders who are among you.” The KJV reads “The elders which are among you I exhort, who am also an elder”; the NWT 1984 reads “to the older men among YOU”; the Douay reads “The ancients therefore that are among you.”

Verses 2–4 · “Shepherd the flock of God” — not for shameful gain, not lording it, and the chief shepherd’s unfading crown

“Shepherd the flock of God that is among you, watching over it, not under compulsion but willingly, according to God; not for shameful gain but eagerly.” “Shepherd” is poimainō and “flock” is poimnion, Paul’s words to the elders of Ephesus at Acts 20:28, “to all the flock … to shepherd the congregation of God” (dictionary). Episkopeō, “watch over,” stands in two verses (dictionary). The other is the “watching” of Hebrews 12:15; the verb belongs with episkopos, the “overseer of your souls” of 1 Peter 2:25. Westcott and Hort do not have the word, and the Byzantine text, with Westcott and Hort, does not have “according to God”: the KJV reads “taking the oversight thereof, not by constraint, but willingly,” with no “according to God”; the ASV reads “exercising the oversight, not of constraint, but willingly, according to the will of God.” Anankastōs, “under compulsion,” stands in one verse (dictionary). “Willingly” is hekousiōs, the “willfully” of Hebrews 10:26 (dictionary). “For shameful gain” is the adverb of the “greedy for shameful gain” that disqualifies at 1 Timothy 3:8 and Titus 1:7 (dictionary). “According to God” is the phrase of 1 Peter 4:6. The KJV reads “Feed the flock of God … not for filthy lucre, but of a ready mind”; the NWT 1984 reads “Shepherd the flock of God in YOUR care”; the NIV reads “Be shepherds of God’s flock.”

“Not as lording it over the portions allotted to you, but becoming examples to the flock.” Katakyrieuō, “lord it over,” stands in four verses (dictionary), among them Jesus’s “those who are recognized as rulers of the nations lord it over them” at Mark 10:42. “The portions allotted to you” is tōn klērōn, the plural of klēros, a lot or a share, as in “allotted his share in this ministry” at Acts 1:17 (dictionary): the people given to each elder’s care, though the Greek names them only as shares. The Douay reads “Neither as lording it over the clergy,” from the Latin in cleris; the English word “clergy” comes from this Greek word through Latin, and here the word names the flock, not its leaders. “Examples” is typoi, as in “be an example to the believers” at 1 Timothy 4:12 (dictionary). The KJV reads “Neither as being lords over God’s heritage”; the ASV reads “neither as lording it over the charge allotted to you”; the NWT 1984 reads “neither as lording it over those who are God’s inheritance”; the NIV reads “not lording it over those entrusted to you.”

“And when the chief shepherd is made manifest, you will receive the unfading crown of glory.” Archipoimēn, “chief shepherd,” stands in one verse (dictionary): Christ, “the shepherd and overseer of your souls” of 1 Peter 2:25 and “the great shepherd of the sheep” of Hebrews 13:20, over the elders who shepherd under him. “Is made manifest” is phaneroō, as Christ was “made manifest at the end of the times” at 1 Peter 1:20. Amarantinos, “unfading,” stands in one verse (dictionary), and its near twin amarantos is the “unfading” inheritance of 1 Peter 1:4. A stephanos is a wreath, the prize of the games, as in “a perishable crown, but we an imperishable one” at 1 Corinthians 9:25 (dictionary). “You will receive” is komizō, the “receiving the outcome of your faith” of 1 Peter 1:9. The KJV reads “when the chief Shepherd shall appear, ye shall receive a crown of glory that fadeth not away”; the NWT 1984 reads “the unfadable crown of glory”; the Douay reads “the prince of pastors.”

Verses 5–7 · “God opposes the proud” — Proverbs 3:34 again, humility tied on, and anxiety cast on God

“Likewise, you younger men, be subject to the elders. And all of you, tie on humility toward one another.” “Likewise” carries on the household instructions of 1 Peter 2:13, 2:18, 3:1 and 3:7. “The younger men” are neōteroi, the “younger men” who carried Ananias out at Acts 5:6; whether “elders” here means the office of verse 1 or simply older men, readings differ, and the NWT 1984 reads “be in subjection to the older men.” Enkomboō, “tie on,” stands in one verse (dictionary). It is built on kombos, a knot, and pictures a garment fastened on. “Humility” is tapeinophrosynē, the “all humility and tears” of Acts 20:19 (dictionary). The Byzantine text adds “being subject to one another”: the KJV reads “all of you be subject one to another, and be clothed with humility”; the ASV reads “gird yourselves with humility, to serve one another”; the NWT 1984 reads “gird yourselves with lowliness of mind”; the Douay reads “insinuate humility,” from the Latin insinuate.

“Because ‘God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble.’” The line is the Greek of Proverbs 3:34, Kyrios hyperēphanois antitassetai, tapeinois de didōsin charin, word for word except that the Greek of Proverbs has “the Lord” where Peter, like James, has “God”; the Hebrew reads “The scoffers he scoffs at, but to the humble he gives grace” (both checked; Proverbs 3 yet to come in this translation). James 4:6 quotes the same line in the same form and goes on to “Resist the devil” (4:7) and “Humble yourselves” (4:10); Peter goes on to “Humble yourselves” (verse 6) and “Resist him” (verse 9), in the other order. “Proud” is hyperēphanos (dictionary). The KJV reads “God resisteth the proud, and giveth grace to the humble”; the NWT 1984 reads “God opposes the haughty ones, but he gives undeserved kindness to the humble ones.”

“Humble yourselves, then, under the mighty hand of God, so that he may exalt you at the proper time, casting all your anxiety on him, because he cares about you.” “Humble yourselves” is tapeinoō, as in “Humble yourselves before Jehovah, and he will exalt you” at James 4:10 (dictionary). “The mighty hand” is the hand of the Exodus: the Greek of Deuteronomy 26:8 has en cheiri krataia where this translation, from the Hebrew, reads “with a strong hand” (Deuteronomy 26:8, Greek checked). Krataios, “mighty,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. “At the proper time” is en kairō; the Latin reads “in the time of visitation,” and so does the Douay, “that he may exalt you in the time of visitation.” “Casting … on him” is epirriptoō, the verb of the cloaks the disciples “threw” on the colt at Luke 19:35. “Anxiety” is merimna, the “anxieties of life” of Luke 21:34 and Paul’s “anxiety for all the congregations” at 2 Corinthians 11:28 (dictionary). The line recalls the Greek of Psalm 55:22 (54:23 in the Greek numbering), epirripson epi kyrion tēn merimnan sou, “cast your anxiety on the Lord,” where the Hebrew has “cast your burden on Jehovah” (both checked; Psalm 55 yet to come in this translation); Peter does not quote it. “He cares” is melei, the word of the disciples’ “does it not matter to you that we are perishing?” at Mark 4:38. The KJV reads “Casting all your care upon him; for he careth for you”; the ASV reads “casting all your anxiety upon him”; the NWT 1984 reads “while YOU throw all YOUR anxiety upon him.”

Verses 8–9 · “Like a roaring lion” — the adversary, the devil, and a brotherhood in the world

“Be sober; stay awake. Your adversary the devil walks around like a roaring lion, seeking someone to swallow up.” “Be sober” is nēphō, as at 1 Peter 1:13 and 4:7 (dictionary); “stay awake” is grēgoreō, Jesus’s “Stay awake and pray” in Gethsemane at Matthew 26:41, and the pair stands together at 1 Thessalonians 5:6, “let us stay awake and be sober” (dictionary). Antidikos, “adversary,” stands in four verses (dictionary). Elsewhere it is an opponent at law: the “accuser” on the road to court at Matthew 5:25 and Luke 12:58, and the widow’s “Give me justice against my adversary” at Luke 18:3. “The devil” is diabolos, “slanderer,” as in “do not give place to the devil” at Ephesians 4:27 (dictionary). Ōryomai, “roar,” stands in one verse (dictionary). The image recalls the Greek of Psalm 22:13 (21:14 in the Greek numbering), “like a lion that snatches and roars,” with the same verb, where the Hebrew has “a lion tearing and roaring” (both checked; Psalm 22 yet to come in this translation). “Swallow up” is katapinō, as in “Death is swallowed up in victory” at 1 Corinthians 15:54. The Byzantine text reads “seeking whom he may swallow up.” The KJV reads “as a roaring lion, walketh about, seeking whom he may devour”; the NWT 1984 reads “Keep YOUR senses, be watchful”; the NIV reads “prowls around like a roaring lion.”

“Resist him, firm in the faith, knowing that the same sufferings are being completed in your brotherhood in the world.” “Resist” is anthistēmi, as in “Resist the devil, and he will flee from you” at James 4:7 and “so that you may be able to resist on the evil day” at Ephesians 6:13. Stereos, “firm,” stands in four verses, as in “God’s firm foundation stands” at 2 Timothy 2:19 and the “solid food” of Hebrews 5:12. Adelphotēs, “brotherhood,” stands in two verses (dictionary), both in this letter: “Love the brotherhood” at 1 Peter 2:17, and here. “Are being completed” is epiteleō, the verb of “will bring it to completion” at Philippians 1:6: what the readers suffer is the same thing the believers elsewhere are going through. The KJV reads “the same afflictions are accomplished in your brethren that are in the world”; the NWT 1984 reads “in the entire association of YOUR brothers in the world”; the NIV reads “the family of believers throughout the world.”

Verses 10–11 · “The God of all grace” — mend, strengthen, make strong, ground

“And the God of all grace, who called you to his eternal glory in Christ, after you have suffered a little while, will himself mend, strengthen, make strong and ground you.” “A little while” is oligon, the “now for a little while” of 1 Peter 1:6. The four verbs: “mend” is katartizō, the word this translation prints “mend” at Galatians 6:1 and 2 Corinthians 13:11 and “equip” at Hebrews 13:21 (dictionary); “strengthen” is stērizō, as in “strengthen your hearts” at James 5:8 (dictionary); stheneō, “make strong,” stands in one verse (dictionary); and “ground” is themelioō, the “rooted and grounded in love” of Ephesians 3:17 (dictionary). Westcott and Hort and Tregelles do not have the fourth verb, and the ASV reads “shall himself perfect, establish, strengthen you.” The Received Text reads “called us” and puts the verbs as a wish: the KJV reads “who hath called us unto his eternal glory by Christ Jesus … make you perfect, stablish, strengthen, settle you.” The NWT 1984 reads “will himself finish YOUR training”; the NIV reads “will himself restore you and make you strong, firm and steadfast.”

“To him be the might forever and ever. Amen.” The doxology of 1 Peter 4:11 comes back with “the might,” kratos, alone (dictionary); here “him” is “the God of all grace” of verse 10. There is no verb in the Greek, as at Hebrews 13:21. The Byzantine text adds “the glory and”: the KJV reads “To him be glory and dominion for ever and ever.” Westcott and Hort and the 28th Nestle-Aland edition do not have “and ever”: the NWT 1984 reads “To him be the might forever”; the ASV reads “To him be the dominion for ever and ever”; the NIV reads “To him be the power for ever and ever.”

Verse 12 · “Through Silvanus” — the one who carried the letter, or the one who helped write it?

“Through Silvanus, the faithful brother, as I consider him, I have written to you in few words, exhorting and testifying that this is the true grace of God. Stand in it.” Silouanos stands in four verses (encyclopedia). The other three are Paul’s: “through me and Silvanus and Timothy” at 2 Corinthians 1:19, and the openings of 1 Thessalonians 1:1 and 2 Thessalonians 1:1. Acts calls him Silas, sent from Jerusalem with the council’s letter at Acts 15:22 and chosen by Paul at Acts 15:40. ⚠ “Through Silvanus I have written” is read two ways, and this translation does not choose. On one reading Silvanus carries the letter; at Acts 15:23 the Jerusalem letter is sent “writing by their hand,” and Judas and Silas take it and report it “by word of mouth” (Acts 15:27). On the other, Silvanus helps write it, as a secretary or a drafter. The NIV reads “With the help of Silas”; the NWT 1984 reads “I have written YOU in few [words]”; the KJV reads “By Silvanus, a faithful brother unto you, as I suppose.”

“In few words … that this is the true grace of God. Stand in it.” “In few words” is di’ oligōn; Hebrews ends with the same apology in other words, “I have written to you in few words” (Hebrews 13:22). “The true grace of God” is charis, the grace of 1 Peter 1:13, “the grace being brought to you” (dictionary). “Stand” is a command in the printed text; the Byzantine text reads “in which you stand”: the KJV reads “the true grace of God wherein ye stand,” the ASV reads “Stand ye fast therein,” and the NWT 1984 reads “in which stand firm.”

Verses 13–14 · ⚠ “She who is in Babylon” — Rome, the city on the Euphrates, or Egypt? Mark “my son,” and the kiss of love

“She who is in Babylon, chosen together with you, greets you, and so does Mark my son.” ⚠ The Greek has no noun: hē en Babylōni syneklektē, “the one in Babylon, chosen together,” feminine. Syneklektos, “chosen together,” stands in one verse (dictionary), and takes up the “chosen exiles” of 1 Peter 1:1. Most readers take “she” to be a congregation, and the KJV supplies the word, “The church that is at Babylon”; the Douay reads “The church that is in Babylon,” from the Latin ecclesia. Others take her to be a woman known to the readers. The ASV reads “She that is in Babylon”; the NWT 1984 and the NIV read “She who is in Babylon.”

⚠ Which Babylon, readings differ, and this translation does not choose. Babylōn stands in eleven verses of the New Testament (encyclopedia). The first four are the old city of the exile, at Matthew 1:11, 1:12 and 1:17 and in Stephen’s “beyond Babylon” at Acts 7:43; six are Revelation’s “Babylon the great,” from 14:8 to 18:21. Here, the reading held by most takes the name as a cover for Rome, the capital of the empire in which the readers are “exiles”; the evidence offered is Revelation’s use of the name and early reports of Peter in Rome (reported, not checked here). Others take it as the city on the Euphrates, where a Jewish community lived, and a third reading names a Roman fort called Babylon in Egypt (both reported, not checked here). The Greek says only “in Babylon.”

“And so does Mark my son. Greet one another with a kiss of love. Peace to all of you who are in Christ.” Markos stands in eight verses (encyclopedia): John called Mark, whose mother’s house Peter went to when he was freed from prison (Acts 12:12), Barnabas’s cousin (Colossians 4:10), and the man Paul called “useful to me for ministry” (2 Timothy 4:11). “My son” is not said to mean a son by birth. Philēma, “kiss,” stands in seven verses (dictionary); four times Paul has “a holy kiss,” as at Romans 16:16, and only here is it “a kiss of love.” The Douay reads “with a holy kiss” and “Grace be to all you,” following the Latin. The Byzantine text adds “Jesus. Amen”: the KJV reads “Peace be with you all that are in Christ Jesus. Amen.” The NWT 1984 reads “a kiss of love.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “fellow elder”; “the glory that is about to be revealed,” as at Romans 8:18; “shepherd” and “flock,” as at Acts 20:28; “watching over,” as at Hebrews 12:15; “willingly”; “shameful gain,” as at 1 Timothy 3:8; “lording it over,” as at Mark 10:42; “the portions allotted”; “chief shepherd”; “unfading,” as at 1:4; “tie on”; “God opposes the proud,” as at James 4:6; “anxiety”; “stay awake,” as at Matthew 26:41; “adversary,” as at Luke 18:3; “swallow up,” as at 1 Corinthians 15:54; “brotherhood,” as at 2:17; “mend,” as at Galatians 6:1; “ground,” as at Ephesians 3:17; “She who is in Babylon,” with no noun supplied; and “a kiss of love.”

Echoes paid. “The sufferings of Christ” and the glory to be revealed from 1 Peter 1:11 and 1 Peter 4:13; the shepherd and overseer of 1 Peter 2:25, now the chief shepherd; the unfading inheritance of 1 Peter 1:4; “likewise” from the household instructions of 1 Peter 2:13; be sober from 1 Peter 1:13 and 1 Peter 4:7; the brotherhood of 1 Peter 2:17; “a little while” from 1 Peter 1:6; the might of the doxology of 1 Peter 4:11; and the chosen exiles of 1 Peter 1:1, now “chosen together.”

The end of the letter. 1 Peter opened with “the chosen exiles of the Dispersion” and ends with greetings from “she who is in Babylon,” another place of exile; it opened with “may grace and peace be multiplied to you” and ends with “Peace to all of you who are in Christ.” Revelation’s “Babylon the great” (14:8; 17:5; 18:2) is where the name comes back.

Next in this book: none; 1 Peter ends here.
