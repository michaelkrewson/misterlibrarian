Restitution law for theft and property damage; a bailment system covering borrowed, deposited, and hired property; the seduction and bride-price law; three capital sentences in a row with no stated rationale — sorcery, bestiality, and sacrifice to other gods; and the law protecting the widow, the orphan, and the stranger, enforced by God's own direct hearing rather than any human court.
c. 1446 BC on the early-date chronology this project foregrounds — continues directly from chapter 21, part of the same Sinai giving of case law.

Notes on this translation

Where verse 1 went — and the difference between night and day

⚠ This chapter has no verse 1, and that is not an error. Hebrew and English Bibles split chapters 21 and 22 at different points: what English readers know as Exodus 22:1 — the five-oxen-for-one-ox theft law — is, in the Hebrew Masoretic Text as printed by Mechon-Mamre, the LAST verse of chapter 21, already on these pages at Exodus 21:37. This translation follows the Hebrew's own chapter boundary rather than resetting the count to match English convention, so every verse in this chapter carries the number familiar from English Bibles — but the Hebrew's own internal numbering, one verse lower throughout, is not what this page shows. Flagged plainly here rather than left for a reader to wonder why the chapter starts at 2.

The housebreaker law that actually opens the chapter draws a real, deliberately time-bound distinction: killing a thief caught breaking in at night carries no bloodguilt (v2), but the identical killing after sunrise DOES (v3) — the text gives no reasoning of its own, simply the rule. Ancient and modern commentators alike read the night/day line as a proxy for what the homeowner could reasonably know: a nighttime intruder's intent is unreadable in the dark, while daylight allows for other options — calling out, identifying the threat, waiting. The theft-restitution clause that follows (v3b) is worth holding next to Exodus 21:2–6: a thief who cannot pay double is SOLD for his theft, the same six-year debt-servitude mechanism already described there, not an added punishment invented here.

The property-damage laws that close out this opening group (vv4–6, grazing animals and spreading fire) fix restitution from the offender's OWN best field and vineyard, not merely an equivalent value — a small but real overcompensation, in the same spirit as chapter 21's multiplied restitution for theft.

Three tiers of borrowed trust

The bailment laws here form a genuinely coherent system, built around who benefits from the arrangement and who holds control. Money or goods left with a neighbor purely as a favor (vv7–9): if the thief is found, double restitution; if not, an oath ‘before God’ settles it — the same phrase already discussed at Exodus 21:6, where ancient and modern readers differ on whether it names a local shrine, a court of judges, or God directly. A borrowed or deposited ANIMAL (vv10–13) follows the same oath principle, with one further, very literal wrinkle: an animal stolen from custody requires restitution, but one killed by a predator does not — PROVIDED the keeper brings the carcass as evidence (v13), a chain-of-custody requirement stated as plainly as any modern insurance clause. And a BORROWED item (vv14–15) carries full liability regardless of how the loss happened, UNLESS the owner was present when it happened (in which case the owner absorbs the risk) or the item was hired rather than borrowed outright (in which case the loss is already priced into the rental fee). Three tiers, three different risk allocations, each one tracking exactly who stood to gain.

⚠ A dowry law, and three sentences with no explanation at all

The seduction law (vv16–17) treats marriage as carrying real economic and legal weight, the same way Exodus 21:7–11's servant-wife law did: a man who seduces an unbetrothed virgin owes her father the standard bride-price, and the father retains an explicit veto — he can refuse the marriage outright and still collect the payment.

⚠ Three capital sentences then fire in immediate succession, with zero elaboration and no stated connection between them: a sorceress (v18), a person who lies with an animal (v19), and anyone who sacrifices to a god other than Jehovah (v20). The text places them side by side; it does not explain why, and this translation does not invent a reason it doesn't give. Two details are worth flagging on their own. First, v18's Hebrew (mekhashefah) is specifically FEMININE — only the female form of the word appears here, though a broader, gender-inclusive term for the same practice appears elsewhere in the Torah (Deuteronomy 18:10–11, not yet on these pages). The shelf itself splits on exactly this point: KJV genders the crime as generically as English allows, ‘thou shalt not suffer a witch to live’, while ASV, NIV, and NWT all keep the Hebrew's own feminine, ‘sorceress’. Second, and much heavier: this exact verse, in the King James Version's wording, was directly invoked for centuries to justify the European and American witch trials, a documented historical fact this translation reports rather than elaborates on.

The one law in this chapter enforced by God alone

The foreigner-protection law (v21) grounds its own argument in memory rather than abstraction: you shall not wrong a foreigner, BECAUSE you yourselves were foreigners in Egypt — the same appeal to lived experience this whole book keeps making. The shelf splits into two families here: KJV and ASV both read ‘stranger’ / ‘sojourner’, while NIV and NWT both modernize to ‘foreigner’ / ‘foreign resident’ — this translation follows the second family, since ‘stranger’ now reads in English as merely unfamiliar rather than foreign-born.

⚠ The widow-and-orphan law that follows (vv22–24) is the one law in this entire chapter with no court, no oath, and no judge anywhere in it. Every other case in this chapter routes through a legal process — restitution, a hearing before God or the judges, a sworn oath. This one is enforced directly and personally: ‘if they cry out to me, I will surely hear their cry… my anger will burn, and I will kill you with the sword.’ The same root (tza'aq / za'aq) is the one this book itself used for Israel's own outcry under Egypt's oppression (2:23; 3:7, 9, already on these pages) — the nation that once cried out and was heard is now warned that the same God hears the very people it has power to oppress in turn.

The interest-free lending law (v25) and the pledged-garment law (vv26–27) close the section on the same personal register: a cloak taken as security must be returned before sunset, because for a poor man it is his only blanket. ‘I am gracious’ (chanun) is one of the only places in this whole chapter where the text gives a reason FOR a law rather than simply stating it — God's own character, named as the ground of the rule.

Rulers, firstfruits, and a chapter that closes on its own opening word

Blasphemy and cursing a ruler are named together in one verse (v28), civil and divine authority treated as a matched category. ⚠ This exact law is quoted centuries later, by name: Acts 23:5 (not yet on these pages) has Paul retract an insult to the high priest by citing it directly — ‘you shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people.’

The firstborn dedication law (vv29–30) sets a specific waiting period for animals — seven days with its mother, given on the eighth — a timing this translation leaves the reader to notice alongside circumcision's own eighth-day rule, rather than asserting a connection the text itself does not make explicit.

The chapter's last law (v31) is the plainest kind of food regulation — do not eat flesh torn by wild animals, feed it to the dogs — and it closes on the same word this chapter's OWN bailment section used as a legal category seven verses into the chapter (v13's torn animal, brought as evidence). More strikingly, ‘holy men’ echoes Exodus 19's ‘kingdom of priests and a holy nation’ (already on these pages) — the chapter's single most ordinary rule, tied by its own last word back to the covenant's grandest language.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Verse numbers in this chapter follow ENGLISH convention rather than the Hebrew's own internal count, which runs one lower throughout — a deliberate exception to this project's usual practice of following Mechon-Mamre's printed numbering exactly, made so that this chapter's citations (and the library's own pre-existing references to ‘Exodus 22:16’, ‘22:18’, and others) point where readers actually expect them to. ‘Bride-price’ kept for mohar rather than ‘dowry’ (KJV), since a dowry properly moves from the bride's family to the groom's, and mohar moves the other way. And the casuistic ‘if… then’ case structure kept exactly as the Hebrew has it, as in the chapter before this one.

A note on order: the Mishpatim continue from Exodus 21 with no gap and close at Exodus 23.
