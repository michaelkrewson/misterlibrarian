📖 2 Timothy

Πρὸς Τιμόθεον Β (Pros Timotheon B) — 'To Timothy, 2' — the second and last letter to Timothy, and, on the traditional reading, the last letter Paul wrote.

Paul's last will and testament — written from a second imprisonment, cold and mostly abandoned, with execution in view. The most personal and moving of the three Pastorals: 'I have fought the good fight, I have finished the race, I have kept the faith.' Its charge is to guard the deposit and hand it on.

Chapter by chapter

2 Timothy 1On the traditional reading the LAST thing Paul wrote — from a second imprisonment, cold and mostly abandoned, with execution in view — and the most personal of the three Pastorals, which is why even some who doubt he wrote the letters think this one keeps his own notes. Where 1 Timothy called Timothy his “true” child, this opens to the “BELOVED” child and toward “the promise of life,” a fitting keynote under a death sentence. ⚠ It traces a faith down three named generations — the grandmother Lois, the mother Eunice, then Timothy — the one place the New Testament follows belief through a family, and down the female line of a mixed marriage. Its heart is a charge not to be ASHAMED: “God did not give us a spirit of cowardice, but of power and love and self-control”; and a banker’s confidence, “I know whom I have believed… he is able to guard my DEPOSIT until that day” — the entrusted gospel, to be held and handed on intact. It ends in a bleak, tender contrast: a whole province “turned away from me,” and one man, Onesiphorus, who “was not ashamed of my chain” and searched Rome until he found the prisoner.2 Timothy 2Paul, in chains, to his “child”: entrust the teaching to faithful people who will teach others, and suffer along with me — the soldier, the athlete crowned only if he competes “lawfully,” the farmer who labors. “Remember Jesus Christ, raised from the dead”; “the word of God is not chained”; and a four-line faithful saying, ⚠ “if we are unfaithful, he remains faithful.” Then ⚠ “rightly handling the word of truth” — the KJV’s “rightly dividing” and its later use — talk that spreads like gangrene, Hymenaeus and Philetus saying the resurrection is past, ⚠ the seal “Jehovah knows those who are his,” vessels for honor, and the Lord’s slave who must not quarrel, with ⚠ those “caught alive” and the three readings of whose will; no vote cast.2 Timothy 3⚠ “In the last days difficult times will set in”: eighteen marks of the people in them, from “lovers of self” to “lovers of pleasure rather than lovers of God,” and “a form of godliness” with its power denied. Teachers who slip into households and take captive “silly women,” with the readings of that word set side by side; ⚠ Jannes and Jambres, Pharaoh’s magicians, named nowhere in Exodus; Antioch, Iconium and Lystra; and “all who want to live a godly life in Christ Jesus will be persecuted.” Then the sacred writings Timothy knew from infancy, and ⚠ “All Scripture is God-breathed” — theopneustos, and two ways of reading a line with no verb; no vote cast.2 Timothy 4The last chapter of the letter, and on the traditional reading the last words Paul wrote. ⚠ “Preach the word; stand ready in season and out of season,” for a time is coming when people will heap up teachers for themselves, “having itching ears,” and wander off to myths. Then ⚠ “I am already being poured out as a drink offering, and the time for me to cast off has come. I have fought the good fight, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith,” and the crown of righteousness laid up. Demas gone, “having loved the present age”; “Luke alone is with me”; Mark, once dropped, now “useful”; the cloak, the books and the parchments left at Troas; ⚠ Alexander the coppersmith, where the NWT prints “Jehovah will repay him” and the Hebrew of the formula has no name; the first defense, when all forsook him, and “the lion’s mouth”; and “come before winter.”

About the book

Where it sits
The SIXTEENTH book of the New Testament, the second of the Pastorals — placed by the collection just after 1 Timothy, though by its own witness it is the latest of Paul's letters, standing at the very end of his life.
Genre
A LETTER, and unlike its two companions barely a manual at all — a personal, valedictory charge to a single trusted successor, heavy with memory, loneliness and farewell. It has less church-order than 1 Timothy or Titus and more of a man's own voice: names of those who left and those who stood, a request for a cloak and some books, and a settled readiness to die.
Author
Named as PAUL, and caught in the Pastorals' authorship dispute (set out under 1 Timothy). ⚠ But 2 Timothy is the special case: it is by far the most personal of the three, thick with circumstantial detail — Onesiphorus, Demas, the cloak left at Troas, the first defence at which 'all deserted me' — and even a good many scholars who judge the Pastorals pseudonymous allow that this letter preserves GENUINE fragments of Paul, a real farewell worked into the collection. The library reports both the whole-cloth and the fragment views, and does not vote.
Date written
Traditionally c. AD 66–67, from a second Roman imprisonment shortly before Paul's execution under Nero; on the majority-critical view of the Pastorals, c. AD 90–110. The date follows the authorship question.
Place
Written from prison in ROME (1:16-17; 2:9, 'bound with chains as a criminal'), to Timothy at Ephesus.
Audience
TIMOTHY — Paul's 'beloved child', now bearing the Ephesian work — summoned to come quickly (4:9, 21) and to bring Mark, the cloak, and the books. Behind the personal address stands a wider readership meant to take the charge as their own: keep the pattern of sound words, endure suffering, and pass the trust to 'faithful people who will be able to teach others' (2:2). The mood is of a movement bracing to continue without its apostle.

How it's laid out

1:1–18Thanksgiving and charge: the faith of Lois and Eunice; rekindle the gift; do not be ashamed; guard the deposit — deserted by Asia, refreshed by Onesiphorus.
2:1–13Images of endurance — the soldier, the athlete, the hardworking farmer; 'if we died with him, we will also live with him'.
2:14–26Handling the word rightly against wranglers — 'the Lord knows those who are his'; a servant of the Lord must be gentle.
3:1–9The hard times to come, and the people who will bring them — 'a form of godliness but denying its power'.
3:10–17Continue in what you learned: 'all Scripture is God-breathed and profitable', the New Testament's central text on Scripture.
4:1–8The final charge: 'preach the word… in season and out'; 'I have fought the good fight… the crown of righteousness is laid up for me'.
4:9–22Last words — come before winter; who left and who stands; the first defence; 'the Lord will rescue me'; greetings.

What it's about

  • ENDURANCE in suffering — the soldier, athlete and farmer of ch. 2; 'suffer along with me' (1:8); the whole letter written by a man taking his own advice under a death sentence.
  • THE DEPOSIT — parathēkē, the entrusted gospel to be guarded 'through the Holy Spirit' (1:12-14) and handed to reliable teachers (2:2); tradition as a trust transmitted intact past the apostles.
  • NOT BEING ASHAMED — of the testimony, of Paul's chain, of the gospel (1:8, 12, 16); the nerve the letter keeps returning to.
  • SCRIPTURE — 3:16, 'all Scripture is God-breathed (theopneustos) and profitable', the New Testament's most quoted line about itself, set in a charge to hold to what was learned in childhood.
  • FAREWELL — the valedictory note unique among Paul's letters: 'I have finished the race… the time of my departure has come' (4:6-8), a settled readiness to die.
  • FAITHFULNESS AND DESERTION — named friends who stood (Onesiphorus, Luke, Timothy) and named people who left (Demas, Phygelus, Alexander the coppersmith); loyalty tested by danger.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. Chapter 1's variants are minor (mainly word order at 1:1 and 1:10). ⚠ As with all three Pastorals the large question is authorial, and 2 Timothy is where it is hardest to press: the letter's dense personal detail makes a pure forgery awkward, and the 'fragment' theories exist precisely to account for material that reads as vintage Paul. The book's single most influential verse, 3:16 ('all Scripture is God-breathed'), turns on where an implied verb falls — 'all Scripture is God-breathed AND profitable' (most versions) or 'all God-breathed Scripture is also profitable' (a grammatically possible minority reading); the difference is small but real, and both are noted where the chapter is treated.

Looking forward

Christ is the one who 'abolished death and brought life and immortality to light through the gospel' (1:10) — the letter's clearest Christological statement, set at his 'appearing' (epiphaneia), the Pastorals' word for his coming. He is 'our Saviour' (1:10), 'raised from the dead, of the seed of David' (2:8, a compressed creed), and the coming Judge 'who will judge the living and the dead… at his appearing and his kingdom' (4:1). ⚠ The famous 'faithful saying' of 2:11-13 folds a hymn into the argument — 'if we died with him, we will also live with him… if we are faithless, he remains faithful, for he cannot deny himself' — grounding endurance in Christ's own constancy. As in the other Pastorals the cross is assumed more than expounded; what the dying Paul presses is the risen Lord who keeps what is entrusted to him 'until that day'.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP, shared with 1 Timothy and Titus — with the special twist that 2 Timothy's personal detail drives the 'genuine fragment' theories. The reading and reach of 3:16 on Scripture. Whether 1:18's prayer for Onesiphorus is offered for a man already dead — one of the few possible New Testament instances, and a point of long division between traditions. And the historical setting the letter assumes: a second Roman imprisonment after a release Acts does not record. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
