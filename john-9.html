Jesus heals a man blind from birth by smearing mud made from spit on his eyes and sending him to wash in the pool of Siloam. The Pharisees, divided over a healer who breaks the Sabbath, interrogate the man twice and then his parents, who deflect every question that risks their own expulsion from the synagogue. Thrown out for refusing to recant, the man is found by Jesus a second time, believes, and worships him — while the Pharisees who insist they already see are told their sin remains.
c. AD 29, immediately continuing the setting of chapters 7-8 — most readers place it still within or just after the Feast of Tabernacles, though the chapter itself gives no date marker of its own.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–5 · ⚠ a question Jesus refuses to answer on its own terms, and a textual fork over who must do the work

The disciples' question assumes a doctrine this chapter never endorses: that suffering is always a receipt for sin, someone's sin, even a child's own sin before he could have committed any. The assumption has deep roots — ‘visiting the iniquity of the fathers upon the children’ (Exodus 20:5, already on these pages) was read by some as license for exactly this kind of reasoning, though Ezekiel 18 (not yet on these pages) already argues at length against it from inside the Hebrew Bible itself. Jesus answers neither branch of the disciples' either/or; he refuses the premise outright, the same move this Gospel's own readers have already seen him make about a different tragedy (Luke 13:2–3, already on these pages, on the Galileans and the tower of Siloam — the same pool this chapter is about to use).

⚠ Verse 4 carries a genuine, meaning-bearing textual fork that the shelf splits along old-versus-new lines. The earliest manuscripts, and on the shelf NIV, read ‘WE must do the works of him who sent me’ — a plural that puts the disciples standing beside Jesus to work before nightfall, which is what this translation follows. A later, Byzantine-leaning tradition keeps the sentence about Jesus alone: Geneva 1599 and Douay-Rheims both read ‘I must work the works of him that sent me’, and RV60 agrees, «me es necesario hacer las obras». The difference is not cosmetic: a reader who hears ‘we’ is handed a share of the daylight-is-short urgency Jesus is describing; a reader who hears ‘I’ is only told something about him.

‘I am the light of the world’ (v5) repeats, almost verbatim, the claim this Gospel made one chapter earlier (John 8:12, already on these pages) — and this chapter, unlike that one, is about to show rather than only say it.

Verses 6–12 · Siloam means ‘Sent’ — mud, a pool, and an ordinary ‘I am’ after an absolute one

Spitting to make a healing paste was an attested ancient practice, not John's invention — but THIS act does something the method alone does not explain: it sends the man away still blind, to wash off a stranger's spit-and-dirt in public, before anything happens. The pool's name does the chapter's real work. Silōam transliterates Hebrew Shiloah, ‘sent, conducted’ (see Siloam), and the narrator stops to gloss it — ‘which means Sent’ — immediately after a chapter that opened on Jesus insisting ‘we must do the works of HIM WHO SENT ME’ (v4). ⚠ The pun is thematic, not strictly lexical: the Gospel's own recurring verb for the Father sending Jesus is pempō (used again at v4), while Siloam's gloss, apestalmenos, comes from the separate verb apostellō — two different Greek roots that both mean ‘send.’ A man blind from birth is sent, by the one Sent, to the pool called Sent, and comes back seeing: the geography enacts the theology without the two words ever being the same word.

The disagreement among the neighbors (vv8–9) turns on an identity question this Gospel keeps asking in bigger forms. ‘Some said, “It is he”… he himself kept saying, “Egō eimi”’ — the identical two Greek words this chapter's own neighboring book has just spent spelling out as an absolute, unpredicated divine claim (John 8:24, 28, 58, already on these pages). Here the construction is wholly ordinary — ‘I am [the man you're asking about]’ — and this translation marks the contrast rather than reading either occurrence back into the other: the same two words carry an everyday identification in one chapter and a claim to eternal being in the one just before it.

Verses 13–17 · why mud, specifically, was the provocation — and a second Sabbath verdict split down the middle

To a reader with no background in rabbinic law, prosecuting someone for mixing spit and dust sounds bizarre; it was not bizarre to the Pharisees who raise it. The Mishnah's later codification of thirty-nine categories of Sabbath-forbidden labor (Shabbat 7:2) includes KNEADING, and ancient and modern commentators alike have read the making of mud as falling under it, or under the neighboring category of ‘building’ by analogy. Jesus does not merely heal on the Sabbath — already contested once at the pool of Bethesda (John 5:10–16, already on these pages) — he heals by a method engineered to multiply the legal objection.

⚠ The Pharisees' own verdict splits exactly along the line this Gospel keeps drawing between the sign and the sign-giver's credentials. ‘This man is not from God, because he does not keep the Sabbath’ reasons from behavior to origin; ‘how can a sinful man perform such signs?’ reasons the other direction, from the sign back to the man. Schisma, ‘division’ (v16), is the same word this Gospel already used of a crowd split over Jesus at the Feast of Tabernacles (John 7:43, already on these pages) — the identical word, the identical argument, now running inside the religious authorities themselves rather than in a crowd they are supposed to be judging.

Verses 18–23 · ⚠ Aposynagōgos — a fear named by a word this Gospel may have coined

The parents answer three questions and dodge the fourth, and the Greek names exactly why: ‘they were afraid of the Jewish leaders.’ Aposynagōgos, ‘put out of the synagogue’ — formal expulsion from the community, not a slur — is a word otherwise unattested before this Gospel, and every one of its three New Testament occurrences is here in John, each time the same threat drawn tighter: first the parents' private dread (here), then open policy among ‘many of the authorities’ (12:42, not yet on these pages), then a plain prediction to the Twelve themselves (16:2, not yet on these pages). ⚠ Many historians read this specific, formal threat as reflecting the later first-century conflict between church and synagogue that this Gospel's own writing community was living through, projected back onto Jesus' own lifetime; the text itself does not say when such a policy began, only that the parents already knew its cost.

Verses 24–29 · a courtroom formula with a thousand-year-old precedent

‘Give glory to God’ is not praise here — it is a demand to confess the truth under oath, and this Gospel is quoting a known legal formula rather than inventing a pious phrase. The Greek Old Testament renders Joshua's own demand of Achan with these exact words, dos doxan tō theō (Joshua 7:19, already on these pages, where the Hebrew idiom is explained in full) — a formula for ‘stop lying and admit what you did,’ not an invitation to worship. The Pharisees are not asking the healed man to thank God; they are putting him under oath to recant.

He answers with a sentence that has outlasted every argument built to contain it: ‘Whether he is a sinner, I don't know. One thing I do know: I was blind, and now I see’ (v25) — refusing their theological premise exactly as Jesus refused the disciples' own premise at the start of this chapter, without any training in argument, only in what happened to him.

Verses 30–34 · the healed man out-argues the court, and the threat of v22 is finally carried out

The man's own case (vv30–33) is built as tightly as anything a trained debater could manage, and the irony is that he is debating men whose job is debate. God does not listen to sinners, but he does listen to the theosebēs, the devout (v31) — a rare word, standing in only one other verse of the whole New Testament, 1 Timothy 2:10 (not yet on these pages), in an entirely unrelated context about how women should dress. ‘Since the world began’ (v32, literally ‘from the age’) is the kind of absolute claim this project checks rather than repeats on faith — the Hebrew Bible records no comparable healing of a man born blind, and this translation finds no reason to doubt the man's own sweeping confidence here.

⚠ The threat the parents feared in v22 is carried out in full within this same chapter, on the man himself, not them. ‘You were born entirely in sin, and are you trying to teach us?’ answers an argument with a slur about origin — the identical move Nicodemus's own interlocutors used against Galilee itself (John 7:52, already on these pages) — and ‘they threw him out’ (v34) is this chapter's own enacted version of being ‘put out of the synagogue,’ even though the Greek here is the plainer ekballō, not aposynagōgos itself.

Verses 35–38 · ⚠ Son of Man, or Son of God? A title the shelf itself cannot agree on

Jesus goes looking for the man the authorities just discarded — the same shape of rescue this Gospel will later give its own name, the Good Shepherd going after sheep not yet gathered into the fold (John 10:16, already on these pages). What he asks him carries a genuine textual fork the shelf itself splits over: the earliest and best-attested manuscripts (including the two earliest papyri covering this verse), and NIV on the shelf, read ‘Do you believe in the Son of Man?’ — the reading this translation follows. ⚠ Geneva 1599 and Douay-Rheims both read ‘Dost thou believe in the Son of God?’ instead, following a Western-leaning tradition — the same split visible on the Spanish shelf. The Living Bible prints neither title, paraphrasing ‘Do you believe in the Messiah?’ — its own interpretive gloss rather than a third manuscript reading.

The one Greek word kyrie (vv36, 38) does two different jobs eight words apart, and this translation keeps them distinct rather than smoothing them to match. At v36 the man does not yet know who he is talking to; ‘sir’ is the ordinary address of a stranger to a stranger, matching NIV's and NVI's ‘sir.’ By v38, told plainly who is speaking to him, he answers ‘Lord’ and prosekynēsen, ‘worshiped’ — a verb this Gospel has already used once of what ‘true worshipers’ owe the Father ‘in spirit and truth’ (John 4:23–24, already on these pages). The healed man is this Gospel's first character to direct that same verb at Jesus himself, in the same breath he is told the truth about who Jesus is.

Verses 39–41 · the word that has carried the whole chapter turns, without warning, from an eye to a mind

For its first thirty-two verses this chapter uses ‘blind’ of one man's eyes; in its last three it is suddenly a verdict on everyone else's understanding, and nothing in the Greek flags the shift — the reader simply has to notice. ‘For judgment I came into this world, so that the blind would see and those who see would become blind’ (v39) answers, almost word for word, the disciples' opening question about whose fault the man's blindness was: it was never a question about one man's eyes at all. The Pharisees supply the proof themselves by asking it: ‘What? Are we blind too?’ (v40) is a question that, asked in good faith, would already be an answer; Jesus's own reply — ‘if you were blind, you would not be guilty of sin. But since you say, “We see,” your sin remains’ (v41) — makes the claim to sight itself, not the blindness, the thing that convicts.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Hoi Ioudaioi kept as ‘the Jewish leaders’ (vv18, 22), the same choice carried since John 1:19 (already on these pages). Kyrie rendered ‘sir’ at v36 and ‘Lord’ at v38, tracking the man's own growing recognition rather than flattening both to one English word. ‘Mud’ rather than ‘clay’ for pēlos, since the man’s own retelling (v11, v15) describes something applied and washed off within minutes, not a potter's material.

Echoes paid. ‘I am the light of the world’ (v5) repeats John 8:12 and gets, in this chapter, the demonstration that chapter's own closing note promised. The disciples' sin-or-punishment question (v2) is the same premise Jesus already refused about the Galileans and the tower of Siloam — this very pool — at Luke 13:4. ‘Give glory to God’ (v24) is the Greek Bible's own formula for Joshua's demand of Achan, already flagged waiting for this verse at Joshua 7:19. And schisma, division (v16), is the same word already splitting a crowd over Jesus at John 7:43.

Echoes planted. Aposynagōgos (v22) names a threat that returns twice more in this Gospel, each time more openly (John 12:42 and 16:2, neither yet on these pages). Jesus going out to find the man the authorities discarded (v35) rehearses, in miniature, the shepherd who goes after sheep not yet gathered into the fold (John 10:16, already on these pages). And the sight/blindness reversal that closes this chapter (vv39–41) is not the last time this Gospel will measure a person by what they refuse to see.

Left open on purpose. Whether the Son of Man or the Son of God is the title Jesus actually used at v35 — a real fork in the earliest witnesses themselves, reported rather than resolved. Whether this chapter's own aposynagōgos reflects a policy already operating in Jesus' lifetime or one his Gospel's first readers were living through decades later — a question of history this translation states rather than settles. And what, exactly, counts as the kneading the Pharisees object to at v14 — the Mishnah that spells out the thirty-nine categories postdates this scene by well over a century, and this chapter never says which rule the man's accusers had in mind.
