Five self-contained laws, each closed off by its own Masoretic paragraph break — after vv9, 14, 17, 21 and 23 — and with no stated connection between them. First the rite for an unsolved killing: a body in a field, elders and judges measuring out to the surrounding towns, and the nearest town’s elders breaking a heifer’s neck in a valley that is neither worked nor sown, washing their hands over it and asking Jehovah to make atonement. Second, the captive woman: shaved head, pared or untended nails, the clothes of her captivity set aside, and a full month of mourning for her parents before anything else may happen — and, if she is later unwanted, freedom rather than sale. Third, the two-wives inheritance rule: a father may not treat the loved wife’s son as firstborn over the hated wife’s actual firstborn, who keeps the double share. Fourth, the stubborn and rebellious son, brought by both parents to the elders at the gate and stoned by the whole town. Fifth, the one-and-a-half-verse rule that a body hung on a tree after execution may not stay there overnight.
Still the plains of Moab in the fortieth year (1:3); no narrated event at all — the code runs on unbroken from chapter 20, whose law of war this chapter’s second paragraph presupposes. ⚠ The Masoretic tradition begins a new weekly Torah portion, Ki Teitzei, at v10, so the chapter straddles a division the chapter numbering does not show. ⭐ Three of its five laws reach backward into narrative already on these pages rather than forward into anything Israel has yet done: the loved-and-hated wives and the demoted firstborn are Genesis 29 and 49:3-4 stated as statute, and the hand-washing oath is the gesture Matthew 27:24 gives Pilate. Like the chapters around it the law assumes settled conditions Israel has not reached — towns with elders and measurable distances between them, herds, inheritances, town gates. Hebrew and English versification agree: 23 verses on both sides.

Verses 1–4 · Eglah arufah, the broken-necked heifer (Deuteronomy 21:1–4) — an unsolved murder answered by a rite with no trial, no accused and no altar

A body in a field, no witness, no suspect. Everything the last four chapters built — the two-or-three witnesses, the referral of a hard case upward to the chosen place, the cities of refuge for a killer who can be named — assumes there is somebody to try. Here there is not, and the law does not shrug. It measures. Your elders and your judges shall go out, and the nearest town inherits a duty it did nothing to earn: geography, not guilt, is what selects the responsible party.

★★ Chalal, a third time, and a third meaning. Deuteronomy 2:24 used this root for BEGIN and 20:6 for releasing a vineyard’s fruit TO COMMON USE; here the same three consonants are a noun, and the noun is a dead man — one pierced through. Inside Deuteronomy the consonants chet-lamed-lamed stand in eight verses, and this chapter owns four of them, vv1, 2, 3 and 6, every one of them the corpse. These three senses are not really three ideas: something shut is opened. A vineyard is opened to eating, a conquest is opened by its first campaign, and a body is opened by whatever went into it.

⭐ The valley is the chapter’s hardest two words, and one other verse decides them. Nachal eitan stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible. This one, and Amos 5:24 (not yet on these pages): let justice roll down like waters, and righteousness like a nachal eitan. Nothing about a rugged gully would make that sentence work; what Amos wants is a stream that does not dry up in the summer, and that is what eitan contributes — permanence. This translation follows Amos. ⚠ The shelf splits, and the split is old. The Latin took it as terrain, and the versions downstream of the Latin keep the terrain: the Douay reads ‘a rough and stony valley’; the KJV reads ‘a rough valley’; the Geneva reads ‘a stony valley’; the RV 1909 reads «un valle áspero»; and the RV60 reads «un valle escabroso». The water reading is the other half of the shelf and it is the more literal one: the ASV reads ‘a valley with running water’; the NWT 1984 reads ‘torrent valley running with water’; the TLB reads ‘where there is running water’; the NIV reads ‘a flowing stream’; and the NVI reads «un arroyo de aguas continuas». ⭐ Which puts five of the thirteen on the terrain side and the remaining eight on the water side of a question that a single verse of Amos answers — and it is the same shape of result this project keeps finding: agreement with the familiar English is not the same thing as precision.

★ The verb for what is done to the heifer is a specialist word, and its other victims are a donkey and a dog. Araf means to break the neck. Outside this chapter the only necks the Torah breaks are a donkey’s, twice, in the same law both times: Exodus 13:13 and Exodus 34:20 (both already on these pages), the firstborn donkey that is not redeemed with a lamb. Isaiah 66:3 (not yet on these pages) puts it in a list of parodied worship — one who sacrifices a lamb is as one who breaks a dog’s neck. ⚠ The tally has to be read rather than matched, because the same three consonants also spell oref, the nape of the neck behind every ‘stiff-necked people’ in the book, and at Deuteronomy 32:2 they spell a verb about rain dripping. ⚠ What matters is that the word carries no sacrificial dignity at all, because this is emphatically NOT a sacrifice: it happens in a valley rather than at the chosen place, the animal is killed by a snapped neck rather than a knife, no blood is caught, nothing is burned, and no part of it is eaten. The heifer is disposed of, not offered.

⚠ Two negatives choose the animal and two more choose the ground, and they are the same two negatives. The heifer has never been worked and has never drawn in a yoke; the valley is never worked and never sown. The identical verb, avad, is the hinge on both sides — the rite requires a creature and a place that human labour has not yet touched. ⭐ Numbers 19:2 (already on these pages) asks for the same untouched quality in the red heifer, unblemished and never yoked, and this project’s note there is worth setting beside this one: two heifers, each taken away from the sanctuary — one outside the camp, one down into a valley — both killed in a way the altar never uses, both dealing with contact with a corpse.

Verses 5–9 · Washing the hands over the blood (Deuteronomy 21:6–7) — the priests come near and say nothing, and Pilate borrows the gesture

⚠ The priests arrive with a full job description and are then given no line and no action. Verse 5 brings them near, states why they were chosen, and hands them jurisdiction over every dispute and every affliction — which is Deuteronomy’s own shorthand, the same pairing 17:8 uses when it sends a hard case up to the chosen place, one legal category and one medical one (nega is the word Leviticus 13 uses of a mark on the skin that a priest must inspect). And then the rite passes them by. It is the elders who wash, the elders who speak, the elders who pray. Whatever this ceremony is, it is not a priestly one, and the chapter tells you so by putting the priests in the frame and leaving them silent.

★★ The one prayer in the ritual is an imperative aimed at God, and it is the atonement verb. Kapper le-amkha, make atonement for your people, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The standard shape of that verb is Leviticus 1:4 (already on these pages), where an offering is accepted and the priest is the one who makes atonement with it. Here there is no offering — the heifer is not one — and the men speaking are not priests, so the elders simply put the verb in the imperative and hand it to God. The verse then reports, in the passive, that it worked: and the blood shall be atoned for them.

★ The oath has a small scribal seam in it. The written consonants of v7 give a singular verb, shafkhah, ‘it shed’; the reading tradition corrects it to the plural shafkhu, ‘they shed’, agreeing with our hands. That ketiv/qere pair is printed above exactly as the Masoretic text prints it, unpointed word first. All thirteen witnesses render the plural.

⭐ The gesture outlived the law by more than a thousand years, and its most famous performance is a denial of exactly what this rite admits. At Matthew 27:24 (already on these pages) Pilate takes water, washes his hands in front of the crowd, and says Ἀθῷός εἰμι ἀπὸ τοῦ αἵματος τούτου — I am innocent of this blood. ⚠ The words are close and the situations are opposite. The elders of Deuteronomy 21 wash their hands over an animal to say something that is simply true — they really did not do it, and nobody has accused them — and having said it they still pay for the animal, still hold the ceremony, and still ask for atonement, because the land has blood on it whether or not they put it there. Pilate washes his to detach himself from a sentence he is at that moment passing.

⚠ The shelf disagrees about what the elders are asking for, and most of it asks for the wrong thing. The Geneva reads v8 ‘be merciful unto thy people Israel’ and the KJV keeps that same ‘Be merciful’; the Douay ‘Be merciful to thy people Israel’; the ASV ‘Forgive … thy people Israel’. Mercy and forgiveness are both things a person asks for; atonement is a thing performed on an object, and it is the word the Hebrew has. ⭐ Only the NIV reaches for it in English, ‘Accept this atonement’. ★ And the Spanish shelf has the sharpest witness of all: the RV 1909 prints the cultic verb itself as an imperative — «Expía á tu pueblo Israel» — where its own RV60 revision retreats to «Perdona a tu pueblo Israel», forgive. On this verse the older Reina-Valera is the more exact of the two.

Verse 9 closes the case with the formula the book reserves for capital matters: you shall burn out the innocent blood from your midst. ⚠ The blood being burned out belongs to the victim, not the killer — the same inversion 19:13 already made, and the reason dam naqi is best read as a stain with a location rather than as anyone’s guilt.

Verses 10–14 · The captive woman (Deuteronomy 21:10–14) — a full month of mourning first, and a verb used elsewhere only of a kidnapper

This is the law that follows immediately on chapter 20’s rules of war, and it legislates the moment those rules produce: a soldier, a column of captives, and a woman he wants. It does not forbid him. What it does is slow him down — her head is shaved, her nails are done, the clothes she was captured in come off, and then a full month passes in which the only thing she is described as doing is weeping for her parents. Whatever else the month is, it is a month in which she is not touched, imposed on a man whose own desire is the reason the law is being written.

⚠ The nails are genuinely undecided in the Hebrew, and every version hides it. The verb is asah — the ordinary word for DO or MAKE, with no direction in it at all. And she shall DO her nails. Cut them, or let them grow? The Hebrew does not say, and it is the one detail in the sequence that could point either way: shaving the head and stripping the captive’s clothing are acts of mourning, and so is letting the nails go untended. The KJV, the ASV, the Geneva and the Douay all print ‘pare her nails’. The RV 1909 and the RV60 both cut them, «cortará sus uñas». The NVI reads «se corte las uñas». The NIV reads ‘trim her nails’. The TLB reads ‘pare her nails’. ⭐ Exactly two of the thirteen keep the Hebrew’s reticence, and they are the pair this project keeps finding on the literal side of a question. The NWT 1984 reads ‘attend to her nails’. The TNM 1987 reads «arreglarse las uñas». Each leaves the direction open exactly as asah does. This translation prints the undecided verb.

★★ She is given a month of days, and the phrase is not the ordinary word for a month. Yerach yamim stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, and 2 Kings 15:13 (not yet on these pages), where Shallum son of Jabesh becomes king in Samaria and reigns yerach yamim before he is struck down. ⚠ Yerach is the moon-word, distinct from the ordinary chodesh this book uses everywhere else for a month; the doubled expression means one whole lunation, start to finish. So the one phrase Hebrew has for a month measured out in full is spent on a captive woman’s grief and on a reign that ended almost as soon as it began — the same span, one of them far too short and the other, from the soldier’s side of the transaction, far too long.

⭐ The verb that protects her afterwards occurs in only one other place, and it is in this same book. If he no longer wants her he must let her go to herself — the Hebrew idiom is exactly that, le-nafshah, to her own person. He may not sell her, and he may not hit’amer in her. That verb stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one and Deuteronomy 24:7 (now on these pages), where the death sentence falls on a man who steals a person from among his brothers, does hit’amer to him, and sells him. ⚠ That is the whole content of the word: it names what a kidnapper does to the human being he has turned into stock. The law tells a soldier that the woman he took in war and then tired of is not merchandise — using the one verb the Bible otherwise reserves for the crime of trafficking a person. The versions grope for it: the KJV and Geneva read ‘make merchandise of her’, the ASV ‘deal with her as a slave’, the Douay ‘oppress her by might’, the RV 1909 «ni mercadearás con ella».

★ The last clause names what happened, and it is the Bible’s hardest word for it. Because you have violated her — innitah, the intensive of anah, which is the verb used of Shechem and Dinah at Genesis 34:2 (already on these pages). ⚠ The older English shelf softens it: the KJV reads ‘humbled her’, and so do the ASV, the Geneva and the Douay. Two modern witnesses reach further: the NVI reads «deshonrado», dishonoured, and the TLB reads ‘humiliated’. ⭐ Whatever the month of mourning is for, the chapter’s own final word on the transaction is not a euphemism, and a translation that prints ‘humbled’ lets the reader past the one sentence in the law that refuses to.

Verses 15–17 · The loved wife and the hated wife (Deuteronomy 21:15–17) — the birthright law that describes Jacob’s household exactly and forbids what Jacob did

★★ Two words in this law belong to one story, and the story is Genesis 29. Senuah, the HATED wife, stands in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible. Three are vv15, 16 and 17 here; two are Isaiah 60:15 and Proverbs 30:23 (neither yet on these pages), where it is a city and a wife in a proverb; and the remaining two are Genesis 29:31 and 29:33 (already on these pages), both of Leah. ⭐ And the other wife in that household is described with this chapter’s other phrase: yefat to’ar, beautiful in form, the wording v11 gives the captive woman, is used of Rachel at Genesis 29:17. The law about a loved wife and a hated wife is written in the two phrases Genesis used of Rachel and Leah, and the son of the hated one whose birthright is at stake in Genesis is Reuben.

⚠ Which makes the law a rebuke of the patriarch, and the Bible says so itself in the same vocabulary. At Genesis 49:3 (already on these pages) Jacob calls Reuben my might and the first of my strength — re’shit oni, the same construction v17 uses, re’shit ono — and in the next line demotes him. 1 Chronicles 5:1 (not yet on these pages) then states the outcome flatly: Reuben was the firstborn, but because he defiled his father’s couch his birthright was given to the sons of Joseph, who was Rachel’s. ⭐ That is precisely the transfer this law forbids — the loved wife’s son advanced over the hated wife’s firstborn — and the verb 1 Chronicles uses for what Reuben did is chalal, the root this chapter opened with, in its fourth sense: to profane.

★★ The double share is written with two words that mean something completely different four chapters ago. Pi shnayim — literally ‘the mouth of two’ — stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible. In this one it is the firstborn’s double portion; at 2 Kings 2:9 (not yet on these pages) it is what Elisha asks of Elijah, a double share of his spirit — framed, in those two words, as the portion a firstborn would inherit; at Zechariah 13:8 (not yet on these pages) it is two parts in three, cut off and perishing. ⚠ And the fourth is Deuteronomy 17:6, already on these pages, where the identical two words mean at the mouth of two witnesses and have nothing to do with inheritance at all. The same string, in the same book, four chapters apart, doing two unrelated jobs.

⚠ ‘Hated’ is a legal term and half the shelf will not print it. Hebrew’s senuah here is comparative — the less-loved of two — which is why the older witnesses can keep the hard word without implying cruelty: the KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay all read ‘hated’, and the RV 1909 and RV60 both keep «aborrecida». ⭐ The revisions are where the softening happens, and it happens on both shelves at once. The NWT 1984 keeps ‘the other hated’, and its 2013 revision drops to ‘the unloved one’. The TNM 1987 keeps «la odiada», and the TNM 2019 moves to «la menos amada», the less loved. The NIV and NVI paraphrase the relation instead of naming it. ⚠ Something real is lost either way: ‘hated’ overstates in English, and ‘less loved’ deletes the word Genesis used of Leah, which is the whole reason this law is written the way it is. This translation keeps hated and puts the comparative here.

Verse 16’s verb is worth a glance: levakker, to TREAT AS FIRSTBORN, is built directly off bekhor itself, the way English might make a verb of ‘to firstborn someone’. The law does not ask a father to feel differently about his two wives. It removes his power to convert the feeling into an estate.

Verses 18–21 · The stubborn and rebellious son (Deuteronomy 21:18–21) — a phrase Jeremiah turns on the whole nation, and a charge the Gospels lay against Jesus

The parents bring the charge and the parents lose control of it. What they hand over at the gate is not a punishment they have chosen; the sentence belongs to all the men of his city, and the father who could not make his son listen has no more say in the outcome than any of them. ⚠ The father’s power in the previous law was over an estate and this one takes away his power over a life — read the two paragraphs together and the chapter is systematically moving the household’s decisions out to the bench at the gate.

★★ The son’s two adjectives are the pair Jeremiah uses of Israel. Sorer u-moreh, stubborn and defiant, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: v18 here, and Jeremiah 5:23 (not yet on these pages) — this people has a stubborn and defiant HEART. ⭐ The prophet takes the law’s clinical description of one impossible teenager and applies it, word for word, to the nation that wrote it; and since the sentence attached to those two words in Deuteronomy is death by stoning, the quotation is doing more than borrowing an adjective.

⚠ Nothing in the Hebrew says the son is a child. Ben is a son of any age, and the two specific accusations of v20 — glutton and drunkard — describe an adult with money and appetite rather than a disobedient boy. The TLB is the one witness that treats the charge as a single vice, ‘a worthless drunkard’, collapsing the pair; the Douay expands it in the other direction, ‘revelling, and to debauchery and banquetings’, three nouns for the Hebrew’s two words. Both are decisions about who this person is, made where the Hebrew declines to make one.

★★ The pair of nouns stands here and its only near-twin reverses the order and drops the sentence. Zolel ve-sove, glutton and drunkard, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Proverbs 23:21 (not yet on these pages) has the same two words in the opposite order, for a drunkard and a glutton shall come to poverty — the same behaviour, and the consequence is that he ends up in rags, not that he is stoned at the gate. Wisdom literature and case law looking at one man and reaching for the same two words.

⭐ And the charge has a New Testament career that nobody quotes it for. At Matthew 11:19 (already on these pages), the Son of Man comes eating and drinking and they say — Ἰδοὺ ἄνθρωπος φάγος καὶ οἰνοπότης, look, a man who is a glutton and a drunkard. ⚠ It reads as ordinary abuse in English. It is the exact charge that, in the law of this chapter, a father and mother lay before the elders of a city as grounds for execution.

Verses 22–23 · Cursed is everyone hanged on a tree (Deuteronomy 21:23) — the one verse of law standing behind Galatians 3:13, and the two ways to read its three words

The hanging in this law is not the execution. The man is already dead — he is put to death, AND you hang him on a tree — so what is regulated here is the public display of a corpse, and the regulation is that it may last less than one day. ★★ And the verb for that limit is shared with exactly one other law. Lo talin, ‘shall not stay the night’, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, and Leviticus 19:13 (already on these pages), the wages of a hired man shall not stay the night with you until morning. ⭐ A labourer’s pay and an executed criminal’s body: two things Hebrew law will not let you keep overnight, and the same clause covers both. Neither is about the dignity of the person who is owed — the wage belongs to the worker and the burial belongs to the ground — but in both cases holding on past sundown is what turns a lawful act into a wrong.

★★ Three Hebrew words, and the shelf has never agreed on which way round they run. Qilelat Elohim, a curse of God, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the two nouns are simply set side by side, with nothing in the Hebrew to say whether God is the one cursing or the one cursed — whether the corpse on the tree is under God’s curse, or is itself an affront to God that the land must be rid of before nightfall. Both readings explain the rest of the verse, which is why they have both survived. ⚠ The versions mostly print a form as ambiguous as the Hebrew. The KJV and the ASV both read ‘accursed of God’. The Geneva reads ‘the curse of God is on him that is hanged’. The Douay reads ‘he is accursed of God that hangeth on a tree’. ⭐ The two revisions on the Spanish shelf are where the ambiguity is deliberately closed. The RV 1909 keeps the noun exactly as Hebrew has it, «maldición de Dios es el colgado» — a curse of God is the hanged one. Its own RV60 revision rewrites that into an explicit passive with an agent, «maldito por Dios es el colgado», cursed BY God. The TNM 1987 and its TNM 2019 revision move the same way, from a thing accursed to a man who ha sido maldecido por Dios. In each pair the older edition preserves a question and the newer answers it. This translation prints the noun.

★★ Paul builds his central argument on this verse and changes it in one specific place. Galatians 3:13 (not yet on these pages) reads ἐπικατάρατος πᾶς ὁ κρεμάμενος ἐπὶ ξύλου — cursed is EVERYONE hanged on a tree. ⚠ Two differences from the Hebrew, and both do work. The Hebrew has no ‘everyone’; Paul adds the universal quantifier, which is what lets a law about one executed criminal reach a particular crucifixion. And Paul’s clause has no God in it: the Hebrew’s Elohim is simply not there in the citation. ⭐ The omission is what makes the argument survivable — Paul is about to say that Christ became a curse for us, and the sentence he needed was one that could carry a curse without naming who pronounced it. The three most disputed words in the verse are the three he leaves out.

⚠ One word of the verse is where a translation’s theology becomes visible, and it should be named plainly rather than scored. The Hebrew is etz, the ordinary word for a tree or for wood. The KJV, ASV, Geneva and TLB all print ‘tree’; the Douay prints ‘tree’ at v23 but ‘gibbet’ at v22, importing a gallows the Hebrew does not name. The NWT 1984 and its 2013 revision read ‘stake’, which is that translation’s published position on the shape of the instrument Jesus died on, applied here in the Hebrew law the position is argued from; the NIV arrives at ‘pole’ from a different direction entirely. ⚠ The Spanish shelf is not evidence of the same thing and should not be read as if it were: TNM 1987 and TNM 2019 read «madero», but so do the RV 1909, the RV60 and the NVI — it is simply what Spanish calls a piece of timber. This translation prints the tree.

The last clause is the reason for the whole law, and it is not mercy toward the executed man. You shall not defile your ground — the same anxiety the chapter opened with, a corpse lying on land that Jehovah is giving as an inheritance. ⭐ Deuteronomy 21 begins with a body nobody may leave unaccounted for in a field and ends with a body nobody may leave hanging overnight, and the argument in both directions is about what the ground can be made to hold.

Echoes paid. Chalal’s third sense, promised by chapter 20’s own closing note: BEGIN at 2:24, RELEASE-TO-COMMON-USE at 20:6, and SLAIN in four verses of this chapter — with a fourth sense, TO PROFANE, surfacing at Genesis 49:4 in the very story vv15–17 are written against. The bekhorah entry’s standing claim that the birthright meant ‘a double share of the inheritance (Deuteronomy 21:17)’ — the verse it pointed at is now on these pages, and the two words it rests on turn out to mean something else four chapters earlier. The nega entry’s note that 17:8’s between nega and nega is genuinely undetermined between a disease and a blow: the word stands in three verses of Deuteronomy — 17:8, 21:5, and 24:8’s skin-disease warning — and only the first two set it beside riv, which leaves the question exactly where 17:8 left it. And dam naqi, whose entry describes innocent blood as a stain with a location rather than anyone’s guilt — this chapter is the ritual built on precisely that premise.

Echoes planted. Hit’amer, the trafficking verb, whose only other occurrence is Deuteronomy 24:7 (now on these pages), three chapters ahead. Taluy, the hanged man, whose other occurrences are Joshua 10:26 and 2 Samuel 18:10 (neither yet on these pages) — five kings left hanging until evening, and Absalom caught in a terebinth. Galatians 3:13 (not yet on these pages), which quotes v23 without its Elohim. And nachal eitan, whose one other verse is Amos 5:24 (not yet on these pages), the sentence about justice rolling down.

Library. Five new dictionary terms in both languages — araf, eitan, on, sorer and talah. Four existing entries extended rather than duplicated — chalal (the third and fourth senses of the same root), bekhorah and senuah (both paying a forward claim they had been carrying since Genesis), and naqi. ⚠ Senuah was the duplicate-word trap: the entry already existed, coined for Genesis 29:31, and it already promised that the law of a loved and a hated wife was this chapter’s — so the right move was to pay that promise inside it rather than mint a second entry. Bekhorah and senuah both gained Spanish twins for the first time, so the count of English terms with no Spanish entry moves down by two rather than up. No new encyclopedia entries: the chapter’s only proper nouns are Israel, Jehovah and Levi, and all three already exist on these pages. ⚠ VIDEO_QUEUE was checked per the standing rule; nothing queued belongs here.

Next installment in this book: Deuteronomy 22 — twenty-nine verses that name no person and no place at all, the same silence chapter 19 keeps. A neighbour’s stray ox must be brought back, and if you do not know whose it is you keep it until he comes looking; a bird’s nest may be taken but not the mother sitting on it; a new roof needs a parapet, because blood on your house is your blood. Then a seam of four short prohibitions: no sowing a vineyard with two kinds of seed, no plowing with an ox and a donkey together, no wearing wool and linen woven as one — and twisted cords on the four corners of your covering, the verse behind every tzitzit worn since. And then the chapter turns: its last seventeen verses are sexual case law, and the very last of them ends on innah, the verb 21:14 has just used of the captive woman.
