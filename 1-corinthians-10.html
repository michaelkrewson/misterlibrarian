The same letter, still from Ephesus. Paul turns from his own rights to the people of the desert, who ate the same food and drank the same drink and still fell, and from there to the table of the Lord and the table of demons. The chapter is the warning against idolatry that the meat question of chapter 8 has been leading toward.

Verses 1–5 · under the cloud and through the sea, and the rock that was Christ

“Our fathers were all under the cloud.” Hoi patēres hēmōn pantes hypo tēn nephelēn ēsan, our fathers were all under the cloud. Paul calls the generation of the exodus our fathers, and the Corinthians, most of them Gentiles, are made heirs of the story. The phrase I do not want you to be ignorant (ou thelō hymas agnoein) comes back in 12:1, already on these pages. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “our forefathers were all under the cloud and all passed through the sea,” the KJV “all our fathers were under the cloud, and all passed through the sea,” and the ASV “our fathers were all under the cloud, and all passed through the sea.”

“Baptized into Moses.” eis ton Mōysēn ebaptisanto, they were baptized into Moses. The preposition is eis, into, not the en that Paul uses of the cloud and the sea in the same verse; the Israelites are put into Moses’ leadership by the crossing. The verse does not say that the cloud or the sea did the baptizing; both are the setting. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “all got baptized into Moses by means of the cloud and of the sea,” and the KJV “baptized unto Moses in the cloud and in the sea.” The translation keeps “into” where the Greek has eis.

“The same spiritual food and drink.” To auto pneumatikon brōma and pneumatikon pōma. The adjective pneumatikos is Paul’s word for what belongs to the Spirit, the word of 2:15, where the spiritual person judges all things. Manna and water are called spiritual because God gave them, and the sentence does not say they were a different kind of food. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “all ate the same spiritual food, and all drank the same spiritual drink,” and the KJV “did all eat the same spiritual meat; and did all drink the same spiritual drink.”

“The rock that followed them, and the rock was Christ.” ek pneumatikēs akolouthousēs petras, hē petra de ēn ho Christos. Exodus 17:6 and Numbers 20:11 describe a rock struck for water. Numbers 21:16–20 describes a well. Neither says that the rock travelled with the camp, so the sentence the rock that followed them is Paul’s own reading, and the note is his. The copula is ēn, was, and the translation keeps it. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “that rock meant the Christ,” which turns was into meant, and the KJV “that Rock was Christ.” The NWT’s meant is a reading of the sentence, not a rendering of the verb. The Greek word is petra, a rock or ledge, and not the word for a stone that is carried.

“God was not pleased with most of them.” eudokēsen ho theos, God was pleased, here in the negative. The verb is the verb of good pleasure. The next clause, they were laid low in the wilderness (katestrōthēsan, strewn or scattered like the dead on a field), refers to the deaths recorded at Numbers 14:29 and the forty years of wandering. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “God did not express his approval, for they were laid low in the wilderness,” and the KJV “with many of them God was not well pleased: for they were overthrown in the wilderness.”

Verse 6 · the examples, and the craving that they were written to prevent

“Now these things became examples.” Tauta de tupoi hēmōn egenēthēsan, these things became types of us. The word tupos is the word for a mark struck by a blow, a model, or a pattern (typos). The Israelites are a model that the Corinthians are to read as a warning, not a sign that they are already saved. So that we would not be people who crave evil things: the verb epithymeō is the verb of craving, the same root that Paul will use of the Israelites in the verse; they craved, and the Corinthians are told not to crave the same things. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “these things became examples for us, in order for us not to desire injurious things, as they desired them,” and the KJV “Now these things were our examples, to the intent we should not lust after evil things, as they also lusted.” The translation keeps the Greek word evil things and does not turn it into a list.

Verse 7 · the people who sat down to eat and drink and rose to play

“Do not become idolaters.” mēde eidōlolatrai ginesthe, do not become idolaters. The word is built from eidōlon, the image or idol (eidōlon), and latris, a worshipper. Paul uses the same root in the next chapter’s context of the idol in 8:4, where an idol is nothing. Here the idol has become something real, for the one who sits down to its feast.

“The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play.” The quotation is Ekathisen ho laos phagein kai piein, kai anestēsan paizein, from Exodus 32:6, where the people sit to eat and drink before the golden calf and rise to play. The verb paizō means to play, to dance, or to revel; the Greek of Exodus is the Septuagint’s, and Paul quotes it word for word. The verse is the golden calf, and the shelf: the NWT 1984 “The people sat down to eat and drink, and they got up to have a good time,” the KJV “The people sat down to eat and drink, and rose up to play.” The translation keeps “rose up to play” because the word is play, not a good time.

Verse 8 · the fornication at Shittim, and the number that Paul gives

“Let us not commit sexual immorality.” mēde porneuōmen, let us not commit fornication. The verb is the root of porneia, the word for sexual immorality in general, and the sentence points to the episode at Shittim in Numbers 25:1–9, where the people go whoring with the daughters of Moab.

“Twenty-three thousand fell in one day.” kai ephesan mia hēmera eikosi treis chiliades. The Hebrew of Numbers 25:9 gives arbaʿah wĕʿeśrîm ʾelep, twenty-four thousand, and Paul gives twenty-three. Paul’s number is not the Hebrew’s, and the note records the difference rather than resolving it. The word epesan, fell, is the word for a fall in battle or in judgment. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “23,000 of them in one day,” the NWT 1984 “only to fall, twenty-three thousand [of them] in one day,” the KJV “and fell in one day three and twenty thousand,” and the ASV “and fell in one day three and twenty thousand.” The translation keeps the number as Paul writes it.

Verse 9 · putting the Christ to the test, and the serpents

“Let us not put the Christ to the test.” mēde ekpeirazōmen ton Christon. The verb ekpeirazō is a strengthened form of peirazō, to test, tempt, or try, and the sentence says the people tested the Christ. The critical text prints ton Christon, and some early witnesses read ton kyrion, the Lord, which would make the sentence match the Septuagint of Numbers 21:5–6, where the complaint is against God and Moses. The note records both readings. The translation keeps the printed text.

“Destroyed by the serpents.” hypo tōn ophewn apōllyanto. The serpents of Numbers 21:6 are sent after the people speak against God and Moses and the food. The verb apollymi, to destroy, is the verb used of the dead in verse 5 as well. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “Neither let us put Jehovah to the test, as some of them put [him] to the test,” the KJV “Neither let us tempt Christ, as some of them also tempted, and were destroyed of serpents.” The NWT prints “Jehovah” where the critical text reads Christ; the translation follows the Greek and says so here.

Verse 10 · murmuring, and the destroyer

“Do not grumble.” mēde gongyzete, do not murmur. The verb is the verb of low complaint, the murmuring of the camp. The people murmur in Numbers 14:2 and in Numbers 17:6 (16:41 in the Hebrew), and the destroyer comes in Numbers 17:11–14 (16:46–49 in the Hebrew).

“The destroyer.” hypo tou olothreutou, by the destroyer. The noun olothreutēs is a title for the agent of death, the verb of the destroyer in Exodus 12:23 and of the plague after Korah. Paul does not name him. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “Neither be murmurers, just as some of them murmured, only to perish by the destroyer,” the ASV “Neither murmur ye, as some of them murmured, and perished by the destroyer.” The KJV has “destroyed of the destroyer” with the same Greek noun.

Verses 11–13 · examples for the end of the ages, the one who stands, and the way out

“As examples, and written for our warning.” tupikōs synebainen ekeinois, these things happened to them as types, and egraphē pros nouthesian hēmōn, they were written for our admonition. The noun nouthesia is the instruction that corrects, from the verb noutheteō that Paul uses of his children in 4:14. The Israelites are the earlier text, and the Corinthians the later.

“On whom the ends of the ages have come.” eis hous ta telē tōn aiōnōn katēntēken. The Greek means the ends of the ages have arrived upon us, and the verb katantaō, to come to, is the verb of an arrival. The sentence says the end is already present for the Corinthians. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “upon whom the ends of the systems of things have come,” and the KJV “upon whom the ends of the world are come.” The translation keeps “ages” for aiōnes and does not use “world.”

“Let the one who thinks he stands take care.” ho dokōn hestanai blepetō mē pesē. The verb dokeō means to think or seem, and Paul does not say the man is standing. The warning is to the one who only seems to stand. The shelf: the ASV “Wherefore let him that thinketh he standeth take heed lest he fall,” and the NWT 1984 “Consequently let him that thinks he is standing beware that he does not fall.”

“No temptation has come to you except what is common to people.” peirasmos hymas ouk eilēphen ei mē anthrōpinos. The word peirasmos can mean a test or a temptation, and the verse keeps both senses in play. The adjective anthrōpinos is the one for what is human; the test is one that people share. God will not let you be tested beyond what you are able to bear. The way out (ekbasis) is the exit, and God makes it along with the test, not after it. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “No temptation has come upon you except what is common to men,” and the KJV “There hath no temptation taken you but such as is common to man,” with “the way to escape” for ekbasis.

Verses 14–15 · flee idolatry, and judge for yourselves

“Flee from idolatry.” phygete apo tēs eidōlolatrias, flee from idolatry. The verb is the verb of running away, the one used of fleeing fornication in 6:18. The command is not a rule about meat; it is the conclusion of the whole argument, and it is addressed to the beloved.

“I speak to sensible people.” hōs phronimois legō, I speak as to people of sense. The adjective phronimos is the word of 4:10, where Paul says the Corinthians are wise in Christ. The sentence invites the reader to decide for himself, and the Greek verb krinate is the verb of judging. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “I speak as to men with discernment; judge for yourselves what I say,” and the ASV “I speak as to wise men; judge ye what I say.”

Verses 16–17 · the cup and the loaf as a sharing, and one body from one loaf

“The cup of blessing that we bless.” to potērion tēs eulogias ho eulogoumen, the cup of blessing which we bless. The verb eulogeō is the one for a blessing said over food, and the cup is the one at the meal. The sentence speaks of a shared cup and a shared loaf, and it calls the two a koinōnia, a sharing (koinōnia). The Greek asks a question with ouchi, which expects yes: it is a sharing.

“Is it not a sharing in the blood of Christ.” koinōnia tou haimatos tou Christou. The genitive names what is shared: the blood, and then the body. Paul uses the same words of the Lord’s supper that he will recount in chapter 11, not yet on these pages. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “The cup of blessing that we bless, is it not a sharing in the blood of the Christ?” and the KJV “is it not the communion of the blood of Christ?” The translation prints sharing and the note keeps the Greek noun; the NWT and KJV have “communion” as the old English for the same word.

“One loaf, one body.” hoti heis artos, hen sōma hoi polloi esmen. The argument is that the sharing makes the many one: because there is one loaf, we who are many are one body. The verb metechomen, we share, is the verb of having a part, the same root as koinōnia. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “Because there is one loaf, we, although many, are one body, for we are all partaking of that one loaf.”

Verse 18 · Israel according to the flesh, and the altar

“Look at Israel according to the flesh.” blepete ton Israēl kata sarka, look at Israel according to the flesh. The phrase kata sarka is the one Paul uses for a human standard, the word sarx for flesh in its ordinary sense. The sentence points to the people who eat the sacrifices, and the priests of the altar are the fleshly Israel of the law.

“Sharers with the altar.” koinōnoi tou thysiastēriou eisin. The Greek word for the altar is thysiastērion, the place of sacrifice, and to share in it is to eat the part of the offering that remains. The argument is an argument from sacrifice: eating the sacrifice is a sharing in the altar. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “LOOK at that which is Israel in a fleshly way: Are not those who eat the sacrifices sharers with the altar?” and the KJV “Behold Israel after the flesh: are not they which eat of the sacrifices partakers of the altar?”

Verses 19–20 · the idol is nothing, and the sacrifice is to demons

“What, then, am I saying?” ti oun phēmi, what then am I saying. Paul asks what his argument has come to, and answers with a claim that sounds like the one he made in 8:4. The idol is nothing. The question is what the sacrifice is.

“Something sacrificed to an idol.” eidolothyton, the word for what is sacrificed to an idol (eidolothyton), a word built from eidōlon and thyō, to sacrifice. Paul concedes that the meat is something in itself, and that an idol is nothing. The next sentence says what the sacrifice is.

“To demons and not to God.” ha thyousin daimoniois kai ou theō thyousin, what they sacrifice they sacrifice to demons and not to God. The word daimonion is the word for a spirit or a demon, and Paul uses it for the powers behind the idols, not for the idols themselves. Deuteronomy 32:17 is the background, where the Israelites sacrifice to shedim, demons, which the Septuagint renders daimoniois. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “But I say that the things which the nations sacrifice they sacrifice to demons, and not to God,” and the KJV “they sacrifice to devils, and not to God.” The translation keeps “demons” for daimonia, as the NWT does; the KJV’s “devils” is the older English for the same word.

Verses 21–22 · the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons, and the jealousy

“You cannot drink the cup of the Lord and the cup of demons.” ou dynasthe potērion kyriou pinein kai potērion daimoniōn. The verb dynasthe is the word for being able, and the sentence says it is impossible to share in both. Here kyrios is the Lord whose table is kept, so the translation keeps “the Lord,” as the rule for Jesus as referent requires; the NWT prints the name Jehovah, and the note says why the translation does not. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “YOU cannot be drinking the cup of Jehovah and the cup of demons,” and the KJV “Ye cannot drink the cup of the Lord, and the cup of devils.”

“Are we provoking the Lord to jealousy?” ē parazēloumen ton kyrion. The verb parazēloō, to make jealous, is the verb of Deuteronomy 32:21, where Jehovah says that the people have made him jealous with what is not God. Paul does not quote the verse; he echoes its verb. Because the echo is to Deuteronomy, the referent is the God of Israel, and the translation prints Jehovah. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “Or ‘are we inciting Jehovah to jealousy’?” and the KJV “Do we provoke the Lord to jealousy?” The NWT puts the echo in quotation marks, which the Greek does not do.

Verses 23–24 · everything is permitted, and the good of the other

“Everything is permitted.” panta exestin, all things are permitted. The phrase is the slogan of the Corinthians that Paul has quoted before, in 6:12, and it returns here with a limit on each side. The first clause is the slogan; the second is Paul’s answer: but not all things are helpful (sympherei, to bring together, to be useful). The second pair ends with oikodomei, builds up, the verb of 8:1 and 8:10. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “All things are lawful, but not all things are advantageous. All things are lawful, but not all things build up,” and the KJV “All things are lawful for me, but all things are not expedient.” The KJV adds “for me” and the Greek does not have it.

“His own good.” mēdeis to heautou zēteitō alla to tou heterou, the imperative forbids seeking one’s own and puts the other’s in its place. The verb zēteō is to seek. The translation prints “the good of the other” because the Greek is ta tou heterou, the other’s. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “Let each one keep seeking, not his own [advantage], but that of the other person.”

Verses 25–26 · the meat market, and the earth that is the Lord’s

“Whatever is sold in the meat market.” pan to en makellōi pōloumenon, everything sold in the meat market. The makellon is the market for meat, the butcher’s stalls, and the meat sold there might have been sacrificed first. Paul says to eat it and to ask no questions for conscience’ sake. The word syneidēsis, conscience (syneidēsis), is the word of 8:7–12. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “Eat whatever is sold in a meat market, making no inquiry because of your conscience,” and the ASV “Whatsoever is sold in the shambles, eat, asking no question for conscience’ sake.”

“The earth is the Lord’s.” tou kyriou gar hē gē kai to plērōma autēs, for the earth is the Lord’s, and its fullness. The sentence is a quotation of Psalm 24:1 in the Hebrew numbering, Psalm 23:1 in the Septuagint, where the Greek is tou kyriou and the Hebrew is Jehovah’s. Because the quotation is of the Hebrew Psalm, the translation prints Jehovah, as the OT rule of this project requires. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “to Jehovah belong the earth and that which fills it,” and the KJV “For the earth is the Lord’s, and the fulness thereof.” The argument is that the meat is God’s whatever the market has done to it, and the Psalm is the warrant.

Verses 27–29 · the dinner with an unbeliever, and the conscience of the other

“If an unbeliever invites you.” ei tis kalei hymas tōn apistōn kai thelete poreuesthai, if one of the unbelievers invites you and you want to go. The word apistos is the one for a person who does not trust, and the dinner is a private meal, not a meal at a temple. Paul tells them to eat what is set before them and to ask no questions.

“This has been offered in sacrifice.” touto hierothyton estin, this is sacrificed to a god. The word hierothytos is the word for a thing offered in sacrifice, and the one who says it is the one who knows. The sentence says not to eat, for the sake of the one who told you. The shelf: the KJV “If any man say unto you, This is offered in sacrifice unto idols, eat not for his sake that shewed it, and for conscience sake,” and the NWT 1984 “But if anyone should say to YOU: “This is something offered in sacrifice,” do not eat on account of the one that disclosed it and on account of conscience.”

“Not your own conscience, but that of the other.” syneidēsin de legō ouchi tēn heautou alla tēn tou heterou, I mean conscience, not your own but the other’s. The sentence turns the word back on the reader, and then asks, why is my freedom judged by another conscience? The shelf: the NWT 2013 “I do not mean your own conscience, but that of the other person,” and the ASV “conscience, I say, not thine own, but the other’s.”

Verse 30 · the thanks, and the slander over it

“If I partake with thanks.” ei egō chariti metechō, if I partake with grace. The noun charis means grace or thanks, and the verb eucharisteō is the verb of the thanksgiving at the table. The word blasphēmeō, to slander, is the verb of a slander against a person, and the sentence asks why Paul should be slandered for giving thanks. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “If I am partaking with thanks, why am I to be spoken of abusively over that for which I give thanks?” and the KJV “For if I by grace be a partaker, why am I evil spoken of for that for which I give thanks?”

Verses 31–33 · the glory of God, no offense, and pleasing all

“Do everything for the glory of God.” panta eis doxan theou poieite, do all things for the glory of God. The sentence turns the meal into worship, and the list of things that can be done, eating, drinking, or anything else, is the list of the chapter. The shelf: the NWT 1984 “do all things for God’s glory,” and the KJV “do all to the glory of God.”

“Give no offense.” aproskopoi, the word is aproskopoi, not stumbling, giving no cause to stumble. The noun skandalon is the cause of stumbling, and the word is the same one Paul has used of the weak brother in 8:9. The three groups are Jews, Greeks, and the congregation of God: the ekklēsia, which this translation renders congregation in every chapter.

“Pleasing all people in all things.” kathōs kagō panta pasin areskō, just as I too please everyone in everything. The sentence repeats the words of 9:22, where Paul became all things to all people, and it gives the purpose: that they may be saved. The shelf: the NWT 2013 “just as I am trying to please all people in all things, not seeking my own advantage, but that of the many, so that they may be saved,” and the KJV “Even as I please all men in all things, not seeking mine own profit, but the profit of many, that they may be saved.” The NWT says “trying to please” for the present tense of areskō, which the translation takes as a present practice.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “congregation” for ekklēsia at 10:32, the fixed rendering of every NT chapter; “conscience” for syneidēsis at 10:25–29, the word of 8:7–12; “builds up” for oikodomei at 10:23, the verb of 8:1 and 8:10; “stumbling” for skandalon and aproskopoi at 10:32; “sharing” for koinōnia at 10:16, the word the older English prints as “communion.”

Echoes paid. The idol is nothing of 8:4 is turned in 10:19–20: it is nothing, but what is sacrificed to it goes to demons. The conscience of 8:7–12 returns in the meat market and the dinner (10:25–29). The right of chapter 9 returns as the slogan “everything is permitted” at 10:23, and the pleasing all of 9:22 returns in its own words at 10:33. The eating of the sacrifices of 8:4 returns at 10:18 as sharing in the altar. The flee of 6:18 returns at 10:14.

Echoes planted. The cup of the Lord and the table of the Lord (10:21) return in chapter 11, where the supper is taught as the Lord’s. The end of 10:11 returns in chapter 15, where the end is the handing over of the kingdom (15:24). The sharing of the blood and the body (10:16) returns in chapter 11 as the words over the cup and the bread.

Next in this book: 1 Corinthians 11, already on these pages. Paul turns from the table of demons to the table of the Lord, and to the practice of the Lord’s supper at Corinth.
