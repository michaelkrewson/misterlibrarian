“Give thanks to Jehovah, for he is good”: a thanksgiving liturgy with four voices, a rescue from the narrow place, a procession to the gate of righteousness, the stone the builders rejected, and a festival bound to the horns of the altar.
Undated: the psalm has no heading, no author and no occasion. The commentators divide on whether it is David’s or Israel’s out of exile, and it is filed here under the monarchy as an undated psalm, as Psalm 100 is.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · Psalm 118:1–4 “Give thanks to Jehovah, for he is good” — four voices, one refrain, and the steadfast love that is forever

⭐ Four voices. The psalm opens as a liturgy with a leader and a congregation: everyone is told to give thanks (verse 1), and then three groups are named to answer, Israel (2), the house of Aaron (3) and those who fear Jehovah (4), each with the same line. Ibn Ezra says the holy ones of Israel are named first because of their high rank, since they owe the most thanks, and then the verse takes in the God-fearers of every people. Rashi reads “those who fear Jehovah” as the sons of Levi, who gathered to Moses at the golden calf (Exodus 32:26) and fought their own people when they said “let us give ourselves a head, and let us go back to Egypt” (Numbers 14:4, both now on these pages). Radak records that the rabbis disagreed about who speaks: some said David, some said the days of the Messiah, and he takes the psalm as Israel speaking together, as one, out of exile. If it is David, he says, the house of Aaron is named because Saul killed Nob, the city of the priests (1 Samuel 22:19, now on these pages), and David is asking them to give thanks for his kingship.

⭐ “His steadfast love is forever”. The refrain ki le-olam chasdo stands in forty-one verses of the Hebrew Bible. Twenty-six of them are the lines of Psalm 136, and five are in this psalm, the four of verses 1–4 and the last verse. The rest are one each in Psalms 106 and 107, Ezra 3:11 and Jeremiah 33:11, and the Chronicles, where the Levites sing it at the bringing in of the ark and at the dedication of the temple (1 Chronicles 16, 2 Chronicles 5 and 7, and at Jehoshaphat’s battle in 2 Chronicles 20). Chesed is “steadfast love”, as at Psalm 100:5 and 103:8 (dictionary), and le-olam is “forever”. The KJV has “his mercy endureth for ever”, the ASV “his lovingkindness endureth for ever”, the NIV “his love endures forever”, the TLB “His loving-kindness is forever”, and the NWT 1984 “his loving-kindness is to time indefinite”. The Greek has eleos, “mercy”, and the Douay follows it: “his mercy endureth for ever”.

⚠ “Give thanks”. The verb is hodu, from yadah, the root of Judah’s name (dictionary). The KJV has “O give thanks unto”, the ASV “Oh give thanks unto Jehovah”, the NIV “Give thanks to”, the TLB “Oh, thank”, and the Douay “Give praise to”, after the Latin. The Greek Psalter has exomologeisthe, “confess”, and puts Allēlouia at the head of the psalm, which the Hebrew does not. This is the last of the six psalms, 113 to 118, that Jewish tradition calls the Hallel. The Mishnah has the people in the temple court recite the Hallel while the Passover lambs are slaughtered (Pesachim 5:7, in Sefaria’s English, not checked against the Hebrew), and at the seder the leader finishes it over the fourth cup (Pesachim 10:7).

Verses 5–9 · Psalm 118:5–9 “From the narrow place I called” — a wide-open place, “Jehovah is for me”, and a trust that is a refuge and not a bet

⭐ “The narrow place… a wide-open place”. Metsar is a narrow place, a strait, and merchav is its opposite (dictionary). Ibn Ezra says the verse names Jah twice, “a witticism of the holy tongue”, and sets the wide place against the narrow one. David’s own song has the same turn: “he brought me out to a broad place” (2 Samuel 22:20, now on these pages). Radak gives the two readings of the psalm: if David speaks, the narrow place is where he hid in caves and strongholds while he fled; if Israel speaks, it is the exile. The ASV has “Out of my distress I called upon Jehovah”, the NIV “When hard pressed, I cried”, and the KJV and the ASV both have “set me in a large place”. The NIV has “he brought me into a spacious place”, and the Douay “enlarged me”. The Greek has ek thlipseōs, “out of affliction”, and eis platusmon, “into a broad place”.

⭐ “Jehovah is for me”. The Hebrew is Yehovah li, “Jehovah is mine” or “for me”, with no verb. Paul’s question runs on the same words, “If God is for us, who is against us?” (Romans 8:31, now on these pages). The Greek Psalter has Kyrios emoi boēthos, “Jehovah is my helper”, and the letter to the Hebrews quotes that form: “Jehovah is my helper; I will not be afraid. What will man do to me?” (Hebrews 13:6, now on these pages). The Douay also has “my helper” here, from the Greek. The TLB has “He is for me!”, and the ASV “Jehovah is on my side”. The question that follows, “What can a human do to me?”, is the one that Psalm 56:12 asks in other word order (now on these pages). Verse 7 puts it more strongly: “Jehovah is for me among my helpers”, and the ASV has “on my side among them that help me”, which keeps the Hebrew’s odd preposition. Ibn Ezra reports Rabbi Moses taking it to mean that Jehovah alone stands against all helpers, and prefers the plainer sense, that Jehovah helps those who help and they see his vengeance.

⚠ “Take refuge… trust”. Verses 8 and 9 use two verbs, chasah, “take refuge”, and batach, “trust” (dictionary), and the shelf mostly runs them together. The KJV has “better to trust in” and “to put confidence in”, the ASV “to take refuge in Jehovah” and “to put confidence in man”, and the NIV “to take refuge in” and “to trust in humans”. The Greek uses one verb for the trust in verse 8 and a second for the hope in verse 9, and the Douay has “to confide in” and “to have confidence in”. Rashi says chasayon is the shade a thing gives, a small thing, and bitachon a sound and strong support, and even so taking refuge in Jehovah is better than the promise of human beings. Nedivim in verse 9 are nobles (dictionary): the KJV, the ASV and the NIV have “princes”, and the TLB “the mightiest king”. Ibn Ezra says they are called so because they are the most honored of the sons of men.

Verses 10–12 · Psalm 118:10–12 “In the name of Jehovah I cut them off” — nations like bees, a fire of thorns, and a verb that three verses use and nobody else

⭐ “I cut them off”. The verb amilam stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, and all three are the refrain of this passage, verses 10, 11 and 12. It is spelled like the verb for circumcision, mul (Genesis 17:10, now on these pages; dictionary), and the lexicons divide on whether it is the same root. Rashi takes it as “I will cut them off” and ties it to the withering of Psalm 90:6 (now on these pages), and Ibn Ezra does the same, adding that Rabbi Moses derived it from another root and calls that “far off”. Radak notes that its vowel changes at a pause and says it means the same, “I will cut them off”. The shelf is split on the sense and on the tense. The KJV has “I will destroy them”, the ASV “I will cut them off”, the NIV “I cut them down”, and the NWT 1984 “I kept holding them off”. The Greek has ēmunamēn autous, “I defended myself against them”, and the Douay has “I have been revenged on them”.

⭐ “All the nations”. Rashi says the psalm speaks of the wars of Gog and Magog, when all the nations will be there, as it is written, “I will gather all the nations to Jerusalem to battle” (Zechariah 14:2). Radak, if David speaks, reads it as Israel and the Philistines when David came to Achish, and the nations that burned Ziklag (1 Samuel 30:1, now on these pages). The next line doubles the verb, sabbuni gam-sebabuni, “they surrounded me, yes, they surrounded me”, and Ibn Ezra says it repeats “the way ‘Jehovah is my strength and my stronghold’ is said again and again”. The NWT 1984 has “They surrounded me, yes, they had me surrounded”.

⚠ “Like bees… a fire of thorns”. Bees are what a swarm of enemies is in Moses’s speech, “they chased you as bees do” (Deuteronomy 1:44), and Isaiah calls Assyria itself “the bee that is in the land of Assyria” (Isaiah 7:18, both now on these pages). The next picture is the opposite of a swarm’s sting: a fire of thorns flares up and is gone. The letters of de’achu, “they were extinguished”, stand in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, Job 6:17 and Isaiah 43:17, “they are extinguished, quenched like a wick” (now on these pages). Only here and at Isaiah 43:17 is it that exact form; Job 6:17 has the passive nid’achu. Rashi says every word of this root falls on springing and leaping, a flame that jumps off the wick and goes out, and Ibn Ezra says it means the same as “quenched” and that thorns do not last. The KJV and the ASV have “they are quenched as the fire of thorns”, the NIV “they were consumed as quickly as burning thorns”, and the NWT 1984 “They were extinguished like a fire of thornbushes”. The Greek has “like bees round a honeycomb”, which adds the comb, and “they were set on fire like a fire among thorns”.

Verses 13–18 · Psalm 118:13–18 “Jah is my strength and song” — pushed to fall, a right hand that does valiantly, and “I will not die, but live”

⭐ “You pushed me hard”. The verb is doubled, dachoh dechitani, “pushing, you pushed me”, and Rashi says the “you” is the enemy. The KJV has “Thou hast thrust sore at me that I might fall”, the NIV “I was pushed back and about to fall”, the TLB “You did your best to kill me”, and the Douay “Being pushed I was overturned”, which is the Greek ōstheis anetrapēn, “pushed, I was overturned”. Ibn Ezra says the Name in “but Jehovah helped me” is the same Name the psalm has named above, as if to say: that Jehovah, there, helped me.

⭐ “Jah is my strength and song”. The words ozzi ve-zimrat Yah stand in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: Exodus 15:2 (now on these pages, where the page prints the name, “Jehovah is my strength and song”), Isaiah 12:2, and here. The form is odd, and Rashi, Radak and Ibn Ezra all stop on it. Zimrat is the form a noun takes before another noun, so it reads “the song of Jah”, and Rashi says it must be joined to the Name, and that “my strength” carries an extra yod to cover both: my strength and the song of the might of the Holy One were salvation to me. Radak says the yod of ozzi stands for both words, so it is zimrati, “my song”, and Ibn Ezra reports Rabbi Moses saying the same and an objection to it. The ASV has “Jehovah is my strength and song”, the NIV “my strength and my defense”, the TLB “my strength and song in the heat of battle”, the NWT 1984 “Jah is my shelter and [my] might”, and the Douay “my strength and my praise”, which is the Greek ischus mou kai hymnēsis mou. Yah is the short form of the name, and the translation prints it “Jah”.

⭐ “The right hand of Jehovah does valiantly”. Verses 15 and 16 give the cry its content: yemin Yehovah, the right hand of Jehovah, three times. The words stand in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: both of these, and Habakkuk 2:16, where it is the cup that comes round to the drunk (now on these pages). Exodus 15:6 has “your right hand, Jehovah”, in the address form, and is not counted. Ibn Ezra says the right hand is named three times because it keeps happening, and that it acts “alone, not a man”. Oseh chayil is “does valiantly” (dictionary). The KJV and the ASV have “doeth valiantly”, the NIV “has done mighty things”, the TLB “has done glorious things”, and the NWT 1984 “is demonstrating vital energy”. The Greek reads verse 16 as “exalted me”, hypsōse me, and the Douay has “hath exulted me”. Radak says romemah, “lifted high”, means a right hand that never slips, unlike the hand of the mighty and the noble. Rashi reads the ringing cry of verse 15 as what will be heard in the tents of the righteous in the time to come, and its words are the right hand’s.

⭐ “I will not die, but live”. “And I will recount the deeds of Jah” uses sapar, “recount” (dictionary). Ibn Ezra says it follows from the right hand: since it does valiantly and I take refuge in it, I will not die at an enemy’s hand. Rashi has Israel speaking, who would have died the death of idolaters, and for him the “discipline” of verse 18 is the exile, where all its iniquities were atoned. He adds that the end of the psalm can be read of David himself, and that the discipline then is the affair of Bathsheba, with “fourfold” of 2 Samuel 12:6 (now on these pages). Radak says the days he spent in evil were God’s discipline to cleanse him, and not because God wanted to kill him. The verb is doubled, yasor yisserani, “disciplining, he disciplined me” (dictionary). The KJV and the ASV have “hath chastened me sore”, the NIV “has chastened me severely”, the TLB “has punished me but not handed me over to death”, and the Douay “chastising hath chastised me”. Paul’s “as punished, and yet not handed over to death” (2 Corinthians 6:9, now on these pages) echoes it without marking a quotation: his paideuomenoi is a form of the verb the Greek Psalter uses for “disciplined”, and his mē thanatoumenoi is “not being put to death”, where the Psalter has “did not give me over to death”.

Verses 19–21 · Psalm 118:19–21 “Open to me the gates of righteousness” — a procession to a gate, and thanks for being answered

⭐ “The gates of righteousness”. The words sha’arei tsedeq, “gates of righteousness”, stand in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one. Tsedeq is “righteousness” (dictionary), and the verse says what goes through the gate: “I will go in through them, I will give thanks to Jah”. The KJV and the ASV have “Open to me the gates of righteousness”, but the NIV has “Open for me the gates of the righteous”, the TLB “Open the gates of the Temple”, and the Douay “the gates of justice”. Rashi asks “And what are the gates of righteousness?” and answers in the next verse: the gate of the sanctuary, “which is Jehovah’s”, where the righteous come in and he gives thanks that he was answered out of the exile. Radak says the gates are a figure for words of praise and thanks, and the speaker is asking the wise to help him give thanks. “This is the gate of Jehovah”, he says, because praise is fitting for God alone, and so the righteous come in by it and give thanks, as he does. Ibn Ezra says the one who has been rescued owes thanks, and “this is the righteousness”, and that the gate is the giving of thanks. The shape of it is the one at Psalm 100:4, which tells the people to enter his gates with thanksgiving (now on these pages).

⚠ “Give thanks”. “I will give thanks to Jah” in verse 19 and “I will give thanks to you” in verse 21 are the same verb as the hodu that opens the psalm (dictionary). The KJV has “I will praise” in both verses, the ASV “I will give thanks” in both, the NIV “give thanks” and “I will give you thanks”, and the TLB “give him my thanks”. The Greek has exomologēsomai and the Douay “give praise to” and “give glory to”.

⭐ “You have answered me”. Verse 21 turns back on verse 5. Ibn Ezra says “you have answered me” answers “Jah answered me with a wide-open place”, and “you have become my salvation” answers “from the narrow place”. The same word is the turn of Psalm 22:22, “you have answered me” (now on these pages). The ASV has “for thou hast answered me, and art become my salvation”, the NIV “you answered me; you have become my salvation”, and the KJV “for thou hast heard me”.

Verses 22–23 · Psalm 118:22–23 “The stone the builders rejected” — the head of the corner, “this came from Jehovah”, and the verse the New Testament quotes five times

⭐ “The head of the corner”. The words rosh pinnah, “head of the corner”, stand in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one (dictionary). It is the verse that Jesus quotes of himself at Matthew 21:42, Mark 12:10 and Luke 20:17, that Peter quotes of him before the rulers at Acts 4:11, and that stands in 1 Peter 2:7, all now on these pages. The two lines of verse 23 are in Matthew and Mark as well, and they have it as this translation does here: “This came from Jehovah, and it is marvellous in our eyes”. The Greek Psalter is the text behind them: lithon hon apedokimasan hoi oikodomountes, houtos egenēthē eis kephalēn gōnias, and para Kyriou egeneto hautē, “from Jehovah this came to be”.

⚠ What the stone is. The Hebrew does not say, and the older commentators say who. Rashi says it is a people, lowly among the nations. Radak says all Israel is called a stone by way of parable, since Israel is the foundation of the world’s building by its statutes and judgments, and the kings despised even the thought that they could be one of the stones, let alone the corner; at the time of salvation Israel became the head of the corner, as a man sets the corners of a house with the large and beautiful stones that hold the rest. If the psalm is David’s, Radak records the rabbis’ reading: it is David when Samuel came to anoint him and Jesse thought him a small boy and a shepherd, and it did not occur to anyone that he would be king. Ibn Ezra says it is a parable of the nations who despised him and were overcome in the end, or that the holy spirit speaks of Israel in exile. The shelf splits on the shape of the thing. The KJV has “the head stone of the corner”, the ASV and the Douay “the head of the corner”, the NIV “the cornerstone”, and the TLB “the capstone of the arch”. A cornerstone is laid at the foundation and a capstone is set on the top, and the Hebrew, like the Greek, says only “head”.

⭐ “This came from Jehovah”. The “this” is feminine in the Hebrew, zot, and so is the “it is” of hi niflat, “it is marvellous”. Rashi says that this is what everyone will say. Radak says the kings of the earth will be amazed and say that this salvation was not from the one who said it but from Jehovah. Ibn Ezra says the stone did not become the head by its own strength, the strength came from Jehovah, and that niflat is from the verb for doing a wonder. The ASV has “This is Jehovah’s doing; it is marvellous in our eyes”, the NIV “it is marvelous in our eyes”, the TLB “it is marvelous to see!”, and the Douay “it is wonderful in our eyes”.

Verses 24–25 · Psalm 118:24–25 “This is the day Jehovah has made” and “save, please” — the day that is not dated, and the cry that became Hosanna

⚠ “This is the day”. The Hebrew does not say which day. Radak says it is the day of the salvation, and that Jehovah “made” it because it could not have come without him, so that Israel’s salvation is a cause to rejoice with them and not to be sad. Ibn Ezra reads the clause with “Perhaps Jehovah will deal with us according to all his wondrous works” (Jeremiah 21:2, now on these pages), the “this” being the wonder of verse 23. The words nagilah ve-nismechah, “let us rejoice and be glad”, stand in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, Isaiah 25:9, and Song of Solomon 1:4 (now on these pages), “we will rejoice and be glad in you”. The KJV and the ASV have “we will rejoice and be glad in it”, the TLB “We will rejoice and be glad in it”, the Douay “let us be glad and rejoice therein”, and the NWT 1984 “We will be joyful and rejoice in it”. The NIV reads the first clause differently, “has done it this very day”. The Greek has agalliasōmetha kai euphranthōmen.

⭐ “Ah, Jehovah, save, please!” Anna Yehovah hoshi’ah na: hoshi’ah, “save”, and na, “please”, are the two halves of hosanna (dictionary), and the crowd shouting it at Matthew 21:9 is quoting this line (now on these pages). It is a cry for help before it is a cry of praise. The ASV has “Save now, we beseech thee, O Jehovah”, the NIV “save us!”, the TLB “Save us. Give us success.”, and the NWT 1984 “do save, please”. The second cry, hatslichah na, “grant success, please”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and Nehemiah’s prayer, “give success, please, to your servant today” (Nehemiah 1:11, now on these pages).

⭐ The lulav and the altar. The Mishnah has the worshippers shake the palm branch in the Hallel at “Give thanks to Jehovah, for he is good”, at the beginning and at the end of the psalm, and at “save, please”, and Beit Shammai says at “grant success, please” as well (Sukkah 3:9, in Sefaria’s English, not checked against the Hebrew). On each day of Sukkot the priests circled the altar once, saying “save, please; grant success, please” (Sukkah 4:5). The Gospels do not name the hymn that Jesus and the disciples sang before they went out to the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:30, now on these pages). If the meal was a Passover, the Mishnah has the second half of the Hallel, which ends with this psalm, said over the fourth cup (Pesachim 10:7).

Verses 26–27 · Psalm 118:26–27 “Blessed is the one who comes in the name of Jehovah” — a blessing from the house, light that shines, and a festival bound with cords or with boughs

⭐ “Blessed is the one who comes”. The words baruch ha-ba, “blessed is the one who comes”, stand in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The crowds shout it at Jesus at Matthew 21:9, Mark 11:9 and John 12:13, with “the King” added at Luke 19:38 and in John, and he says it back to Jerusalem at Matthew 23:39 and Luke 13:35, all now on these pages. The Greek Psalter has Eulogēmenos ho erchomenos en onomati Kyriou, “blessed is the one who is coming in the name of Jehovah”, and Matthew has it word for word. In the psalm it is a greeting at the temple: the second half, “we have blessed you from the house of Jehovah”, is plural. Rashi says it is said to those who bring the first fruits and to the pilgrims at the feasts. Radak says the priests say it to all who come to serve God: we have blessed you from the house, so that you may be blessed in his name; come and serve him as we do. Ibn Ezra says it is what the priests say to everyone who comes to the house, because you honor him with sacrifices. The ASV has “Blessed be he that cometh in the name of Jehovah”, the NIV “Blessed is he who comes”, the TLB “the one sent by”, and the NWT 1984 “Blessed be the One coming in the name of Jehovah”.

⭐ “He has given us light”. Vayaer lanu is “and he has given us light”, from the verb of the priests’ blessing, “Jehovah make his face shine on you” (Numbers 6:25, now on these pages), which fits the priests who have just blessed from the house. Ibn Ezra says the light is that the offerings are brought in the morning and not in the night. The ASV has “and he hath given us light”, the Douay “he hath shone upon us”, and the NWT 1984 “he gives us light”. The Greek has epephanen hēmin, “he has appeared to us”.

⚠ “Bind the festival with cords”. The words isru chag, “bind the festival”, stand in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the verse has three readings on the shelf. Chag is the festival, and the commentators all read it as the festival offering, the animal. Rashi says the sacrifices that were clean and unblemished were tied to the legs of the beds until they were brought to the court, to the horns of the altar. Radak says the lamb is brought bound with cords until it reaches the horns of the altar, and Ibn Ezra adds that the blood is thrown there. The KJV and the ASV have “bind the sacrifice with cords”, and the Geneva has “bind the sacrifice with cords” as well. The NIV has “with boughs in hand, join in the festal procession”, and the NWT 1984 “Bind the festival procession with boughs”, taking avotim as thick-leaved branches, like the “leafy tree” of the festival of Sukkot (Leviticus 23:40, now on these pages), and not as cords. The Douay has “Appoint a solemn day, with shady boughs”, and the Greek has sustēsasthe heortēn en tois pukazousin, “form a festival among the thick-leaved”. This translation prints the words the Hebrew has, “Bind the festival with cords”, and leaves the reading to the note. The horns are the corners of the altar (Exodus 27:2, now on these pages; dictionary).

Verses 28–29 · Psalm 118:28–29 “You are my God” — the speaker at the altar, the priests’ answer, and a psalm that ends where it began

⭐ “You are my God”. There are two words for “my God” in verse 28, Eli and Elohai. Ibn Ezra says the speaker is the one who comes, who brings the festival offering, and that it should not surprise us that the psalm never says at the start who the speakers are, for that is a witticism of songs, as in “I have taken off my tunic” (Song of Solomon 5:3). Radak says Israel speaks: it is fitting for me to give thanks to God over all the nations, since you are my God, because you showed your power and brought me out of the exile, and because you took your judgments from those who rebelled against you. The KJV has “Thou art my God, and I will praise thee”, the ASV “Thou art my God, and I will give thanks unto thee”, and the NIV “You are my God, and I will praise you”. The Greek and the Douay add a line after “I will exalt thee”, “I will praise thee, because thou hast heard me, and art become my salvation”, which is verse 21 again.

⭐ “Give thanks to Jehovah”. Verse 29 is verse 1, word for word, so the psalm ends where it began and reads in a ring. Ibn Ezra says the priests and the Levites answer the speaker with “Give thanks to Jehovah”, and that this is the joy named at the beginning, “let us rejoice and be glad in it”, to tell the deed. Radak says the psalm begins in thanks and ends with “Give thanks” to praise the psalm and the greatness of what was said in it, and that the last line is said against all the nations, since his steadfast love is with everyone and forever with what he has made. If the psalm is David’s, he adds, the stone is David when Samuel came to anoint him, and Jesse said he was a small boy, a shepherd who was not counted. The KJV has “O give thanks unto” at the head and the same at the foot, and the NWT 1984 “Give thanks to Jehovah, YOU people, for he is good”, in both verses.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “Give thanks” for hodu and for the odeh of verses 19, 21 and 28; “steadfast love” for chesed, as in Psalms 100 and 103; “Jah” for Yah; “the narrow place” and “a wide-open place”; “Jehovah is for me”; “What can a human do to me?”, as in Psalm 56:12; “take refuge” for chasah and “trust” for batach; “nobles”; “I cut them off” for amilam, three times; “extinguished”, as at Isaiah 43:17; “Jah is my strength and song”, as at Exodus 15:2; “does valiantly”; “I will recount” for sapar; “disciplined”; “the gates of righteousness”; “the head of the corner”, as at Matthew 21:42; “marvellous”; “save, please”; “Blessed is the one who comes in the name of Jehovah”, as at Matthew 21:9; and “Bind the festival with cords”, with the boughs left to the note. The Hebrew has no written and read pairs in this psalm.

Echoes paid. The steadfast love of Psalms 100:5 and 103:8, and the refrain of Psalm 136 and the Chronicles; “What can a human do to me?” (Psalm 56:12); “he brought me out to a broad place” (2 Samuel 22:20); Paul’s “If God is for us” (Romans 8:31) and the letter to the Hebrews’ “Jehovah is my helper” (Hebrews 13:6); “Jah is my strength and song” (Exodus 15:2); “not handed over to death” (2 Corinthians 6:9); the thanksgiving at the gate (Psalm 100:4); the cup of the right hand of Jehovah (Habakkuk 2:16); “you have answered me” (Psalm 22:22); the stone (Matthew 21:42; Mark 12:10–11; Luke 20:17; Acts 4:11; 1 Peter 2:7); Hosanna and “blessed is the one who comes” (Matthew 21:9; Mark 11:9; Luke 13:35, 19:38; John 12:13; Matthew 23:39); the priests’ blessing (Numbers 6:25); the boughs of Sukkot (Leviticus 23:40); the horns of the altar (Exodus 27:2); and the hymn before the Mount of Olives (Matthew 26:30).

Echoes planted. The rest of the Hallel, Psalms 113–117, and the refrain’s home in Psalm 136; Gog and Magog (Zechariah 14:2); “Jah is my strength and song” again at Isaiah 12:2; “let us rejoice and be glad” in Isaiah 25:9; and the stone of Isaiah 28:16. None of these is translated here yet. (The withering of Psalm 90:6, planted here earlier, is now on these pages: Psalm 90:6.)

⚠ A note on order: the Psalms stand on these pages at 1, 22, 23, 27, 34, 37, 46, 51, 56, 91, 100, 103, 118, 119, 121, 139 and 150, chosen by how often they are read rather than in sequence. The sequential thread is unchanged: next in sequence is Psalm 2.
