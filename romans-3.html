The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. The argument turns from the indictment of Jew and Gentile to its consequence, the objector’s questions about the Jew’s advantage and God’s faithfulness, the chain of quotations from the Psalms and Isaiah, the world made accountable, and the ‘but now’ of God’s righteousness apart from law.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 and 2; this library dates the letter no more closely than those chapters do.

Verses 1–2 · τὸ περισσὸν … ὠφέλεια … πρῶτον μὲν … ἐπιστεύθησαν … τὰ λόγια to perisson … ōpheleia … prōton men … episteuthēsan … ta logia — Romans 3:1–2: what is left of the Jew’s advantage, and what were they “entrusted” with?

“What, then, is the advantage of the Jew?” (Romans 3:1). Ti oun to perisson tou Ioudaiou, ē tis hē ōpheleia tēs peritomēs? The two questions are the two that Romans 2:25–29 left open: if the real Jew is the one inwardly, and circumcision is of the heart, what is left of being a Jew and of the sign? Perisson is the “surplus,” the margin over and above what someone else has, and ōpheleia is “benefit, profit.” Paul puts the objection in the mouth of a hearer and answers it at once, and the answer is not “none”: polu kata panta tropon, “much, in every way.” KJV “What advantage then hath the Jew?” and ASV “What advantage then hath the Jew?” keep the word; NWT 1984 “What, then, is the superiority of the Jew,” NIV “What advantage, then, is there in being a Jew,” and TLB “what’s the use of being a Jew?” turn a question about a margin into one about usefulness.

“For, first, they were entrusted with the oracles of God” (Romans 3:2). Prōton men gar hoti episteuthēsan ta logia tou theou. Paul says “first” and the chapter never says “second”: the phrase prōton men stands in five verses of the New Testament (Romans 1:8, this verse, 1 Corinthians 11:18, Hebrews 7:2 and James 3:17, the last three not yet on these pages), and the list of what the Jews were given is set out at Romans 9:4–5, where the Greek lists “the adoption and the glory and the covenants and the lawgiving and the worship and the promises,” then “the fathers,” and, from them, “the Christ according to the flesh.” Here he writes one item. The apparatus gives gar, “for,” after men to Westcott–Hort, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont and omits it for Tregelles; the printed text has it, and this translation prints “For, first.”

⚠ One family of words runs through verses 2–3 and English cannot show it. Episteuthēsan, “they were entrusted,” is the passive of pisteuō; the next verse has ēpistēsan (“some were unfaithful”), apistia (“their unfaithfulness”) and pistin tou theou (“the faithfulness of God”). The Greek ear hears one root four times in two sentences: they were pisteuō-ed with the oracles; some were a-pisteō; will the a-pistia cancel God’s pistis? The dictionary entry pistis follows the noun, and apistia its negation.

“Oracles” (logia). Logion, “oracle, utterance,” stands in four verses of the New Testament (logia): Acts 7:38, where Moses received “living oracles to give to us”; this verse; Hebrews 5:12; and 1 Peter 4:11 (the last two not yet on these pages). This translation prints “oracles” at both of the places that are on these pages, and the Greek does not say which oracles are meant — the Law, the Prophets, the promises — only that they were put in someone’s keeping. KJV “the oracles of God,” ASV “the oracles of God” and Geneva “the oracles of God” keep the word; Douay “the words of God,” NIV “the very words of God” and NWT 1984 “the sacred pronouncements of God” say it more plainly; TLB “his laws” narrows it to commands.

Verses 3–8 · ἠπίστησάν … ἀπιστία … καταργήσει … μὴ γένοιτο … δικαιωθῇς … νικήσεις … συνίστησιν … κατὰ ἄνθρωπον … βλασφημούμεθα ēpistēsan … apistia … katargēsei … mē genoito … dikaiōthēs … nikēseis … synistēsin … kata anthrōpon … blasphēmoumetha — Romans 3:3–8: can human unfaithfulness cancel God’s faithfulness, and who is slandering Paul?

“Unfaithful” and “the faithfulness of God” (Romans 3:3). Ei ēpistēsan tines, mē hē apistia autōn tēn pistin tou theou katargēsei? The verb and noun can mean “were unfaithful” (they broke the trust of verse 2) or “did not believe,” and the shelf splits on exactly that: KJV “some did not believe,” Douay “some of them have not believed” and Geneva “some did not believe” read it as disbelief; ASV “some were without faith,” NWT 1984 “some did not express faith” stay close to the root; NIV “some were unfaithful” reads it as breach of trust, and TLB “they broke their promises to God” says so outright. The genitive on the other side is God’s own: KJV “the faith of God,” Douay “the faith of God” and Geneva “the faith of God” print the noun that the others print as ASV “the faithfulness of God,” NIV “God’s faithfulness” and NWT 1984 “the faithfulness of God”; none on the shelf reads it as faith placed in God. This translation prints “unfaithful” and says here that the Greek allows the other.

“Abolish” (katargeō), at both ends of the chapter. The verb of verse 3 is the verb of the last verse, 3:31: “will their unfaithfulness abolish the faithfulness of God?” opens the chapter and “Do we then abolish law through the faith?” closes it. This translation prints “abolish” at both, as at 1 Corinthians 13:8 (“they will be abolished”); the entry katargeō follows the verb. The shelf keeps one expression at both ends in two versions only: ASV “of none effect” at both verses and NIV “nullify” at both. KJV has “without effect” in verse 3 and “make void” in verse 31; Douay has “without effect” in verse 3 and “destroy” in verse 31; NWT 1984 has “without effect” in verse 3 and “abolish” in verse 31.

“May it never be!” (mē genoito), three times. The optative mē genoito, “may it not happen,” stands in fifteen verses of the New Testament, ten of them in Romans and three of those ten in this chapter: verses 4, 6 and 31. The only one outside Paul is Luke 20:16, where this translation prints the same “May it never be!” The Greek has no word for “God” in it. KJV “God forbid” and ASV “God forbid” supply one; NIV “Not at all!” and NWT 1984 “Never may that happen!” do not.

“That you may be justified in your words” (Romans 3:4). Hopōs an dikaiōthēs en tois logois sou kai nikēseis en tō krinesthai se. The citation matches Psalm 51:6 in the Hebrew numbering (51:4 in English), lema’an titsdaq bedavrekha tizkeh veshofteka, which this translation renders “so that you are proved right when you speak, and clear when you judge.” Two differences. First, the Hebrew has God as the judge, “when you judge”; Paul’s Greek has krinesthai se, “when you are judged,” with God as the one in the dock. That the Greek Psalter reads as Paul does is reported here, not checked. KJV “overcome when thou art judged,” NWT 1984 “win when you are being judged” and ASV “prevail when thou comest into judgment” follow Paul; NIV “prevail when you judge” follows the Hebrew. Second, the apparatus gives nikēseis, the future, to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28, and nikēsēs, the subjunctive, to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont; and it gives kathōs (“as”) to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont and kathaper (“just as”) to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles. The verb dikaioō is the verb of verses 20, 24 and 28 (dictionary), and here it is said of God: in a chapter about how people are justified, the first use puts God himself on trial and has him vindicated. The first half of the verse, “let God be true, and every man a liar” (pas anthrōpos pseustēs), has the words of Psalm 116:11 in the Hebrew, kol ha-adam kozev, “all men are liars,” said by the psalmist “in my haste”; that the Greek Psalter reads pas anthrōpos pseustēs is reported here, not checked.

Verses 5–8: the objector speaks, and Paul will not borrow his words. Synistēsin (verse 5), “demonstrates, stands together with, commends,” is the shelf’s other split: KJV “commend,” Douay “commend” and Geneva “commend” keep the old sense of “recommend”; NIV “brings out God’s righteousness more clearly” and NWT 1984 “brings God’s righteousness to the fore” give the result. Kata anthrōpon legō, “I speak in a human way,” marks the question as one a person would ask. KJV “I speak as a man” and NIV “I am using a human argument” both keep the apology. In verse 7 the apparatus gives de, “but,” to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 and gar, “for,” to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “For if the truth of God” has the second and ASV “But if the truth of God” the first. The “lie” is a hypothetical: if my falsehood makes God’s truth overflow to his glory, why am I still judged? The verb blasphēmoumetha (verse 8), “we are slandered,” is the verb of Romans 2:24, where “the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles because of you”; in chapter 2 the teacher’s conduct slanders God, and here the apostle himself is slandered, by some who say he teaches “Let us do the evil things that the good things may come.” Paul answers the charge in five Greek words, hōn to krima endikon estin: “the judgment on such people is just.” NIV “Their condemnation is just” and KJV “whose damnation is just” make it a sentence about the accusers.

Verses 9–18 · προεχόμεθα … προῃτιασάμεθα … ὑφʼ ἁμαρτίαν … ἠχρεώθησαν … τάφος ἀνεῳγμένος … ἰὸς ἀσπίδων … ὁδὸν εἰρήνης … φόβος θεοῦ proechometha … proēitiasametha … hyph’ hamartian … ēchreōthēsan … taphos aneōgmenos … ios aspidōn … hodon eirēnēs … phobos theou — Romans 3:9–18: what does “under sin” mean, and where do these six quotations come from?

“Are we better off?” (Romans 3:9). Proechometha stands in this verse alone, and its voice is not settled: as a middle it asks “do we have an advantage, do we stand out?”; as a passive, “are we surpassed?” KJV “are we better than they?” and ASV “are we better than they?” add “than they”; Douay “Do we excel them?” and Geneva “are we more excellent?” add a comparison; NIV “Do we have any advantage?” and NWT 1984 “Are we in a better position?” leave the comparison out; and TLB “are we Jews better than others?” names the people. Paul’s own answer, ou pantōs, is “not at all.” The verb proēitiasametha, “we charged beforehand,” also stands in this verse alone; the charge it looks back on is chapters 1 and 2, the Gentiles at 1:18 and the Jew at 2:17–29. Hyph’ hamartian, “under sin,” with sin as a power one is under, stands in two verses of the New Testament in this form (hypo hamartian): this one and Galatians 3:22 (not yet on these pages). NIV “under the power of sin” adds the word “power.”

⚠ The chain of quotations: one formula, several passages. Verse 10 opens with kathōs gegraptai, “as it is written,” and no formula follows in verses 11–18; the whole run reads as one quotation, but the Hebrew behind it is several passages. Each is given here in the Hebrew numbering, with the English number where it differs:

• Verse 10, “There is no righteous one, not even one.” The words “righteous one” are not in Psalm 14:1–3, whose Hebrew says ein oseh tov, ein gam echad, “there is none who does good, not even one” (14:3). The closest line in the Hebrew that has the righteous is Ecclesiastes 7:20: “there is not a righteous man on earth who does good and does not sin.” Paul’s sentence stands between them, and this page does not decide whether he quoted one, the other, or both.

• Verses 11–12 follow Psalm 14:2–3 closely: the Hebrew has God looking down to see “if there is one who understands, who seeks God” (maskil, doresh et-elohim), then hakol sar, “all has turned aside,” yachdav ne’elachu, “together they have become impure,” and “there is none who does good, not even one.” Psalm 53:3–4 repeats the same lines. Paul’s ēchreōthēsan, “they became useless,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament (ēchreōthēsan); where the Hebrew has ne’elachu, the archive’s English prints “become impure.” KJV “unprofitable,” ASV “unprofitable,” Douay “unprofitable” and Geneva “unprofitable” keep the older word; NWT 1984 “worthless” and NIV “worthless” modernize it.

• Verse 13a is Psalm 5:10 (Hebrew; 5:9 in English): “their throat is an open grave; they make smooth their tongue.” Paul’s “with their tongues they practiced deceit” is the second half. KJV “open sepulchre,” ASV “open sepulchre” and Douay “open sepulchre” keep the word “sepulchre”; NWT 1984 “opened grave” keeps the participle, aneōgmenos, which says the grave has been opened and is not simply open.

• Verse 13b is Psalm 140:4 (Hebrew; 140:3 in English): “vipers’ venom is under their lips.” Paul’s Greek has “asps.”

• Verse 14 is Psalm 10:7: “his mouth is full of cursing and deceit and oppression.” The Hebrew has “deceit” where Paul has pikrias, “bitterness”; that the Greek Psalter reads “cursing and bitterness” is reported here, not checked.

• Verses 15–17 are Isaiah 59:7–8: “Their feet run to evil, and they make haste to shed innocent blood … the way of peace they do not know.” (Isaiah 59 is not yet on these pages.)

• Verse 18 is Psalm 36:2 (Hebrew; 36:1 in English): “there is no fear of God before his eyes” — what Paul writes, with “his eyes” in the Hebrew where Paul has “their eyes.”

Of the passages the Hebrew lets this page name, five are Psalms and one is Isaiah, and the pieces are strung along the body: larynx, throat; glōssai, tongues; cheilē, lips; stoma, mouth; podes, feet; ophthalmoi, eyes. The list runs from the throat through the tongue, lips and mouth to the feet, and ends at the eyes; the last verse is the one about not fearing God.

Verses 19–20 · ἐν τῷ νόμῳ … φραγῇ … ὑπόδικος … ἐξ ἔργων νόμου … πᾶσα σὰρξ … ἐπίγνωσις ἁμαρτίας en tō nomō … phragē … hypodikos … ex ergōn nomou … pasa sarx … epignōsis hamartias — Romans 3:19–20: who is “in the law,” why must every mouth be stopped, and what are “works of law”?

“Those in the law” (Romans 3:19). Hosa ho nomos legei tois en tō nomō lalei. The preposition is en, “in,” not hypo, “under,” the word of “under sin” in verse 9. Only Douay “to them that are in the law” prints “in”; KJV “to them who are under the law,” ASV “to them that are under the law,” NIV “to those who are under the law” and NWT 1984 “to those under the Law” say “under,” and the verse then reads as if the law, like sin, were a power. “The law” here is the whole body just quoted, since verses 10–18 come from the Psalms and Isaiah and verse 19 calls what they say “what the law says”; the Greek does not say whether it means that these books are “law” or that “law” can mean the Scriptures at large.

“Every mouth stopped” and “accountable” (Romans 3:19). Phragē, the aorist passive of phrassō, “to stop, to fence in,” stands in three verses of the New Testament in these forms (phragē): this one; 2 Corinthians 11:10 (not yet on these pages), where Paul says “this boasting will not be stopped”; and Hebrews 11:33, where those who act “through faith” “stopped the mouths of lions.” The adjective hypodikos, “answerable, brought to trial,” stands in this verse alone (hypodikos, dictionary); it is built on dikē, “justice, a case,” and means a defendant who is under the court. KJV “guilty before God,” TLB “guilty before Almighty God” give the verdict; ASV “brought under the judgment of God,” Geneva “subject to the judgment of God” and Douay “made subject to God” give the position; NIV “held accountable to God” and NWT 1984 “liable to God for punishment” give the liability. The word “world” is kosmos (dictionary), and it is the same world God is to judge in verse 6.

“Works of law” and “no flesh” (Romans 3:20). Ex ergōn nomou has no article before either noun, and the phrase ergōn nomou stands in six verses of the New Testament: this verse, 3:28, Galatians 2:16 (three times in one verse, in the Greek), 3:2, 3:5 and 3:10 (the Galatians verses not yet on these pages). Paul does not define the phrase here. NWT 1984 “by works of law” keeps the missing article; KJV “by the deeds of the law,” ASV “by the works of the law” and NIV “by the works of the law” supply it, and the article turns a phrase about a kind of doing into one about the Mosaic code. The clause “no flesh will be justified before him” is the sentence of Psalm 143:2 in the Hebrew, ki lo yitsdaq lefanekha kol chai, “for no living one is righteous before you,” with “flesh” for “living one” and “works of law” added; Galatians 2:16 has the same Greek sentence without “before him.” Epignōsis (dictionary), “recognition, full knowledge,” is the noun for coming to know a thing by meeting it: Paul says law brings the recognition of sin, and in Romans 7:7 he says “I did not know sin except through law.” KJV “the knowledge of sin” and NIV “we become conscious of our sin” split on whether this is information or experience.

Verses 21–23 · Νυνὶ δέ … χωρὶς νόμου … πεφανέρωται … διὰ πίστεως Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ … οὐ … διαστολή … ὑστεροῦνται τῆς δόξης Nyni de … chōris nomou … pephanerōtai … dia pisteōs Iēsou Christou … ou … diastolē … hysterountai tēs doxēs — Romans 3:21–23: what is the “but now,” and is it faith in Christ or the faith of Christ?

“But now” (Romans 3:21). Nyni de. Verses 9–20 close the case against everyone; this is the turn. The verb pephanerōtai is a perfect, “has been made visible, and stands so,” the verb of Romans 1:19 (“God made it plain to them”): what was hidden is now in view. “God’s righteousness” (dikaiosynē theou) is the phrase of 1:17 (“in it God’s righteousness is revealed”), and verse 5 of this chapter has used it already; the entry dikaiosynē follows the argument over the genitive. It is “apart from law” and yet “borne witness to by the Law and the Prophets”: the Greek has nomos twice in the verse, once without the article (“apart from law”) and once with it (“the Law,” the first of the Scriptures), and this translation capitalizes only the second. NWT 1984 “apart from law” keeps the missing article; KJV “without the law,” ASV “apart from the law,” Douay “without the law,” Geneva “without the Law” and NIV “apart from the law” add it.

⚠ “Faith in Jesus Christ” or “the faith of Jesus Christ” (Romans 3:22). Dia pisteōs Iēsou Christou. The genitive can mean “faith in” (Christ as the object of trust) or “the faith, the faithfulness, of” (Christ’s own, shown in his death). The string pisteōs Iēsou / Christou stands in five verses of the New Testament: this one; Romans 3:26 (ek pisteōs Iēsou); and Galatians 2:16, Galatians 3:22 and Philippians 3:9 (the last three not yet on these pages). The shelf prints both: KJV “faith of Jesus Christ,” Douay “faith of Jesus Christ” and Geneva “faith of Jesus Christ” keep the “of,” which an English reader can hear either way; ASV “faith in Jesus Christ,” NIV “faith in Jesus Christ” and NWT 1984 “the faith in Jesus Christ” choose “in.” Verse 3 is the precedent for the genitive and it is God’s: pistin tou theou is God’s own faithfulness. This translation prints “faith in Jesus Christ” here, since verse 22 goes on at once with “for all who believe,” and says in this note that the Greek allows the other; it does not decide. Verse 26 has the same genitive again and is taken up in the note on verses 24–26. The apparatus gives kai epi pantas, “and upon all,” after eis pantas to Robinson–Pierpont only: KJV “unto all and upon all them that believe,” Douay “unto all and upon all them that believe in him” and Geneva “unto all, and upon all that believe” carry the longer reading.

“No distinction” and “fall short” (Romans 3:22–23). Ou gar estin diastolē is the answer to “no partiality” of 2:11, said now of the remedy and not the judgment; the same words come back with “of Jew and Greek” at Romans 10:12 (not yet on these pages). Hēmarton is an aorist, “sinned,” and hysterountai a present, “are falling short, are lacking”: the sinning is in the past and the shortage goes on. Douay “do need the glory of God” and Geneva “are deprived of the glory of God” read the verb as lack or loss; KJV “come short of the glory of God,” ASV “fall short of the glory of God,” NIV “fall short of the glory of God” and NWT 1984 “fall short of the glory of God” read it as falling short of a mark. “The glory of God” recalls Romans 1:23, where people “exchanged the glory of the incorruptible God” for images, and 2:7 and 2:10, where “glory and honor” go to those who do good (dictionary).

Verses 24–26 · δωρεὰν … ἀπολυτρώσεως … ἱλαστήριον … ἐν τῷ αἵματι … ἔνδειξιν … πάρεσιν … ἀνοχῇ … δίκαιον καὶ δικαιοῦντα dōrean … apolytrōseōs … hilastērion … en tō haimati … endeixin … paresin … anochē … dikaion kai dikaiounta — Romans 3:24–26: what is a hilastērion, and what does God demonstrate by passing over sins?

“Freely” and “redemption” (Romans 3:24). Dōrean, “as a gift, for nothing” (dictionary), is the adverb of the free gift, and apolytrōsis (dictionary) is release by a ransom. The grammar is a participle, dikaioumenoi, “being justified,” hanging from verse 23: the same “all” who sinned are the ones being justified. KJV “justified freely by his grace,” ASV “justified freely by his grace” and NIV “justified freely by his grace” keep the order; TLB “declares us” not guilty says outright what “justified” means in the court Paul has been building.

⚠ “A place of atonement” (Romans 3:25). Hon proetheto ho theos hilastērion. The word hilastērion stands in two verses of the New Testament (hilastērion): this one, and Hebrews 9:5, where the Greek has the cherubim “overshadowing the hilastērion” (not yet on these pages). There it stands over the ark “covered on all sides with gold” (Hebrews 9:4): the kapporet, the cover of Exodus 25:17, which the entry reports the Greek Old Testament calls hilastērion (reported, not checked here). On the Day of Atonement the Hebrew has the blood sprinkled “upon the ark-cover … and before the ark-cover” (Leviticus 16:14–15). The family stands in six verses (hilas-): Luke 18:13 (the tax collector’s “be merciful to me”), this verse, Hebrews 2:17 and 9:5, and 1 John 2:2 and 4:10 (the last four not yet on these pages). Three readings are on the shelf and this page does not vote: (1) a place, the cover where atonement is made; (2) a means, a sacrifice that turns away wrath (“propitiation”) or removes sin (“expiation”); (3) a result, reconciliation. KJV “propitiation,” ASV “propitiation” and Douay “propitiation” keep the old Latin-derived word; NWT 1984 “offering for propitiation” adds “offering”; NIV “sacrifice of atonement” chooses the means; Geneva “a reconciliation” chooses the result; and TLB “to take the punishment for our sins and to end all God’s anger against us” turns the noun into a whole theory. This translation prints “a place of atonement,” since the word names a cover in its only other New Testament verse, and puts the range here; the entry hilastērion follows the word. “Put forward” (proetheto) is a public showing, and the sentence breaks after it: the commas of “through faith, in his blood” keep both attachments possible. The apparatus gives dia tēs pisteōs, “through the faith,” to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and dia pisteōs to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles.

“The passing over of the sins previously committed” (Romans 3:25). Paresis stands in this verse alone (paresin); it is not aphesis, the word of “forgiveness of sins” in seven verses such as Acts 5:31 (aphesin hamartiōn). Paul chooses the rarer word, “letting go by, passing over,” and it sits beside the “forbearance” of verse 26. The entry paresis follows it. ASV “the passing over of the sins done aforetime” is the only version on the shelf that keeps it; KJV “remission of sins that are past,” Douay “the remission of former sins” and Geneva “the forgiveness of the sins that are passed” read it as forgiveness, NWT 1984 “forgiving the sins that occurred in the past” turns it into a verb, and NIV “he had left the sins committed beforehand unpunished” spells out what “passing over” would mean without a verdict.

“Forbearance,” “demonstration,” “at the present time” (Romans 3:25–26). Anochē, “forbearance,” stands in two verses of the New Testament (anochē): Romans 2:4, where the teacher “despises the riches of his kindness and forbearance and patience,” and this verse. The noun endeixis, “demonstration, proof,” stands in four verses (endeixin): twice in this passage, at 2 Corinthians 8:24 (not yet on these pages) and at Philippians 1:28; Paul says it twice, and the second time adds en tō nyn kairō, “at the present time,” the nyni of verse 21 made into a date. The entries anochē and endeixis follow them. KJV “the forbearance of God,” ASV “the forbearance of God,” Douay “the forbearance of God,” NIV “in his forbearance” and NWT 1984 “exercising forbearance” print “forbearance”; Geneva “the patience of God” prints “patience,” the word this translation gives at Romans 2:4 to the third word of the list, makrothymia. The purpose clause is the end of the sentence: eis to einai auton dikaion kai dikaiounta, “so that he himself may be righteous and the one who justifies.” The question the passage answers is the one verse 5 asked: can God be righteous and pass over sin? KJV “that he might be just, and the justifier,” ASV “that he might himself be just, and the justifier” and NIV “so as to be just and the one who justifies” keep the double claim.

“The one who has faith in Jesus” (Romans 3:26). Ton ek pisteōs Iēsou carries the genitive of verse 22 again, and the shelf divides the same way: ASV “him that hath faith in Jesus” and NWT 1984 “the man that has faith in Jesus” agree, KJV “him which believeth in Jesus” reads it as “in,” and Douay “of the faith of Jesus Christ” and Geneva “of the faith of Jesus” keep the “of.” This translation prints “the one who has faith in Jesus” here for the reason given at verse 22, and the range is the same.

Verses 27–31 · καύχησις … ἐξεκλείσθη … νόμου πίστεως … λογιζόμεθα … εἷς ὁ θεός … ἱστάνομεν kauchēsis … exekleisthē … nomou pisteōs … logizometha … heis ho theos … histanomen — Romans 3:27–31: where did the boasting go, what is a “law of faith,” and what does “God is one” prove?

“Where, then, is the boasting?” (Romans 3:27). Pou oun hē kauchēsis? Exekleisthē. The boasting is that of the teacher of Romans 2:17 (“boast in God”) and 2:23 (“you who boast in the law”), both the verb kauchasai; chapter 2 aimed at it, and here it is gone in two words. Exekleisthē, “it has been shut out,” is the aorist passive of ekkleiō, which stands in two verses of the New Testament (ekkleiō): this one and Galatians 4:17, where the people who court the Galatians “want to shut you out” (not yet on these pages). KJV “It is excluded,” Douay “It is excluded,” NIV “It is excluded” and Geneva “It is excluded” use a legal word; NWT 1984 “It is shut out” keeps the door.

⚠ “A law of faith” (Romans 3:27). Dia nomou pisteōs. The Greek says “law of faith,” and the genitive is as open as “faith of Jesus” in verse 22. KJV “the law of faith,” Douay “the law of faith” and NWT 1984 “the law of faith” keep the phrase; ASV “a law of faith” makes it a rule; NIV “the law that requires faith” turns the genitive into a verb. Whether “law” means a principle, the Torah itself read by faith, or something else is not said. Romans 8:2 uses the same shape twice, “the law of the Spirit of life … the law of sin and of death.”

“We reckon” (Romans 3:28). Logizometha stands in this verse alone in this form (logizometha), and its verb is the verb of 2:3 (“do you suppose”) and 2:26 (“will not his uncircumcision be counted as circumcision?”); it is the accountant’s verb, and in Romans 4:3 it is the verb of “it was reckoned to him as righteousness.” The apparatus gives gar, “for,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and oun, “therefore,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which also reverses the order to pistei dikaiousthai: KJV “Therefore we conclude” and ASV “We reckon therefore” have the second; NWT 1984 “For we reckon” and NIV “For we maintain” have the first. Douay “For we account” has gar.

“If indeed God is one” (Romans 3:29–30). Eiper heis ho theos. This is the Shema in a Greek sentence, Deuteronomy 6:4, “Jehovah our God, Jehovah is one,” used as a premise: if God is one, he cannot be the God of one people only, and the question of verse 29 answers itself, “Yes, of the nations also.” The apparatus gives eiper, “if indeed,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and epeiper, “since indeed,” to Robinson–Pierpont: ASV “if so be that God is one” has the first and KJV “Seeing it is one God” the second. The two prepositions, ek pisteōs for the circumcision and dia tēs pisteōs for the uncircumcision, are different, and the second noun has the article, “the faith”: NIV “by faith and the uncircumcised through that same faith” reads the article as “that same,” and NWT 1984 “as a result of faith and uncircumcised people righteous by means of their faith” reads it as “their.” This translation prints “the faith.”

“We establish law” (Romans 3:31). Histanomen stands in this verse alone (histanomen); the apparatus gives histōmen to Robinson–Pierpont. The question of verse 31 is the first question of the chapter put the other way: verse 3 asked whether human unfaithfulness abolishes God’s faithfulness; verse 31 asks whether faith abolishes law. Each gets the same “may it never be,” and the second is answered here with only a counter-claim, nomon histanomen, “we establish law.” How, the chapter does not say; Romans 8:4 says the requirement of the law is “fulfilled in us, who walk not according to flesh but according to Spirit.” KJV “we establish the law,” ASV “we establish the law,” Douay “we establish the law,” Geneva “we establish the Law” and NWT 1984 “we establish law” agree; NIV “we uphold the law” is the exception.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “oracles” for logia (v. 2), as at Acts 7:38; “unfaithful,” “unfaithfulness” and “faithfulness” for the one root of v. 3; “abolish” for katargeō at both v. 3 and v. 31, as at 1 Corinthians 13:8; “May it never be!” for mē genoito at vv. 4, 6 and 31, as at Luke 20:16; “justified” for dikaioō in every use (vv. 4, 20, 24, 26, 28, 30); “works of law” with no article at v. 20 and v. 28; “faith in Jesus Christ” for pisteōs Iēsou Christou (v. 22) and “has faith in Jesus” for the same genitive (v. 26), with the range in the note; “a place of atonement” for hilastērion (v. 25); “the passing over” for paresis (v. 25); “forbearance” for anochē (v. 26), as at 2:4.

Echoes paid. “To the Jew first” (1:16, 2:10) — the chapter’s first question, v. 1, asks what that priority leaves standing. No partiality (2:11) — now “no distinction,” v. 22. God’s righteousness revealed (1:17) — v. 5 and vv. 21–22. Forbearance (2:4) — v. 26. Boasting (2:17, 2:23) — shut out at v. 27. Circumcision (2:25–29) — the chapter’s first question and its last argument, v. 30. The glory of God (1:23) — v. 23. The name blasphemed (2:24) — v. 8.

Echoes planted. “We reckon” (v. 28), which Romans 4:3 takes up as “it was reckoned to him as righteousness”; the circumcised and the uncircumcised (v. 30), which Romans 4:9 asks about again; “no distinction” (v. 22), which comes back at Romans 10:12; “the advantage” (v. 1), inventoried at Romans 9:4–5; the law of faith (v. 27), set against 8:2; and “we establish law” (v. 31), whose how is not given here and whose nearest answer is 8:4.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether pistis Iēsou Christou means faith in Christ or the faith of Christ (v. 22); whether hilastērion names a place, a means or a result (v. 25); which Hebrew line stands behind “there is no righteous one” (v. 10); and what “the law” includes in v. 19 and what nomos means in “law of faith” (v. 27).

Next in this book: Romans 4 — Abraham. “What shall we say Abraham, our forefather according to the flesh, has found?” If he was justified by works, 4:2 says, he has something to boast about; and 4:3 answers with what the Scripture says, that Abraham believed God and it was reckoned to him as righteousness (Genesis 15).
