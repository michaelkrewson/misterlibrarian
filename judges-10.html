After Abimelech, Tola of Issachar judges Israel from Shamir for twenty-three years, and Jair the Gileadite for twenty-two, with thirty sons and thirty towns that keep his name. Israel again serves the gods of its neighbours, and God sells it to the Philistines and Ammon, who oppress it for eighteen years across the Jordan and in Judah, Benjamin and Ephraim. Israel confesses; God refuses at first to save it again, and then the people put away their foreign gods, and his soul is cut short by their misery. Ammon camps in Gilead and Israel at Mizpah, and the princes of Gilead ask who will lead the war.
Within the period of the judges, with no date the chapter gives for itself. It gives the judges' years (Tola 23, Jair 22) and the eighteen years of Ammon's oppression (10:8), and comes after Abimelech's three years (9:22). The early/late question applies (c. 1200 BC on the early reckoning this project foregrounds, c. 1100 BC on the late).

Verses 1–2 · Tola, Issachar, and the twenty-three years, Judges 10:1–2: a judge from the tribe of Issachar who lives in the hills of Ephraim

★★★ Tola arises “after Abimelech” to save Israel. The verb qum, “arose,” is the one the book uses when God raises up a judge (Judges 2:16, now on these pages). The chapter gives no reason for the rising; it follows the bad king with a judge from another tribe.

★★ Tola is from Issachar, but lives in Shamir, in the hill country of Ephraim. The book gives his father’s name and grandfather’s, Puah and Dodo, and then sets him in Ephraim. The town of Shamir is not otherwise identified in the chapter.

★ He judges for twenty-three years, dies, and is buried in Shamir. The verb shaphat, “judged,” is the book’s verb for what a judge does, and Israel’s peace is counted in years of it.

Verses 3–5 · Jair and the thirty towns, Judges 10:3–5: thirty sons on thirty young donkeys, and the villages that keep his name

★★★ Jair the Gileadite judges for twenty-two years, and has thirty sons who ride thirty young donkeys. The Hebrew word ayarim is the word for young asses, the KJV’s “colts”; the shelf is divided on whether they are donkeys or full-grown asses (see the last note of this group).

★★ Their thirty towns are called Havvoth-jair, “the villages of Jair,” to this day. The name is the book’s own, and the chapter says the name still held in its day. The same villages are linked to Jair son of Manasseh in Numbers 32:41, already on these pages, and Deuteronomy 3:14.

★★ He dies and is buried in Kamon. The place is named only here in the chapter, and the note does not give its location.

★ The shelf reads the donkeys differently. The KJV and ASV say “ass colts”; the NWT 1984 says “full-grown asses”, and the NWT 2013 “donkeys”. The TNM 1987 says “asnos adultos”, full-grown asses, and the TNM 2019 says “burros”. The Hebrew is the word for the young of the ass, so “full-grown” is the reading that has to be argued for.

Verses 6–9 · The gods of the neighbours, Judges 10:6–9: the Baalim, Ashtaroth, and the gods of five peoples, and eighteen years under Ammon

★★★ Israel “again added” to doing evil, and serves the gods of its neighbours. The verb yasaf, “added,” means doing more of the same. The list is the book’s full census of the gods Israel takes up: the Baalim and the Ashtaroth, the plural forms of the Canaanite names, and then the gods of five peoples.

★★ The list of peoples is also the list of Israel’s enemies. Aram, Moab, Ammon and the Philistines each appear as oppressors earlier in the book (Judges 3:8, 3:12, 3:14, 3:31, now on these pages). Sidon is named as an oppressor only here, in v. 12. The gods are the peoples’ gods, and the peoples are the ones that will punish Israel for serving them.

★★ The anger burns, and Israel is sold to the Philistines and to Ammon. The verb makar, “sold,” is the one for selling a person into another’s power, and the chapter says God sold them. The next verses name the length of the oppression: eighteen years, and the Ammonites cross the Jordan to fight in Judah, Benjamin and Ephraim.

★★ The oppression reaches across the Jordan. “All the children of Israel who were across the Jordan in the land of the Amorites, which is in Gilead” (v. 8) are the tribes of Gilead. The Ammonites cross the river to fight in the west as well, so the war is on both banks.

Verses 10–14 · The confession and the answer, Judges 10:10–14: “We have sinned,” and God says he will save them no more

★★★ The people confess: “We have sinned against you.” The verb chata, “sinned,” is the book’s word for missing the mark, and the confession names the sin: forsaking God and serving the Baalim. The verb za‘aq, “cried,” is the one of Exodus 2:23, already on these pages, where Israel cries out in Egypt.

★★★ God answers by listing the saves: Egypt, the Amorites, Ammon, the Philistines, the Sidonians, Amalek and Maon. The list is a recital of the book’s own oppressions. Amalek and Ammon were named earlier (Judges 3:13); Sidon and Maon appear only here. The Hebrew for the last name is Maon. The KJV and ASV read “Maonites”, but the NWT (both editions) and the TNM (both) read “Midianites”. The Hebrew we print does not say Midian, and this note does not decide between the two names; the chapter’s argument is the same either way.

★★ “Yet you have forsaken me; therefore I will save you no more.” The verb yasaf comes again, and the answer is the sternest in the book: God will not save. The next verse turns it into a taunt.

★★★ “Go and cry to the gods you have chosen.” The verb bachar, “chosen,” is the one for choosing a god (see Joshua 24:15, now on these pages). The gods the people chose are the ones God tells them to ask. The irony is the point of the verse.

Verses 15–16 · The people leave it to God, and his soul is cut short, Judges 10:15–16: “do to us whatever seems good in your eyes”

★★★ The people hand God the decision: “do to us whatever seems good in your eyes; only deliver us this day.” The phrase kekhol ha-tov be-einekha, “as all that is good in your eyes,” leaves God the choice; what they ask for is only deliverance today.

★★★ They put away the foreign gods, and God’s soul is cut short by Israel’s misery. The verb qatsar is the one for shortness, and the phrase qatsar nefesh means impatience, a soul that cannot wait (compare Numbers 21:4, already on these pages). The shelf divides on how to render it: the KJV and ASV say “grieved”; the NWT 1984 says “impatient”; the NWT 2013 says “could no longer tolerate”; the TNM 1987 says “se impacientó”; the TNM 2019 says “ya no pudo soportar más ver sufrir a Israel”. The Mister reading keeps the Hebrew’s shortness: God cannot bear to wait any longer while Israel suffers.

Verses 17–18 · The camps at Gilead and Mizpah, Judges 10:17–18: an assembly, and the question of who will lead it

★★ Ammon camps in Gilead, and Israel gathers and camps at Mizpah. The name Mizpah means “watchtower”; this note does not locate the Gilead one.

★★★ The princes of Gilead ask who will begin the fight against Ammon, and promise him the headship. The verb chalal, “begin,” is the one for opening a war, and the promise is headship over all the inhabitants of Gilead. The chapter leaves the question open; the answer comes in Judges 11 (now on these pages).

★ The shelf reads the question differently. The KJV says “what man is he that will begin”; the NWT 2013 says “Who will take the lead”; the TNM 2019 says “¿Quién nos dirigirá en la batalla?”. The question is the same in all of them: who will go first.

Ecos cumplidos. Several of the peoples of v. 6 oppressed Israel in Judges 3 (now on these pages), and God’s speech in vv. 11–14 recalls the saves of the book so far. The Jair of Numbers 32:41, already on these pages, is probably the same name.

Ecos planteados. Jephthah, the Gileadite who will answer the question of v. 18, is in Judges 11 (now on these pages). The war with Ammon is the one that the Gilead princes’ question opens.

Decisiones. The Hebrew ayarim is rendered “young donkeys”, and qatsar nefesh is kept as “cut short” with the shelf readings beside it. Maon is kept as the Hebrew name, and the Midianite reading is noted rather than adopted. The “Baalim” and “Ashtaroth” keep their plural forms.

Biblioteca. New entries are needed for Tola, Shamir, Kamon, Havvoth-jair and the Gilead Mizpah, and the chapter links them. Maon is left unlinked: the name is disputed on the shelf and has no entry yet.

⚠ A note on order: Judges stands on these pages at chapters 1–11. Judges 12 and the rest of the book remain open.
