A psalm with no heading, opening with a question: why the nations are in tumult and the kings of the earth take their stand against Jehovah and his anointed, saying let us tear off their bonds; the one who sits in the heavens laughs and then speaks in anger; the king set on Zion, my holy mountain, tells the decree, You are my son, today I have fathered you; ask of me and I will give nations, you will break them with a rod of iron; the closing call to the kings to be wise, serve Jehovah with fear, kiss the son lest you perish in the way, and happy are all who take refuge in him.
No heading and no date in the psalm itself. ⚠ Nothing in it names a king or a war; the New Testament calls it David’s (Acts 4:25) and reads it of the Messiah, and the shelf is not agreed on whether the last line says ‘son’.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · Psalm 2:1–3 Why have the nations been in tumult — the kings take their stand — against Jehovah and his anointed — let us tear off their bonds

⭐ No heading. The psalm has none, which is rare in the first book of the Psalms, and it opens with a question, lamma, “why”. It is the second psalm and is often read with the first: that one begins “Happy is the man” and this one ends “happy are all who take refuge in him”, and the word that is the man’s own meditating there is the nations’ muttering here (Psalm 1:2, now on these pages). The shelf prints verse 1 as: the KJV “Why do the heathen rage, and the people imagine a vain thing?”, the ASV “Why do the nations rage, And the peoples meditate a vain thing?”, the NIV “Why do the nations conspire and the peoples plot in vain?”, the NWT 1984 “Why have the nations been in tumult And the national groups themselves kept muttering an empty thing?” and the Douay “Why have the Gentiles raged, and the people devised vain things?”.

⭐ Mutter. The verb of the second line is yehgu, from hagah: “mutter an empty thing” stands in this one verse. Hagah is a low sound, a murmur of speech, and it is what the one who loves Jehovah’s law does with it, day and night; here it is what the peoples do with nothing. The ASV prints “meditate”, as it does at Psalm 1:2, and the NWT 1984 “kept muttering”. The TLB has “What fools the nations are to rage against the Lord!”, with the “fools” of the translators.

⭐ Kings, and his anointed. The kings take their stand and the rulers have taken counsel together against two: “against Jehovah and against his anointed” stands in this one verse. The KJV has “The kings of the earth set themselves, and the rulers take counsel together, against the Lord, and against his anointed”, the NIV “The kings of the earth rise up and the rulers band together against the Lord and against his anointed”, the NWT 1984 “The kings of earth take their stand And high officials themselves have massed together as one”, the Geneva and the Douay “against the Lord and against his Christ” and the TLB “against the Lord and his Messiah, Christ the King”. Mashiach, “anointed”, is the word Hannah’s song ends on (1 Samuel 2:10), and the prayer of the church in Jerusalem quotes verses 1–2 of this psalm about Herod and Pilate (Acts 4:25–26), both now on these pages.

⭐ An old form of “them”. Verse 3 is what they say: the KJV has “Let us break their bands asunder, and cast away their cords from us”, the NIV “Let us break their chains and throw off their shackles”, the Douay “and let us cast away their yoke from us” and the TLB “Come, let us break his chains”. The psalm uses the old poetic ending -emo for “them” five times in verses 3–5: moserotemo and avotemo, “their bonds”, “their cords”; lamo, “at them”; eleimo, “to them”; yevahalemo, “he terrifies them”. The bonds are the yoke on the nations, and the tearing is a revolt against the one who holds the other end.

Verses 4–6 · Psalm 2:4–6 He who sits in the heavens laughs — then he speaks in his anger — and I have set my king on Zion

⭐ He laughs. “He who sits in the heavens laughs” stands in this one verse, and “the Lord holds them in derision” stands in this one verse. The KJV has “He that sitteth in the heavens shall laugh: the Lord shall have them in derision”, the NIV “The One enthroned in heaven laughs; the Lord scoffs at them”, the Douay “He that dwelleth in heaven shall laugh at them: and the Lord shall deride them” and the TLB “But God in heaven merely laughs! He is amused by all their puny plans”. ⚠ The word in the second half is Adonai, “Lord”, and the NWT 1984 has “The very One sitting in the heavens will laugh; Jehovah himself will hold them in derision”, so it prints the Name where the Hebrew has the title. The Name is written three times in the psalm, at verses 2, 7 and 11. The laugh of God at the plots of the wicked is also in Psalm 37:13 (now on these pages).

⭐ Then he speaks. The KJV has “Then shall he speak unto them in his wrath, and vex them in his sore displeasure”, the NIV “He rebukes them in his anger and terrifies them in his wrath”, the NWT 1984 “At that time he will speak to them in his anger”, the Douay “Then shall he speak to them in his anger, and trouble them in his rage” and the TLB “in fierce fury he rebukes them and fills them with fear”. The verb of terrifying, yevahalemo, is from bhl, the root of the alarm that comes on Belshazzar and Daniel in Aramaic (Daniel 5:6, Daniel 7:15, now on these pages).

⚠ I have set. “And I—I have set my king” stands in this one verse, and the verb, nasachti, stands in two verses, here and Proverbs 8:23, where Wisdom says she was nissakhti, set up, before the earth. Nasakh is to pour, as a libation is poured, and the shelf prints it as “set” (KJV: “Yet have I set my king upon my holy hill of Zion”; ASV: “Yet I have set my king Upon my holy hill of Zion”) or “installed” (NIV: “I have installed my king on Zion, my holy mountain”; NWT 1984: “I, even I, have installed my king Upon Zion, my holy mountain”); the TLB has “I have enthroned him in Jerusalem, my holy city”, which is not in the Hebrew. And the Douay has the king speak: “But I am appointed king by him over Sion his holy mountain, preaching his commandment”.

Verses 7–9 · Psalm 2:7–9 I will tell of the decree — You are my son; today I have fathered you — ask of me — you will break them with a rod of iron

⚠ Who speaks? Verse 7 begins asapperah el-choq, “I will tell of the decree”, and the speaker is the king of verse 6, telling what Jehovah said to him. “You are my son; I—today I have fathered you” stands in this one verse, and so does “today I have fathered you”. The KJV has “I will declare the decree: the Lord hath said unto me, Thou art my Son; this day have I begotten thee”, the ASV “I will tell of the decree: Jehovah said unto me, Thou art my son; This day have I begotten thee”, the Geneva “this day have I begotten thee” and the Douay “Thou art my son, this day have I begotten thee”. The NIV has “You are my son; today I have become your father”, the NWT 1984 “I, today, I have become your father” and the TLB “This is your Coronation Day”. The verb is yelidtikha, “I have borne you” or “fathered you”: the day of his becoming king is the day God became his father, as in 2 Samuel 7:14, “I will be to him a father, and he shall be to me a son” (now on these pages).

⭐ This verse in the New Testament. Paul says it of the raising of Jesus (Acts 13:33), where the psalm is called “the second psalm”; some ancient copies of Acts read “the first”, which fits the way Psalm 1 and 2 were sometimes counted as one. The letter to the Hebrews uses it to say that Jesus is more than the angels (Hebrews 1:5) and that he did not give himself the priesthood (Hebrews 5:5). These pages have the second.

⭐ Ask. She’al mimmeni, “ask of me”, is a command to ask, and what is given is the nations: the KJV has “Ask of me, and I shall give thee the heathen for thine inheritance, and the uttermost parts of the earth for thy possession”, the NIV “Ask me, and I will make the nations your inheritance, the ends of the earth your possession”, the NWT 1984 “Ask of me, that I may give nations as your inheritance” and the TLB “Only ask and I will give you all the nations of the world”. It is the scene of Daniel 7:14, where one like a son of man is given “dominion, and glory, and kingdom” over all peoples (now on these pages).

⚠ Break, or shepherd? “you will break them with a rod of iron” stands in this one verse, and the verb is written tr’m, which can be read two ways: tero’em, “you will break”, or tir’em, “you will shepherd them”. The Hebrew text is pointed for “break”, and so the KJV has “Thou shalt break them with a rod of iron; thou shalt dash them in pieces like a potter’s vessel”, the NIV “You will break them with a rod of iron; you will dash them to pieces like pottery”, the NWT 1984 “You will break them with an iron scepter, As though a potter’s vessel you will dash them to pieces” and the Geneva “Thou shalt crush them with a scepter of iron”. The Douay reads it as ruling: “Thou shalt rule them with a rod of iron”; the TLB has “Rule them with an iron rod; smash them like clay pots!”. And Revelation reads it as shepherding: “he will shepherd them with an iron rod, as clay pots are shattered” (Revelation 2:27, 12:5, 19:15, now on these pages). The potter’s vessel that is dashed is also the flask of Jeremiah 19:11 (now on these pages).

Verses 10–12 · Psalm 2:10–12 And now, O kings, be wise — serve Jehovah with fear — kiss the son — happy are all who take refuge in him

⭐ And now. The turn is ve’attah, “and now”: the kings who were told what Jehovah laughs at are told what to do. The KJV has “Be wise now therefore, O ye kings: be instructed, ye judges of the earth”, the ASV “Now therefore be wise, O ye kings: Be instructed, ye judges of the earth”, the NIV “Therefore, you kings, be wise; be warned, you rulers of the earth”, the NWT 1984 “And now, O kings, exercise insight; Let yourselves be corrected, O judges of the earth”, the Douay “And now, O ye kings, understand: receive instruction, you that judge the earth” and the TLB “O kings and rulers of the earth, listen while there is time”. The kings are called shoftei arets, “judges of the earth”, and the psalm that began with the earth’s kings against Jehovah ends with them being told to serve him.

⭐ Fear, and trembling. “Serve Jehovah with fear” stands in this one verse, and “rejoice with trembling” stands in this one verse: the two words that do not usually go together, gilu, rejoice, and re’adah, trembling. The KJV has “Serve the Lord with fear, and rejoice with trembling”, the NIV “Serve the Lord with fear and celebrate his rule with trembling” (“his rule” is the translators’) and the NWT 1984 “Serve Jehovah with fear And be joyful with trembling”.

⚠ Kiss the son. The line is “nashqu var”, which stands in this one verse, and nobody’s English is certain of it. Nashqu is “kiss” (an order); bar is the Aramaic word for “son” (the word of “one like a son of man”, kevar enash, Daniel 7:13, now on these pages), where Hebrew would say ben, the word used in verse 7; it is also a Hebrew word for “pure”, as in “pure of heart” (Psalm 24:4, now on these pages). The shelf: the KJV has “Kiss the Son, lest he be angry, and ye perish from the way”, the ASV “Kiss the son, lest he be angry, and ye perish in the way”, the Geneva “Kiss the Son”, the NIV “Kiss his son, or he will be angry and your way will lead to your destruction”, the NWT 1984 “Kiss the son, that He may not become incensed”, the TLB “Fall down before his Son and kiss his feet before his anger is roused and you perish” and the Douay “Embrace discipline, lest at any time the Lord be angry, and you perish from the just way”. The capital S is the KJV’s; the Hebrew has no capitals. This translation prints “the son” and says here that the word is hard.

⭐ Perish in the way; happy. The warning is the closing of Psalm 1: “you perish in the way” stands in this one verse, and Psalm 1:6 ends “the way of the wicked will perish” (now on these pages). The KJV has “when his wrath is kindled but a little”, the ASV “For his wrath will soon be kindled”, the NIV “for his wrath can flare up in a moment”, the NWT 1984 “For his anger flares up easily” and the Douay “When his wrath shall be kindled in a short time”. And the last line: “happy are all who take refuge in him” stands in this one verse. The KJV has “Blessed are all they that put their trust in him”, the ASV “Blessed are all they that take refuge in him”, the NIV “Blessed are all who take refuge in him”, the NWT 1984 “Happy are all those taking refuge in him”, the Douay “blessed are all they that trust in him” and the TLB “But oh, the joys of those who put their trust in him!”. The word for happy, ashrei, is the first word of Psalm 1; and take refuge, chasah, is what the shield of Psalm 18:30 is for (now on these pages). “In him” is bo, and the Hebrew does not say whether it is Jehovah or the son.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “tumult” for ragash and “mutter” for hagah at verse 1; “anointed” for mashiach at verse 2, “king” at verse 6 and “son” for ben at verse 7 and for bar at verse 12; “bonds” and “cords” at verse 3; “fathered” for yalad at verse 7; “wise” for hiskil and “corrected” for nivvasar at verse 10; “happy” for ashrei at verse 12. The Name is written three times in the psalm, at verses 2, 7 and 11, and Adonai, “Lord”, once, at verse 4.

Echoes paid. Psalm 1:2 and 1:6; Psalm 18:30; Psalm 24:4; Psalm 37:13; 1 Samuel 2:10; 2 Samuel 7:14; Daniel 5:6, 7:13, 7:14 and 7:15; Jeremiah 19:11; Acts 4:25–26 and 13:33; Hebrews 1:5 and 5:5; Revelation 2:27, 12:5 and 19:15.

Echoes planted. Proverbs 8:23.

⚠ A note on order: the Psalms stand on these pages at 1, 2, 8, 18, 19, 22, 23, 24, 25, 27, 32, 34, 37, 40, 42, 46, 51, 56, 62, 63, 90, 91, 100, 103, 118, 119, 121, 130, 138, 139, 145 and 150, chosen by how often they are read rather than in sequence. The sequential thread is unchanged: next in sequence is Psalm 3.
