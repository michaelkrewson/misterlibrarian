📖 1 John

Ἰωάννου Αʹ (Iōannou A) — 'Of John, 1' — the traditional Greek title. The book itself carries no author's name, no greeting, and no address: it opens straight into 'that which was from the beginning.'

The warmest and most searching of the letters — barely a letter at all, with no opening or closing formulas, more a sermon or a circular meditation. Written to a church torn by people who had walked out of it denying that Christ came in the flesh, it answers with a testimony that has SEEN and TOUCHED the Word of life, and with two of Scripture's greatest sentences: 'God is light' and 'God is love.'

Chapter by chapter

1 John 1The warmest and most searching of the letters — and barely a letter at all: no greeting, no name, no address, just a voice that has SEEN and TOUCHED what it preaches. ⚠ It opens like the Gospel of John ("that which was from the beginning… the word of life") and then, against secessionists who denied Christ came in the flesh, puts hands on it: "that which we have heard… seen with our eyes… and our hands HANDLED." The eyewitnesses share what they saw so the readers may have FELLOWSHIP (koinōnia) — with them, and so with the Father and the Son. Then the letter's first great declaration: ⚠ "God is LIGHT, and in him is no darkness at all" — the first of the Johannine "God is" sayings, met by "God is love" at the summit (4:8). To walk in the light is to live truthfully, and "the blood of Jesus his Son cleanses us from all sin" — a present, continual cleansing, not sinlessness. The chapter closes by quoting and demolishing three slogans of the perfectionist opponents ("we have no sin"; "we have not sinned") and answering them with one honest admission: "if we CONFESS our sins, he is faithful and just to forgive."

About the book

Where it sits
One of the CATHOLIC or 'general' epistles, and — unlike its two shorter companions — one of the best-attested and least-disputed books in the early canon: quoted by Polycarp and used freely by the second century, and placed by Eusebius among the undisputed books (where he lists 2 and 3 John among the 'spoken-against'). Its authority was never seriously in question.
Genre
Formally the odd one out among New Testament 'letters' — it has NO epistolary frame at all: no sender, no recipient, no greeting, no travel plans, no farewell. It reads as a written sermon, a pastoral tract, or a circular addressed to a cluster of congregations, structured not by argument-in-sequence but by circling and returning to a few themes (light, love, truth, the Son) in a spiral. Its Greek is the simplest in the New Testament in vocabulary and the subtlest in effect, built on stark antitheses — light and darkness, truth and lie, love and hate, the Father and the world.
Author
The book is anonymous, but its style, vocabulary and thought-world are so close to the GOSPEL OF JOHN — the Word, life, light, truth, love, remaining, 'from the beginning', the world — that common authorship with the Gospel and the two shorter letters is granted by nearly everyone. WHO that is remains the open question of the whole Johannine corpus: John the apostle, son of Zebedee, by strong early tradition; or a distinct figure ('John the elder'), or a 'Johannine school' carrying the apostle's tradition, on many critical readings. ⚠ The 'we' of the prologue claims eyewitness testimony ('our hands handled'); whether that is the apostle's own voice or the collective voice of a tradition that goes back to him is part of what is debated. The library reports the positions and casts no vote.
Date written
Traditionally late in the first century, c. AD 85–95, from the same period and setting as 2 and 3 John. Nothing inside the letter is externally datable; the date is inferred from its relationship to the Gospel and the shorter letters and from the crisis it addresses — a schism already unfolding, whose leaders have recently left (2:19).
Place
By strong early tradition, EPHESUS in Asia Minor, where John is said to have spent his last years — written to a group of congregations in the same region, among whom the secession had happened and the false teaching was spreading.
Audience
A cluster of churches in the Johannine circle, shaken by a SCHISM: a group had 'gone out from us' (2:19), denying that Jesus is the Christ come in the flesh (2:22; 4:2-3) and, it seems, claiming a sinlessness or a spiritual advancement that put them beyond ordinary morality (1:8-10). The letter is written to reassure those who STAYED — 'that you may know that you have eternal life' (5:13) — by giving them tests to tell true from false: right confession of the Son, obedience to his commands, and love of the brothers and sisters.

How it's laid out

1:1–4Prologue — 'that which was from the beginning… we have heard, seen, handled', the word of life, and fellowship with the Father and Son; the Gospel's overture in a letter's key.
1:5–2:17Walking in the light — God is light, the blood that cleanses, confession of sin against the perfectionists; the advocate and the propitiation; keeping his commandments, and not loving the world.
2:18–3:24The antichrists and the children of God — those who denied the Son and left; abiding in what was heard from the beginning; 'see what love the Father has given us', and love in deed and truth.
4:1–5:12Testing the spirits, and God is love — the confession of Christ in flesh; 'God is love' and love perfected; faith that overcomes the world; and the testimony that God gave us eternal life in his Son.
5:13–21Conclusion — 'that you may know that you have eternal life'; confidence in prayer, the sin unto death, and 'keep yourselves from idols.'

What it's about

  • GOD IS LIGHT / GOD IS LOVE — the two great declarations (1:5; 4:8, 16) that frame the letter's moral world; light meaning holiness and truth, love meaning the self-giving shown at the cross ('he laid down his life for us', 3:16).
  • FELLOWSHIP (koinōnia) and abiding — the shared life 'with the Father and with his Son' (1:3) and the call to 'remain' (menō) in him, in his word, and in love; the opposite of the secessionists who ran ahead and left.
  • TESTS of the real thing — 1 John gives criteria for assurance: right belief (Jesus is the Christ come in the flesh), right behaviour (keeping his commandments), and right love (of the brothers and sisters). 'By this we know…' tolls through the letter.
  • The reality of the INCARNATION — against a proto-docetic denial, the letter grounds everything in a Christ truly heard, seen and handled (1:1), who came 'in flesh' (4:2) and 'by water and blood' (5:6).
  • SIN and cleansing — held in a deliberate tension: believers still sin and must confess it (1:8-10), yet the one born of God does not go on sinning (3:9); the blood cleanses continually and Christ is the advocate when we fail (2:1-2).
  • ASSURANCE — the letter's stated purpose: 'that you may KNOW that you have eternal life' (5:13); confidence before God, answered prayer, and no fear in love (4:18).

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. The Greek of 1 John is the plainest in the New Testament by vocabulary — short words, simple connectives, endless repetition — and among the hardest to render well, because its power is in cadence and antithesis that flatten in paraphrase. ⚠ Chapter 1 carries one notable variant, at 1:4, where the earliest witnesses read 'that OUR joy may be complete' and much of the tradition 'YOUR joy' (hēmōn / hymōn, a single letter, confused throughout the New Testament); this translation prints the critical text's 'our' and flags the alternative. The letter's most famous textual problem lies later — the 'Johannine Comma' of 5:7-8, the explicit Trinitarian sentence absent from every early Greek manuscript and now recognised as a Latin gloss — flagged there when the translation reaches it. The seven-version shelf under every chapter compares the NIV, KJV, Douay-Rheims, The Living Bible, the 1599 Geneva, ASV, and NWT.

Looking forward

1 John's Christ is the Word of life made tangible — heard, seen, looked upon and HANDLED (1:1) — the eternal life 'which was with the Father and was made manifest'. Against those who split the human Jesus from a divine Christ, the letter insists on one Son come 'in flesh' (4:2), 'by water and blood' (5:6), whose real blood 'cleanses us from all sin' (1:7). He is the ADVOCATE (paraklētos) with the Father and the PROPITIATION for our sins — 'and not for ours only but for the whole world' (2:2). And he is the ground of assurance: 'whoever has the Son has life' (5:12). The whole letter turns on confessing this Son rightly — 'who is the liar but the one who denies that Jesus is the Christ?' (2:22) — because to have the Son is to have the Father, and to deny him is to have neither.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP, shared with the whole Johannine corpus — the apostle John, 'John the elder', or a Johannine school — and with it the relation of the letter to the Fourth Gospel (which came first, and whether the same hand wrote both). The nature and identity of the OPPONENTS: proto-docetists, followers of Cerinthus (who by tradition separated Jesus from the Christ), or an early gnosticising movement — reconstructed only from what the letter denies. The tension between 'if we say we have no sin, we deceive ourselves' (1:8) and 'no one born of God sins' (3:9), which readers have resolved in several ways. And the later intrusion of the JOHANNINE COMMA (5:7-8), the Trinitarian sentence that entered the Latin tradition and the KJV but no early Greek text. The library reports the positions and casts no vote.
