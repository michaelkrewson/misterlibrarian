At the return of the year David sends Joab and all Israel against Ammon and stays in Jerusalem; from his roof he sees a woman washing, learns she is Bathsheba, daughter of Eliam and wife of Uriah the Hittite, sends and takes her, and she tells him she is pregnant; he recalls Uriah from the front and tries to send him home, and when Uriah will not go down to his house while the ark and the army are in booths, even drunk, David writes a letter to Joab that Uriah carries himself; Joab sets Uriah where the best defenders are and he dies, Joab’s messenger carries the news, David tells Joab not to let it be evil in his eyes, Bathsheba mourns and David gathers her into his house, and the thing was evil in the eyes of Jehovah.
After the wars of 2 Samuel 10; the chapter gives no date beyond “the return of the year”. The child is born after the mourning, and the next chapter names Solomon after another son.

And it happened at the return of the year, at the time when the messengers go out, that David sent Joab and his servants with him and all Israel; and they ruined the sons of Ammon, and laid siege to Rabbah. And David was dwelling in Jerusalem.note
Y sucedió a la vuelta del año, al tiempo en que salen los mensajeros, que David envió a Joab y a sus siervos con él y a todo Israel; y arruinaron a los hijos de Amón, y sitiaron Rabá. Y David estaba morando en Jerusalén.

10

Y se lo contaron a David: «Urías no bajó a su casa». Y dijo David a Urías: «¿No has venido de camino? ¿Por qué no bajaste a tu casa?»

Y David lo llamó, y comió delante de él y bebió; y lo embriagó; y salió por la tarde a acostarse en su lecho con los siervos de su señor, pero a su casa no bajó.
14
וַיְהִי בַבֹּקֶר, וַיִּכְתֹּב דָּוִד סֵפֶר אֶל-יוֹאָב; וַיִּשְׁלַח, בְּיַד אוּרִיָּה.

Y sucedió por la mañana que David escribió una carta a Joab; y la envió por mano de Urías.
15

Y los flecheros dispararon contra tus siervos desde el muro; y murieron algunos de los siervos del rey; y también tu siervo Urías el heteo ha muerto».
25
וַיֹּאמֶר דָּוִד אֶל-הַמַּלְאָךְ, כֹּה-תֹאמַר אֶל-יוֹאָב אַל-יֵרַע בְּעֵינֶיךָ אֶת-הַדָּבָר הַזֶּה--כִּי-כָזֹה וְכָזֶה, תֹּאכַל הֶחָרֶב; הַחֲזֵק מִלְחַמְתְּךָ אֶל-הָעִיר וְהָרְסָהּ, וְחַזְּקֵהוּ.

26

Y la mujer de Urías oyó que Urías su marido había muerto; y se lamentó por su marido.
27

And when the mourning had passed, David sent and gathered her into his house; and she became his wife, and bore him a son. But the thing that David had done was evil in the eyes of Jehovah.note
Y pasado el luto, envió David y la recogió en su casa; y ella fue su mujer, y le dio a luz un hijo. Pero la cosa que David había hecho fue mala a los ojos de Jehová.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verse 1 · 2 Samuel 11:1 — “at the time when the messengers go out”, Rabbah besieged, and “David was dwelling in Jerusalem”

The messengers, or the kings. “At the return of the year, at the time when the messengers go out”: the Hebrew text as written has hammal’akhim, “the messengers”, where six of the seven versions on the shelf have “kings” (the TLB has “when wars begin”). “At the return of the year” stands in four verses. They are this one, 1 Kings 20:22 and 26, where the king of Aram comes up at the return of the year, and 2 Chronicles 36:10, where Nebuchadnezzar sends. Always it is the season when armies take the field again. “The going out of the kings” stands in one verse, the parallel in the Chronicles (1 Chronicles 20:1), and the Greek has tōn basileōn, “of the kings”. The ASV has “at the time when kings go out to battle”; the KJV “at the time when kings go forth to battle”; the NIV “at the time when kings go off to war”; the NWT 1984 “at the time that kings sally forth”; the Douay “at the time when kings go forth to war”; the Geneva “in the time when kings go forth to battle”; the TLB “at the time when wars begin”. This translation keeps what the Hebrew text says and does not read the Chronicles’ word into it: a season of messengers going out, and a king who does not.

Ruined, and laid siege. “And David sent Joab and his servants with him and all Israel; and they ruined the sons of Ammon, and laid siege to Rabbah”: the verb is shachat, “to ruin, to spoil”, and the siege is vayyatsuru, “and they hemmed in”, the war that chapter 10 left standing at the gate (2 Samuel 10:14, already on these pages). Rabbah is the city of the sons of Ammon (Rabbah). The ASV has “they destroyed the children of Ammon, and besieged Rabbah”; the NWT 1984 “they might bring the sons of Am”; the NIV “They destroyed the Ammonites and besieged Rabbah”; the Douay “they spoiled the children of Ammon, and besieged Rabba”; the TLB “to destroy the Ammonites”. In the chapter before, David crossed the Jordan himself (10:17); here he sends “all Israel” and sends the others out.

David was dwelling in Jerusalem. “And David was dwelling in Jerusalem”: yoshev, “sitting, dwelling”, a participle, so the sentence says what David was doing while the war went on. The root comes back in verse 12, when David tells Uriah “Stay here” and Uriah “stayed” in Jerusalem. The ASV has “But David tarried at Jerusalem”; the KJV “But David tarried still at Jerusalem”; the NIV “But David remained in Jerusalem”; the NWT 1984 “while David was dwelling in Jerusalem”; the Douay “but David remained in Jerusalem”; the TLB “But David stayed in Jerusalem”. The Chronicles give the same sentence and follow it at once with “And Joab smote Rabbah, and overthrew it” (1 Chronicles 20:1); the story of the next twenty-six verses is told only here, between those two clauses.

Verses 2–5 · Bathsheba: the roof, “Is this not Bathsheba?”, “sent … took”, “sanctifying herself”, and “I am pregnant”

The roof, and a woman washing. “And it happened at the time of evening that David rose from his bed and walked about on the roof of the king’s house; and from the roof he saw a woman washing; and the woman was very beautiful to look at”: the verb of her washing, rachatz, is the one David will use in verse 8, “wash your feet”. “Very beautiful to look at” is tovat mar’eh me’od, the words of Rebekah (Genesis 24:16, where the first word is written without the vav, and 26:7, already on these pages). The ASV has “saw a woman bathing; and the woman was very beautiful to look upon”; the KJV and the Geneva “a woman washing herself”; the NWT 1984 “the woman was very good in appearance”; the NIV “The woman was very beautiful”; the TLB “a woman of unusual beauty taking her evening bath”; the Douay “arose from his bed after noon”, the only version that moves the hour. The roof is the roof of the king’s house, and the next time a roof is named in David’s house Absalom’s tent stands on it (2 Samuel 16:22).

Is this not Bathsheba? “And David sent and inquired after the woman; and one said: ‘Is this not Bathsheba, daughter of Eliam, wife of Uriah the Hittite?’”: the answer gives her a father and a husband and no first word of her own; the one who answers is “one”, nameless. An Eliam son of Ahithophel the Gilonite is named among David’s Thirty (2 Samuel 23:34), and the Chronicles list “Bath-shua the daughter of Ammiel” as the mother of Solomon (1 Chronicles 3:5); whether the Eliam is the same, the text does not say. “Uriah the Hittite” stands in ten verses, five of them in this chapter (3, 6, 17, 21, 24); the others are 2 Samuel 12:9, 10; 23:39; 1 Kings 15:5 and 1 Chronicles 11:41, and the last of the Thirty is “Uriah the Hittite” (2 Samuel 23:39). His name ends in Jehovah’s: Uriyyah, “Jehovah is my light”. He is a Hittite like Ahimelech who went down to Saul’s camp with David (1 Samuel 26:6, already on these pages). The Geneva has “Is not this Bathsheba the daughter of Eliam, wife to Uriah the Hittite?”; the KJV “Is not this Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Uriah the Hittite?”; the NIV “She is Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam and the wife of Uriah the Hittite”; the TLB “she was Bathsheba, the daughter of Eliam and the wife of Uriah”; the Douay “Bethsabee the daughter of Eliam, the wife of Urias the Hethite”. The Greek has Eliab for the father.

Sent … took. “And David sent messengers, and took her; and she came to him, and he lay with her”: the verbs are David’s, “sent”, “took”, “lay”, and the Hebrew gives her no speech here. The king “takes” as Samuel had said he would: “your daughters he will take” (1 Samuel 8:13; 8:11, 16, already on these pages). Chapter 9 had a king who gave a field back (2 Samuel 9:7); this is the chapter in which he takes. The ASV has “David sent messengers, and took her; and she came in unto him, and he lay with her”; the NIV “David sent messengers to get her. She came to him, and he slept with her”; the NWT 1984 “David sent messengers that he might take her”; the TLB “David sent for her and when she came he slept with her”.

Sanctifying herself from her uncleanness. “She was sanctifying herself from her uncleanness”: “sanctifying herself” (mitqaddeshet) stands in one verse, and “from her uncleanness” (mittum’atah) stands in one verse as well, the same one. The uncleanness is the one of Leviticus 15, a woman’s “monthly separation” of seven days (Leviticus 15:19), after which “she shall be clean” (15:28, both already on these pages). It is usually read as the end of her period, which would put the child beyond doubt as David’s; the Hebrew says only what she was doing. The NIV has “(Now she was purifying herself from her monthly uncleanness.)”; the TLB “(She had just completed the purification rites after menstruation.)”; the NWT 1984 “while she was sanctifying herself from her uncleanness”; the ASV “(for she was purified from her uncleanness)”; the Douay “presently she was purified from her uncleanness”; the Geneva “(now she was purified from her uncleanness)”. The Greek has hagiazomenē apo akatharsias autēs, “being sanctified from her uncleanness”.

I am pregnant. “And the woman conceived; and she sent and told David, and said: ‘I am pregnant’”: harah anokhi, two words, and they are the only words Bathsheba speaks in the chapter; she mourns in verse 26, but without a speech. The NIV has “I am pregnant”; the NWT 1984 “I am pregnant”; the ASV “I am with child”; the KJV “I am with child”; the Geneva “I am with child”; the Douay “I have conceived”; the TLB gives no words, “she sent a message to inform him”. The Greek has en gastri echō, “I have in the womb”. She “sent” to David as David has been the one who “sent”.

Verses 6–13 · Uriah recalled — three welfares, “wash your feet”, “in booths”, “as your soul lives”, and a king who makes him drunk

The welfare of the war. “And David sent to Joab: ‘Send Uriah the Hittite to me’ … and David asked after the welfare of Joab, and the welfare of the people, and the welfare of the war”: the Hebrew has lishlom, “after the peace of”, three times, the last of the war itself, and the word is the one in vayyashlimu, “they made peace”, that closed the last chapter (2 Samuel 10:19, already on these pages). The ASV has “how Joab did, and how the people fared, and how the war prospered”; the KJV “how Joab did, and how the people did, and how the war prospered”; the NIV “how Joab was, how the soldiers were and how the war was going”; the NWT 1984 “how Joab was getting along and how the people were getting along and how the war was getting along”; the Douay “how Joab did, and the people, and how the war was carried on”; the TLB “how Joab and the army were getting along and how the war was prospering”.

Wash your feet. “Go down to your house, and wash your feet. And Uriah went out from the king’s house, and a portion from the king went out after him”: rachatz again, as of the woman on the roof. Whether “feet” is only the traveler’s feet or a delicate word for more, the Hebrew does not say, and the king’s next questions leave both open. Masse’t is a portion lifted from a table and sent, a mark of favor. The ASV has “Go down to thy house, and wash thy feet” and “a mess of food from the king”; the KJV and the Douay “a mess of meat from the king”; the NIV “Go down to your house and wash your feet” and “a gift from the king”; the NWT 1984 “Go down to your house and bathe your feet” and “the king’s courtesy gift”; the Geneva “the king sent a present after him”; the TLB “told him to go home and relax”.

The door of the king’s house. “And Uriah lay down at the door of the king’s house, with all the servants of his lord; and he did not go down to his house”: his lord is the king and his “lord Joab” (verse 11) is the one in the field; the Hebrew uses the same word for both, adon. The ASV has “slept at the door of the king’s house with all the servants of his lord”; the NWT 1984 “lay down at the entrance of the king’s house with all the other servants of his lord”; the NIV “slept at the entrance to the palace with all his master’s servants”; the TLB “at the gateway of the palace with the other servants of the king”. David is told, and asks: “Have you not come from a journey? Why did you not go down to your house?”

In booths. “The ark, and Israel, and Judah, are dwelling in booths; and my lord Joab and the servants of my lord are camped in the open field; and shall I go into my house, to eat and to drink and to lie with my wife? As you live, and as your soul lives, I will not do this thing”: the three verbs, eat, drink, lie with, are the ones David has just been doing and doing to him; “to lie with my wife” (lishkav im ishti) is the verb of verse 4. The booths are the booths of the festival, “You shall dwell in booths for seven days” (Leviticus 23:42, already on these pages), and the ark has gone out with the army, as against the king’s own “house of cedar” with the ark “within the curtain” (2 Samuel 7:2, already on these pages). “As your soul lives” stands in eleven verses. They include Hannah to Eli (1 Samuel 1:26), Abner to Saul (17:55), David to Jonathan (20:3), Abigail to David (25:26, all already on these pages), this verse, and six others (2 Samuel 14:19; 2 Kings 2:2, 4, 6; 4:30; Jeremiah 38:20). The ASV has “abide in booths”; the NWT 1984 “dwelling in booths”; the KJV “abide in tents”; the NIV “are staying in tents”; the Douay “dwell in tents”; the TLB “camping out in open fields”. The Greek has en skēnais, “in tents”.

He made him drunk. “Stay here today also, and tomorrow I will send you away … and he made him drunk; and in the evening he went out to lie on his bed with the servants of his lord, but to his house he did not go down”: shev, “stay”, is the root of David’s own “dwelling” in verse 1, and Uriah “stayed” a second day. The ASV has “he made him drunk” and “to lie on his bed with the servants of his lord”; the NIV “David made him drunk”; the NWT 1984 “he got him drunk”; the TLB “got him drunk”. Three times the king calls and three times Uriah does not go down, once at the door, once sober and once drunk.

Verses 14–17 · The letter “by the hand of Uriah” — “the strong battle”, and Joab’s men of valor

By the hand of Uriah. “And it happened in the morning that David wrote a letter to Joab; and he sent it by the hand of Uriah”: the phrase is the one of chapter 10, where David sent comforters “by the hand of his servants” (2 Samuel 10:2, already on these pages). The man carries his own sentence. A queen will later write letters in a king’s name against a man who is to be set “at the head of the people” and stoned (1 Kings 21:8–10). The NIV has “wrote a letter to Joab and sent it with Uriah”; the ASV “wrote a letter to Joab, and sent it by the hand of Uriah”; the Geneva “wrote a letter to Joab, and sent it by the hand of Uriah”; the Douay “wrote a letter to Joab: and sent it by the hand of Urias”; the TLB “gave it to Uriah to deliver”.

The strong battle. “Set Uriah in front of the strong battle, and turn back from behind him, that he may be struck down and die”: hachazaqah, “the strong”, is the root of Joab’s “Be strong, and let us be strong” in chapter 10 (2 Samuel 10:12) and of David’s “make your battle strong” in verse 25. The verb of the last clause is nikkah, “be struck”, the verb of “struck” through these wars, and here the stroke is arranged by withdrawing. The ASV has “Set ye Uriah in the forefront of the hottest battle, and retire ye from him, that he may be smitten, and die”; the NIV “Put Uriah out in front where the fighting is fiercest”; the NWT 1984 “in front of the heaviest battle charges”; the Douay “Set ye Urias in the front of the battle, where the fight is strongest”; the Geneva “Put ye Uriah in the forefront of the strength of the battle”; the TLB “pull back and leave him there to die!” The Greek has tou polemou tou krataiou, “the strong battle”.

Where he knew that valiant men were. “When Joab was watching the city, that he put Uriah at the place where he knew there were men of valor. And the men of the city came out and fought with Joab; and some of the people fell, some of David’s servants; and Uriah the Hittite also died”: Joab puts Uriah where the best defenders stand, anshei chayil, and Joab is the one of whom David has said that the sons of Zeruiah “are too hard for me” (2 Samuel 3:39, already on these pages). Others fall with Uriah; the Hebrew says “also”. The ASV has “when Joab kept watch upon the city, that he assigned Uriah unto the place where he knew that valiant men were”; the KJV “when Joab observed the city”; the NIV “while Joab had the city under siege, he put Uriah at a place where he knew the strongest defenders were”; the NWT 1984 “while Joab was keeping guard over the city”; the Douay “as Joab was besieging the city, he put Urias in the place where he knew the bravest men were”; the TLB “close to the besieged city where he knew that the enemies’ best men were fighting”. For the death the ASV has “and there fell some of the people, even of the servants of David; and Uriah the Hittite died also”; the NIV “some of the men in David’s army fell; moreover, Uriah the Hittite died”; the TLB “Uriah was killed along with several other Israeli soldiers”.

Verses 18–25 · Joab’s message — Abimelech at Thebez, “Do not let this thing be evil in your eyes”, and the sword that devours

If the king’s wrath rises. “And Joab sent and told David all the matters of the war; and he commanded the messenger, saying: ‘When you have finished telling the king all the matters of the war, it shall be that if the king’s wrath rises, and he says to you: “Why did you go near the city to fight? Did you not know that they would shoot from the wall?’”: Joab writes the king’s anger before it has come, and the words to put out the fire after it. Davar, “word, matter, thing”, runs through the chapter: “the matters of the war” in verses 18 and 19, “this thing” in verses 11 and 25, “the thing that David had done” in verse 27. The ASV has “if the king’s wrath arise” and “they would shoot from the wall”; the NIV “the king’s anger may flare up” and “they would shoot arrows from the wall”; the NWT 1984 “if the rage of the king comes up” and “they would shoot from on top of the wall”; the Douay “many darts are thrown from above off the wall”; the Geneva “hurl from the wall”.

Abimelech at Thebez. “Who struck Abimelech son of Jerubbesheth? Did not a woman throw an upper millstone on him from the wall, so that he died at Thebez?”: “an upper millstone” (pelach rekhev) stands in two verses. They are this one and Judges 9:53 (already on these pages), where “a certain woman threw an upper millstone on Abimelech’s head and cracked his skull”, and he called his armor-bearer to kill him “so that men will not say of me, A woman killed him” (9:54, already on these pages). Joab reaches for the best-known warning about going near a wall. “Jerubbesheth” stands in one verse; the Judges have Jerubbaal, Gideon, so that bosheth, “shame”, stands for baal, as it does in Ish-bosheth and Mephibosheth. The Douay has “Jerobaal” and the Greek Ierobaal. The ASV has “did not a woman cast an upper millstone upon him from the wall, so that he died at Thebez?”; the NWT 1984 “Was it not a woman that pitched an upper millstone upon him”; the NIV “Didn’t a woman drop an upper millstone on him from the wall”; the KJV “did not a woman cast a piece of a millstone upon him from the wall”; the Douay “did not a woman cast a piece of a millstone upon him from the wall”. The text draws no line from the woman on the wall to the woman on the roof; a reader may.

Dead also. “And the messenger went, and came and told David all that Joab had sent him for. And the messenger said to David: ‘The men prevailed against us, and came out to us into the field; and we were upon them as far as the entrance of the gate. And the shooters shot at your servants from the wall; and some of the king’s servants died; and your servant Uriah the Hittite is dead also’”: the messenger gives the report and not the speech about Abimelech; David’s anger does not rise, so the sentence is not needed. The ASV has “The men prevailed against us”; the NIV “The men overpowered us”; the NWT 1984 “The men proved superior to us”. For the shooters the ASV has “the shooters shot at thy servants from off the wall”; the NIV “the archers shot arrows at your servants from the wall”; the NWT 1984 “the shooters kept shooting at your servants from on top of the wall”; the Douay “the archers shot their arrows at thy servants”.

Do not let this thing be evil in your eyes. “Do not let this thing be evil in your eyes; for the sword devours in this way and in that. Make your battle strong against the city, and tear it down. And strengthen him”: “Do not let it be evil in your eyes” stands in two verses. They are God’s word to Abraham over Hagar and Ishmael, “Let it not be grievous in your eyes” (Genesis 21:12, already on these pages), and this one. “The sword devours” stands in three verses. They are Abner’s “Shall the sword devour for ever?” (2 Samuel 2:26, already on these pages), this one, and Nahum 2:14. The ASV and the KJV have “the sword devoureth one as well as another”, which gives the Hebrew kazeh vekazeh, “like this and like this”, its sense and loses its shape. The ASV has “Let not this thing displease thee, for the sword devoureth one as well as another”; the NIV “Don’t let this upset you; the sword devours one as well as another”; the NWT 1984 “Do not let this matter appear bad in your eyes, for the sword eats up one as well as another”; the Geneva “Let not this thing trouble thee”; the Douay “Let not this thing discourage thee: for various is the event of war”; the TLB “tell Joab not to be discouraged”. The Greek has mē ponēron estō en ophthalmois sou to rhēma touto, and the same words, ponēron … en ophthalmois, return in verse 27.

Verses 26–27 · “She mourned for her husband”, “gathered her into his house”, and “the thing … was evil in the eyes of Jehovah”

She mourned. “And the wife of Uriah heard that Uriah her husband was dead; and she mourned for her husband”: the Hebrew has two words for “husband”, ish, “her man”, and then ba’al, “her owner, her lord”; and she is not named Bathsheba here, but “the wife of Uriah”. The verb of her mourning, sapad, is the verb of David’s order at Abner’s death, “mourn before Abner” (2 Samuel 3:31, already on these pages). The ASV has “she made lamentation for her husband”; the KJV “she mourned for her husband”; the NIV “she mourned for him”; the NWT 1984 “wail over her owner”; the TLB “When Bathsheba heard that her husband was dead, she mourned for him”. The Greek has ekopsato, “she beat herself”.

Gathered her into his house. “And when the mourning had passed, David sent and gathered her into his house; and she became his wife, and bore him a son”: “And the mourning passed” stands in one verse. The verb of what David does with her is no longer “took” but asaf, “gathered”, the verb of Saul’s gathering any valiant man to himself (1 Samuel 14:52, already on these pages) and, in Habakkuk’s fishermen, of what is brought into the net (Habakkuk 1:15, already on these pages). The ASV has “David sent and took her home to his house”; the NIV “David had her brought to his house”; the NWT 1984 “David immediately sent and took her home to his house”; the Douay “David sent and brought her into his house”; the TLB “David sent for her and brought her to the palace”. The son is not named; the next chapter names Solomon (2 Samuel 12:24), after another son.

Evil in the eyes of Jehovah. “But the thing that David had done was evil in the eyes of Jehovah”: Jehovah is not named anywhere in the chapter until this last clause. “And the thing was evil” stands in three verses. They are Abraham’s grief at Sarah’s demand about Ishmael (Genesis 21:11), Samuel’s at the people’s demand for a king (1 Samuel 8:6, both already on these pages), and this one. It answers David’s own “Do not let this thing be evil in your eyes” in verse 25, with the same verb and the same noun, and the eyes are now Jehovah’s. The NWT 1984 has “appeared bad in the eyes of Jehovah”; the ASV “But the thing that David had done displeased Jehovah”; the KJV “But the thing that David had done displeased”; the NIV “But the thing David had done displeased”; the Douay “was displeasing to”; the Geneva “the thing that David had done, displeased”; the TLB “was very displeased with what David had done”. The Greek has ponēron ephanē to rhēma … en ophthalmois kyriou, “the thing appeared evil in the eyes of the Lord”. Nathan’s question will be “to do that which is evil in My sight” (2 Samuel 12:9); the book of Kings will keep one exception in David’s record, “save only in the matter of Uriah the Hittite” (1 Kings 15:5); and a psalm carries the occasion in its heading, “after he had gone in to Bathsheba” (Psalm 51:2, already on these pages).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “the messengers” as the Hebrew has it, with the Chronicles’ “kings” in the note; “ruined” and “laid siege”; “dwelling” and “stayed” for the root yashav; “sent” and “took”; “sanctifying herself”; “the welfare of” three times; “wash” in verses 2 and 8; “booths”; “the strong battle” and “make your battle strong”; “an upper millstone”; “Do not let this thing be evil in your eyes” and “the thing … was evil in the eyes of Jehovah”; “husband” for both ish and ba’al.

Echoes paid. The chapter before and its kindness (2 Samuel 9:7; 10:2, 12, 14, 17, 19); the king who takes (1 Samuel 8:6, 11, 13, 16); Rebekah’s beauty (Genesis 24:16; 26:7); the uncleanness of Leviticus 15 (15:19, 28); the booths of the festival (Leviticus 23:42); the ark within the curtain (2 Samuel 7:2); “as your soul lives” (1 Samuel 1:26; 17:55; 20:3; 25:26); Ahimelech the Hittite (1 Samuel 26:6); the sons of Zeruiah too hard for David (2 Samuel 3:39); the sword that devours (2 Samuel 2:26); mourning for Abner (2 Samuel 3:31); Abimelech and the woman on the wall (Judges 9:53–54); Hagar’s “grievous in your eyes” (Genesis 21:11–12); Saul’s gathering of the valiant (1 Samuel 14:52); and the heading of Psalm 51.

Echoes planted. The Chronicles’ account, which omits the affair (1 Chronicles 20:1); the Thirty and Eliam and Uriah (2 Samuel 23:34, 39; 1 Chronicles 11:41); Bath-shua daughter of Ammiel (1 Chronicles 3:5); Absalom’s tent on the roof (2 Samuel 16:22); Nathan’s “evil in My sight”, the sword that shall never depart, and Solomon’s birth (2 Samuel 12:9, 10, 24); the exception in David’s record (1 Kings 15:5); Jezebel’s letters and Naboth (1 Kings 21:8–10); the Aramean kings who return at the turn of the year (1 Kings 20:22, 26); “as your soul lives” again (2 Samuel 14:19). None of these is translated here yet.

Next in this book: 2 Samuel 12 — Jehovah sends Nathan to David, who tells of a rich man and a poor man’s one ewe lamb; “You are the man”; David says “I have sinned against Jehovah”, the child falls ill and dies, Solomon is born, and Joab takes the city of waters at Rabbah and sends for David to take the crown.
