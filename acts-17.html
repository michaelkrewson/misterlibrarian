Paul and Silas go through Amphipolis and Apollonia to Thessalonica, where Paul reasons from the Scriptures for three sabbaths until a mob drags Jason before the politarchs and the missionaries are sent out by night to Beroea; the Beroeans examine the Scriptures daily until the Thessalonians arrive, and Paul is taken to the sea and on to Athens, leaving Silas and Timothy behind. At Athens he argues in the synagogue and the marketplace, is brought to the Areopagus, and speaks from an altar ‘to an unknown god’ to a day, a man and a resurrection; some jeer, some will hear him again, and Dionysius and Damaris believe.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are ‘three sabbaths’ at Thessalonica (v2), ‘by night’ (v10), ‘daily’ at Beroea (v11), ‘every day’ in the Athenian marketplace (v17) and ‘another time’ (v32). It follows Acts 16 at Philippi; this library dates the events no more closely than the book does.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–4 · Διοδεύσαντες … Θεσσαλονίκην … κατὰ τὸ εἰωθὸς … ἐπὶ σάββατα τρία διελέξατο … διανοίγων καὶ παρατιθέμενος … ἔδει παθεῖν … προσεκληρώθησαν … σεβομένων Ἑλλήνων Diodeusantes … Thessalonikēn … kata to eiōthos … epi sabbata tria dielexato … dianoigōn kai paratithemenos … edei pathein … prosekklērōthēsan … sebomenōn Hellēnōn — Thessalonica: three sabbaths, Scriptures “opened” and “set out,” and the converts who were “allotted” to Paul and Silas

Through Amphipolis and Apollonia to Thessalonica (Acts 17:1). Diodeusantes, “having journeyed through,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and so do the names Amphipolis and Apollonia: Luke gives the two towns one clause and no stop, and says nothing of where the party slept or how far they went in a day. The road ends at Thessalonica, “where there was a synagogue of the Jews” — at Philippi there had been no such building, only a place where “we supposed there was a place of prayer” (Acts 16:13). The Greek reads ēn synagōgē, “there was a synagogue”; the Robinson-Pierpont text adds an article, ēn hē synagōgē, “the synagogue,” and Westcott-Hort, Tregelles and Nestle-Aland 28 read as this page does. The shelf follows them: KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 “a synagogue of the Jews,” NIV and TLB “a Jewish synagogue.” The congregation that begins here is the one Paul wrote to in 1 Thessalonians 1 and 2 Thessalonians 1.

“According to Paul’s custom,” and three sabbaths (Acts 17:2). Kata to eiōthos stands in two verses of the New Testament, both Luke’s: of Jesus, who “went in, as was his custom, on the Sabbath day into the synagogue” (Luke 4:16), and here, of Paul. Dielexato, “reasoned,” is the first use of dialegomai in Acts and the verb does not stand in Luke’s Gospel; it stands in thirteen verses of the New Testament, ten of them in Acts: here, at v17 below, and in eight verses from 18:4 to 24:25 (18:4 and 18:19 are on these pages, the other six not yet); the other three are Mark 9:34, Hebrews 12:5 and Jude 9. Epi sabbata tria says what Paul did on three sabbaths; it does not say that the visit lasted three weeks, and Paul himself writes that the Philippians sent to his need “even in Thessalonica” “both once and twice” (Philippians 4:16). KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “three sabbath days,” NIV “three Sabbath days,” NWT 1984 “three sabbaths,” TLB “three Sabbaths in a row.”

“Opening up” and “setting out” the Christ who “had to” suffer (Acts 17:3). Dianoigōn is the verb of Luke 24: the risen Jesus “was opening the Scriptures to us” on the road (Luke 24:32) and “opened their minds to understand the Scriptures” (24:45), and the Lord “opened” Lydia’s heart at Acts 16:14; here it is Paul who does the opening, and the thing opened is the Scriptures. Paratithemenos is the verb of setting a meal in front of people: at Luke 9:16 the disciples are given the loaves “to set before the crowd.” What Paul sets before the synagogue is a case: ton christon edei pathein kai anastēnai ek nekrōn, “that the Christ had to suffer and rise from the dead.” The edei, “had to,” is the word of Luke 24:26, “Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things?” (24:26), and the two infinitives, “suffer” and “rise from the dead,” are those of 24:46; Luke does not say which Scriptures Paul opened. KJV “Christ must needs have suffered,” Geneva “Christ must have suffered,” ASV “it behooved the Christ to suffer,” NWT 1984 “it was necessary for the Christ to suffer,” NIV “had to suffer,” but Douay “was to suffer” (no necessity) and the TLB “explaining the prophecies about the sufferings of the Messiah” (no “had to” at all). The last clause is not settled in print: Westcott-Hort and Nestle-Aland 28 read ho christos, ho Iēsous, “the Christ — the Jesus”; Tregelles leaves out the first article, and Robinson-Pierpont leave out the second (“the Christ Jesus”).

“Allotted” to Paul and Silas, and the worshiping Greeks (Acts 17:4). Prosekklērōthēsan stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. It is the passive of a verb built on klēros, “a lot,” the noun of the lots cast for Matthias (Acts 1:26); the Greek says that these converts “were allotted” to Paul and Silas, and does not say who did the allotting. KJV and ASV “consorted with,” NIV “joined,” NWT 1984 “associated themselves with,” Geneva “joined in company,” Douay “were associated to” — every one of them a verb the converts do or have done to them, never a lot. The Greeks are tōn sebomenōn Hellēnōn, “the worshiping Greeks,” the participle of 13:43 and 13:50 and of Lydia at 16:14, and it returns at v17 below; the women are tōn prōtōn, “the leading ones.” KJV and ASV “devout Greeks,” NIV “God-fearing Greeks,” NWT 1984 “Greeks who worshiped [God],” TLB “godly Greek men” (the Greek has no “men”); and for the women KJV and ASV “chief women,” NIV “prominent women,” NWT 1984 “principal women,” Douay “noble women.”

Verses 5–9 · ζηλώσαντες … ὀχλοποιήσαντες … τῇ οἰκίᾳ Ἰάσονος … πολιτάρχας … οἱ τὴν οἰκουμένην ἀναστατώσαντες … ὑποδέδεκται … τῶν δογμάτων Καίσαρος … βασιλέα ἕτερον … τὸ ἱκανὸν λαβόντες zēlōsantes … ochlopoiēsantes … tē oikia Iasonos … politarchas … hoi tēn oikoumenēn anastatōsantes … hypodedektai … tōn dogmatōn Kaisaros … basilea heteron … to hikanon labontes — a mob, the house of Jason, the politarchs, “turned the inhabited world upside down,” “another king,” and the security taken from Jason

Jealous, and a mob made to order (Acts 17:5). Zēlōsantes, “becoming jealous,” is the verb of Joseph’s brothers (Acts 7:9), and its noun is what filled the Jews at Pisidian Antioch when they saw the crowds (13:45, “filled with jealousy”): the cause Luke gives for the riot is the same one he gave there. Ochlopoiēsantes, “having formed a mob,” is a verb of “crowd” and “make” that stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the men they took along are tōn agoraiōn, “of the marketplace,” an adjective that stands only here and at Acts 19:38. KJV “lewd fellows of the baser sort,” ASV “vile fellows of the rabble,” Geneva “vagabonds and wicked fellows,” Douay “wicked men of the vulgar sort,” NIV “bad characters from the marketplace,” TLB “worthless fellows from the streets.” The apparatus has a split here: Robinson-Pierpont read Proslabomenoi de hoi Ioudaioi hoi apeithountes, “the Jews who would not be persuaded,” and have no zēlōsantes; the KJV and Geneva print “which believed not” and, in the same sentence, “moved with envy,” so they carry both. They seek Paul and Silas at Jason’s house; the Greek says that they wanted to bring them eis ton dēmon, “to the people,” the word for the people of a city gathered as one, and the word Luke uses of the crowd that shouted at Herod (12:22).

The politarchs, and what “turned the inhabited world upside down” says (Acts 17:6–8). Politarchas stands in two verses of the New Testament, 17:6 and 17:8, and nowhere else. It is the title Luke gives the city’s magistrates, and the library reports that it is known from inscriptions found in Thessalonica itself (see the Thessalonica entry; this page has not examined the stones). The charge is hoi tēn oikoumenēn anastatōsantes: anastatoō stands in three verses of the New Testament, here, in the question put to Paul at Acts 21:38 about the Egyptian who “stirred up a revolt,” and in Galatians 5:12 (neither of the last two yet on these pages); oikoumenē is “the inhabited world” of Luke 2:1, the one Caesar Augustus decreed should be registered, and of Acts 11:28. It is the word Paul will use of the judgment at v31 below: the accusers say that these men have upset the oikoumenē, and Paul tells the Athenians that God is about to judge it. KJV and ASV “turned the world upside down,” NWT 1984 “overturned the inhabited earth,” NIV “caused trouble all over the world,” TLB “turned the rest of the world upside down,” Geneva “subverted the state of the world,” and the Douay “set the city in an uproar,” which has no “world” in it. Etaraxan, “they troubled” (v8), is the verb of tarassontes, “troubling,” at v13, and the crowd and the politarchs are the two audiences it names here.

“Taken in,” “the decrees of Caesar,” and “another king” (Acts 17:7). Hypodedektai is the perfect of hypodechomai, “to receive as a guest,” and the verb is Luke’s for a house that takes Jesus in: Martha “welcomed him” (Luke 10:38) and Zacchaeus “received him joyfully” (19:6); in the New Testament it stands in those two verses, here, and in James 2:25 (not yet on these pages). Dogmatōn, “decrees,” is the noun of Luke 2:1 and of Acts 16:4, where it was the decrees of Jerusalem handed on to the cities; here it is Caesar’s, and Luke does not say which Caesar. Basilea heteron legontes einai Iēsoun, “saying that there is another king, Jesus,” is a charge Luke has heard before: the elders bring Jesus to Pilate “saying that he himself is Christ, a king” (Luke 23:2), and the Jewish leaders shout at Pilate that “Everyone who makes himself a king speaks against Caesar” (John 19:12). The Greek has the king as the new thing (heteron, “another”), and the order of the words is reversed in Robinson-Pierpont. KJV “do contrary to the decrees of Caesar,” ASV “act contrary to the decrees of Caesar,” NWT 1984 “act in opposition to the decrees of Caesar,” NIV “defying Caesar’s decrees,” Geneva “do against the decrees of Caesar,” TLB “guilty of treason,” which names a crime the Greek does not.

“The sufficient” from Jason (Acts 17:9). Labontes to hikanon, literally “taking the sufficient,” is a phrase of two verses in the New Testament: here and Mark 15:15, where Pilate, to hikanon poiēsai, “wishing to satisfy the crowd,” releases Barabbas. Commentators report that both are the Greek of Latin legal phrases for giving and taking security; this page has not checked the Latin. What the politarchs took, and in what form, Luke does not say. KJV “taken security of Jason,” ASV “taken security from Jason,” NWT 1984 “sufficient security from Jason,” Geneva “sufficient assurance of Jason,” Douay “taken satisfaction of Jason,” NIV “post bond,” TLB “posted bail.” Paul writes to this congregation of what it suffered (1 Thessalonians 1:6), and of how it “turned to God from the idols” (1:9).

Verses 10–15 · εὐγενέστεροι … ἀνακρίνοντες τὰς γραφὰς … εὐσχημόνων … σαλεύοντες … ἕως ἐπὶ τὴν θάλασσαν … ὑπέμεινάν τε ὅ τε Σιλᾶς καὶ ὁ Τιμόθεος … ἤγαγον ἕως Ἀθηνῶν eugenesteroi … anakrinontes tas graphas … euschēmonōn … saleuontes … heōs epi tēn thalassan … hypemeinan te ho te Silas kai ho Timotheos … ēgagon heōs Athēnōn — Beroea: “more noble,” the Scriptures examined daily, the Thessalonians who came to shake the crowds, the road to the sea, and Athens

“More noble”: Beroea and the daily examination (Acts 17:10–12). Beroea stands in three verses of the New Testament, 17:10, 17:13 and 20:4 (the last not yet on these pages). Eugenesteroi, “more noble,” is the comparative of eugenēs, “well-born”: the nobleman of the parable who “went to a distant country to obtain a kingdom” (Luke 19:12), and the “not many well-born” of 1 Corinthians 1:26; the comparative stands here alone. Luke does not make it a matter of birth. He defines it with the clause that follows: they received the word meta pasēs prothymias, “with all eagerness,” and they were anakrinontes tas graphas, “examining the Scriptures,” every day, ei echoi tauta houtōs, “to see whether these things were so.” Anakrinō is the verb of an inquiry, and in Luke-Acts its other uses are all of a court putting a person through one: Pilate, “having examined him in your presence” (Luke 23:14), Peter, “if we are being examined today” (Acts 4:9), Herod, who “examined the guards” (12:19), and two more at 24:8 and 28:18 (not yet on these pages). Here the thing put to the question is the Scripture, by the people Paul has just brought it to. KJV and Geneva “searched the scriptures daily,” ASV “examining the Scriptures daily,” NWT 1984 “carefully examining the Scriptures daily,” Douay “daily searching the scriptures,” NIV “examined the Scriptures every day.” For “more noble” KJV and ASV “more noble than,” NWT 1984 “more noble-minded than,” NIV “of more noble character than,” and the TLB “more open-minded,” which takes the noble out. The women of v12 are euschēmonōn, “of good standing,” the adjective used of the women at Pisidian Antioch (13:50) and of Joseph of Arimathea (Mark 15:43).

“Shaking and troubling the crowds” (Acts 17:13). The Thessalonian Jews come to Beroea saleuontes kai tarassontes tous ochlous. Saleuō, “to shake,” stands in four verses of Acts: David “shall not be shaken” (2:25), the place where the congregation prayed was shaken (4:31), the foundations of the prison at Philippi were shaken (16:26), and here, of a crowd; the earthquake that Luke told with no angel is the nearest picture of what these men do to a town. The apparatus: Robinson-Pierpont have no kai tarassontes, “and troubling.” KJV “stirred up the people” has one verb where ASV has “stirring up and troubling the multitudes,” Douay “stirring up and troubling the multitude,” NWT 1984 “incite and agitate the masses” and NIV “agitating the crowds and stirring them up.”

“As far as the sea,” Silas and Timothy, and the road to Athens (Acts 17:14–15). Heōs epi tēn thalassan is “as far as to the sea”; Robinson-Pierpont read hōs epi tēn thalassan, “as if to the sea,” and that is the reading behind the KJV and Geneva “as it were to the sea,” where ASV has “as far as to the sea,” NWT 1984 “as far as the sea,” NIV “to the coast” and TLB “on to the coast.” Luke does not say whether they went by sea or by land. Timothy was last named at 16:1; Luke names him in six verses of Acts, 16:1, 17:14, 17:15, 18:5, 19:22 and 20:4, and here he and Silas are left behind in Beroea while Paul goes on to Athens. His escorts are hoi kathistanontes, “those conducting,” and the command they carry back is that the two should come hōs tachista, “as quickly as possible” (v15); NIV “those who escorted Paul,” ASV “that conducted Paul,” TLB “those accompanying Paul.” Silas and Timothy next appear at Acts 18:5.

Verses 16–21 · παρωξύνετο τὸ πνεῦμα αὐτοῦ … κατείδωλον … ἐν τῇ ἀγορᾷ … τοῖς παρατυγχάνουσιν … Ἐπικουρείων καὶ Στοϊκῶν … σπερμολόγος … ξένων δαιμονίων … Ἄρειον Πάγον … καινὴ διδαχή … ξενίζοντα … καινότερον parōxyneto to pneuma autou … kateidōlon … en tē agora … tois paratynchanousin … Epikoureiōn kai Stoikōn … spermologos … xenōn daimoniōn … Areion Pagon … kainē didachē … xenizonta … kainoteron — Athens: a spirit provoked, a city full of idols, the marketplace, the philosophers, the “seed-picker,” “foreign divinities,” and the Areopagus

“Provoked” by a city “full of idols” (Acts 17:16). Parōxyneto is the verb of love that “does not sharpen into anger” (1 Corinthians 13:5), and its noun is the “sharp disagreement” that parted Paul and Barnabas (Acts 15:39); the verb and the noun together stand in four verses of the New Testament, the fourth being Hebrews 10:24 (not yet on these pages). Here the thing provoked is Paul’s own spirit. Kateidōlon stands in this verse alone: a compound of kata and eidōlon, “idol,” for which see eidōlon; “full of idols” is this page’s rendering of a word that means something like “down in idols.” ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 “full of idols,” but KJV “wholly given to idolatry,” Douay “wholly given to idolatry” and Geneva “subject to idolatry” turn the idols into an abstraction, and the TLB “all the idols he saw everywhere.” For the verb of the spirit: KJV “his spirit was stirred in him,” ASV “his spirit was provoked within him,” NWT 1984 “came to be irritated,” NIV “greatly distressed,” TLB “deeply troubled.”

Reasoning in the synagogue and in the marketplace (Acts 17:17). Dielegeto, “he was reasoning,” is the verb of v2, here in the imperfect: he is doing it daily. The Jews and tois sebomenois, “the worshipers,” get the synagogue (the participle of v4); the agora, the marketplace, gets tous paratynchanontas, “those who happened to be there,” a participle that stands in this verse alone. The marketplace is where Paul and Silas were dragged at Philippi (16:19), and where the men for the Thessalonian mob were found (v5). KJV “disputed he in the synagogue with the Jews,” ASV “reasoned in the synagogue,” NWT 1984 “began to reason in the synagogue,” NIV “reasoned in the synagogue.”

“Seed-picker” and “foreign divinities” (Acts 17:18). The Epicureans and the Stoics are named here and nowhere else in the New Testament, and Luke says nothing of what either school taught. Spermologos stands in this verse alone. Its two parts are sperma, “seed,” and a verb of gathering or speaking; the sense the lexicons give it is a bird that picks up seeds and so a man who picks up scraps of other men’s talk (reported, not checked on this page), and this page prints the picture. KJV, ASV and NIV “babbler,” NWT 1984 “chatterer,” Douay “word sower,” TLB “a dreamer.” The charge that follows is xenōn daimoniōn dokei katangeleus einai. Daimonion is the word of the Gospels for a hostile spirit, and this is the only verse of Acts where it stands; here the philosophers use it, as Greeks did, for a divine being, and the English shelf has to choose: KJV, ASV and Geneva “strange gods,” NIV “foreign gods,” NWT 1984 “foreign deities,” Douay “new gods,” TLB “foreign religion.” Paul’s own word at v22 shares its root. Katangeleus, “proclaimer,” stands in this verse alone and is the agent noun of the verb of v3 and v23; what he is proclaiming, Luke says, is “Jesus and the resurrection.”

The Areopagus, and a root of strangeness (Acts 17:19–21). Epi ton Areion Pagon is “to the Areopagus,” “the hill of Ares”; Luke does not say whether he means the hill or the council that sat there, and this page prints the name. Geneva “Mars’ street” gives it the Roman name of the god, NIV “a meeting of the Areopagus,” TLB “the forum at Mars Hill” (with a forum of its own), where KJV, ASV, Douay and NWT 1984 print “Areopagus” at v19, and the name is repeated at v22 below. Kainē didachē, “new teaching,” is the phrase of Mark 1:27, where the crowd says the same of Jesus in the synagogue (the Greek order there differs in Robinson-Pierpont). The strangeness of the Athenians’ own sentence is a family: xenōn (v18), xenizonta, “strange things” (v20), and xenoi, “foreigners” (v21), three words of one root in three verses; for the root see xenos. Xenizō is the verb of lodging a guest at 10:6, 10:18 and 10:32; here it is what is brought to the ear. The aside of v21 is Luke’s, not the Athenians’: they ēukairoun for nothing but “telling or hearing something newer,” and eukaireō stands in three verses of the New Testament, Mark 6:31 (no leisure even to eat), here, and 1 Corinthians 16:12 (not yet on these pages). The comparative kainoteron, “newer,” is the only one of its kind in the New Testament. NIV “the latest ideas,” TLB “the latest new ideas,” KJV and ASV “some new thing,” Geneva “some news,” NWT 1984 “something new.”

Verses 22–25 · Ἄνδρες Ἀθηναῖοι … δεισιδαιμονεστέρους … τὰ σεβάσματα … βωμὸν … Ἀγνώστῳ θεῷ … ὃ … ἀγνοοῦντες εὐσεβεῖτε … ὁ θεὸς ὁ ποιήσας τὸν κόσμον … χειροποιήτοις ναοῖς … θεραπεύεται … προσδεόμενός τινος … διδοὺς πᾶσι ζωὴν καὶ πνοήν Andres Athēnaioi … deisidaimonesterous … ta sebasmata … bōmon … Agnōstō theō … ho … agnoountes eusebeite … ho theos ho poiēsas ton kosmon … cheiropoiētois naois … therapeuetai … prosdeomenos tinos … didous pasi zōēn kai pnoēn — “very much given to the fear of divinities,” the altar “to an unknown god,” and the God who is not served by hands

“Very much given to the fear of divinities” (Acts 17:22). Andres Athēnaioi, “men of Athens,” is how Paul begins; at Lystra it was andres, “Men, why are you doing these things?” (14:15). Deisidaimonesterous is a comparative, and an adjective that stands in this verse alone; the noun deisidaimonia stands at Acts 25:19 (not yet on these pages), and both are built from deidō, “to fear,” and daimōn, the root of the daimonia of v18. The Greek can be praise (“unusually reverent”) or blame (“over-anxious about the powers”), and Luke does not say which Paul means; this page prints the two roots in English and decides nothing. KJV, Geneva and Douay “too superstitious,” ASV “very religious,” NIV “very religious,” TLB “very religious,” NWT 1984 “more given to the fear of the deities than others are.” The place is named again in this verse, and the KJV turns to “Mars’ hill” here, the Geneva to “Mars’ street” and the NIV to “the meeting of the Areopagus.”

“To an unknown god” (Acts 17:23). Sebasmata, “the things you revere,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 2 Thessalonians 2:4 (not yet on these pages), and bōmos, “altar,” in this verse alone (the altars of Israel are another word). The inscription is Agnōstō theō, with no article: “to an unknown god.” ASV “TO AN UNKNOWN GOD,” NIV “to an unknown god,” NWT 1984 “To an Unknown God,” but KJV “TO THE UNKNOWN GOD,” Geneva “UNTO THE UNKNOWN GOD,” Douay “To the unknown God” and TLB “To the Unknown God” — four of the seven put in the article, and the capital that goes with it. The Greek writer Pausanias, in his tour of Greece, names “altars of gods called Unknown” (bōmoi … theōn … onomazomenōn Agnōstōn) at Phaleron, the harbour of Athens, and “an altar of unknown gods” at Olympia (Pausanias 1.1.4 and 5.14.8, read in the Greek); both are plural, and Luke’s inscription is singular. The relative pronoun is the next problem: ho oun agnoountes eusebeite, touto egō katangellō is neuter, “what therefore you revere without knowing it, this I proclaim,” and Robinson-Pierpont read the masculine, hon … touton, “whom … him.” The shelf splits on it: ASV “What therefore ye worship in ignorance, this I set forth unto you,” Douay “What therefore you worship, without knowing it, that I preach to you,” NIV “the very thing you worship,” NWT 1984 “what YOU are unknowingly giving godly devotion to” are neuter; KJV “Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you,” Geneva “Whom ye then ignorantly worship, him show I unto you” and TLB “You have been worshiping him without knowing who he is” are masculine. One root runs through the speech: agnōstō and agnoountes here, and agnoias, “of ignorance,” at v30. Eusebeō stands in two verses, here and 1 Timothy 5:4 (not yet on these pages).

The God who made the world, and the hands that do not serve him (Acts 17:24–25). Two earlier sentences in Acts name God as the one who made everything, and neither has this wording: the prayer of the congregation says “you who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and everything in them” (4:24), Paul at Lystra says “a living God, who made the heaven and the earth and the sea and all that is in them” (14:15), and here it is ho poiēsas ton kosmon kai panta ta en autō, “who made the world and all the things in it,” with kosmos, a word that stands in this verse alone in Acts. Cheiropoiētois naois katoikei, “does not dwell in handmade temples,” has the adjective and the verb of Stephen’s “the Most High does not dwell in things made with hands” (7:48); Stephen said it to the council in Jerusalem, and Paul says it with the Athenians’ own temples in view. Therapeuetai, “is attended to,” is the verb of healing everywhere else in Acts (4:14, 5:16, 8:7, and 28:9, not yet on these pages), and here the one verse where it means to serve; prosdeomenos, “needing,” stands in this verse alone. KJV and Geneva “worshipped with men’s hands,” ASV “served by men’s hands,” Douay “served with men’s hands,” NWT 1984 “attended to by human hands,” NIV “served by human hands,” TLB “human hands can’t minister to his needs.” The giver is didous, “giving” — the participle of Lystra, where he “did good, giving you rains from heaven” (14:17) — and what he gives is zōēn kai pnoēn kai ta panta, “life and breath and all things”; Robinson-Pierpont read kata panta, “in all respects,” for the last two words.

Verses 26–29 · ἐξ ἑνὸς πᾶν ἔθνος … ὁρίσας προστεταγμένους καιροὺς καὶ τὰς ὁροθεσίας … ζητεῖν … ψηλαφήσειαν … ἐν αὐτῷ γὰρ ζῶμεν καὶ κινούμεθα καὶ ἐσμέν … τινες τῶν καθʼ ὑμᾶς ποιητῶν … Τοῦ γὰρ καὶ γένος ἐσμέν … τὸ θεῖον … χαράγματι ex henos pan ethnos … horisas prostetagmenous kairous kai tas horothesias … zētein … psēlaphēseian … en autō gar zōmen kai kinoumetha kai esmen … tines tōn kath’ hymas poiētōn … Tou gar kai genos esmen … to theion … charagmati — “from one,” the appointed times and boundaries, groping for God, “in him we live,” the poets, and “offspring”

“From one” — one what? (Acts 17:26). Epoiēsen te ex henos pan ethnos anthrōpōn: “he made from one every nation of humankind.” The Greek has henos, “one,” and no noun, and the apparatus shows the gap being filled: Robinson-Pierpont add haimatos, “blood,” and Westcott-Hort, Tregelles and Nestle-Aland 28 do not. The shelf fills it five ways. KJV “of one blood” and Geneva “of one blood” carry the added word; NIV “From one man” and NWT 1984 “out of one [man]” supply a man; the TLB “from one man, Adam” names him, which the Greek does not; and ASV “of one every nation” and Douay “of one, all mankind” leave the gap open, as this page does. Next, horisas prostetagmenous kairous kai tas horothesias tēs katoikias autōn: horothesias, “boundaries,” stands in this verse alone, and the verb horizō that gives it its root returns at v31 (“a man whom he appointed”). Luke does not say whether the kairoi are the seasons of the year or periods of history: at Lystra the same word was the “fruitful seasons” God gives (14:17), and here it is something “appointed.” NIV “appointed times in history,” NWT 1984 “appointed times,” Douay “appointed times,” ASV “appointed seasons,” Geneva “seasons which were ordained.”

“Grope” for God, and “not far” (Acts 17:27). Zētein ton theon ei ara ge psēlaphēseian auton kai heuroien: the infinitive of purpose, and then two optatives, “if perhaps they might grope for him and find him.” Psēlaphaō stands in four verses of the New Testament: Luke 24:39, where the risen Jesus says “Touch me and see,” here, Hebrews 12:18 (not yet on these pages) and 1 John 1:1. The verb of the disciples touching a body is the verb of the nations reaching for God, “though indeed he is not far from each one of us.” The apparatus has kyrion, “the Lord,” for theon in Robinson-Pierpont, and that is the word in the KJV “seek the Lord” and Geneva “seek the Lord,” where ASV, NIV, NWT 1984 and Douay have “God.” ASV “feel after him,” KJV “feel after him,” NWT 1984 “grope for him,” NIV “reach out for him,” TLB “feel their way toward him.”

“In him we live,” and the poets (Acts 17:28). En autō gar zōmen kai kinoumetha kai esmen: “in him we live and move and exist.” Then comes the one place in the speech where Paul names a source: hōs kai tines tōn kath’ hymas poiētōn eirēkasin, “as some of the poets among you have said,” Tou gar kai genos esmen, “For we are also his offspring.” Poiētēs, “maker,” is “poet” only here; it is also the “doers” of Romans 2:13 and of James 1:22, 1:23 and 1:25, and of James 4:11 (not yet on these pages). Paul says “poets” in the plural. The line is the fifth line of the Phaenomena of Aratus, read here in the Greek: tou gar kai genos eimen, the same words as Luke’s except that the poet has eimen where Luke has esmen. A Stoic hymn of Cleanthes is reported to have a close phrase, ek sou gar genos esmen; this page has not opened that poem. The first half of the verse is not attributed by Luke to anyone: the note on Titus 1 reports a guess that it is a Cretan poet’s (Titus 1), and that is a guess. Genos, “offspring,” is the word of family and birth in Acts (“of the family of Abraham,” 13:26); KJV, ASV, Douay and NIV “offspring,” NWT 1984 “progeny,” Geneva “generation,” TLB “sons of God.”

“The divine,” and what gold and silver cannot be (Acts 17:29). To theion, “the divine,” is the neuter adjective used as a noun, “the divine,” and it has that sense in this verse alone (the same letters are “sulphur” at Luke 17:29, and the adjective theios is at 2 Peter 1:3 and 1:4, not yet on these pages). The conclusion is drawn from the quotation: genos oun hyparchontes tou theou ouk opheilomen nomizein, “being then the offspring of God, we ought not to suppose.” Charagmati technēs kai enthymēseōs anthrōpou is “an engraving of craft and human thought”: the thing made is shaped by skill (technē) and by a person’s own thinking (enthymēsis). KJV, ASV and Geneva “the Godhead,” Douay “the divinity,” NWT 1984 “the Divine Being,” NIV “the divine being.”

Verses 30–34 · τοὺς χρόνους τῆς ἀγνοίας ὑπεριδών … παραγγέλλει … μετανοεῖν … ἔστησεν ἡμέραν … ἐν ἀνδρὶ ᾧ ὥρισεν … πίστιν παρασχών … ἀνάστασιν νεκρῶν … ἐχλεύαζον … ἐκ μέσου αὐτῶν … κολληθέντες … Διονύσιος ὁ Ἀρεοπαγίτης … Δάμαρις tous chronous tēs agnoias hyperidōn … paraggellei … metanoein … estēsen hēmeran … en andri hō hōrisen … pistin parascheōn … anastasin nekrōn … echleuazon … ek mesou autōn … kollēthentes … Dionysios ho Areopagitēs … Damaris — times of ignorance overlooked, a command to repent, a day, a man, “assurance,” the resurrection of the dead, and who stayed

“Overlooked,” and a command to repent (Acts 17:30). Hyperidōn, “overlooking,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. It is the sentence that answers Lystra, where God “in the generations gone by let all the nations go in their own ways” (14:16), and Peter’s “I know that you acted in ignorance” (3:17); the root of “ignorance,” agnoia, is the root of the altar’s Agnōstō at v23. KJV “winked at,” Douay “having winked at,” Geneva “regarded not,” ASV “overlooked,” NIV “overlooked,” NWT 1984 “has overlooked,” TLB “tolerated.” The verb of what God now does is a point of the apparatus: Tregelles, Nestle-Aland 28 and Robinson-Pierpont read paraggellei, “commands,” and Westcott-Hort read apaggellei, “announces.” Paraggellō is the verb of the order that the council gave the apostles (5:28) and of Paul’s order to the spirit in the servant girl (16:18); this is the same verb with God as the one giving it. KJV “commandeth,” ASV “commandeth,” NIV “commands,” TLB “commands,” NWT 1984 “is telling,” Douay “declareth” (which gives the sense of apaggellei), Geneva “admonisheth.” The metanoein, “to repent,” is metanoia, and the Douay prints it “do penance.”

“A man whom he appointed,” and the “assurance” (Acts 17:31). Estēsen hēmeran en hē mellei krinein tēn oikoumenēn en dikaiosynē en andri hō hōrisen: “he has set a day on which he is going to judge the inhabited world in righteousness by a man whom he appointed.” Three words come back. Oikoumenēn is the world the accusers said was overturned (v6). Hōrisen is the verb of horisas at v26 (“having fixed”), and the same verb is Peter’s “the one determined by God as judge of living and dead” (10:42), and of the “determined plan” at 2:23 and the disciples who “determined” to send relief (11:29). The speech has not named the man: from v22 to v31 it does not say “Jesus” or “Christ.” Pistin parascheōn pasin is the hard phrase: pistis is “faith,” and here it is something God provides to all, with the raising from the dead as the thing provided; the shelf has to turn it into something else. KJV, ASV and Geneva “assurance,” NIV “proof,” NWT 1984 “guarantee,” Douay “giving faith to all,” TLB “pointed him out.” This page prints “assurance.”

“A resurrection of the dead,” and the jeer (Acts 17:32–33). Anastasin nekrōn, with no article, is the phrase the Athenians hear; it is the phrase, in the genitive, of Acts 23:6, 24:21 and 26:23 (not yet on these pages) and of Romans 1:4. Echleuazon, “they began to jeer,” stands in this verse and, as diachleuazontes, at 2:13, where Pentecost’s crowd says the apostles are full of new wine. KJV, ASV and Douay “mocked,” NIV “sneered,” NWT 1984 “mock,” TLB “laughed.” The others say akousometha sou peri toutou kai palin, “we will hear you about this again”: KJV “We will hear thee again of this matter,” NIV “We want to hear you again on this subject,” NWT 1984 “We will hear you about this even another time,” TLB “We want to hear more about this later.” Luke does not say that any of them came back. Paul exēlthen ek mesou autōn, “went out from the midst of them”; at v22 he had stood en mesō, “in the midst” of the Areopagus.

Dionysius and Damaris (Acts 17:34). Kollēthentes, “joined,” is the verb of the “join himself to or approach one of another tribe” that Peter said is unlawful (10:28), of Philip told to “join” the chariot (8:29) and of Saul trying to join the disciples at Jerusalem (9:26); in Acts it stands in five verses, those and 5:13 and here. Dionysios ho Areopagitēs is a name and a title that stand in this verse alone, and so is Damaris: Luke names two of “others,” one man and one woman, and gives the man the title of the place where Paul spoke. KJV and ASV “Dionysius the Areopagite,” Geneva “Dionysius Areopagite,” Douay “Dionysius, the Areopagite,” NIV “a member of the Areopagus,” NWT 1984 “a judge of the court” of the Areopagus, TLB “a member of the City Council” — the last three decide, for the reader, that the Areopagus is a court or a council. Writings of much later date were put under this man’s name; that is reported here and has not been checked, and nothing in Luke connects him to them.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “reasoned” for dialegomai at vv2 and 17, so that the verb that begins the chapter is the verb in the marketplace; “proclaim” for katangellō at vv3 and 23, so the Christ of the synagogue and the unknown god of the altar are announced with one verb; “allotted” for prosekklērōthēsan at v4, and not “joined”; “politarchs” at vv6 and 8, a title and not a translation; “the inhabited world” for oikoumenē at vv6 and 31, so the world that is said to be overturned is the world that is to be judged; “foreign divinities” for xenōn daimoniōn at v18 and “the fear of divinities” for deisidaimonesterous at v22, two words of one root; “Areopagus” at vv19, 22 and 34, with no hill and no court added; “from one” at v26, with no noun; “appointed times” and “boundaries” at v26 and “appointed” at v31, one verb; “assurance” for pistis at v31.

Echoes paid. “According to his custom” — Luke 4:16, at v2. The idols the Thessalonians left — 1 Thessalonians 1:9, at v16. The likeness of “corruptible man” — Romans 1:23, at v29. “Opening,” and the suffering Christ who “had to” — Luke 24:32, 24:45 and 24:26, at v3. The decrees of Caesar — Luke 2:1 and Acts 16:4, at v7. The charge of another king — Luke 23:2 and John 19:12, at v7. “Not made with hands” — 7:48, at v24. God as the giver — 14:17, at v25. “Times of ignorance” — 14:16 and 3:17, at v30. The judge who was “determined” — 10:42, at v31. The jeer at Pentecost — 2:13, at v32.

Echoes planted. Timothy and Silas, at 18:5, and Beroea, at 20:4. The verb “reasoned,” at 18:4, 18:19, 19:8, 19:9, 20:7, 20:9, 24:12 and 24:25. The marketplace, at 19:38. “Resurrection of the dead”, at 23:6, 24:21 and 26:23.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether “politarchs” were found on stones in Thessalonica, which the library reports. What “the sufficient” of v9 was. Whether the Areopagus of vv19, 22 and 34 is a hill or a council. What the philosophers took “Jesus and the resurrection” to be. Whether “seed-picker” means a gleaner of scraps, as the lexicons report. Where Pausanias’s altars stood, and whether Luke’s was one of them. Which poets are meant in v28. Whether the first half of v28 quotes anyone. Whether the “Dionysius” of v34 had any part in the works later written in his name.
