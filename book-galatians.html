📖 Galatians

Πρὸς Γαλάτας (Pros Galatas) — 'To the Galatians.' ⚠ And the name is a genuine puzzle, because 'Galatia' meant two different things at once: the ETHNIC region of central Asia Minor settled by GAULS — Celtic tribes who migrated east in the 3rd century BC, so 'Galatians' are literally Gauls in Anatolia — and the larger Roman PROVINCE of that name reaching south to the towns of Paul's first journey. Which one he means decides the letter's date, and the question has never been settled.

The angriest letter in the New Testament, and the one that decided what Christianity would be — a short, furious defense of the claim that Gentiles are made right with God through faith and not by becoming Jews first. Luther called it 'my epistle, to which I am betrothed'.

Chapter by chapter

Galatians 1The angriest letter in the New Testament, and you can hear it in what is MISSING. Every other letter Paul wrote opens with a paragraph of thanks for its readers — even the exasperating Corinthians got one. This one says “grace to you and peace,” and then, where the thanks should stand, drops straight into “I am astonished that you are so quickly deserting…” A first-century reader would feel the cold the instant the courtesy did not come. Rival missionaries had followed Paul into his congregations in Galatia teaching that Gentile converts must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to belong fully to God — and, evidently, that Paul’s own authority was second-hand, borrowed from the apostles in Jerusalem. So the letter opens by denying that twice in its first six words (“an apostle — not FROM men nor THROUGH a man”), pronounces a curse on anyone preaching a different gospel — including himself, including “an angel from heaven,” and says it twice — and then spends the rest of the chapter proving its independence with a travelogue: after the revelation he did not go to Jerusalem, he went to Arabia; three years later he visited Cephas for a fortnight and met no other apostle but James the Lord’s brother; the Judean congregations had never even seen his face. ⚠ And the chapter that begins in fury ends in something else entirely — the churches he had once tried to destroy repeating a rumour about him, “the one who once persecuted us is now announcing the faith he once tried to destroy,” and glorifying God because of it.

About the book

Where it sits
The NINTH book of the New Testament and the fourth of Paul's letters. Its influence is out of all proportion to its six chapters: it is the shortest full statement of justification by faith, the seed-form of Romans, the text of Luther's 1535 commentary and of the Reformation's central claim, and the source of 'the fruit of the Spirit' and of 'neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, no male and female — for you are all one in Christ Jesus' (3:28).
Genre
A LETTER, and the most polemical Paul wrote — the only one with NO THANKSGIVING; where every other letter thanks God for its readers, this one goes straight from 'grace to you' to 'I am astonished.' It follows the shape of a courtroom defense: charge, autobiography as evidence, scriptural argument, and the practical consequence.
Author
PAUL — uncontested, and unusually personal: he takes the pen himself at the end, remarking on his own handwriting ('see with what large letters I am writing to you with my own hand', 6:11), which suggests either poor eyesight or emphasis, and possibly both.
Date written
⚠ Either c. AD 48 or the mid-50s, and the gap is the sharpest unresolved date question in Paul. On the SOUTH-GALATIAN view (the Roman province) the letter is addressed to the congregations of the first journey and can be Paul's EARLIEST surviving letter, written just before the Jerusalem council of Acts 15 — which would explain why he never cites the council's decision, the one piece of evidence that would have settled his argument outright. On the NORTH-GALATIAN view (the ethnic Celtic highlands) it belongs beside Romans, whose argument it anticipates in rougher, hotter form.
Place
Unknown — Antioch, Ephesus, Corinth and Macedonia have all been proposed, depending on the date.
Audience
Congregations Paul founded, into which rival Jewish-Christian missionaries — the 'circumcision party', traditionally called JUDAIZERS — had come teaching that Gentile believers must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to belong fully to God's people. They appear to have argued that Paul's authority was second-hand, derived from and subordinate to the Jerusalem apostles, which is why the letter opens by denying it twice in its first sentence.

How it's laid out

1:1–10No thanksgiving — 'I am astonished'; a different gospel that is no gospel; the double anathema.
1:11–2:21Autobiography as evidence: the revelation, Arabia, the fifteen-day visit to Cephas, the Jerusalem agreement, and the public confrontation at Antioch.
3:1–4:31The argument from scripture: Abraham believed and it was counted to him; the law as guardian until faith came; sons and not slaves; Hagar and Sarah.
5:1–6:10Freedom, and what it is for — 'for freedom Christ set us free'; the works of the flesh and the fruit of the Spirit; bearing one another's burdens.
6:11–18The postscript in Paul's own large handwriting — the cross, the new creation, and the marks of Jesus on his body.

What it's about

  • JUSTIFICATION BY FAITH, stated for the first time and in the heat of a real fight — 'a person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ' (2:16).
  • FREEDOM — the letter's own word for what is at stake; 'for freedom Christ set us free; stand firm, then, and do not be burdened again by a yoke of slavery' (5:1).
  • The ONE GOSPEL, against which every 'different' one is measured, and under whose curse even an angel would fall.
  • GENTILE INCLUSION without conversion to Judaism — the practical question the whole letter exists to settle, and the reason Christianity did not remain a sect within Judaism.
  • SPIRIT against FLESH — introduced here as the pair that structures chapter 5, and Paul's answer to the charge that freedom from law means moral chaos.
  • ONE IN CHRIST — 'neither Jew nor Greek, neither slave nor free, no male and female' (3:28), the most socially explosive sentence Paul ever wrote.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. Galatians is well attested — P46 (c. AD 200) carries it nearly complete — and chapter 1 is textually calm. The letter's Greek is the roughest Paul wrote: sentences break off, the syntax outruns itself, and 2:14-21 slides from a quoted rebuke into an argument without ever marking where the quotation stops (this translation, like most, leaves that seam unmarked, because the Greek does).

Looking forward

Galatians' Christ is defined by what he ACHIEVED and by what that makes unnecessary. He 'gave himself for our sins to rescue us out of the present evil age' (1:4) — the opening sentence, and salvation framed as extraction rather than improvement. He is the one in whom Paul was crucified so that 'it is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me' (2:20); the one who 'became a curse for us' by hanging on a tree (3:13); the seed to whom the promises to Abraham were made; and the Son sent 'when the fullness of time had come, born of a woman, born under law' (4:4). The letter's whole force is that if righteousness came through law, 'then Christ died for nothing' (2:21).

Where the debates are

WHICH GALATIA, and therefore the DATE — the sharpest unresolved chronological question in Paul, and it decides whether this is his first letter or a late one. How Galatians 2:1-10 relates to the Jerusalem council of Acts 15 (the same meeting told from inside, an earlier visit, or two irreconcilable accounts). Whether pistis Christou (2:16) is faith IN Christ or the faithfulness OF Christ — the same question as Romans 3:22. Where the quotation of Paul's rebuke to Peter stops. And what the 'works of the law' actually denotes: moral effort in general, or specifically the boundary-markers — circumcision, food laws, sabbath — that separated Jew from Gentile, which is the 'New Perspective' reading. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
