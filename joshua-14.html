Eleazar the priest and Joshua divide the west-bank land by lot, nine and a half tribes' worth, with the Levites again given no territorial share. Before the lot is actually cast, Caleb -- eighty-five years old -- comes to Joshua at Gilgal and claims Hebron directly, on the strength of a forty-five-year-old oath from Moses: he was forty when sent to spy the land, and has 'filled up after Jehovah' ever since, unlike the ten brothers whose report made the people's heart melt. Joshua blesses him and grants it outright, the one inheritance in the whole division never called 'by lot'. The chapter closes on the land's rest from war.
A legal/administrative chapter like 13, giving its own numbers rather than a narrated day: 45 years since the spies' report (Numbers 13-14), itself 40 years before the Jordan crossing's own anniversary point -- so Caleb's arithmetic implies roughly seven years of active conquest between the crossing and this scene, a span the narrative itself never states as a figure. It sits between the Transjordan recap of chapter 13 and the actual lot-casting for Judah that opens chapter 15. Same contested early/late-date question as the rest of the conquest (c. 1406 BC on the early date this project foregrounds, c. 1230-1210 BC on the late).

Verses 1–5 · The division formula, Eleazar's first appearance in the whole book, and a bookkeeping sentence that finally explains itself

★★★ Eleazar is named before Joshua, exactly as the roster that appointed them both said he would be — and this is his first appearance by name anywhere in the book of Joshua. Numbers 34:17 (already on these pages) named ‘Eleazar the priest, and Joshua the son of Nun’ as the two men who would divide the land, in that order, and this project’s own Eleazar entry has carried the observation since it was written that the order is not deference: the conquest's own general is named second to the priest who will draw the lot. A consonantal search for his name across the whole book turns up nothing in chapters 1–13 — every battle in this book was fought without him on the page at all — and he steps into the text for the first time exactly where Numbers always meant him to: at the land's division, not its war.

★★ V2's ‘by the lot’ reports a result; the verb that actually performs it is still one chapter away. Be-goral, ‘by lot,’ is this project's own goral dictionary entry's standing word for the instrument Joshua 13:6 (now on these pages) already commanded into use with the causative ‘cause it to fall,’ hapfileha — but that casting verb, vayehi ha-goral, ‘and the lot fell,’ does not appear until the very next chapter opens Judah's own district list. This chapter sits between the command and the casting: a summary of a decision already made, before the narrative actually shows the lot in the priest's hand.

★★ The Levites' landlessness is stated a fourth time in two chapters — and only now does the text explain the arithmetic. Joshua 13:14 and 13:33 (now on these pages) already said twice that Levi has no inheritance because Jehovah is his inheritance; vv3–4 here say it again, but for the first time add the bookkeeping that makes the tribal count come out even: ‘the sons of Joseph were two tribes, Manasseh and Ephraim,’ so one tribe's absence (Levi, stepping out) is exactly offset by another's doubling (Joseph, splitting in two) — twelve shares of land for thirteen tribal names, every time this book does the sum.

★ V5 opens with the same compliance formula this book has used of the war, now applied to the peace. Ka'asher tsivvah Yehovah et-Mosheh, ken…, ‘just as Jehovah commanded Moses, so…,’ is the same opening half of the sentence Joshua 11:15 (now on these pages) used to certify Joshua's own obedience through the whole war — there a three-link chain, Jehovah to Moses to Joshua; here, a shorter form said of the whole nation dividing land rather than one man waging it, the identical hinge-phrase outliving the campaign it first certified.

Verses 6–12 · Caleb's speech — the third place that calls him ‘the Kenizzite,’ an idiom this translation has kept whole since Numbers, and a report quoted word for word forty-five years later

★★★ V6 is the second, and v14 the third, of only three places in the whole Hebrew Bible that call Caleb ‘the Kenizzite’ — and this project's own Numbers 32 page already counted them in advance. Searched consonantally, the exact phrase ‘the Kenizzite’ stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: Numbers 32:12 (already on these pages), the first of the three that name Caleb and the one whose own closing note promised these two would follow; the two here; and Genesis 15:19 (already on these pages), where the identical gentilic names one of the ten peoples promised to Abram's descendants — a whole nation sharing its name with the loyalist who, by this project's own Caleb entry, may himself be descended from it rather than from Israel at all.

★★★ ‘Filled up after Jehovah’ is said a third time in this one short speech, exactly as this translation has kept it since Numbers — and the chain the idiom runs through ends, centuries later, in its own negation. Mille acharei Yehovah is the whole phrase this project's own Numbers 14:24 page chose to keep literal rather than smooth into ‘wholly followed’ — Jehovah's own description of Caleb alone, at the moment of the ten spies' bad report. Numbers 32:12 repeats the identical two words of Caleb and Joshua together, decades later, and this chapter repeats them three more times of Caleb alone — his own mouth at v8 (‘I filled up after’), Moses' oath quoted in his mouth at v9 (‘you have filled up after’), and the narrator's own voice at v14 (‘he filled up after’). ⚠ Deuteronomy 1:36's own use of the identical two Hebrew words breaks this translation's own pattern — that page renders it ‘has fully followed’ rather than keeping the filling image whole, the one link in this chain where this project's own practice does not agree with itself; this chapter does not go back and fix that page, only states the difference plainly. ⚠ The idiom's last word in the Hebrew Bible is a negation: 1 Kings 11:6 (fetched directly; not yet on these pages) writes that Solomon lo mille acharei Yehovah, ke-David aviv, ‘did not fill up after Jehovah, as David his father’ — the identical verb and preposition this chapter uses three times of Caleb, now with a bare lo, ‘not,’ in front of it, the one explicit failure of an idiom this book has only ever used as praise.

★★ V8 does not summarize the old accusation against the ten spies — it reuses the very verb, of the very men, that first made it. Himsiu et-lev ha'am, ‘made the heart of the people melt,’ is the Hiphil, causative form of masas — the same causative Deuteronomy 1:28 (already on these pages) uses for the identical accusation in Moses' own mouth, ‘our brothers have made our heart melt, saying: the people is greater and taller than we… and moreover we have seen the sons of the Anakim there.’ Caleb is not reporting a general memory; he is naming, forty-five years later and with the same verb, exactly what his ten colleagues did — now calling them by the plain word ‘brothers’ rather than any harsher one. The Anakim themselves migrate with the sentence: the detail that frightened the whole camp at Deuteronomy 1:28 is the same people Caleb now asks to fight at v12, by name, forty-five years older.

⚠ Caleb's own arithmetic yields a number the book's narrative never once states outright. V10's forty-five years, read against Deuteronomy 2:14's (already on these pages) explicit thirty-eight years from Kadesh-barnea to the crossing of the brook Zered, leaves a remainder of roughly seven years unaccounted for between Israel's entry across the Jordan and this very scene — the length of the whole war this book has just finished narrating, nowhere given a figure of its own. This is an inference built from two stated numbers, not a third number the text supplies; conservative chronologies have long read it this way, and this translation states the arithmetic without promoting it past what it is.

★ V7's ‘as it was in my heart’ is Caleb's own description of the very report this project's own Caleb entry already credits him with. He does not claim the ten spies lied about what they saw — Numbers 13:27-29 (already on these pages) shows all twelve agreeing on the facts — only that his own account of those same facts came ‘from his heart’ where theirs, a few verses later in this very speech, ‘melted’ the heart of everyone who heard it: two different fates for the same organ, in the same ten-verse speech.

Verses 13–15 · The one inheritance in this book that is not decided by lot, a naming note repeated word for word in the very next book, and the rest this project has been promising since Joshua 11

★★★ Caleb's own grant is the one inheritance in this whole book that v2's own summary does not cover. Every other tribe in this chapter receives its share ‘by the lot,’ be-goral (v2); Caleb's is never once called that. V13 says only that ‘Joshua blessed him, and gave Hebron to Caleb... for an inheritance’ — a direct grant resting on a forty-five-year-old oath (vv9–10) and a blessing, with no token drawn and no mention of chance anywhere in the scene. The chapter that opens by stating the rule — the land is divided by lot — closes by showing the one man in it whose own portion the rule was never applied to.

★★★ V15's naming note is repeated almost word for word in the very next book, crediting a different conqueror. The naming formula ‘and the name of Hebron was formerly Kiriath-arba’ stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, and Judges 1:10 (already on these pages), which uses the identical words before crediting the actual strike on Hebron's three named Anakim — Sheshai, Ahiman and Talmai, the same three Numbers 13:22 (already on these pages) named among the scouts' original fright — to ‘Judah’ as a tribe rather than to Caleb by name. Whether the two accounts describe one event under two different credits, or two separate actions at the same town, this translation states both naming notices and does not harmonize their subjects.

⚠ This project's own Judges page already called Arba ‘the ancestor of the Anakim’ — before this verse, the one it was built from, existed on these pages to check it against. The Hebrew here says only ha-adam ha-gadol ba-anakim, ‘the great man among the Anakim,’ with no stated genealogical sense at all; on the shelf fetched for this chapter, KJV, ASV and the NWT 1984 all read him as an individual, and the NVI alone reads ‘un importante antepasado de los anaquitas,’ an important ANCESTOR — the identical move Judges 1's own note already made as settled fact, without a hedge, for a town it had not yet reached in this book's own order. This translation follows the shelf majority and keeps Arba an individual at this verse; it does not go back and revise the Judges page, only states the divergence here, at the source verse the claim was always about.

★★★ ‘The land had rest from war’ is the payoff this project has been carrying since Joshua 11. Shaqat, this chapter's own closing word, is the identical verb Joshua 11:23 (now on these pages) first used to close the whole war narrative, and this project's own dictionary entry for the root has promised since that chapter that its repetition here, three chapters later, would be ‘a formula repeated word for word of Caleb's inheritance’ — now carried out. Where Joshua 11 said it of the whole conquered land, this verse narrows the identical claim to one man's own hill country, the only piece of ground in the entire land division this chapter bothers to certify as settled rather than merely allotted.

Echoes paid. Numbers 34:17's Eleazar-before-Joshua order, and this project's own Eleazar entry's reading of it, is carried out at v1 — his first appearance by name in this whole book. Numbers 32:12's promise that two more places would call Caleb ‘the Kenizzite’ is paid at v6 and v14. Numbers 14:24 and 32:12's ‘filled up after Jehovah,’ kept literal rather than smoothed, recurs three more times at vv8, 9 and 14. Deuteronomy 1:28's ‘our brothers made our heart melt,’ with its Anakim, is quoted almost verbatim by Caleb at v8 and v12. Joshua 13:6's lot-casting command is reported as an accomplished fact at v2. Joshua 11:23's ‘rest from war,’ already promised to recur here by this project's own shaqat entry, closes this chapter at v15. Judges 1:10's own naming note for Hebron, and its own ‘ancestor of the Anakim’ gloss, are read back against the verse they were built from at v15.

Echoes planted. Joshua 15 (not yet on these pages) is where the lot this chapter's own v2 only summarizes actually falls for the first time, opening Judah's own district list, with Caleb inside it sending his nephew Othniel after Kiriath-sepher for his daughter Achsah's hand. 1 Kings 11:6 (not yet on these pages) is where ‘filled up after Jehovah,’ said three times of Caleb in this chapter, is said for the only time in the negative, of Solomon.

Decisions. Hebrew and English verse numbers align through the whole chapter, with no versification shift. ‘Jehovah’ continues this project's standing rendering of the tetragrammaton; nachalah is ‘inheritance’ throughout, matching every prior chapter's use. Mille acharei Yehovah is printed ‘filled up after Jehovah’ at vv8, 9 and 14, kept literal to match this project's own Numbers 14 and 32 pages rather than the idiomatic ‘fully followed’ this project's own Deuteronomy 1 page used of the identical two words — a divergence this chapter states rather than silently repeats or corrects. Ha-qenizzi is ‘the Kenizzite,’ kept as a bare gentilic rather than explained in the verse text. Hineh at v10 is ‘look,’ matching this project's standing rendering of the particle. The Masoretic text marks an open petuchah after v5 and after v15, reproduced as the source marks them. Shaqat at v15 is ‘had rest,’ matching Joshua 11:23.

Library. One new dictionary entry in both languages: mille acharei, ‘filled up after,’ tracing the idiom from Numbers through this chapter to its one negation at 1 Kings 11:6. Five existing encyclopedia entries extended, each in both languages: Eleazar (his own forward-citation of this chapter's v1 carried out, and his first appearance in the book of Joshua by name), Caleb (his own forward-citation of Joshua 14 carried out), Hebron, the Anakim, and Kadesh. Two existing dictionary entries extended, each in both languages: goral (this chapter's own summary use of ‘by the lot,’ and the one grant in the chapter the word is never applied to) and shaqat (its own forward-citation of this chapter's v15 carried out).

⚠ A note on order: Joshua stands on these pages at chapters 1–14. Joshua 15–24 remain the open sequence; next in this book is Joshua 15, where the lot actually falls for the first time, opening Judah’s own district list — and Caleb, now inside his own tribe’s share, sends his nephew Othniel after Kiriath-sepher for his daughter Achsah’s hand.
