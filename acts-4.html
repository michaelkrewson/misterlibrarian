The evening of the healing and the next morning: Peter and John, held overnight, are brought before the Sanhedrin with Annas, Caiaphas and the high-priestly family and asked by what power and in what name the lame man was healed; the council can only forbid them to speak, and when they are released the congregation prays together from Psalm 2, is shaken, and is filled again. The chapter closes on the community of goods and on Barnabas, who sells a field.
Continuous with chapter 3 for the arrest (‘it was already evening,’ v3) and the hearing (‘the next day,’ v5), and so undated beyond falling after Pentecost. The closing paragraph (vv32–37) is not a day but a stretch of time — its verbs are imperfect, a habit repeated whenever need arose — and the text does not say how long.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–4 · καταγγέλλειν ἐν τῷ Ἰησοῦ τὴν ἀνάστασιν τὴν ἐκ νεκρῶν katangellein en tō Iēsou tēn anastasin tēn ek nekrōn — proclaiming the resurrection “in Jesus” to the one party that says there is none

⚠ Of every group that could have come, the one that arrives is the party whose whole objection is to the sermon’s subject. The Sadducees are the party Luke has already shown denying the resurrection outright — those who say there is none (Luke 20:27, already on these pages) — and what the apostles are proclaiming is tēn anastasin tēn ek nekrōn, ‘the resurrection, the one from the dead.’ The priests and the temple’s commander come with them, but Luke gives the irritation a content: they are annoyed at a doctrine, not a crowd. Diaponoumenoi is a rare verb — it stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Acts 16:18 (not yet on these pages), where Paul turns round, worn out by a slave girl’s days of shouting. KJV ‘grieved,’ NIV ‘greatly disturbed,’ ASV ‘sore troubled,’ NWT ‘annoyed.’

“In Jesus” is a preposition the shelf cannot agree on. En tō Iēsou can mean ‘in the person of Jesus’ (the resurrection is happening, and is shown, in him), ‘by Jesus’ (his power at work) or ‘in the case of Jesus.’ KJV reads ‘through Jesus’; ASV, NIV and Douay keep ‘in Jesus’; the NWT chooses ‘in the case of Jesus,’ and the Geneva adds the word Name, so that the apostles preach the resurrection in the Name of Jesus — a gloss, and one this chapter will spend its middle verses justifying. This translation keeps the bare ‘in Jesus’ because it is the Greek, and because the clause is sharper left alone: a party that denies any resurrection hears that the resurrection from the dead has already happened to one man. The preposition ek, ‘out of,’ makes it an exit from the company of the dead, not a doctrine about what comes after.

“The captain of the temple” (ho stratēgos tou hierou) commands the temple’s own guard, and he has been on these pages before, in the plural: the ‘captains of the temple’ with whom Judas bargained (Luke 22:4) and who came out to the garden with swords and clubs (22:52, both already on these pages). The singular office returns in the next chapter (Acts 5:24 and 26, not yet on these pages), where he fetches the apostles back without violence, for fear of the people. NIV prints ‘captain of the temple guard,’ TLB ‘captain of the Temple police,’ the Douay ‘officer of the temple.’

“The number of the men” — Luke counts differently from chapter 2. At 2:41 the unit was psychai, ‘souls,’ about three thousand; here it is andres, men, about five thousand, and egenēthē, ‘came to be,’ reads like a running total, though Luke does not say whether the three thousand are inside it. It is the same count in the same unit as the feedings in the Gospels — ‘about five thousand men’ at Luke 9:14, John 6:10, Matthew 14:21 and Mark 6:44 — and the text draws no connection between them.

Verses 5–10 · ἐν τίνι οὗτος σέσωσται en tini houtos sesōstai — by what means this man has been saved — a question about a healing, answered in the vocabulary of salvation

Three bodies and a family. The assembly is the same kind of triad Luke gave at Luke 22:66 — rulers, elders and scribes here — and then a parenthesis names who is in the room: Annas “the high priest,” Caiaphas, John, Alexander, and the rest of the high-priestly family. ⚠ The title attached to Annas is the puzzle the encyclopedia sets out: Luke dated John’s preaching under ‘the high priest Annas and Caiaphas’ with a single noun (Luke 3:2), and John’s Gospel takes Jesus to Annas first, though it is Caiaphas who is high priest that year (John 18:13, both already on these pages). The text of Acts does not say which of the two Luke has in mind, and this translation does not decide it. Of John and Alexander Luke says only that they belong to the family.

“Filled with the Holy Spirit” is a promise being paid. Luke had written, in the Gospel, the instructions for exactly this room: when they are brought before the synagogues and the rulers and the authorities, they are not to worry how they will defend themselves or what they will say, because the Holy Spirit will teach them in that very hour what they must say (Luke 12:11–12, already on these pages). Peter’s speech is the first test of it. The verb, plēstheis, is the one Pentecost used (2:4), and it is an aorist: not ‘Peter was still full from chapter 2’ but a filling for this occasion — and the same verb is used again of the whole congregation at v31.

“Examined” for a good deed. Anakrinometha is the verb of a judicial inquiry — Pilate uses it of Jesus (Luke 23:14, already on these pages) — and its object is euergesia, a benefaction, an act of kindness; the cognate verb is what Peter will say of Jesus’s whole career, ‘he went about doing good’ (euergetōn, Acts 10:38, not yet on these pages). The court is investigating a kindness. KJV ‘the good deed done to the impotent man,’ NIV ‘an act of kindness shown to a man who was lame,’ NWT ‘a good deed to an ailing man.’

⚠ “By what means this man has been saved” — the hinge of the whole speech is a verb the English shelf will not repeat. The Greek of v9 is sesōstai, the perfect of sōzō — ‘has been saved,’ with its result still standing. Three verses later the same root returns as the noun sōtēria, ‘salvation,’ and the infinitive sōthēnai, ‘to be saved’ (v12). Greek does not force the choice between healing and salvation that English does, and Peter answers a question about a healing in the vocabulary of salvation without any word marking the change of subject. Not one of the seven English witnesses prints the same root at v9 and at v12: KJV and ASV ‘made whole,’ NIV and TLB ‘healed,’ NWT ‘made well,’ and the Douay and Geneva ‘made whole’ — and then ‘salvation’ at v12 in all seven. That is smooth English, and it quietly deletes Peter’s argument. This translation prints ‘saved’ at v9, which is strange enough to send a reader here; the work of ‘made well’ is already done by hygiēs, ‘sound,’ in v10.

Who stands where. The indictment of v10 is the one of 3:13–15 in two clauses — ‘whom you crucified, whom God raised’ — and the mitigating word of 3:17, ‘in ignorance,’ is not repeated to the rulers. A thread of standing runs through the chapter: the priests ‘came upon’ them (v1, epestēsan, literally ‘stood over’), the court ‘stood them in the middle’ (v7), the healed man ‘stands’ before the court sound (v10, parestēken), is ‘standing’ beside the apostles (v14), and the kings of the Psalm ‘took their stand’ (v26, parestēsan). Of all those who stand in this chapter, the one whose standing is the argument is the man whose feet and ankles ‘were made strong’ in 3:7.

Verses 11–12 · οὐδὲ γὰρ ὄνομά ἐστιν ἕτερον ὑπὸ τὸν οὐρανὸν τὸ δεδομένον ἐν ἀνθρώποις ἐν ᾧ δεῖ σωθῆναι ἡμᾶς oude gar onoma estin heteron hypo ton ouranon to dedomenon en anthrōpois en hō dei sōthēnai hēmas — “no other name under heaven”: the rejected stone, the name, and a salvation Peter calls necessary

⚠ Peter changes the verb of the stone — and the person. The saying is Psalm 118:22 (not yet on these pages), and Jesus himself has already quoted it three times on these pages: Matthew 21:42, Mark 12:10, Luke 20:17. All three have the builders apedokimasan, ‘rejected after testing.’ Acts has exouthenētheis, ‘treated as nothing,’ and the builders are no longer a third party: hyph’ hymōn, ‘by you.’ Whether Peter is quoting a different Greek form of the Psalm or adapting it, this project’s archive — which holds the Hebrew Bible and the Greek New Testament, not the Greek Old Testament — cannot say. What it can say is where else Luke uses the verb: of Herod, who treated Jesus with contempt before sending him back to Pilate (Luke 23:11, already on these pages) — the same Herod who stands in this chapter’s prayer at v27. KJV and ASV print ‘set at nought,’ the NWT ‘treated by YOU builders as of no account,’ and NIV and Douay ‘rejected’ — the Gospels’ verb, not this one.

The chapter’s real subject is a name. The court asks ‘in what name’ (v7); the answer at v10 is a name and its full title; v12 makes it the only one; and the ban of vv17–18 is a ban on a name, not on a teaching or a person: ‘speak no more on this name,’ ‘teach on the name of Jesus.’ Chapter 3 had already put the name inside the healing — ‘in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene, rise up and walk’ (3:6) and ‘his name has made this man strong’ (3:16). The Greek varies its preposition: the court asks en, Peter answers en, and the ban is epi — the same epi tō onomati that stood at the baptism of 2:38, which this translation prints ‘on the name’ and so prints here.

“We must be saved” — the Greek has a necessity and a ‘we.’ Dei, ‘it is necessary,’ is Luke’s word for what has to happen in God’s plan; it is what the risen Jesus says of his own suffering (Luke 24:26, already on these pages). And hēmas, ‘us,’ is Peter’s own: he does not say ‘you must be saved’ to the court; he is inside the sentence. Six of the seven English witnesses keep ‘must’ — KJV ‘whereby we must be saved,’ ASV ‘wherein we must be saved,’ NWT ‘by which we must get saved’ — and the TLB turns it into ‘no other name for men to call upon to save them,’ which imports the language of Acts 2:21, everyone who calls on the name, and removes both the necessity and the ‘we.’

How far the sentence reaches is the oldest argument about it. Read at its widest, v12 says that no one is saved apart from knowing and confessing the name of Jesus; read in its setting, it answers a courtroom question — ‘by what name was this man healed?’ — with the claim that the one name behind the healing is also the only ground of salvation, and says nothing about how much any given person must know. Readers have taken it both ways, and the library sets the two out without choosing. The Greek marks no limit in either direction: en allō oudeni, ‘in no other,’ and a name ‘the given one’ (to dedomenon), a perfect participle, which makes the giving a completed fact that stands.

“Head of the corner” stays literal (see kephalē gōnias), because the Greek leaves open whether it is the foundation stone or the capstone. Ephesians 2:20 (already on these pages) draws the same picture with a different word, akrogōniaios, and 1 Peter 2:7 (not yet on these pages) quotes the same verse of the Psalm.

Verses 13–22 · τὴν παρρησίαν … ἀγράμματοί … καὶ ἰδιῶται tēn parrēsian … agrammatoi … kai idiōtai — boldness, and “unlettered and ordinary”: what the Sanhedrin saw in Peter and John

“Boldness” is the chapter’s word, and it comes three times. Parrēsia — from pan, ‘all,’ and rhēsis, ‘speech’: saying everything, openly, without holding back — stands at v13, where the council sees it in Peter and John; at v29, where the congregation asks for it; and at v31, where it is given. This translation prints ‘boldness’ at all three. The shelf does not: NIV ‘courage,’ then ‘great boldness,’ then ‘boldly’; the NWT ‘outspokenness’ at v13 but ‘boldness’ at vv29 and 31; and the Douay, which translates the Latin, ‘constancy’ at v13 and ‘confidence’ at vv29 and 31 — firmness in the first place and assurance in the others, where the Greek has one word for all three. What astonishes the council at v13 is the very thing the congregation prays to keep at v29.

“Unlettered and ordinary” — agrammatoi and idiōtai, two words doing less than the shelf makes them do. Agrammatos, ‘without letters,’ stands nowhere else in the New Testament; it says the men had none of the scribal and legal schooling the Sanhedrin’s own members had, not that they could not read. Idiōtēs, from idios, ‘one’s own,’ is a private person as against an official or a professional; it stands in four other verses (1 Corinthians 14:16, 23 and 24 and 2 Corinthians 11:6, not yet on these pages), and English ‘idiot’ descends from it by way of Latin, as the dictionaries trace it — a long journey for a word that began as ‘layman.’ The shelf spreads across the range: KJV and ASV ‘unlearned and ignorant,’ the Douay ‘illiterate and ignorant,’ the Geneva ‘unlearned men and without knowledge,’ NIV ‘unschooled, ordinary men,’ NWT ‘unlettered and ordinary,’ TLB ‘uneducated non-professionals.’ The versions that print ‘ignorant’ turn a statement about training into one about intelligence, which the Greek does not make, and ‘illiterate’ claims more than agrammatos requires. This translation follows the NIV and the NWT in keeping the sense of training and dropping ‘ignorant.’

“They had been with Jesus” — the question is an old one. In John’s Gospel the Jewish leaders at the festival ask about the teacher: how does this man know letters when he has never studied (John 7:15, already on these pages)? Now the pupils are the unlettered ones, and the council answers the question it never answered about the master: they recognize (epeginōskon, an imperfect — they kept recognizing) that these men sun tō Iēsou ēsan, ‘were with Jesus.’ The preposition is sun, ‘together with,’ where Mark 3:14 gave the purpose of the Twelve’s appointment as meta, ‘that they might be with him’: the verse is that appointment seen from the outside.

“Nothing to say in reply” — the argument is standing there. The healed man is the whole defense: he is physically beside the apostles (v14), and Luke adds, only now, that he was more than forty (v22) — lame from his mother’s womb, as 3:2 said, and so nobody’s misdiagnosis. The council’s private deliberation is the most candid passage in the chapter: they concede the fact (gnōston sēmeion, ‘a notable sign,’ and ou dynametha arneisthai, ‘we cannot deny it’) and then, with one alla, ‘but,’ turn to containment: so that it does not spread. They never argue that it did not happen; they argue about its distribution. KJV ‘a notable miracle’ and NWT ‘a noteworthy sign’ reach for the same adjective; the Douay ‘a known miracle’ is closest to gnōston, ‘known.’

⚠ “Let us straitly threaten them” — a doubled verb the critical text does not print. At v17 the KJV reads ‘let us straitly threaten them,’ and the ‘straitly’ stands for a doubled form — apeilē apeilēsometha, ‘let us threaten with a threat,’ a Hebrew-shaped emphasis. The Greek this translation follows has only the plain verb, apeilēsōmetha, and the printed edition marks a variant at this word. The older printed Greek behind the KJV is not in this project’s archive, so the doubled form is reported here, not checked. The ASV ‘let us threaten them’ and the NIV ‘we must warn them’ print no doubling. At v21, where Luke has the prefixed prosapeilēsamenoi, ‘having threatened further,’ nothing varies.

The ban, and the question handed back. Katholou, ‘at all,’ in v18 is a word the New Testament uses only here; the order covers speaking and teaching, and it is the name that is forbidden. Peter and John do not argue; they put the question back to the judges — krinate, ‘you judge’ — whether it is right before God to listen to you rather than to God. When the apostles stand before the same body again, the question has become a statement, and the verb has changed: ‘We must obey God rather than people’ (peitharchein dei, Acts 5:29, not yet on these pages). Here the necessity is put the other way round, as an incapacity: ou dynametha, ‘we cannot,’ keep from speaking of ha eidamen kai ēkousamen, ‘what we saw and heard.’ The same pair of verbs, in the perfect tense where Luke has the aorist, grounds the message of 1 John 1:3.

Verses 23–28 · Δέσποτα, σὺ ὁ ποιήσας τὸν οὐρανὸν καὶ τὴν γῆν Despota, sy ho poiēsas ton ouranon kai tēn gēn — Master, you who made the heaven and the earth — and Psalm 2 read as a script

“With one accord” for the third time on these pages, and “Master” for the third time in the New Testament’s direct address. Homothymadon — the word chapter 1’s note called Luke’s for the congregation at its best, and which 2:46 brought back — is here the manner of the prayer: they raise one voice. And they address God with despota, the word for the owner of slaves. Three verses in the New Testament address God by it: Simeon’s ‘Now you are releasing your slave, Master’ (Luke 2:29), this one, and the martyrs’ cry at Revelation 6:10 (both already on these pages). Two of the three set it against doulos — Simeon the slave, and here, at v29, ‘your slaves’ — so the title is half of a pair. NIV and NWT print ‘Sovereign Lord,’ the ASV, Geneva and TLB ‘O Lord,’ and the KJV and Douay a plain ‘Lord’ — each folding despota into the kyrie of v29. This translation keeps ‘Master,’ as at Luke 2:29.

The Psalm is quoted whole, and its first verb is a horse. Verses 25–26 quote Psalm 2:1–2 (not yet on these pages). The Hebrew opens lamah ragshu goyim — ‘why are the nations in an uproar’ — and yehgu rik, they mutter in vain. The Greek verb behind ‘rage,’ ephryaxan, stands nowhere else in the New Testament and is one the lexicons connect with the snorting and prancing of a spirited horse; this translation keeps ‘rage’ and puts the animal in the note. In v26 the Greek has the phrase epi to auto, ‘in the same place,’ which Luke has used all through the opening chapters of the congregation itself: 1:15, 2:1, 2:44, 2:47. The rulers gather in the same place as the believers do, and the verb of gathering, synagō, stands in vv5, 26, 27 and 31: the court gathered (v5), the kings and rulers gathered (vv26–27), and the place where the congregation was gathered shook (v31).

⚠ Verse 25 is the roughest sentence in the prayer, and the shelf splits on what it says. The Greek strings its genitives together — roughly ‘who, through the Holy Spirit, by the mouth of David your servant, our father, having said’ — and the printed edition marks two points of variation inside it. The result shows on the shelf: ASV ‘by the Holy Spirit, by the mouth of our father David thy servant,’ NWT ‘through holy spirit said by the mouth of our forefather David’ — but the KJV and Geneva print only ‘by the mouth of thy servant David,’ with neither the Holy Spirit nor ‘our father’ in the clause. This translation follows the printed text, rough join and all.

“Anointed” becomes a verb — echrisas, “whom you anointed.” The Psalm’s last word is tou christou autou, ‘his Anointed,’ and the prayer reads it by turning the noun into its verb: ‘your holy servant Jesus, whom you anointed’ (v27). Luke has put the verb in Jesus’s own mouth at Nazareth, ‘he has anointed me’ (Luke 4:18, already on these pages), and will have Peter say the same at Caesarea (Acts 10:38, not yet on these pages); see chriō. At v26 the Greek reads kyrios for the Psalm’s Jehovah, and this translation prints ‘Jehovah’ there, as at every Old Testament quotation; the NWT does the same — ‘against Jehovah and against his anointed one’ — while KJV, ASV and NIV print ‘the Lord.’

The cast of v27 is the Psalm’s list, item for item. Kings, rulers, nations, peoples: on the usual reading, Herod is the king, Pontius Pilate the ruler, the nations the Gentiles who carried out the sentence, and ‘the peoples of Israel’ — where the plural is the Psalm’s own laoi, applied to Israel itself, which puts Israel among the peoples the Psalm accuses and not outside them. That is the sharpest turn in the prayer: 3:17 had said the crowd acted in ignorance, and the congregation now counts the whole city among the Psalm’s conspirators. The pair of Herod and Pilate is not new to the reader: Only Luke’s Gospel reports that these two, enemies before, became friends on the day Jesus passed between them (Luke 23:12, already on these pages) — a friendship the prayer now cites as the Psalm’s own ‘gathered together.’

One noun, three English words: David is a pais and so is Jesus. The prayer calls David ‘your servant’ (paidos sou, v25) and Jesus ‘your holy servant’ (ton hagion paida sou, v27, and again at v30) with one Greek noun, the one chapter 3’s note laid out at 3:13 and 3:26 (see pais). The shelf does not keep the identity. The KJV has ‘thy servant David’ and then ‘thy holy child Jesus’ at vv27 and 30 (it had ‘Son’ at 3:13 and 3:26); the Douay has ‘thy servant,’ then ‘thy holy child Jesus’ at v27 and ‘thy holy Son Jesus’ at v30, two English words for the same Greek words three verses apart; the Geneva has ‘thy servant David’ and then ‘thine holy Son Jesus’ at both; the TLB joins them at v27, ‘your anointed Son, your holy servant.’ The NIV, ASV and NWT hold ‘servant’ for both men throughout, and so does this translation: the prayer’s point, the Psalmist and the one he spoke of under the same title, survives only there.

“Marked out beforehand” — proōrisen, and a sentence that will not give up either half. The cast list ends on a clause that holds two things at once: Herod, Pilate and the rest were gathered together against the servant — and they did what God’s hand and counsel had marked out beforehand to happen. Horizō is the verb of boundaries, so the picture is a plan with its edges already drawn. Luke has used the same root and the same boulē, ‘counsel,’ at Acts 2:23 (‘the determined plan and foreknowledge of God’) and at Luke 22:22, where the Son of Man goes kata to hōrismenon, ‘according to what has been determined’; in both, the people who carry it out are still answerable (‘woe to that man’ in the Luke verse). The verb itself recurs only in Paul: Romans 8:29–30, Ephesians 1:5 and 11 (already on these pages) and 1 Corinthians 2:7 (not yet on these pages). How the plan sits beside human responsibility is a question traditions have answered differently, and the verse does not answer it; it declines to drop either half. KJV ‘determined before,’ ASV and NWT ‘foreordained,’ NIV ‘decided beforehand,’ Douay ‘decreed’ — and the TLB, which turns the decree into a limit on permission: ‘in your wise power will let them do.’

Verses 29–31 · δὸς τοῖς δούλοις σου μετὰ παρρησίας πάσης λαλεῖν τὸν λόγον σου dos tois doulois sou meta parrēsias pasēs lalein ton logon sou — grant your slaves to speak your word with all boldness — and a congregation filled again

What the prayer asks for, and what it does not. The court’s threats are named (epide epi tas apeilas, ‘look upon their threats’) and then left in God’s hands; there is no request that they stop, that the apostles be spared, or that the court be changed. The two requests are for speech and for signs: boldness to keep speaking, and God’s hand stretched out ‘for healing’ so that signs and wonders keep happening through the name — the name the court has just forbidden. Hands run through the chapter: the authorities laid their hands on the apostles at v3, God’s hand marked out the course of events at v28, and here the same hand is asked to reach out for healing, as Peter’s own hand had taken the lame man by the right hand in 3:7.

⚠ “Jehovah” in a vocative. The Greek of v29 is kyrie, ‘Lord,’ and it is the God who has just been addressed as Master and Maker of heaven and earth (v24), with ‘your holy servant Jesus’ named in the third person at v30, so the referent is neither Jesus nor open. Where the New Testament’s ‘Lord’ is plainly the God of Israel rather than Jesus, this translation prints the name, and says here that the Greek reads kyrios. The NWT reads ‘And now, Jehovah, give attention to their threats’; the KJV ‘And now, Lord, behold their threatenings.’

“They were all filled” — Pentecost’s verb, used a third time, with a different result. Eplēsthēsan hapantes is the wording of 2:4, ‘they were all filled with the Holy Spirit,’ and the same verb as Peter’s plēstheis at v8. The people filled at Pentecost are filled again, and the point is made twice in this chapter — once for a speaker facing a court, once for a congregation facing a ban — so being filled is not recorded as a single event. What follows is not the same, either: at 2:4 the filling ended in other tongues, here in ton logon tou theou meta parrēsias, ‘the word of God with boldness’ — plain speech, the thing the prayer asked for. A small difference in the Greek is worth stating: at 2:4 the Spirit is without the article (pneumatos hagiou), at v31 with it (tou hagiou pneumatos). The verb of the shaking, esaleuthē, is one Luke’s Gospel uses of a flood that could not shake a well-founded house (Luke 6:48) and of the powers of the heavens (Luke 21:26, both already on these pages). The KJV and ASV print ‘the place was shaken,’ NIV and NWT ‘was shaken,’ and the Douay ‘was moved.’

Verses 32–35 · ἦν αὐτοῖς πάντα κοινά ēn autois panta koina — everything was common to them — one heart and soul, and not a needy person among them

“One heart and one soul” — the singulars are the point. A multitude, a plēthos, with one kardia and one psychē. They are the two nouns of the great commandment, to love God with all the heart and all the soul (Luke 10:27, already on these pages, drawing on Deuteronomy 6:5); here a pair once spoken to an individual describes a crowd. The next clause says what it looks like: not one elegen — ‘was saying,’ an imperfect — that any of his possessions was idion, his own. The sentence is about what they said of their belongings; it does not say the legal title moved.

The phrase is the one Acts 2 used. ‘Everything in common’ is hapanta koina at 2:44 and panta koina here, and the printed edition marks a variant at this word. Koinos (see koinos) is the word that elsewhere in the New Testament means ‘common’ in the sense of unclean; here it is used in its warmest sense. NIV ‘they shared everything they had,’ TLB ‘everyone was sharing,’ KJV and ASV ‘they had all things common.’

⚠ The tenses tell you it was ongoing, and not a single sale. Verses 34–35 are in the imperfect: whoever owned fields or houses, selling them, kept bringing (epheron) the proceeds and kept laying (etithoun) them at the apostles’ feet, and distribution was being made (diedideto) to each as anyone had need. It is a pattern repeated whenever need arose, which the NIV says outright — ‘from time to time those who owned land or houses sold them’ — and the NWT hints at with ‘would sell them.’ The KJV and ASV both print ‘sold them, and brought,’ which reads like a one-off past event and loses the repetition. Nor does the text say the property was handed over on command: chapter 2 described it, and Peter will say in the next chapter, to the man who sold a piece of property and kept back some of the price, that while it remained it was his, and once sold it was in his own authority (Acts 5:4, not yet on these pages). The giving was voluntary, and that is how the next chapter frames what goes wrong.

“Not a needy person among them” — Deuteronomy’s promise, in the past tense. Endeēs, ‘needy,’ is a word the New Testament has only here, and the sentence matches in sense the promise of Deuteronomy 15:4, ‘there shall be no needy among you,’ which the note on that verse already set beside this one. Whether Luke is echoing the Greek Old Testament’s wording or simply describing, this library cannot settle from its own archive, which holds the Hebrew Bible and the Greek New Testament but not the Septuagint; the correspondence of sense is plain and no correspondence of wording is claimed. What the verse adds is the tense and the scope: Deuteronomy 15 makes the promise depend on obedience and says in v11 that the needy will not cease from the land, while Luke reports it as a fact about one group, for a time. NIV ‘no needy persons among them,’ NWT ‘not one in need among them,’ KJV ‘any among them that lacked.’

“Rendering” the witness. The apostles apedidoun to martyrion — the verb is apodidōmi, ‘to give back,’ the verb of paying what is owed, as in ‘give back to Caesar the things that are Caesar’s’ (Luke 20:25, already on these pages) — so that testimony to the resurrection is something delivered as a debt, and martyrion is the cognate of the martys, ‘witness,’ of 1:8 and 2:32. The shelf reads it as plain giving or testifying: KJV ‘gave the apostles witness,’ ASV ‘gave the apostles their witness,’ NIV ‘continued to testify.’ This translation prints ‘rendering,’ which keeps the giving-back without claiming that the word means more than it does here.

Verses 36–37 · υἱὸς παρακλήσεως huios paraklēseōs — son of encouragement — a Levite, a Cypriot, a landowner

A nickname the apostles gave, and the meaning Luke supplies himself. The man is Iōsēph, Joseph, surnamed Barnabas ‘by the apostles,’ and Luke interprets the name for his Greek reader: huios paraklēseōs. How the Aramaic behind ‘Barnabas’ yields that meaning is a standing puzzle among readers, not something this text settles. Paraklēsis is a wide noun — calling alongside — whose range runs from comfort through exhortation to encouragement, and the shelf spreads across it: KJV and Douay ‘The son of consolation,’ ASV ‘Son of exhortation,’ NIV ‘son of encouragement,’ NWT ‘Son of Comfort,’ TLB ‘the encourager.’ This project already prints the noun two ways: ‘consolation’ for what Simeon was waiting for (Luke 2:25) and ‘encouragement’ for what a letter brought to Antioch (Acts 15:31, both already on these pages). It uses ‘encouragement’ here, the word closest to what the book goes on to show the man doing — Acts 11:23 (not yet on these pages) has him urging the new believers at Antioch, with the cognate verb parekalei, to remain true to the Lord.

⚠ Joseph, or Joses? The Greek printed here reads Iōsēph and carries an apparatus mark at the name. Two witnesses on the shelf print the other form: the KJV ‘Joses, who by the apostles was surnamed Barnabas’ and the Geneva ‘Joses which was called of the Apostles, Barnabas’; the ASV, NIV, Douay and NWT print ‘Joseph.’ The two are variant forms of one name, and this translation prints Joseph.

A Levite who owns a field — a small surprise, and not one the text raises. The law gave the tribe of Levi no territorial inheritance: ‘In their land you shall have no inheritance’ is said to Aaron (Numbers 18:20), and of the Levites generally, ‘among the children of Israel they shall inherit no inheritance’ (18:23, both already on these pages). Luke notes without comment that this Levite owned a field, in a single line that gives both facts: Leuitēs, Kyprios tō genei, ‘a Levite, a Cypriot by birth.’ The text offers no explanation, and readers have supplied two: that the rule concerned the allotment of tribal territory and not private purchase, and that a Levite born on Cyprus, in the dispersion, was not in practice bound by it. Neither is in the verse; the library notes the tension and does not resolve it.

⚠ The grammar changes at v36, and the change is the point. Verses 34–35 were imperfects: a habit, many people. Verse 37 is a run of aorists — pōlēsas ēnenken kai ethēken, ‘sold, brought, and laid’ — one act by one named man, in the same three verbs the imperfects had used for the many. Luke does this to mark an example; and in the next chapter he will use the same three verbs, at the same place, at the apostles’ feet, to mark a counter-example (Acts 5:1–2, not yet on these pages).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “boldness” for parrēsia at vv13, 29 and 31, one word for what the council sees, the congregation asks for and is given; “has been saved” at v9, so that sesōstai and the sōtēria of v12 are visibly one root; “treated as nothing” for exouthenētheis, kept apart from the Gospels’ “rejected”; “unlettered” and “ordinary” for agrammatoi and idiōtai; “on the name” for epi tō onomati at vv17–18, as at 2:38; “Master” for despota, as at Luke 2:29, with “slaves” at v29; “servant” for pais of David (v25) and of Jesus (vv27, 30), as at 3:13 and 3:26; “marked out beforehand” for proōrisen; “Jehovah” at vv26 and 29, where the Greek reads kyrios of the God of Israel; “one heart and one soul” and “everything in common”; “rendering” the witness for apedidoun; “needy” for endeēs; and “son of encouragement” for huios paraklēseōs.

Echoes paid. The Spirit who teaches what to say — Luke 12:11–12 promised it for exactly this room, and v8 is the payment. The Sadducees — Luke 20:27 had them deny the resurrection; vv1–2 bring them face to face with its proclamation. Annas and Caiaphas — the doubled title of Luke 3:2 and the order of John 18:13, met a third time at v6. Herod and Pilate — the new friendship of Luke 23:12, cited at v27 as the Psalm’s fulfillment, and the verb of contempt from Luke 23:11 returning in the stone of v11. Despota and doulos — Simeon’s pair at Luke 2:29, at the other end of Luke’s two volumes. Homothymadon (v24) — a third time after chapter 1 and 2:46. The name — the healing of 3:6 and 3:16, now the whole subject of a court. “In ignorance” — the plea of 3:17 is not repeated to the rulers (v10), who answer with a ban (vv17–18). No needy among them — the sentence Deuteronomy 15:4’s note set beside this chapter. Barnabas and Cyprus — named in the encyclopedia as waiting for this verse.

Echoes planted. “We cannot” (v20), which becomes “we must obey God rather than people” in Acts 5:29; sold, brought, laid at the apostles’ feet (vv34–37), the three verbs that open chapter 5 with a different outcome; the captain of the temple, back at 5:24 and 26; the ban on a name, which the apostles will carry out of a second hearing rejoicing at being dishonored “for the Name” (5:41); being filled (vv8, 31), which this book does not treat as one event; and Barnabas, who is on these pages again in chapter 15, where the encourager and Paul part at v39 over a younger man.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. En tō Iēsou (v2), where the preposition can mean in, by, or in the case of. Whether the five thousand (v4) includes the three thousand of 2:41. Which of Annas and Caiaphas is “the high priest” in Luke’s mind (v6). How far “no other name” (v12) reaches — the sentence is stated and its scope left to the reader. The doubled verb at v17, reported from the Greek behind the KJV and not checked against it. The rough Greek of v25, whose shape the shelf disagrees on. The plan and the plotters (v28), set side by side and not reconciled. Whether Luke is echoing Deuteronomy’s wording at v34, which the archive cannot settle. And Joseph or Joses, and a Levite who owns a field (vv36–37).

Next in this book: chapter 5 — an offering that goes wrong, in the same three verbs and at the same place; the apostles arrested a second time and walked out of the prison by night; and a Pharisee named Gamaliel who tells the council to leave them alone.
