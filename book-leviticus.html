📖 Leviticus

וַיִּקְרָא Vayiqra — 'And he called' — the book's first word: Jehovah calling Moses from the tent. In the scroll the last letter of that first word, the aleph, is written SMALL — a scribal tradition as old as our earliest manuscripts (see the chapter-1 notes).
Λευιτικόν (Leuitikon) — 'The Levitical (book)' — the Greek title, 'the book concerning the Levites,' the priestly tribe whose worship it governs; the Vulgate's Leviticus gives us the English name. The Jewish tradition also calls it Torat Kohanim, 'the priests' manual.'

The priests' manual and the book of holiness — how a sinful people may draw near to a holy God and live in his presence without being consumed.

Chapter by chapter

Leviticus 1The manual of worship opens: from the tent he has just filled, Jehovah CALLS Moses and gives the law of the burnt-offering — the herd, the flock, and the poor person's two birds, each ascending whole in smoke, 'a soothing aroma to Jehovah.'Leviticus 2The offering that requires nothing to die: fine flour, oil, and frankincense, baked in an oven or cooked on a griddle or in a pan, with a small handful burned as a 'memorial portion' and the rest kept as food for the priests. No leaven, no honey, may ever go on the altar — but salt must never be missing, 'the salt of the covenant of your God.' And the earliest offering of all needs no kitchen at all: fresh green ears of grain, roasted over fire and crushed by hand, still standing in the field a moment before.Leviticus 3The offering that no one is required to finish alone: the peace-offering, only ever the fat of it turned to smoke — kidneys, the lobe on the liver, and, when the animal is a sheep, the whole fat tail cut off close to the backbone — while the meat itself becomes a shared meal, part for the priests, the rest for the very family that brought it. And the chapter that gives away the whole system in one closing line: never eat the fat, never eat the blood, a rule this book will spend two more chapters defending — of which, centuries later, only one half survives into a very different set of instructions, written for people who never kept the rest of this law at all.Leviticus 4The offering laws turn from gift to guilt: a graduated sin-offering, its whole shape scaled to who sinned. The anointed priest and the entire congregation get the elevated version — blood carried into the tent itself, sprinkled seven times before the veil, the animal's body burned outside the camp untouched by anyone, not even the priests. A ruler and an ordinary person get the lesser version — blood on the outer altar only, the body becomes food like any other offering. Four cases, four different animals, one word bounding every one of them: shegagah, through error — nothing here is for a sin worn openly — and one phrase closing every single case, regardless of rank: he shall be forgiven.Leviticus 5The atonement law widens: three cases where guilt arrives before knowledge does — a withheld testimony, an unnoticed touch of something unclean, a rash oath forgotten — followed by a sin-offering graded to what a person can actually afford, down to a handful of flour for the poorest, with no oil and no frankincense, the only bloodless sin-offering the whole system knows. Then the guilt-offering: the one sacrifice bundled with cash restitution, first for trespassing against holy things, then — in the very same words — for cheating a neighbor over a deposit, a pledge, a lost object found and denied. Repay in full, add a fifth, and only then bring the ram. ⚠ This chapter runs the full Hebrew count, verses 1-26; most English Bibles split its back half off into 'Leviticus 6:1-7' instead.Leviticus 6The same offering laws, restated a second time — not for the worshippers who bring them, but for the priests who run the altar. The fire that must never go out, day or night. The priests' own share of the grain-offering, hedged with new restrictions Leviticus 2 never spelled out. The anointed priest's own daily offering, morning and evening from the day of his ordination — burned entirely, with no eaten portion at all, because a priest cannot eat his own gift as both giver and receiver. And the sin-offering's law closes on a hard material fact: an earthen pot the meat was boiled in gets smashed, a bronze one just gets scrubbed — holiness spreading by contact, cleaned where it can be, destroyed where it can't. ⚠ Continues Leviticus 5's own versification split: this is Hebrew chapter 6 in full, corresponding to English 6:8-30.Leviticus 7The priestly economics closing the whole seven-chapter offering-law section: who is paid from which sacrifice, spelled out to the hide. The peace-offering splits into three occasions with three different clocks — a thanksgiving offering eaten the very same day, paired with the one bread rule in the whole system that allows leavened loaves onto the altar precinct; a vow or freewill-offering given an extra day; anything left to a third day becomes tainted meat, worthless retroactively, as if never offered. A flat, absolute ban on eating fat or blood follows, addressed to all Israel rather than just the priests. And every peace-offering permanently taxes two cuts for the priesthood — the breast, waved before Jehovah, and the right thigh, a contribution — carried forward by the offerer's own hands, not the priest's. Closes on a formal colophon naming six offerings and ending, at last, what opened at Leviticus 1.Leviticus 8Seven chapters of law, and now the story that carries it out: Exodus 29's own ordination instructions, given fifteen chapters and years earlier, are finally performed almost word for word. Aaron is washed, then fully vested — tunic, sash, robe, ephod, breastplate, turban — before a single drop of oil touches him; the tabernacle itself is anointed first, the man second. A sin-offering bull and burnt-offering ram enact laws already legislated but never yet carried out, with Aaron and his sons only laying hands on the animals' heads, the ordinary offerer's own gesture — they cannot yet act as priests, because they are not yet priests. Then the ram of consecration: blood touched to the tip of the right ear, the thumb of the right hand, the big toe of the right foot, before raw fat and bread are placed directly on Aaron's own open palms and waved there — the hand, quite literally, filled. Seven days of confinement at the tabernacle door follow, ending on the identical six-word formula that once sealed the tabernacle's own construction: they did all that Jehovah commanded.Leviticus 9The eighth day arrives — the confinement is over, and the priesthood goes to work for the first time. Moses hands Aaron the altar itself: draw near, offer your own sin-offering and burnt-offering, and only then the people's. For the first time in the whole book, Aaron slaughters, dips his own finger in the blood, touches the altar's horns himself — the hands filled eight days ago are now doing the filling. He does it once for himself, then again, almost verse for verse, for the whole nation. He blesses the people; he and Moses bless them again together; and fire comes out from before Jehovah and consumes the offering in front of everyone, while the whole camp shouts for joy and falls on its face. No Masoretic paragraph break separates any of it — the entire chapter is one unbroken scene, running straight into whatever comes next.Leviticus 10The same fire that closed the last chapter in triumph opens this one in catastrophe. Nadab and Abihu, Aaron's own eldest sons, offer strange fire before Jehovah, and fire comes out and consumes them where they stand — the identical clause, word for word, that had just consumed the offering. Moses answers with a citation, not a comfort, and Aaron's entire recorded response is one word: he was silent. Kinsmen, not his surviving sons, carry the bodies out, because the working priests may not so much as leave the tent's entrance. Jehovah speaks to Aaron directly — one of the only times in the whole book — banning wine before the priesthood's daily task of distinguishing holy from common. And the chapter closes on an unexpected turn: the father who said nothing when his sons died speaks up, unprompted, in defense of his surviving two, and wins the argument.Leviticus 11The food law — and the first time this book says why. Two tests decide a land animal, a split hoof and the cud, and four famous near-misses fail one of them: the camel, the hyrax, the hare, and the pig, which gets a single verse and no special comment at all. Fins and scales settle the water in three verses without naming one creature. Twenty birds are simply listed, roughly half of them names no translation can identify with confidence — one Hebrew word turns up once among the birds and once among the lizards, and no version renders it the same way twice. Locusts are the one insect Israel may eat, and two of the four insect names occur nowhere else in the Bible, so this translation prints them untranslated rather than guessing. Then the law follows a dead lizard into the kitchen: a wooden bowl is washed, a clay pot is smashed, an oven is broken down — while a spring or cistern stays clean no matter what falls in, the one thing here that cannot be defiled, and the word later Judaism took for its own ritual bath. The word for belly in v42 carries the most famous scribal mark in the Torah: an oversized vav the Talmud calls the middle letter of the whole scroll — a tradition that does not survive being counted, as the Talmud half-admits itself. And forty-three verses of bare criteria finally get a reason: be holy, for I am holy. The chapter ends on a single infinitive, to distinguish — creation's own verb, and the one Aaron was handed one chapter ago.Leviticus 12Eight verses on childbirth, and three of the hardest things in the book. A woman — in a verb odd enough to name the whole Torah portion — ‘conceives seed’ and bears a son: seven days unclean, then thirty-three more in what the Hebrew calls the blood of her PURITY, a second stage most translations quietly erase by turning it into a process of being cleansed from blood. A daughter doubles both numbers, and the text gives no reason at all — every explanation ever offered, from Philo's embryology to the medieval readings that blamed Eve, was supplied afterwards by readers working backwards from the figures, and the chapter itself refuses to let the asymmetry become a price: the offering is identical for a son or a daughter. Circumcision on the eighth day interrupts for exactly one verse, so that the mother's uncleanness never delays the child's rite. Then she brings a lamb and a bird — and a sin-offering, though she has done nothing wrong, which is the strongest single argument that the word means purification rather than guilt. The chapter ends on a means test: if she cannot afford a lamb, two birds will do. That is the offering Luke reports Mary bringing.Leviticus 13Fifty-nine verses of pure diagnosis, and the word this book is known for has been mistranslated for two thousand years. ‘Leprosy’ is the King James’s word, inherited from the Septuagint’s own perfectly reasonable Greek — lepros just meant ‘scaly’; the disease modern medicine calls leprosy went by a wholly different Greek name, elephantiasis, and the two were never confused until ‘leprosy’ narrowed centuries later into the name of one disease alone. What the priest actually examines is depth, spreading, and hair colour — three tests run with obsessive precision across a swelling, a scab, a bright spot, a healed boil’s scar, a burn’s scar, and a scale on the scalp or beard that turns the hair thin and yellow instead of white. One rule stops most readers cold: if the disease covers the ENTIRE body, head to foot, with no raw flesh showing anywhere, the man is pronounced clean — only living tissue counts as active disease, not the colour that first raised the alarm. A hapax word, bohaq, is printed untranslated rather than guessed at. And the identical affliction, in the very same chapter, breaks out in wool, in linen, and in leather — proof inside the text itself that whatever this is, it cannot be Hansen’s disease, because no bacterial infection grows on a coat. It closes on the diagnosed man’s own voice: torn clothes, loosened hair, a covered lip, and a cry of ‘unclean, unclean’ from outside the camp — the very isolation two Gospels report Jesus reaching across, on purpose, with his own hand.Leviticus 14Leviticus 13 diagnosed; this chapter readmits. A person cleared of scaly disease meets the priest OUTSIDE the camp — the boundary never crosses to fetch him — for a rite that kills one bird over living water and dips a second, still-living bird in its blood before setting it free over open country. Seven days follow, then a total, deliberate shave of every hair on the body, head to eyebrows. Then the eighth day, and the chapter’s real payoff: blood and oil touched to the tip of the right ear, the thumb of the right hand, the big toe of the right foot — the exact three points, in the exact order, that consecrated Aaron himself into the priesthood fifteen chapters ago on the worst and best day of his life. A poor person’s cheaper version follows, birds standing in for lambs, the cost cut without the rite being cut. Then the law repeats a third time, for a house — plague breaking out in the plaster of a wall, examined, quarantined, scraped, and, if it returns, torn down stone by stone — a law given for a land the wilderness camp hasn’t reached yet, since nobody living in a tent owns a wall. It closes on a six-line colophon naming every category two chapters have covered: the affliction, the scale, the garment, the house, the swelling, the scab, the bright spot — all of it existing, the text says plainly, to teach the one distinction the priesthood was handed at its own ordination: unclean from clean.Leviticus 15The law that closes the whole clean/unclean block, and the one Leviticus 12 already assumed its reader knew — twice, that chapter compared a new mother's uncleanness to 'the days of her niddah' without ever explaining the word, because this is where the explaining happens. An abnormal male discharge gets the most elaborate contact-contamination law in the entire Torah: a bed, a seat, a saddle, being spat on, an earthen vessel broken outright versus a wooden one merely rinsed. Then, in three quiet verses, the chapter turns to something nobody expects a law code to legislate so plainly: an ordinary emission of semen, and ordinary marital intercourse — the identical 'unclean until evening' assigned to a couple doing exactly what Genesis commanded them to do, no offering, no shame, no priest involved at all. Ordinary menstruation gets the same procedural calm. And then the harder case: a woman bleeding for many days beyond her expected week — the precise condition the Gospels report a woman living with for twelve straight years, until she comes up behind a crowd and touches a hem, and the uncleanness this chapter spent thirty verses tracking runs, for once, the other direction. It closes on the one sentence that states why any of it exists at all: so that a people living beside their own God do not die in their uncleanness by defiling the tent pitched in their own camp.Leviticus 16The book's own center, and — uniquely in Leviticus — told in a single unbroken paragraph, no pause anywhere in the whole chapter until it closes. Aaron, dressed down to plain linen instead of his ordination gold, sacrifices a bull for himself and his household, then turns to two identical goats and casts lots: one lot for Jehovah, one for a name this translation refuses to resolve into a single English word — Azazel, a name three genuine scholarly readings claim (a wilderness cliff, a folk etymology behind the word 'scapegoat', a demon's proper name) and none of them settle. The first goat is slaughtered, its blood carried past the veil into the space no one else may enter, sprinkled on the ark's own cover — the literal 'cover', not the 'mercy seat' Tyndale invented centuries later. The second goat, still alive, gets both of Aaron's hands laid on its head and every sin of Israel spoken over it aloud, before a man 'kept ready for it' leads it out to a land the Hebrew calls, in three words, cut off. Everyone who touches any part of this day's ritual — releasing the goat, burning what's left of the sacrifices — becomes unclean by the contact and must wash before returning to camp, even on the one day dedicated entirely to becoming clean. It closes by making itself permanent: once a year, forever, on the tenth day of the seventh month, the day the world will come to call Yom Kippur.Leviticus 17Sixteen verses, tight as a legal brief, and — like the chapter before it — told without a single internal pause. The law: no ox, lamb, or goat may be killed anywhere but at the tent of meeting's own door, brought as a peace-offering rather than butchered in an open field. The reason, half-buried in the middle of the command, is the real subject of the chapter — some in the camp had been sacrificing to 'the goat-demons', se'irim, real objects of popular worship this law suppresses in the same breath that centralizes every altar into one. Burnt-offerings get the identical rule two verses later, no exceptions by species or by who's offering. Then the chapter turns from where blood is shed to what happens to it afterward: never eaten, under any circumstance, native-born or foreigner alike, because — in the clearest single sentence anywhere in the Torah for what a nefesh actually is — 'the life of the flesh is in the blood.' A hunted animal's blood, never destined for an altar in the first place, is poured out and covered with dust instead. And eating a carcass or an animal killed by a predator — trefah, the direct ancestor of the word 'treif' — brings the same uncleanness as touching one already did three chapters back.Leviticus 18Thirty verses that open by naming their own alternative — “not as they do in Egypt, not as they do in Canaan” — before laying out, one prohibition per verse, twelve forbidden degrees of kinship, each one set apart in the Masoretic text by its own paragraph break, a structural emphasis found nowhere else in this book. Then, without any change of register, the list keeps going: marrying two sisters as rivals, sex during a woman's monthly separation, adultery, giving a child to Molech, a man lying with a male as with a woman, an animal. All of it — every item on the list, without exception — gets called by the same word: abomination. The chapter closes on one of the most physical images anywhere in the Torah — the land itself, personified, vomiting out the nations who did these things before Israel arrived — and refuses, twice over, to let Israel imagine itself exempt: the same fate waits for native-born and foreigner alike if they do what the land's previous inhabitants did. This chapter completes the run from Leviticus 1 straight through 19, with no gap remaining.Leviticus 19The heart of the Holiness Code: “You shall be holy, for I, Jehovah your God, am holy” — unpacked into thirty-five verses of field edges left for the poor, honest wages, no partiality in court, no standing idly by a neighbor's blood, and the single most-quoted line in the Torah, “you shall love your neighbor as yourself,” repeated word for word sixteen verses later for the stranger. Mixed seeds, mixed cloth, the corners of a beard, tattoos, honest scales — the ordinary and the cosmic held in one list, closed fifteen times by the same three words: I am Jehovah.Leviticus 20Leviticus 18's own catalog, told a second time — but this time with a sentence attached to every item. Give a child to Molech, and the town stones you; and if the town looks away, Jehovah says he'll do it himself, a backstop clause aimed less at the guilty than at the community that might let them go. Curse a parent — the same clause, word for word, that Exodus already fixed in an earlier law code — and it's death. Adultery, a father's wife, a daughter-in-law, two men, a man and a beast: death, each one closed with the same grim formula, “their blood is upon them,” a way of saying the sentence isn't the executioner's doing. One case alone gets burned rather than stoned, the single admission in an otherwise flat list that not every offense weighed the same. Then the list turns stranger: a brother and sister who look at each other is called, of all things, chesed — the Bible's own word for loyal, covenant kindness, turned savagely on its head, one bitter pun away from the very first time that word was ever spoken, by Lot, at the gate of Sodom. And two more cases end not in a courtroom at all but in a simple, flat prediction: childless. This chapter closes exactly where it opened — mediums, spiritists — with its own reason for existing spoken once in the middle of it all: you shall be holy, for I am holy.Leviticus 21Holiness law for the priesthood itself, in two tiers. An ordinary priest may go to a funeral only for his five closest relatives — parent, child, sibling, an unmarried sister — and even then keeps the same mourning rules the whole nation already lives by: no shaved bald spot, no trimmed beard, no cuts in the flesh for the dead. His marriage list excludes a prostitute, a woman the text calls "profaned," a divorcee. Then the chapter tightens every one of those rules for one man alone: the high priest may not so much as loosen his hair or tear his own clothes, may not leave the sanctuary, may not defile himself even for his own mother or father, and may marry nothing but a virgin of his own people — not even the widow an ordinary priest could take. A priest's own daughter, if she becomes a prostitute, is burned, the same fire two chapters back reserved for exactly one other offense. And it closes on the longest list of physical conditions anywhere in this book — blind, lame, a broken limb, a hunchback, a dwarf, crushed testicles — barring a man from the altar itself, while the text goes out of its way to say what that bar does NOT touch: he still eats the holy bread, still belongs, still draws provision as a priest. The line the chapter draws is about who may cross one particular threshold, not about who counts.Leviticus 22Two holiness laws that turn out to be the same law twice. First: who gets to eat the priestly share of Israel's offerings — actual food, not an abstraction. A priest with a skin disease, a discharge, or corpse-contact is barred from his own table until a wash and sundown clear him; an outsider or a hired hand may never eat it, full stop, but a slave a priest bought with his own money can — property status, oddly, counting for more than a wage. A priest's daughter loses her claim on it the day she marries out, and gets it back, just as automatically, if she comes home a childless widow. Eat it by accident and the fix is exact: what it's worth, plus a fifth, handed to the priest — the same formula this book already used three chapters back. Then the chapter turns from the men to the animals, and gives, for the first time, the actual list behind a word this book has used since its own opening page: tamim, "without blemish." Blind. Broken. A wart. A scab. Crushed. It is, item for item, the same list that barred a man with a limp or a crushed hand from the altar one chapter earlier — the identical standard, first for the ones who serve, now for what they carry to the fire. A newborn animal gets a week with its mother before anyone may touch it. A mother and her calf may not die on the same day. And a thanksgiving offering can't be saved for later — whatever gratitude means here, it does not keep overnight.Leviticus 23The whole religious year, laid out in forty-four verses and seven appointed times — the fullest calendar the Torah ever gives in one place. It opens, before naming a single annual feast, with the ordinary weekly Sabbath, filed under the identical Hebrew word as everything that follows: an appointed time, not a lesser rhythm running underneath the real calendar. Then Passover, a single evening with no feast-day rest attached to it at all, followed immediately by seven days of unleavened bread that do carry one. A sheaf of the first barley, waved before anyone else's grain is touched. A fifty-day count from that sheaf to a new grain offering of two leavened loaves — and, dropped into the middle of the count with no ritual content of its own, the field-edge law for the poor and the foreigner, because this is harvest, and the law belongs exactly here. A memorial of trumpet-blasts in the seventh month that gets two verses and no explanation. The Day of Atonement, already given its ritual four chapters back, now given its date on the calendar and, for the first time, a penalty for skipping it. And last, seven days living in branch-built shelters, the one festival in the whole chapter that states outright why it exists: so future generations will know their ancestors once had nothing sturdier than this. Six days of ordinary work forbidden outright; on the festival days themselves, a narrower kind of work forbidden instead — a distinction this chapter draws without ever once explaining it, and an entire later category of religious law grew out of exactly that silence.Leviticus 24Three things that have almost nothing to do with each other, filed under one chapter. First, two housekeeping laws restated almost word for word from Exodus: keep a lamp burning every night without fail, and keep twelve loaves of bread on a table before God, replaced every single week, eaten only by priests, in a holy place. Then, with no transition at all, a story — a man with an Israelite mother and an Egyptian father gets into a fight in the camp, and in the heat of it says God's own name out loud as a curse. Nobody knows what to do with him, so they lock him up and wait for a direct answer from Jehovah. The answer, when it comes, is not just his sentence — it is a law: death for cursing God's name, whether you were born in the camp or just living in it. And then, still riding the momentum of that one case, the law keeps going, past blasphemy into everything else a person can do to hurt another person's body: a life for a life, a fracture for a fracture, an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth — whatever you did to someone else, done back to you, exactly, no more and no less. One standard, the chapter says twice, for the foreigner and the native alike. Then the man is taken outside the camp, and the sentence the law had just finished describing is carried out on the very case that produced it.Leviticus 25Two clocks running at once, one inside the other. Every seventh year, the land itself gets to stop — no sowing, no pruning, and whatever the fields grow on their own belongs to nobody in particular: the owner, the hired hand, the resident foreigner, and the wild animals all eat from it on exactly the same terms. Count off seven of those cycles — forty-nine years — and the fiftieth gets a trumpet blast on the single most solemn day of the year, and a phrase that would end up cast onto a bell in Philadelphia three thousand years later: proclaim liberty throughout the land, to all its inhabitants. Every family gets its land back. Every Israelite who sold himself into service walks free. The chapter then does something almost no other law code from the ancient world bothers to do: it works out the actual math. Selling land isn't really selling land — it's leasing however many harvests are left until the reset, and the price has to be calculated accordingly, no cheating either direction. A poor relative's land can be bought back by the nearest family member who can afford it, or by the man himself if he gets back on his feet, or, failing both, by the fiftieth year showing up regardless. Houses inside a walled city get one weird exception — miss the one-year window and it's gone for good, no matter what year of the cycle it is — unless you're a Levite, in which case the ordinary rule comes back, because Levites don't have farmland to lose in the first place. No charging interest against a countryman who's fallen on hard times. And when it comes to actual servitude, the law draws a line it never draws anywhere else this bluntly: an Israelite who sells himself to survive is never, under any framing, a slave — he's a hired hand with an expiration date, gone at the jubilee at the latest — while it's only the surrounding nations whose people can be bought outright, for good, as property. The whole chapter closes on the reason underneath all of it, repeated twice like a bracket around everything in between: you don't actually own any of this — the land, or each other — because it already belongs to the one who got you out of Egypt.Leviticus 26The book's own bottom line, laid out as a straight trade: obey, and here's what you get; don't, and here's what happens instead. The blessing side runs ten verses — rain on schedule, harvests so good the threshing runs right into the next grape season, peace secure enough to sleep with the doors open, victory so total that five chase off a hundred and a hundred chase off ten thousand, and, underneath all of it, God's own presence moving through the camp the way it once moved through a garden. Then the trade flips, and the curse side runs three times as long, in four distinct stages, each one worse than the last, each one only arriving because the last one got ignored. First sickness and losing to your enemies. Then the sky turns to iron and the ground to bronze, so nothing falls and nothing grows. Then wild animals start picking off livestock and children both, and the roads go empty because no one will risk them. Then siege — actual siege, ten women baking bread in one shared oven because there's no longer flour or fuel for ten separate ones, and still nobody gets full. And then the worst sentence in the whole book: parents eating their own children, because that's where the road actually ends if it's walked all the way to the finish. Cities flattened, shrines torn down, the survivors scattered into enemy territory so thoroughly that a rustling leaf is enough to send them running — not because anything is actually chasing them, just because guilt has already done the chasing on its own. And there, at the very bottom, the chapter doesn't stop. It turns: if they ever admit what they did, if the resistance in them finally breaks, God remembers the old promise anyway — Jacob, then Isaac, then Abraham, working backward through the family like flipping through old photographs — and the door back stays open no matter how far gone things got. The book closes its main legal argument here, with a formal sign-off: these are the rules, this was the deal, sealed at the mountain.Leviticus 27The book's own price list, tacked on after the main argument already closed. If you vow a PERSON to God — not sacrifice them, just promise their value — here's exactly what that costs: a flat rate by age and sex, fifty shekels for a man in his prime down to three for an infant girl, no negotiating, though if you're too poor to pay it the priest is required to set a number you can actually afford. Vow an animal that could already be sacrificed, and no swapping a worse one in later — try it, and now you owe both animals, not one. Vow a house or a field, and a priest appraises it fairly, though you can always buy it back later for the appraised price plus a 20% surcharge, since walking back a promise to God isn't free. Land gets tied to the same fifty-year clock introduced two chapters ago: dedicate your own inherited field and its price depends on how many years are left until everything resets; dedicate a field you merely bought, and it was never really yours to give in the first place — it snaps back to its original family regardless. You can't vow an animal's firstborn, because it was already spoken for before you ever got the idea. And then the chapter turns genuinely hard: something placed under a total ban — devoted, no takebacks, no buying it back at any price — including, in the starkest sentence in the book, a person. No ransom. It ends on tithes: one tenth of the harvest, and one tenth of the herd, counted out one animal at a time with no cherry-picking allowed — the tenth one is the tenth one, whether it's the runt or the prize of the litter. And then, after thirty-four verses of appendix, one final line closes the entire book: these are the rules God gave Moses, at the mountain, for Israel. Numbers picks up next, one month later, same mountain, ready to move.

About the book

Where it sits
The third book of the TORAH (the Law / Pentateuch, the five books of Moses), at the very center of the five — and, fittingly, the book at the heart of Israel's worship.
Genre
Law and priestly instruction (torah) — largely DIVINE SPEECH: God dictating to Moses from the tent of meeting, with a spine of narrative (the ordination of Aaron, the death of Nadab and Abihu) running through the middle.
Author
By ancient and traditional reckoning, Jewish and Christian alike, Leviticus is the work of MOSES — more than any other book it presents itself as words Moses received directly: some fifty-six times 'Jehovah spoke to Moses, saying…' The events sit at Mount Sinai in the second year after the Exodus (15th century BC on the early date this translation follows). The critical tradition since Wellhausen assigns most of the book to a late 'Priestly' source (P), with the Holiness Code of chapters 17–26 as a distinct hand (H); this translation leads with the traditional Mosaic authorship — see 'Where the debates are' below.
Date written
On the traditional view, essentially Mosaic: given at Sinai c. 1445 BC, in the roughly one month between the raising of the tabernacle (Exodus 40, the first day of the second year) and Israel's departure from Sinai (Numbers 10:11). (The critical view dates the Priestly material's composition far later, around the Babylonian exile, 6th–5th century BC.)
Place
Mount Sinai — the whole book is spoken there, at and from the newly-built tent of meeting, before the wilderness march resumes.
Audience
Israel newly redeemed and newly given God's dwelling in their midst — a people who now face the terrifying practical question the tabernacle raises: how does a holy God live in the camp of a sinful people without destroying it? Leviticus is the answer.

How it's laid out

1–7The offerings — burnt, grain, peace, sin, and guilt: the five sacrifices, from the worshipper's side (chs. 1–5) and the priest's (chs. 6–7).
8–10The priesthood — Aaron and his sons ordained and installed; then Nadab and Abihu offer 'strange fire' and are struck dead: holiness is not to be improvised.
11–15Clean and unclean — foods, childbirth, skin disease, and bodily discharges: the daily boundaries between the holy and the common.
16The Day of Atonement (Yom Kippur) — the book's center: the two goats, one for Jehovah and one 'for Azazel,' and the once-a-year entry into the Holy of Holies.
17–26The Holiness Code — 'You shall be holy, for I, Jehovah your God, am holy': sexual, social, and sabbatical law, the festivals, the jubilee, and the covenant's blessings and curses.
27Vows and tithes — an appendix on things dedicated to Jehovah and their redemption.

What it's about

  • HOLINESS — the book's heartbeat: 'You shall be holy, for I am holy' (11:44; 19:2; 20:26). To be holy (qadosh) is to be set apart, belonging to God.
  • ATONEMENT and BLOOD — 'the life of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood that makes atonement' (17:11); the sacrificial system is God's provided way to cover sin.
  • DRAWING NEAR — every offering is a qorban, 'that which is brought near': worship as approach, not appeasement of a distant deity.
  • The PRESENCE of God in the camp — the whole book exists because the glory now dwells in the tent; everything is arranged so that Israel can live next to holiness.
  • CLEAN and UNCLEAN — a daily grammar of the holy and the common that teaches a people to distinguish, as God distinguished light from dark at creation.
  • 'Love your neighbor as yourself' (19:18) — the verse Jesus named, with Deuteronomy 6:5, as the hinge of the whole Law.

The source text

Translated from the Hebrew MASORETIC TEXT — the digital Hebrew of Mechon-Mamre (the Leningrad/Aleppo tradition), consonants with the Masoretes' vowel-points and cantillation, and its scribal peculiarities kept — including the famous SMALL ALEPH that ends the book's first word (1:1). The scroll's own paragraph breaks are shown as it marks them: petuchah {פ} (open) and setumah {ס} (closed). The seven-version shelf under every chapter compares the NIV, KJV, Douay-Rheims, The Living Bible, the 1599 Geneva, ASV, and NWT.

Looking forward

No book is read more christologically by the New Testament. The letter to the Hebrews takes Leviticus as one long shadow: the sacrifices that must be repeated because they cannot finally take away sin (Hebrews 10:1–4), the high priest who enters the Holy of Holies once a year with blood not his own — all pointing to a single, final offering. The demand for a victim 'without blemish' (tamim, 1:3) becomes 'a lamb without blemish or spot' (1 Peter 1:19); the Day of Atonement's two goats — one killed, one bearing the sins away — are read as two sides of one cross; and the poor person's two birds (1:14) are the very offering Mary and Joseph bring for the infant Jesus (Luke 2:24). This translation marks those echoes as they come; it does not force them.

Where the debates are

The live questions are AUTHORSHIP and DATE, as with all the Torah, and they travel together. The traditional view — Jewish and Christian for over two millennia — is Mosaic authorship at Sinai in the 15th century BC; the book's relentless 'Jehovah spoke to Moses' frame makes it the Pentateuch's strongest claimant to direct Mosaic dictation. The dominant academic view since Wellhausen assigns Leviticus to the 'Priestly' source (P), edited into shape around the Babylonian exile (6th–5th c. BC), and further isolates chapters 17–26 as a separate 'Holiness Code' (H) — pointing to differences of vocabulary and emphasis between them. Conservatives answer that ancient Near Eastern ritual texts of the second millennium BC are closely comparable, that the tabernacle-centered (not temple-centered) worship fits the wilderness setting the book claims, and that thematic variation is not the same as separate authorship. This library's rule holds: the readings are laid out with their pedigrees, and it does not cast a vote — the same posture it takes on Genesis's composition and the date of Daniel.
