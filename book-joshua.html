Chapter by chapter

Joshua 1The book of the crossing opens on the worst possible news — "Moses my servant is dead" — and refuses to let it stop anything: rise, cross the Jordan. Three times Joshua is told to be strong and resolute, and the courage he most needs turns out to be for keeping the scroll, not the sword. Then rations, a marching order, an old promise called in, and the people handing the charge back: "only be strong and resolute."Joshua 2Two spies leave Shittim for Jericho and lodge with Rahab, a prostitute whose house is built into the city wall. She hides them under the flax on her roof, sends the king’s men off toward the fords with a lie, and then, on the roof, recites Israel’s own words back to it — the Song of the Sea’s melting inhabitants, Deuteronomy’s ‘God in the heavens above and on the earth below’ — before asking for chesed and a true sign. The sign is a cord whose Hebrew name, tiqvah, everywhere else in the Bible means hope; scarlet, like the thread once tied on Tamar’s son; hung in a marked house whose door no one may leave, the Passover night turned inside out. Three days in the hills, and the spies bring Joshua back not a map but her sentence: the land is given, and all who live in it melt away.Joshua 3The crossing. Israel waits three days at the river’s edge, then follows the ark of the covenant two thousand cubits behind it — carried now by priests, not the Kohathites of Numbers 4 — into a Jordan ‘full over all its banks all the days of harvest.’ The moment the priests’ feet are wet, the water from upstream stands up ‘in one heap,’ ned, the Song of the Sea’s own word, far away at a town called Adam whose riverbank has fallen in at least twice since; the water below runs out to the Salt Sea, and the whole nation crosses on dry land opposite Jericho while the priests stand still in the riverbed. God speaks once, to say who the day is for: ‘This day I will begin to make you great’ — Deuteronomy 2:25’s sentence with Joshua as its object. A living God, without an article; the Lord of the whole earth, a title the Bible uses here for the first time; and a written at Adam under a read from Adam.Joshua 4Twelve men go back into the riverbed for twelve stones, one on each man’s shoulder, and set them down where the nation sleeps its first night in the land — a memorial for the sons of Israel for ever, with a question built into it: when your sons ask tomorrow, what are these stones to you? The same catechism the Torah wrote for Passover night, and the crossing is dated to the tenth of the first month, the day the Passover lamb is chosen. Twelve more stones stand in the middle of the Jordan ‘until this day’ — or the same twelve: the Hebrew says neither OTHER nor THE, the Greek and the Latin say other, the NIV says one. Reuben, Gad and half Manasseh cross in battle order, forty thousand of some hundred and ten thousand, keeping Numbers 32’s oath in its own words; Jehovah makes Joshua great ‘in the eyes of all Israel,’ the Torah’s last three words; and when the priests’ soles are pulled loose onto the bank the river comes back over all its banks. The chapter opens with a Masoretic break in the middle of a verse, tells the ark’s exit twice, and ends with the fathers taught to say us of a sea they never saw.Joshua 5The kings west of the river hear, and their heart melts — Rahab’s sentence, in the narrator’s mouth, over a written we the Masoretes read as they. Then a knife: make yourself knives of flint and circumcise the sons of Israel again, a second time, because everyone who left Egypt is dead and no one born on the way was ever cut. A nation sits still in enemy country until it revives, and the camp gets its name — today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from off you — from a verb that rolls a stone off a well. The first Passover in the land, on the plains of Jericho; the next morning they eat the land, roasted grain and unleavened bread, on the morrow after the Passover — the day Numbers 33 gives for leaving Egypt — and the morning after that the manna ceases, with the sabbath’s own verb. Then a man with a drawn sword outside Jericho, who answers are you for us or for our adversaries? with a single no, and gives Joshua the sentence Moses heard at the bush — with one sandal instead of two, and no ground.Joshua 6Jericho, shut up tight against Israel, falls to a war plan with no battle in it: march around the walled city once a day for six days behind the ark and seven trumpets of rams' horns, say nothing, and on the seventh day march around seven times and shout. The walls fall flat, the city and everything in it is devoted to destruction, and one household is spared by name — Rahab, the prostitute who hid the spies, whose scarlet cord is still in the window. Joshua closes the chapter with a curse on whoever rebuilds Jericho — a curse the book of Kings will report, by name, coming true.Joshua 7One man takes from Jericho’s devoted things, and the chapter opens by blaming a nation for it. The spies say Ai is small; about three thousand go up and run, thirty-six die on the descent, and the heart of the people melted and turned to water — Rahab’s sentence about her own people, said now of Israel, the third melted heart in the book. Joshua tears his clothes with Jacob’s word, falls on his face with Goliath’s, and prays the wilderness generation’s own complaint word for word — to give us into the hand of the Amorites, to destroy us — the sentence he once tore his clothes to answer. God’s reply is a word written one letter short, Get up!, and five and alsos, more than any verse in the Bible: Israel has become devoted to destruction, as Deuteronomy 7:26 warned. The nation is sifted tribe, clan, household, man, with the verb that captured Jericho and no lot named; Judah is taken first; and Achan — a Zerahite, descendant of the twin with the scarlet thread — confesses in the grammar of Genesis 3:6 and the vocabulary of Deuteronomy 7:25: I saw … I coveted them … I took them. A mantle of Shinar, a tongue of gold, a heap of stones, and a valley named Trouble that Hosea will call a door of hope.Joshua 8Ai falls the second time, and this time by a plan rather than a miracle: thirty thousand men by night, an ambush behind the city, a decoy that lets Israel ‘be struck’ on purpose and run ‘as at first’ — performing the very collapse that just cost them a battle and a man. Jehovah opens the chapter with the exact two verbs Moses used on the edge of the land the first time, before the spies were ever sent, and this time the concession is real: only Jericho’s cherem is lifted, and the plunder is Israel’s to keep. Joshua rises early a fourth and final time, with the elders restored to his side; a javelin held out and not lowered decides a battle the way a raised hand once decided one against Amalek; and the king of Ai is buried under the identical phrase that buried Achan one chapter before — a great heap of stones, still there, it says, until this day. Then the campaign stops, and Joshua builds the altar Deuteronomy promised on Mount Ebal, half the nation facing Gerizim and half facing Ebal, and copies the law onto stone — a ‘copy’ whose own mistranslation, centuries later, will name a book that has not been written yet.
