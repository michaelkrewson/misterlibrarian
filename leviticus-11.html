🕰
The clean and the unclean — the first law given to Moses AND Aaron together, immediately after the priesthood has been charged to tell one from the other. Two tests sort the land animals, fins and scales sort the water, twenty birds are simply listed, and locusts are carved out as the one edible insect. Then the law follows contamination into the kitchen — a clay pot smashed, an oven broken down, a spring left untouchable — and closes on the reason none of the preceding forty-three verses had given: be holy, for I am holy.
⚠ Undated within the chapter itself, like most of Leviticus. It carries no time marker of any kind and follows straight on from Leviticus 10 with no Masoretic break between them, so it reads as instruction given in the same Sinai window — after the tabernacle's completion (Exodus 40:17, already on these pages) and before the census that opens Numbers (Numbers 1:1, already on these pages) — but whether it belongs to the eighth day itself or simply comes next in the scroll is not something the text says.

Leviticus 11 — The Mister Translation

1
וַיְדַבֵּר יְהוָה אֶל-מֹשֶׁה וְאֶל-אַהֲרֹן, לֵאמֹר אֲלֵהֶם.
Jehovah spoke to Moses and to Aaron, saying to them:note
Jehová habló a Moisés y a Aarón, diciéndoles:
2
דַּבְּרוּ אֶל-בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, לֵאמֹר: זֹאת הַחַיָּה אֲשֶׁר תֹּאכְלוּ, מִכָּל-הַבְּהֵמָה אֲשֶׁר עַל-הָאָרֶץ.
Speak to the children of Israel and say: These are the living creatures that you may eat, of all the beasts that are on the earth.note
Hablad a los hijos de Israel y decid: Estos son los seres vivientes que podéis comer, de entre todos los animales que hay sobre la tierra.
3
כֹּל מַפְרֶסֶת פַּרְסָה, וְשֹׁסַעַת שֶׁסַע פְּרָסֹת, מַעֲלַת גֵּרָה, בַּבְּהֵמָה--אֹתָהּ, תֹּאכֵלוּ.
Any that has a split hoof — cloven clear through — and brings up the cud, among the beasts: that one you may eat.note
Todo el que tenga la pezuña partida —hendida por completo— y que rumie, de entre los animales: ese podéis comerlo.
4
אַךְ אֶת-זֶה, לֹא תֹאכְלוּ, מִמַּעֲלֵי הַגֵּרָה, וּמִמַּפְרִסֵי הַפַּרְסָה: אֶת-הַגָּמָל כִּי-מַעֲלֵה גֵרָה הוּא, וּפַרְסָה אֵינֶנּוּ מַפְרִיס--טָמֵא הוּא, לָכֶם.
But these you shall not eat, among those that bring up the cud and those that have a split hoof: the camel, because it brings up the cud but does not have a split hoof — it is unclean for you.note
Pero de estos no comeréis, de entre los que rumian y los que tienen la pezuña partida: el camello, porque rumia pero no tiene la pezuña partida —es impuro para vosotros.
5
וְאֶת-הַשָּׁפָן, כִּי-מַעֲלֵה גֵרָה הוּא, וּפַרְסָה, לֹא יַפְרִיס; טָמֵא הוּא, לָכֶם.
And the hyrax, because it brings up the cud but does not have a split hoof — it is unclean for you.note
Y el damán, porque rumia pero no tiene la pezuña partida —es impuro para vosotros.
6
וְאֶת-הָאַרְנֶבֶת, כִּי-מַעֲלַת גֵּרָה הִוא, וּפַרְסָה, לֹא הִפְרִיסָה; טְמֵאָה הִוא, לָכֶם.
And the hare, because she brings up the cud but does not have a split hoof — she is unclean for you.note
Y la liebre, porque rumia pero no tiene la pezuña partida —es impura para vosotros.
7
וְאֶת-הַחֲזִיר כִּי-מַפְרִיס פַּרְסָה הוּא, וְשֹׁסַע שֶׁסַע פַּרְסָה, וְהוּא, גֵּרָה לֹא-יִגָּר; טָמֵא הוּא, לָכֶם.
And the pig, because it has a split hoof, cloven clear through, but does not chew the cud — it is unclean for you.note
Y el cerdo, porque tiene la pezuña partida, hendida por completo, pero no rumia —es impuro para vosotros.
8
מִבְּשָׂרָם לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ, וּבְנִבְלָתָם לֹא תִגָּעוּ; טְמֵאִים הֵם, לָכֶם.
You shall not eat of their flesh, and you shall not touch their carcasses; they are unclean for you.note
De su carne no comeréis, y sus cadáveres no tocaréis; son impuros para vosotros.
9
אֶת-זֶה, תֹּאכְלוּ, מִכֹּל, אֲשֶׁר בַּמָּיִם: כֹּל אֲשֶׁר-לוֹ סְנַפִּיר וְקַשְׂקֶשֶׂת בַּמַּיִם, בַּיַּמִּים וּבַנְּחָלִים--אֹתָם תֹּאכֵלוּ.
These you may eat, of all that is in the waters: anything that has fins and scales in the waters, in the seas and in the streams — those you may eat.note
Esto podéis comer de todo lo que hay en las aguas: todo lo que tenga aletas y escamas en las aguas, en los mares y en los arroyos —eso podéis comerlo.
10
וְכֹל אֲשֶׁר אֵין-לוֹ סְנַפִּיר וְקַשְׂקֶשֶׂת, בַּיַּמִּים וּבַנְּחָלִים, מִכֹּל שֶׁרֶץ הַמַּיִם, וּמִכֹּל נֶפֶשׁ הַחַיָּה אֲשֶׁר בַּמָּיִם--שֶׁקֶץ הֵם, לָכֶם.
But anything that has no fins and scales, in the seas and in the streams, among all that swarms in the waters and among every living creature that is in the waters — they are detestable to you.note
Pero todo lo que no tenga aletas y escamas, en los mares y en los arroyos, de entre todo lo que bulle en las aguas y de entre todo ser viviente que hay en las aguas —son detestables para vosotros.
11
וְשֶׁקֶץ, יִהְיוּ לָכֶם; מִבְּשָׂרָם לֹא תֹאכֵלוּ, וְאֶת-נִבְלָתָם תְּשַׁקֵּצוּ.
Detestable they shall be to you: you shall not eat of their flesh, and their carcasses you shall hold in detestation.note
Detestables serán para vosotros: de su carne no comeréis, y sus cadáveres tendréis por detestables.
12
כֹּל אֲשֶׁר אֵין-לוֹ סְנַפִּיר וְקַשְׂקֶשֶׂת, בַּמָּיִם--שֶׁקֶץ הוּא, לָכֶם.
Anything that has no fins and scales in the waters is detestable to you.note
Todo lo que no tenga aletas y escamas en las aguas es detestable para vosotros.
13
וְאֶת-אֵלֶּה תְּשַׁקְּצוּ מִן-הָעוֹף, לֹא יֵאָכְלוּ שֶׁקֶץ הֵם: אֶת-הַנֶּשֶׁר, וְאֶת-הַפֶּרֶס, וְאֵת, הָעָזְנִיָּה.
And these you shall detest among the birds; they shall not be eaten, they are detestable: the eagle, the bearded vulture, and the black vulture;note
Y estos tendréis por detestables entre las aves; no se comerán, son detestables: el águila, el quebrantahuesos y el buitre negro;
14
וְאֶת-הַדָּאָה--וְאֶת-הָאַיָּה, לְמִינָהּ.
the kite, and the falcon of its own kinds;note
el milano, y el halcón según sus especies;
15
אֵת כָּל-עֹרֵב, לְמִינוֹ.
every raven of its own kinds;note
todo cuervo según sus especies;
16
וְאֵת בַּת הַיַּעֲנָה, וְאֶת-הַתַּחְמָס וְאֶת-הַשָּׁחַף; וְאֶת-הַנֵּץ, לְמִינֵהוּ.
the ostrich, the nighthawk, and the gull; and the hawk of its own kinds;note
el avestruz, el chotacabras y la gaviota; y el gavilán según sus especies;
17
וְאֶת-הַכּוֹס וְאֶת-הַשָּׁלָךְ, וְאֶת-הַיַּנְשׁוּף.
the little owl, the cormorant, and the great owl;note
el mochuelo, el cormorán y el búho;
18
וְאֶת-הַתִּנְשֶׁמֶת וְאֶת-הַקָּאָת, וְאֶת-הָרָחָם.
the barn owl, the pelican, and the Egyptian vulture;note
la lechuza común, el pelícano y el alimoche;
19
וְאֵת, הַחֲסִידָה, הָאֲנָפָה, לְמִינָהּ; וְאֶת-הַדּוּכִיפַת, וְאֶת-הָעֲטַלֵּף.
the stork, the heron of its own kinds, the hoopoe, and the bat.note
la cigüeña, la garza según sus especies, la abubilla y el murciélago.
20
כֹּל שֶׁרֶץ הָעוֹף, הַהֹלֵךְ עַל-אַרְבַּע--שֶׁקֶץ הוּא, לָכֶם.
Every winged swarming thing that goes on all fours is detestable to you.note
Todo animal alado que bulle y anda sobre cuatro patas es detestable para vosotros.
21
אַךְ אֶת-זֶה, תֹּאכְלוּ, מִכֹּל שֶׁרֶץ הָעוֹף, הַהֹלֵךְ עַל-אַרְבַּע: אֲשֶׁר-לא לוֹ כְרָעַיִם מִמַּעַל לְרַגְלָיו, לְנַתֵּר בָּהֵן עַל-הָאָרֶץ.
Yet these you may eat, of all the winged swarming things that go on all fours: those that have jointed legs above their feet, to leap with them on the ground.note
Pero de estos podéis comer, de entre todos los animales alados que bullen y andan sobre cuatro patas: los que tienen patas articuladas por encima de sus pies, para saltar con ellas sobre la tierra.
22
אֶת-אֵלֶּה מֵהֶם, תֹּאכֵלוּ--אֶת-הָאַרְבֶּה לְמִינוֹ, וְאֶת-הַסָּלְעָם לְמִינֵהוּ; וְאֶת-הַחַרְגֹּל לְמִינֵהוּ, וְאֶת-הֶחָגָב לְמִינֵהוּ.
These of them you may eat: the locust of its own kinds, the solam of its own kinds, the hargol of its own kinds, and the grasshopper of its own kinds.note
De ellos podéis comer estos: la langosta según sus especies, el solam según sus especies, el hargol según sus especies y el saltamontes según sus especies.
23
וְכֹל שֶׁרֶץ הָעוֹף, אֲשֶׁר-לוֹ אַרְבַּע רַגְלָיִם--שֶׁקֶץ הוּא, לָכֶם.
But every other winged swarming thing that has four feet is detestable to you.note
Pero todo otro animal alado que bulle y tiene cuatro patas es detestable para vosotros.
24
וּלְאֵלֶּה, תִּטַּמָּאוּ; כָּל-הַנֹּגֵעַ בְּנִבְלָתָם, יִטְמָא עַד-הָעָרֶב.
And by these you will make yourselves unclean; anyone who touches their carcasses will be unclean until evening,note
Y por estos os haréis impuros; todo el que toque sus cadáveres quedará impuro hasta la tarde,
25
וְכָל-הַנֹּשֵׂא, מִנִּבְלָתָם--יְכַבֵּס בְּגָדָיו, וְטָמֵא עַד-הָעָרֶב.
and anyone who carries any of their carcasses shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until evening.note
y todo el que lleve algo de sus cadáveres lavará sus ropas, y quedará impuro hasta la tarde.
26
לְכָל-הַבְּהֵמָה אֲשֶׁר הִוא מַפְרֶסֶת פַּרְסָה וְשֶׁסַע אֵינֶנָּה שֹׁסַעַת, וְגֵרָה אֵינֶנָּה מַעֲלָה--טְמֵאִים הֵם, לָכֶם; כָּל-הַנֹּגֵעַ בָּהֶם, יִטְמָא.
Every beast that has a split hoof but is not cloven clear through, and does not bring up the cud, is unclean for you; anyone who touches them will be unclean.note
Todo animal que tenga la pezuña partida pero no hendida por completo, y que no rumie, es impuro para vosotros; todo el que los toque quedará impuro.
27
וְכֹל הוֹלֵךְ עַל-כַּפָּיו, בְּכָל-הַחַיָּה הַהֹלֶכֶת עַל-אַרְבַּע--טְמֵאִים הֵם, לָכֶם; כָּל-הַנֹּגֵעַ בְּנִבְלָתָם, יִטְמָא עַד-הָעָרֶב.
And everything that goes on its paws, among all the animals that go on all fours, they are unclean for you; anyone who touches their carcasses will be unclean until evening.note
Y todo lo que anda sobre sus zarpas, de entre todos los animales que andan sobre cuatro patas, son impuros para vosotros; todo el que toque sus cadáveres quedará impuro hasta la tarde.
28
וְהַנֹּשֵׂא, אֶת-נִבְלָתָם--יְכַבֵּס בְּגָדָיו, וְטָמֵא עַד-הָעָרֶב; טְמֵאִים הֵמָּה, לָכֶם. {ס}
And the one who carries their carcasses shall wash his clothes, and be unclean until evening; they are unclean for you.note
Y el que lleve sus cadáveres lavará sus ropas, y quedará impuro hasta la tarde; son impuros para vosotros.
29
וְזֶה לָכֶם הַטָּמֵא, בַּשֶּׁרֶץ הַשֹּׁרֵץ עַל-הָאָרֶץ: הַחֹלֶד וְהָעַכְבָּר, וְהַצָּב לְמִינֵהוּ.
And these are unclean for you among the swarming things that swarm on the earth: the mole-rat, the mouse, and the great lizard of its own kinds;note
Y estos son impuros para vosotros entre los animales bullidores que bullen sobre la tierra: la rata topo, el ratón y el lagarto grande según sus especies;
30
וְהָאֲנָקָה וְהַכֹּחַ, וְהַלְּטָאָה; וְהַחֹמֶט, וְהַתִּנְשָׁמֶת.
the gecko, the monitor lizard, the wall lizard, the skink, and the chameleon.note
el geco, el varano, la lagartija, el esconcio y el camaleón.
31
אֵלֶּה הַטְּמֵאִים לָכֶם, בְּכָל-הַשָּׁרֶץ; כָּל-הַנֹּגֵעַ בָּהֶם בְּמֹתָם, יִטְמָא עַד-הָעָרֶב.
These are the ones unclean for you among all that swarms; anyone who touches them when they are dead will be unclean until evening.note
Estos son los impuros para vosotros entre todo lo que bulle; todo el que los toque cuando estén muertos quedará impuro hasta la tarde.
32
וְכֹל אֲשֶׁר-יִפֹּל-עָלָיו מֵהֶם בְּמֹתָם יִטְמָא, מִכָּל-כְּלִי-עֵץ אוֹ בֶגֶד אוֹ-עוֹר אוֹ שָׂק, כָּל-כְּלִי, אֲשֶׁר-יֵעָשֶׂה מְלָאכָה בָּהֶם; בַּמַּיִם יוּבָא וְטָמֵא עַד-הָעֶרֶב, וְטָהֵר.
And anything on which one of them falls when they are dead becomes unclean — any vessel of wood, or clothing, or hide, or sacking, any vessel with which work is done: it must be put in water, and it is unclean until evening; then it is clean.note
Y todo aquello sobre lo que caiga alguno de ellos cuando estén muertos queda impuro —cualquier objeto de madera, o ropa, o cuero, o saco, cualquier objeto con el que se haga trabajo: será metido en agua, y queda impuro hasta la tarde; luego queda puro.
33
וְכָל-כְּלִי-חֶרֶשׂ--אֲשֶׁר-יִפֹּל מֵהֶם, אֶל-תּוֹכוֹ: כֹּל אֲשֶׁר בְּתוֹכוֹ יִטְמָא, וְאֹתוֹ תִשְׁבֹּרוּ.
And any earthen vessel into which one of them falls — everything inside it becomes unclean, and it you shall smash.note
Y toda vasija de barro dentro de la cual caiga alguno de ellos —todo lo que hay dentro queda impuro, y ella la quebraréis.
34
מִכָּל-הָאֹכֶל אֲשֶׁר יֵאָכֵל, אֲשֶׁר יָבוֹא עָלָיו מַיִם--יִטְמָא; וְכָל-מַשְׁקֶה אֲשֶׁר יִשָּׁתֶה, בְּכָל-כְּלִי יִטְמָא.
Any food that may be eaten, on which such water has come, becomes unclean; and any drink that may be drunk, in any such vessel, becomes unclean.note
Todo alimento que se pueda comer, sobre el que haya venido tal agua, queda impuro; y toda bebida que se pueda beber, en cualquier vasija así, queda impura.
35
וְכֹל אֲשֶׁר-יִפֹּל מִנִּבְלָתָם עָלָיו, יִטְמָא--תַּנּוּר וְכִירַיִם יֻתָּץ, טְמֵאִים הֵם; וּטְמֵאִים, יִהְיוּ לָכֶם.
And anything on which any part of their carcass falls becomes unclean — an oven or a stove shall be broken down; they are unclean, and unclean they shall stay for you.note
Y todo aquello sobre lo que caiga algo de sus cadáveres queda impuro —horno u hornillo será derribado; son impuros, e impuros seguirán siéndolo para vosotros.
36
אַךְ מַעְיָן וּבוֹר מִקְוֵה-מַיִם, יִהְיֶה טָהוֹר; וְנֹגֵעַ בְּנִבְלָתָם, יִטְמָא.
Only a spring, or a cistern, a gathering of water, stays clean; but whoever touches a carcass in it becomes unclean.note
Solo un manantial, o una cisterna, un depósito de agua, seguirá puro; pero el que toque un cadáver en él quedará impuro.
cross-refs⤷ Gen 1:10
37
וְכִי יִפֹּל מִנִּבְלָתָם, עַל-כָּל-זֶרַע זֵרוּעַ אֲשֶׁר יִזָּרֵעַ--טָהוֹר, הוּא.
And if any part of their carcass falls on any sowing-seed that is to be sown, it is clean.note

Kosher law’s two tests — and the four animals famous for failing them

The whole system of clean and unclean land animals rests on two observable tests, and an animal has to pass both. It must have a hoof that is split, and split all the way through (the Hebrew doubles the root — shosa’at shesa, ‘cloven a cleaving’ — the same emphatic doubling this translation has flagged elsewhere as Hebrew’s way of underlining a word), and it must bring up the cud. No reason is ever given for either test. Nothing here is about hygiene, disease, or the animal’s character; the text simply names two things a shepherd can check by looking, and draws a line.

⚠ Four animals are singled out precisely because they are near-misses — each passes one test and fails the other — and one of them has drawn more scientific objection than any other verse in the book. The camel, the hyrax, and the hare are said to bring up the cud without a split hoof (vv4–6); the pig has the split hoof and does not chew (v7). But the hyrax is not a ruminant, and neither is the hare: neither has the multi-chambered stomach of a cow, and modern zoology does not class either as chewing cud at all. The objection is real and worth stating plainly rather than explaining away. What the Hebrew actually says is ma’aleh gerah, ‘bringing up what has been swallowed’ — a description of visible behaviour, not an anatomical claim, and both animals do re-process food in ways an ancient observer would have seen and recognised: the hyrax grinds its jaws in a constant sideways chewing motion, and the hare re-ingests its own soft night droppings. Whether that counts as ‘chewing the cud’ depends entirely on whether the phrase is a scientific category or a shepherd’s one. This is a food law addressed to herdsmen, and it sorts animals by what a herdsman can see — which leaves the law itself perfectly workable either way: the list of animals that may be eaten is the same under either reading.

The hyrax (Hebrew shaphan) is where the shelf falls apart worst, and where the versions’ own ages show. KJV, ASV and Geneva all print ‘coney’ — a word that meant rabbit in 1599 and means nothing to most readers now; Douay, translating the Vulgate rather than the Hebrew, simply carries the Latin across untranslated as ‘cherogrillus’; NIV prints ‘hyrax’ and NWT ‘rock badger.’ The animal is a hyrax — a stout, rock-dwelling Middle Eastern mammal that is neither rabbit nor badger — so this translation names it, on the grounds that a reader who has never met the word can look it up, while a reader who meets ‘coney’ or ‘rock badger’ will confidently picture the wrong animal. ⚠ Note also that the pig, which centuries of readers have taken to be the point of this chapter, gets exactly one verse and no special comment at all — it is simply the fourth item on a list of near-misses, no more emphasised than the hare.

Fins and scales — a rule that names no animal, and a second Hebrew word for unclean

The water law is the shortest and cleanest section in the chapter, and it names not one single creature. Where the land animals get four worked examples and the birds get twenty proper names, the sea gets a bare criterion: fins and scales, in the seas or in the streams. Nothing is identified, nothing illustrated. A rule stated this way needs no updating and no expert — anyone holding the animal can settle it.

⚠ And here the chapter reaches for a different word. Up to this point everything forbidden has been tame, ‘unclean’ — a status word, the opposite of tahor, and one that can be contracted, waited out, and washed off. From v10 the water creatures are called sheqets, ‘a detestable thing’ — not a state you can catch but a judgement on the creature itself, and one the chapter turns into a verb aimed at the reader: ‘their carcasses you shall hold in detestation’ (v11). The two words are not synonyms, and which one a version keeps is a real test of it. KJV, ASV and Geneva keep the distinction with ‘abomination’ against ‘unclean’; NWT keeps it too, and arguably most clearly, with ‘a loathsome thing’ against ‘unclean’. NIV flattens both Hebrew words into the single English word ‘unclean’ — smooth, readable, and it silently deletes a distinction the chapter is built on. This is the case that most rewards checking the whole shelf rather than the most familiar member of it.

The twenty unclean birds of Leviticus 11 — a list no two translations can agree on

Twenty birds are forbidden and no criterion is ever given for any of them. The land animals got two tests and the fish got one; the birds get a list, and a reader is simply expected to know what the names mean. That is the difficulty, because we largely do not. Something close to half of these twenty Hebrew words appear only here and in the parallel list in Deuteronomy 14, which makes them effectively untestable — there is no second context to check a guess against. This translation prints the best-supported modern identification for each, and this note is the place to say plainly that several of them are guesses.

⚠ The clearest proof that nobody knows is a single word that appears in this chapter twice, in two different lists. The tinshemet is the second-to-last bird in v18 and also the last of the ground creatures in v30 — a bird in one list and something that crawls in the other. No version renders it the same way in both places, because no version can. This translation prints ‘barn owl’ in v18 and ‘chameleon’ in v30, following the majority reading of each list; the honest position is that one Hebrew word is doing two jobs and we cannot recover both.

The versions diverge here more widely than anywhere else in the book. At v18 KJV has ‘the swan, and the pelican, and the gier eagle’ while NIV has ‘the white owl, the desert owl, the osprey’ — three names against three names with not one bird in common. At v19 the last-but-one bird is a ‘lapwing’ in KJV and Geneva and a ‘hoopoe’ in ASV and NIV — a medieval misidentification that the older English versions inherited and the newer ones corrected. ⚠ Douay is the most revealing of all, because it does not pretend: translating the Vulgate rather than the Hebrew, it simply carries the Latin’s own Greek-derived names straight across into English — ‘the griffon, and the osprey… the porphyrion… the charadrion… the houp.’ Those are not English words and were never meant to be. A version that leaves the name untranslated is telling the reader something true.

One name in the list is not obscure at all, and it argues with itself. The chasidah, ‘stork’ (v19), is built directly on chesed, covenant loyalty — the bird’s own name means ‘the loyal one,’ given for how attentively storks tend their young — and it is on the forbidden list anyway. A scavenging diet outweighs a name that means kindness. Centuries later Zechariah sends Wickedness out of the land carried on the wings of exactly this bird (Zechariah 5:9, already on these pages), which may be the point: the vision does not choose an eagle or a dove.

Locusts are kosher — the one insect Israel may eat, and a verse where the written and spoken texts say opposite things

Winged swarming things are detestable as a class, and then four of them are carved back out. The test is anatomical and precise: jointed legs above the feet, made for leaping (v21). That is a locust’s hind leg, described by someone who had looked at one. Four kinds are then named, and these are the only insects the Torah ever permits — which is why John the Baptist’s desert diet of locusts and wild honey (Mark 1:6, already on these pages) is austere without being irregular: he is eating the one bug the law allows.

⚠ Verse 21 carries one of the sharpest scribal notes in the Torah, and the two readings are opposites. The consonants as written (the ketiv) spell לא, ‘not’ — which would make the verse permit insects that do not have leaping legs. The reading tradition (the qere) says the word is לו, ‘it has’ — permitting the ones that do. The two words differ by a single letter and reverse the law. Every version follows the qere, and so does this one, because v22’s list of actual leaping insects settles it; but the written text is what it is, and the Masoretes preserved both rather than choosing.

⚠ The four insect names divide cleanly in two, and this translation treats the halves differently. Arbeh (locust) and chagav (grasshopper) are used repeatedly elsewhere in the Bible, so there is real evidence for what they mean. Solam and hargol are true hapax legomena — each occurs exactly once in the entire Hebrew Bible, in this verse, with no second context anywhere to test a guess against. So the first two are translated and the last two are printed as they stand, solam and hargol, rather than dressed in English names the evidence cannot support.

The shelf handles the same uncertainty in three ways. KJV guesses and gets one plainly wrong — ‘the beetle,’ which does not leap and could not qualify under v21’s own test. NIV guesses more confidently and more plausibly: ‘locust, katydid, cricket or grasshopper.’ ⚠ Geneva does something better than either: it translates only the first name and then simply transliterates the Hebrew for the other three and stops — ‘the solean after his kind, the hargol after his kind, and the hagab after his kind’ — declining in 1599 to invent an English bug it could not identify. Four centuries of scholarship later that instinct is still right, and this translation follows it, drawing the line one name earlier: hagab turns up often enough elsewhere to be rendered, while solam and hargol do not.

‘Unclean until evening’ — what touching a carcass actually costs

The chapter now turns from what may be eaten to what may be touched, and the penalty is startlingly small. Contact with a forbidden carcass makes a person unclean until sundown; carrying one adds a load of laundry. There is no offering, no fine, no priest, no confession — the condition simply expires with the day. That is the crucial thing about tame and the thing English ‘unclean’ most often obscures: it is not a sin and not a stain on character. It is a temporary ritual status, incurred by ordinary unavoidable contact, and cleared by time and water.

⚠ Two of these rules are effectively unavoidable, which seems to be deliberate. Anyone who works with animals will touch a dead one; v27’s ‘everything that goes on its paws’ covers the dog and the household cat. The law does not treat that as a failure to be prevented, only as a state to be tracked — and the tracking is what matters, because uncleanness is what bars a person from the sanctuary. The whole apparatus exists to answer one practical question: who may approach, and when. A day’s wait is the answer for almost everyone in this chapter.

The smashed pot, the broken oven — and the one thing a carcass cannot make unclean

Eight small ground creatures are named, and then the law does something the rest of the chapter has not: it follows the contamination into the kitchen. A dead lizard falling into a wooden bowl means the bowl is washed and waits out the day. A dead lizard falling into a clay pot means the pot is smashed. The difference is material and physical: fired clay is porous, absorbs what touches it, and cannot be scoured clean; wood, cloth and leather can. This is the same reasoning — and very nearly the same sentence — that Leviticus 6 (already on these pages) applied to holiness running the other direction, where a clay pot used to boil the sin-offering is broken and a bronze one merely scrubbed. Holiness and uncleanness are both treated as things that soak in, and both meet the same limit in the same material.

⚠ Verse 36 is the one place in the chapter where uncleanness simply stops. A spring, or a cistern — a gathering of water — stays clean no matter what falls in it. The exemption is worth noticing because it is not a loophole; it is the seed of an institution. That phrase, miqveh mayim, is the same one Genesis used for the seas at their naming (Genesis 1:10, already on these pages), and it is the word later Judaism took for the ritual bath itself, the mikveh. The one thing in this chapter that cannot be defiled becomes the standing means of removing defilement.

⚠ Verses 37–38 turn on a single detail that later law built a great deal on: dry seed is immune, and wet seed is not. A carcass falling on grain set aside for sowing leaves it clean; the same carcass falling on grain that has had water put on it makes it unclean. Deliberate contact with liquid is what makes a foodstuff susceptible in the first place — a rule stated here in one verse, and elaborated by the rabbis into an entire tractate. ASV renders v36’s phrase almost word for word as ‘a gathering of water’; KJV reads it as a quantity instead — ‘a pit, wherein there is plenty of water’ — which loses the noun that became the name of the bath.

When a clean animal dies on its own

Two verses quietly close a gap the chapter had left open. Everything so far has assumed the animal was forbidden to begin with. Here a permitted animal — an ox, a sheep, one of the creatures v3 expressly allows — simply dies, and the same carcass rules apply: touching it costs a day, eating it or carrying it costs a day and a wash. What makes an animal edible, then, is not only its species but how it died. A clean animal that dies of itself is not food.

⚠ The rule has a long afterlife. This is the root of the requirement that a permitted animal be deliberately slaughtered rather than merely found dead — and it is one half of the pair Genesis had already assumed long before Sinai, where Noah is expected to know which animals are clean without ever being told (Genesis 7:2, already on these pages). The distinction in this chapter is being legislated, not invented.

Gachon — the middle letter of the Torah — and ‘be holy, for I am holy’

The chapter’s closing ban is its broadest: not a list but a manner of moving. Whatever goes on its belly, whatever goes on all fours, whatever has many feet — the categories are locomotion, not species, and between them they sweep up everything that crawls. ⚠ ‘Goes on its belly’ is the same posture Genesis pronounced on the serpent — ‘on your belly you shall go’ (Genesis 3:14, already on these pages) — and the noun gachon occurs in exactly two verses in the whole Hebrew Bible: that one and this one.

⚠ And the word for belly in v42 — gachon — carries the most famous scribal marking in the Torah. Its ו (vav) is written oversized in the scroll, one of a small set of enlarged letters the Masoretes preserved, and the Talmud gives the reason: it is the middle letter of the Torah. The passage (Kiddushin 30a) explains that the earliest scholars were called soferim, ‘counters,’ because they counted the letters, and it names this vav as the midpoint of them all. ⚠ What makes the tradition worth reporting honestly is that it does not survive being checked. Counted against a standard scroll of roughly 304,805 letters, this vav lands at about number 157,236 — some 4,800 letters past the true midpoint. The Talmud itself sees the problem coming: when Rav Yosef is asked which half the vav belongs to, he answers that the ancient scribes were expert in the full and defective spellings and ‘we are not expert’ — the count cannot be redone. The mark in the scroll is older than any explanation of it, and the explanation on offer does not add up.

⚠ The same Talmudic passage names the Torah’s middle words, and they are in the previous chapter of this book. The doubled verb darosh darash — ‘he inquired carefully’ — is Leviticus 10:16 (already on these pages), the moment Moses goes looking for a missing goat. Two consecutive chapters of Leviticus hold the two halves of one counting tradition: the middle words in the search for the goat, the middle letter in the belly of the crawling thing. The scribes who marked them were not making a theological point. They were checking their arithmetic, in a book, letter by letter.

Then the reason arrives, and it is the first time in this book that the food law is grounded in anything at all. Forty-three verses of criteria and lists have given no motive; v44 gives one: ‘be holy, for I am holy.’ Holy means set apart before it means anything moral, and the argument is by imitation — the people are to be marked off because God is. ⚠ This is the formula’s first appearance here, and it becomes the refrain of the Holiness Code: Leviticus 19:2 (already on these pages) opens with the identical sentence and then spends thirty-five verses unpacking it into field edges left for the poor, wages paid on time, and honest scales. The same clause that governs what is on the plate governs what is in the ledger. And v45 anchors it to history rather than to nature: not ‘be holy because these animals are unfit’ but ‘be holy because I brought you up out of Egypt.’

The law’s last word is a verb from Genesis 1

The chapter closes with a formal colophon — ‘this is the law of…’ — the same signing-off formula that ended the offering laws at Leviticus 7 (already on these pages), running back through the chapter’s four categories in the order it took them: beast, bird, water creature, swarming thing. And then the final verse states the purpose of the whole chapter in one infinitive: lehavdil, to distinguish.

⚠ That verb is not chosen at random, and this is the third time these pages have met it in a load-bearing place. It is creation’s own working verb — God makes largely by separating, light from darkness, waters from waters (Genesis 1:4, already on these pages). It is the priesthood’s job description, handed to Aaron one chapter ago in the middle of the worst day of his life: ‘to distinguish between the holy and the common, and between the unclean and the clean’ (Leviticus 10:10, already on these pages). And now it is the stated point of a chapter about lizards and fins. The same act runs from the first day of the world to a rule about which pot to smash. Whatever else this chapter is doing, it is presenting eating as an exercise in the verb God started with.

⚠ KJV and Geneva render the infinitive ‘to make a difference’; ASV and NWT both have ‘to make a distinction’; NIV makes it a command to the reader instead — ‘you must distinguish’ — which reads well and quietly changes the grammar, since the Hebrew is an infinitive attached to the law, not an imperative attached to the people. Douay, following the Vulgate, turns the purpose into knowledge rather than separation: ‘that you may know the differences.’ This translation keeps the plain infinitive, and keeps the same English word the priesthood was given in Leviticus 10:10, so that the echo is audible in English as it is in Hebrew.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 11 both run 1–47 — no versification split, checked verse by verse against the Masoretic text rather than assumed. Three Masoretic paragraph breaks fall in this chapter (a closed break after v28 and after v38, an open break at v47), dividing it into the food law proper (1–23), the law of contact and contamination (24–38), and the closing charge with its colophon (39–47). ‘Detestable’ is kept for sheqets throughout and ‘unclean’ for tame, never blurred into one word, because the chapter uses both and means different things by them — the distinction NIV drops. ‘Hyrax’ is printed for shaphan rather than the traditional ‘coney’ or ‘rock badger,’ on the reader test: an unfamiliar right name can be looked up, while a familiar wrong one will simply be believed. ‘To distinguish’ renders the closing infinitive lehavdil with the same English verb already used for the priesthood’s charge at Leviticus 10:10, so the echo survives translation. The bird and lizard names are printed as the best-supported modern identifications and flagged in the notes as guesses where they are guesses; nothing here is presented as more certain than it is.

Echoes paid. Lehavdil, creation’s own verb of separation (Genesis 1:4), handed to the priesthood at Leviticus 10:10 and now named as this whole chapter’s purpose in its last verse; miqveh mayim, the gathering of water that named the seas at Genesis 1:10, reappearing as the one thing a carcass cannot defile; the clean/unclean distinction Noah was already assumed to know at Genesis 7:2, legislated here for the first time; the porous clay pot that must be smashed, matching Leviticus 6’s identical ruling about holiness; and the chasidah, the stork whose name means loyalty and whose diet earns it a place on this list, already met carrying Wickedness out of the land at Zechariah 5:9.

Echoes planted. ‘Be holy, for I am holy’ (vv44–45), which becomes the refrain of the Holiness Code and opens Leviticus 19 word for word; the locust permitted here, still the one insect a desert prophet could eat at Mark 1:6; and the enlarged ו of gachon, whose counting tradition’s other half — the middle words of the Torah — is already on these pages one chapter back, at Leviticus 10:16.
