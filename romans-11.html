The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. Chapter 10 ended on Israel, “a disobedient and contradicting people”; chapter 11 asks whether God has pushed away his people, answers with Paul’s own tribe and Elijah’s seven thousand, speaks to the nations about an olive tree they must not boast over, states a mystery about a hardening “until” the fullness of the nations comes in and the salvation of “all Israel,” and ends in a doxology.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 10. The only time-words the chapter uses are its own: “at the present time” (verse 5), “until this very day” (verse 8), “until the fullness of the nations comes in” (verse 25), and “once” and “now” (verses 30–31).

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · Λέγω οὖν, μὴ ἀπώσατο ὁ θεὸς τὸν λαὸν αὐτοῦ; μὴ γένοιτο … οὐκ ἀπώσατο ὁ θεὸς τὸν λαὸν αὐτοῦ ὃν προέγνω … ὡς ἐντυγχάνει τῷ θεῷ κατὰ τοῦ Ἰσραήλ Legō oun, mē apōsato ho theos ton laon autou? mē genoito … ouk apōsato ho theos ton laon autou hon proegnō … hōs entynchanei tō theō kata tou Israēl — Romans 11:1–2: “Has God pushed away his people? May it never be!” The third question in a row that begins “I say” and expects “no,” an answer taken from Paul’s own tribe, and the verb that Romans 8 uses for pleading for the holy ones, turned against Israel

“I say, then, God has not pushed away his people, has he?” (Legō oun, mē apōsato ho theos ton laon autou, Romans 11:1). Chapter 10 ended with Israel under Isaiah’s words, “a disobedient and contradicting people” (10:21), and the first sentence of chapter 11 asks what follows from that. It is built like the two questions before it: Legō, “I say,” and mē, the particle with which a Greek question expects the answer “no” (“I say, they did not fail to hear, did they?” at 10:18, “I say, Israel did not fail to know, did they?” at 10:19). The shape comes round once more at verse 11. The verb is apōtheō (dictionary), “push away,” and it stands in six verses of the New Testament (apōtheō, searched): Acts 7:27, where the man who was wronging his neighbor “pushed him away” (Moses); 7:39, where “our fathers” “pushed him away” (Moses again); Acts 13:46, where Paul and Barnabas say to the Jews of Pisidian Antioch, “Since you push it away” (the word of God), and then “look — we are turning to the nations”; 1 Timothy 1:19, where some have “pushed away” a good conscience; and these two verses. In the other four the one who pushes away is a human being. Here, and in the next verse, God is the one who might, and the answer is that he did not. The letter’s own answer is mē genoito, “May it never be!” (dictionary), and the phrase stands in fifteen verses of the New Testament, ten of them in Romans (mē genoito, searched): 3:4, 3:6, 3:31, 6:2, 6:15, 7:7, 7:13, 9:14, this verse and verse 11. Those last two are the last two in the letter. The answer at 9:14 defended God against a charge of unrighteousness; these defend him against a charge of abandonment. The shelf for the question: KJV “Hath God cast away his people? God forbid”, Geneva “Hath God cast away his people? God forbid”, Douay “Hath God cast away his people? God forbid” and ASV “Did God cast off his people? God forbid” keep the English of 1611 for both the verb and the oath; NIV “Did God reject his people? By no means!” and NWT 1984 “Never may that happen!” modernize the answer; and the TLB “has God rejected and deserted his people the Jews?” puts two verbs where the Greek has one and adds who the people are.

“God did not push away his people” (ouk apōsato ho theos ton laon autou, Romans 11:2). These are the words of two verses of the Hebrew Bible: lo yitosh YHVH, “Jehovah will not forsake,” stands in two verses (searched), 1 Samuel 12:22 and Psalm 94:14, and in both “his people” is the object. In my own translation of the Hebrew, Samuel has “For Jehovah will not forsake his people, for his great name’s sake, because Jehovah was pleased to make you a people for himself,” and the psalm has “For Jehovah will not forsake his people, and his inheritance he will not leave” (neither chapter is yet on these pages). The Greek Old Testament puts apōtheō in both (in Brenton’s printed text, checked): Hoti ouk apōsetai Kyrios ton laon autou, “For the Lord will not push away his people,” at 1 Kingdoms 12:22 and Psalm 93:14 in the Greek numbering. Paul’s sentence is that one, with two changes: the future has become an aorist, so that a promise has become a report (“did not push away”), and “the Lord” has become “God.” It is not introduced by “it is written.” It also leaves out the grounds the old verses give (“for his great name’s sake,” “his inheritance”) and gives its own: the people are the ones “whom he knew beforehand,” and the evidence offered next is a man.

“For I too am an Israelite, from the seed of Abraham, of the tribe of Benjamin” (kai gar egō Israēlitēs eimi, ek spermatos Abraam, phylēs Beniamin, Romans 11:1). The argument starts from a person. Israēlitēs stands in nine verses of the New Testament (Israēlitēs, searched): John 1:47 (Nathanael, “truly an Israelite, in whom there is no deceit”), Acts 2:22, 3:12, 5:35, 13:16 and 21:28 (the address “Men, Israelites” in speeches and in a riot), Romans 9:4 (“who are Israelites,” of his kinsmen), this verse, and 2 Corinthians 11:22 (not yet on these pages). That last verse is a set of three short replies, kagō, “so am I”: “Hebrews? … Israelites? … Seed of Abraham?” This verse gives the second and third and adds the tribe. The tribe of Benjamin stands in four verses of the New Testament (Beniamin, searched): here, Philippians 3:5 (not yet on these pages), Acts 13:21, where “Saul the son of Kish, a man of the tribe of Benjamin” is the first king, and Revelation 7:8, the sealed tribe. Paul’s other name is Saul (Acts 13:9, “Saul, who is also Paul”).

“Whom he knew beforehand” (hon proegnō). The verb is the verb of 8:29 (“those he knew beforehand, he also marked out beforehand to be formed with the image of his Son”), and it stands in five verses of the New Testament (proginōskō, searched): Acts 26:5 (the Jews who are “having known me beforehand from the first”), 8:29, this verse, 1 Peter 1:20 (Christ, “known beforehand before the foundation of the world”) and 2 Peter 3:17 (not yet on these pages). At 8:29 the ones known beforehand are the ones called according to purpose; here they are “his people,” and the verse does not say whether the two groups are the same. The shelf: KJV “foreknew”, ASV “foreknew”, Douay “foreknew” and NIV “foreknew” keep the compound; Geneva “knew before” splits it; NWT 1984 “first recognized” (the 2013 revision keeps “first recognized”) reads the verb as an act of regard; and the TLB “whom he chose from the very beginning” replaces knowing with choosing.

“Or do you not know what the scripture says in the passage about Elijah, how he pleads with God against Israel?” (ē ouk oidate en Ēlia ti legei hē graphē, hōs entynchanei tō theō kata tou Israēl, Romans 11:2). The question ē ouk oidate, “Or do you not know,” stands in five verses of the New Testament (ē ouk oidate, searched): this one and four in 1 Corinthians 6 (not yet on these pages). The same English question, “Or do you not know,” is printed at 6:3 and 7:1 for a different Greek verb, ē agnoeite; all three are the voice of a teacher who assumes the answer. The Greek is en Ēlia, literally “in Elijah,” a way of naming the stretch of scripture where he stands (reported, not checked); on the shelf KJV “of Elias” and Douay “of Elias” keep the old spelling of the name; ASV “of Elijah” and Geneva “of Elijah” modernize it; NIV “in the passage about Elijah” spells out the idiom; and NWT 1984 “in connection with” him is close to it. The verb entynchanō (dictionary) is the verb of Romans 8, where the Spirit “pleads for holy ones according to God” (8:27) and Christ “also pleads for us” (8:34); the root stands in six verses (entynchanō, searched), the other three being Acts 25:24, Hebrews 7:25 and the compound at 8:26. The preposition there is hyper, “on behalf of”; here it is kata, “against.” The shelf: KJV “maketh intercession to God against Israel” uses for Elijah the English the same version uses at 8:27 and 8:34 (“maketh intercession for the saints”, “maketh intercession for us”). ASV “pleadeth with God against Israel” has another word here, and so has NWT 1984 “pleads with God against Israel”; Douay “calleth on God against Israel”, Geneva “communeth with God against Israel” and NIV “appealed to God against Israel” each choose their own; and the TLB “was complaining to God about the Jews” says what it concludes.

The apparatus has one note at the end of the verse: the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont adds legōn, “saying,” after Israēl, and the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition do not. KJV “against Israel, saying,” and Geneva “against Israel, saying,” carry the word; the others do not. The SBLGNT closes the verse with a question mark, so that how Elijah “pleads with God against Israel” is still part of the question, and the speech of verse 3 follows it with no verb of saying.

Verses 3–6 · Κύριε, τοὺς προφήτας σου ἀπέκτειναν … ὁ χρηματισμός … Κατέλιπον ἐμαυτῷ ἑπτακισχιλίους ἄνδρας … λεῖμμα κατʼ ἐκλογὴν χάριτος Kyrie, tous prophētas sou apekteinan … ho chrēmatismos … Katelipon emautō heptakischilious andras … leimma kat’ eklogēn charitos — Romans 11:3–6: “I left for myself seven thousand men.” Elijah’s complaint, a divine answer, a Baal with a feminine article, and a remnant that is the work of grace

“Lord, they have killed your prophets, they have dug down your altars, and I was left, alone” (Kyrie, tous prophētas sou apekteinan, ta thysiastēria sou kateskapsan, kagō hypeleiphthēn monos, Romans 11:3). The speech is Elijah’s at 1 Kings 19:10 and, in the same Hebrew words, at 19:14 (1 Kings 19 is not yet on these pages; the Hebrew and the Greek are checked). Paul changes it in three ways. He turns it round: the Hebrew has the sons of Israel forsaking the covenant, tearing down the altars and killing the prophets “with the sword,” and Paul has the prophets first, the altars second, and neither the covenant nor the sword. He leaves out its first clause. Elijah begins qanno qinneti la-YHVH, “I have been very zealous for Jehovah, God of armies,” and the Greek Old Testament has zēlōn ezēlōka tō Kyriō pantokratori (checked); the root is the root of “a zeal for God” in 10:2 and of “make jealous” in 10:19, in verses 11 and 14 of this chapter and in 1 Corinthians 10:22, and Paul quotes none of it. And he addresses the speech to God: the Hebrew has no vocative, and the Greek Old Testament has “the Lord Almighty” as the object of the zeal (in the third person), so Kyrie is Paul’s, and this page prints “Lord” for it as it does for the vocative at 10:16. NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah, they have killed your prophets”; KJV, ASV, Douay and NIV print “Lord.” The Greek verb for the altars, kataskaptō, “dig down,” is the verb of the Greek Old Testament’s 1 Kings 19:10 (checked), and in the New Testament it stands in two verses (kataskaptō, searched): this one, and Acts 15:16, where the same root is the “ruins” of David’s tent that God will rebuild. The last clause, zētousin tēn psychēn mou, is “they are seeking my life” (the Hebrew adds “to take it”); the word psychē is printed “life” where it means a life. The shelf: KJV “they seek my life”, ASV “they seek my life” and Douay “they seek my life”; NWT 1984 “they are looking for my soul”; NIV “they are trying to kill me.” The apparatus has one note in the verse: Robinson and Pierpont read kai ta thysiastēria, “and your altars,” where the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition read ta thysiastēria with no conjunction; KJV “and digged down thine altars”, Geneva “and dug down thine Altars” and NIV “and torn down your altars” have the “and”; ASV “they have digged down thine altars”, Douay “they have dug down thy altars” and NWT 1984 “they have dug up your altars” do not.

“But what does the divine answer say to him?” (alla ti legei autō ho chrēmatismos, Romans 11:4). Chrēmatismos is a noun that stands in one verse of the New Testament (chrēmatismos, searched). It belongs to the verb chrēmatizō (dictionary), which in this library already means “being warned in a dream” at Matthew 2:12 and, in the participle, “it had been disclosed to him by the Holy Spirit” at Luke 2:26; the noun names what is given in such an answer, a response (reported, not checked in a lexicon). The shelf: KJV “the answer of God”, ASV “the answer of God” and Geneva “the answer of God”; Douay “the divine answer” (as this page prints it); NIV “God’s answer”; NWT 1984 “the divine pronouncement”; the TLB “how God replied.”

“I left for myself seven thousand men, who did not bend a knee to Baal” (Katelipon emautō heptakischilious andras, hoitines ouk ekampsan gony tē Baal, Romans 11:4). The answer is 1 Kings 19:18. The Hebrew is vehish’arti beYisrael shiv’at alafim, “and I have left in Israel seven thousand,” with “all the knees which have not bowed to Baal, and every mouth which has not kissed him” to follow; the Greek Old Testament has kai kataleipseis en Israēl hepta chiliadas andrōn (checked), “and you will leave in Israel seven thousand men.” Paul has the first person and the aorist, katelipon, “I left,” so that the future has become a thing done; and he adds emautō, “for myself,” which neither text has. The numeral heptakischilioi is one word and stands in one verse of the New Testament (heptakischilioi, searched). The only thing Paul says about the seven thousand is what God did with them: he left them. KJV “I have reserved to myself” and NIV “I have reserved for myself” keep the “for myself” and change the verb; ASV “I have left for myself” and NWT 1984 “I have left seven thousand men over for myself” keep the verb; Douay “I have left me seven thousand men” and Geneva “I have reserved unto myself” have the pronoun; the TLB “I have seven thousand others besides you who still love me and have not bowed down to idols!” turns a census into a reassurance.

The name has a feminine article. The Greek is tē Baal, “to the Baal,” with tē and not the masculine tō. The Greek Old Testament (in Brenton’s printed text, checked) has the masculine tō Baal at 1 Kings 19:18 itself, and the feminine in Jeremiah (tē Baal at 2:8, 7:9, 19:5 and 23:27; tēs Baal at 2:23 and 23:13) and in Zephaniah (tēs Baal at 1:4). The text does not say why. The explanation usually offered (reported, not checked) is that readers said bosheth, “shame,” in place of the name. The Hebrew Bible does show that substitution in names: Saul’s son is Eshbaal in 1 Chronicles 8:33 and Ish-bosheth in 2 Samuel 2:8, and Jonathan’s son is Merib-baal in 1 Chronicles 8:34 and Mephibosheth in 2 Samuel 4:4 (in each pair the two names are usually read as one man). And Hosea has Jehovah say, of the day to come, “you will call me Ishi, and you will call me Baali no more. For I will take away the names of the Baals out of her mouth” (Hosea 2:18–19 in the Hebrew numbering, 2:16–17 in English; my translation; not yet on these pages). This page prints “Baal” and leaves the article to the note. For the god, the encyclopedia entry is Baal, and for the prophet Elijah.

“A remnant, according to a choosing of grace” (leimma kat’ eklogēn charitos, Romans 11:5). Leimma (dictionary) stands in one verse of the New Testament (leimma, searched), and it is one of five words from the root “leave” in this stretch of the letter: hypoleimma, “remnant,” at 9:27; enkatelipen, “had left us a seed,” at 9:29; hypeleiphthēn, “I was left,” in verse 3; katelipon, “I left,” in verse 4; and this noun. The phrase en tō nyn kairō, “at the present time,” is the phrase of 3:26 (“to demonstrate his righteousness at the present time”) and, with a genitive, of 8:18 (“the sufferings of the present season”, where this page prints “season”); it stands in four verses (nyn kairō, searched), the fourth being 2 Corinthians 8:14. Eklogē, “choosing” (dictionary), stands in seven verses of the New Testament (eklogē, searched): Acts 9:15 (“a vessel of choosing,” printed “a chosen vessel”), Romans 9:11 (“the purpose of God according to choosing”), this verse, verses 7 and 28, 1 Thessalonians 1:4 and 2 Peter 1:10. What the phrase adds is “of grace,” the next verse’s subject. KJV “according to the election of grace”, ASV “according to the election of grace” and Douay “according to the election of grace” print the Latinate noun; NIV “a remnant chosen by grace” turns the noun into a participle; NWT 1984 “according to a choosing due to undeserved kindness” keeps the noun and glosses “grace”; the TLB “a few being saved as a result of God’s kindness in choosing them.”

“But if by grace, no longer out of works, since then grace no longer becomes grace” (ei de chariti, ouketi ex ergōn, epei hē charis ouketi ginetai charis, Romans 11:6). The Greek has no verb in the first clause and the conjunction epei, “since,” with nothing after it to say “otherwise,” so the sentence reads as an argument from its own result: if the choosing were out of works, grace would stop being grace. The pair of nouns is the pair of 4:4 (“to the one who works the wage is not reckoned according to grace but according to debt”) and of 9:11 and 9:32 (“as out of works”). The apparatus has a long reading here. The Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont adds a second sentence: ei de ex ergōn, ouketi estin charis, epei to ergon ouketi estin ergon, “But if out of works, it is no longer grace, since the work is no longer work.” The SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition do not have it, and the shelf splits on it. KJV “But if it be of works, then is it no more grace: otherwise work is no more work” and Geneva “but if it be of works, it is no more grace: or else were work no more work” print it. The other five have the first sentence alone, and for its end the shelf reads ASV “otherwise grace is no more grace”, KJV “otherwise grace is no more grace”, NWT 1984 “otherwise, the undeserved kindness no longer proves to be undeserved kindness”, NIV “if it were, grace would no longer be grace”; and the TLB “it isn’t free when it is earned.”

Verses 7–10 · τί οὖν; ὃ ἐπιζητεῖ Ἰσραήλ … ἐπωρώθησαν … Ἔδωκεν αὐτοῖς ὁ θεὸς πνεῦμα κατανύξεως … Γενηθήτω ἡ τράπεζα αὐτῶν εἰς παγίδα ti oun? ho epizētei Israēl … epōrōthēsan … Edōken autois ho theos pneuma katanyxeōs … Genēthētō hē trapeza autōn eis pagida — Romans 11:7–10: “the rest were hardened.” A “spirit of stupor” put together from Deuteronomy and Isaiah, and a table that becomes a trap, from David

“What Israel is seeking, this it did not obtain, but the choosing obtained it” (ho epizētei Israēl, touto ouk epetychen, hē de eklogē epetychen, Romans 11:7). The tenses carry a story: Israel is seeking (present) and did not obtain (aorist), as at 9:31, where Israel, “pursuing a law of righteousness, did not arrive at law.” “The choosing” is the abstract noun of verse 5 standing for the people it picked out, as the shelf reads it: KJV “the election hath obtained it” and ASV “the election obtained it” keep the abstraction; NIV “The elect among them did” and NWT 1984 “the ones chosen obtained it” say who. The last clause has the verb pōroō (dictionary, under pōrōsis), and the root stands in eight verses of the New Testament (pōro-, searched): the noun at Mark 3:5, verse 25 of this chapter and Ephesians 4:18; the verb at Mark 6:52 and 8:17 (the disciples’ hearts), John 12:40, here, and 2 Corinthians 3:14 (“their minds were hardened,” with “until today” a few words later, as in verse 8). It is a different verb from the one in 9:18, “whom he wishes he hardens” (sklērynei). Here the verb is a passive with no stated agent, and verse 8 is where the agent appears. The shelf has three words for the one verb: ASV “were hardened”, Geneva “have been hardened” and NIV “were hardened”; KJV “were blinded”, Douay “have been blinded” and the TLB “the eyes of the others have been blinded” (which moves the verb to the eyes of the next verse); and NWT 1984 “had their sensibilities blunted.”

“God gave them a spirit of stupor, eyes not to see and ears not to hear, until this very day” (Edōken autois ho theos pneuma katanyxeōs, ophthalmous tou mē blepein kai ōta tou mē akouein, heōs tēs sēmeron hēmeras, Romans 11:8). The verse is a quotation that Paul has put together from two places, and the two are not in the same book. The back half is Deuteronomy 29:3 (29:4 in English Bibles), which this site prints “But Jehovah has not given you a heart to know, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, until this day.” The Greek Old Testament has it in the same order, kai ouk edōke Kyrios ho Theos hymin kardian eidenai, kai ophthalmous blepein, kai ōta akouein heōs tēs hēmeras tautēs (in Brenton’s printed text, checked). Paul has “eyes … ears … until the day,” and the verb “gave” (edōken), but the negative has gone: Moses says Jehovah has not given a heart to know, eyes to see or ears to hear, and Paul says God gave eyes and ears of a particular kind. The front half, “a spirit of stupor,” is katanyxis from Isaiah 29:10, in the Greek pepotiken hymas Kyrios pneumati katanyxeōs, “the Lord has given you to drink a spirit of stupor” (checked). The Hebrew there is ruach tardemah, “a spirit of deep sleep,” and the noun tardemah stands in six verses of the Hebrew Bible (searched): the sleep God lets fall on the human at Genesis 2:21 and on Abram at Genesis 15:12, Isaiah 29:10, Job 4:13 and 33:15, and Proverbs 19:15. The Greek noun katanyxis stands in one verse of the New Testament (katanyxis, searched), and its verb katanyssomai, “be pierced,” in one, Acts 2:37, where the crowd, hearing Peter, “were pierced to the heart.” The same family of words names the numbness in this verse and the sting in that one, and the Greek does not say that the stupor is permanent: “until this very day” is the day of Moses’s sentence, carried forward to Paul’s. The apparatus has one note here, kathōs against kathaper (the SBLGNT, Nestle–Aland and Robinson–Pierpont have the first, Westcott–Hort and Tregelles the second), both “just as,” and the English does not change. The shelf for the noun: KJV “the spirit of slumber” and Geneva “the spirit of slumber”; ASV “a spirit of stupor” and NIV “a spirit of stupor”; Douay “the spirit of insensibility”; NWT 1984 “a spirit of deep sleep”, the Hebrew sense; and the TLB “God has put them to sleep, shutting their eyes and ears.” The KJV also sets the whole quotation in brackets and puts “unto this day” outside them, so that the words belong to “were blinded” of verse 7 and not to the quoted sentence; the other versions leave them inside it. NIV “eyes that could not see and ears that could not hear” turns Paul’s tou mē blepein, “so as not to see,” into a statement of inability.

“Let their table become a snare and a trap and a stumbling-block and a repayment to them” (Genēthētō hē trapeza autōn eis pagida kai eis thēran kai eis skandalon kai eis antapodoma autois, Romans 11:9). “And David says” points to the psalm called David’s, Psalm 69 (verses 22–23 in English Bibles, 23–24 in the Hebrew numbering and 68:23–24 in the Greek; not yet on these pages). The Hebrew is yehi shulchanam lifnehem lefach, velishlomim lemoqesh, “Let their table before them become a snare, and for their peace a trap” (my translation), and the Greek is Genēthētō hē trapeza autōn enōpion autōn eis pagida, kai eis antapodosin, kai eis skandalon, three things and no “trap” (checked). Paul has four: he drops “before them,” adds thēra, “a hunting, a catching,” a noun that stands in one verse of the New Testament (thēra, searched), and uses antapodoma where the Greek psalm has antapodosis. The Hebrew velishlomim can be read with the vowels of shalom, “peace,” or of shillumim, “repayments” (reported, not checked in a lexicon), and the Greek psalm took the second; “repayment” is its word and Paul’s. It is a word the chapter returns to: antapodoma stands in two verses of the New Testament (antapodoma, searched), this one and Luke 14:12, and the verb of verse 35, antapodothēsetai, “it will be repaid,” belongs to the same family. In the psalm, the verse just before the curse has the sufferer given gall for food and vinegar for thirst (Greek 68:22, checked), so the table that becomes a trap is set in answer to a poisoned meal. Paul quotes this psalm again at Romans 15:3 (not yet on these pages), “The reproaches of those who reproach you fell on me,” which is the second half of Psalm 69:10 in the Hebrew numbering, and John 2:17 quotes the first half, ho zēlos tou oikou sou katafagetai me, “the zeal for your house will devour me” (Greek checked; the Hebrew is qin’at beitkha), the same root as qinneti in Elijah’s speech and as the zeal and jealousy of this part of the letter. The shelf: KJV “Let their table be made a snare, and a trap, and a stumblingblock, and a recompence unto them” and ASV “Let their table be made a snare, and a trap, And a stumblingblock, and a recompense unto them” have the four nouns; Geneva “a snare, and a net, and a stumbling block, even for a recompense” changes the second; Douay “a snare, and a trap, and a stumbling block, and a recompense”; NIV “May their table become a snare and a trap, a stumbling block and a retribution for them”; NWT 1984 “Let their table become for them a snare and a trap and a stumbling block and a retribution”; and the TLB “Let their good food and other blessings trap them into thinking all is well between themselves and God” explains what the table is meant to be.

“And always bend their back over” (kai ton nōton autōn dia pantos synkampson, Romans 11:10). The first half of the verse is Psalm 69:24 (Hebrew numbering), tēchshachnah eineihem mere’ot, “let their eyes be darkened so that they do not see,” in the Greek Skotisthētōsan hoi ophthalmoi autōn tou mē blepein (checked), and Paul takes the whole line from the Greek psalm without change, apart from writing dia pantos as two words. The second half has the verb synkamptō, “bend together,” which stands in one verse of the New Testament (synkampson, searched), where the Hebrew has umotneihem tamid ham’ed, “and make their loins shake continually” (my translation). (The verb that Luke 13:11 uses for the woman who “was bent over,” synkyptō, is another word.) KJV “bow down their back alway” and ASV “bow thou down their back always” keep the verb; NIV “their backs be bent forever” makes it a passive; NWT 1984 “always bow down their back” follows the Greek. Paul does not say what the bent back carries.

Verses 11–15 · Λέγω οὖν, μὴ ἔπταισαν ἵνα πέσωσιν … πόσῳ μᾶλλον τὸ πλήρωμα αὐτῶν … ἐθνῶν ἀπόστολος … τίς ἡ πρόσλημψις εἰ μὴ ζωὴ ἐκ νεκρῶν Legō oun, mē eptaisan hina pesōsin … posō mallon to plērōma autōn … ethnōn apostolos … tis hē proslēmpsis ei mē zōē ek nekrōn — Romans 11:11–15: “they did not trip so that they might fall, did they?” A trespass that is riches, a fullness that is more, an apostle who glorifies his ministry in order to make his own people jealous, and “life from the dead”

“I say, then, they did not trip so that they might fall, did they? May it never be!” (Legō oun, mē eptaisan hina pesōsin? mē genoito, Romans 11:11). The fourth “I say” question in two chapters (10:18, 19, 11:1, this one), and the second to be answered by mē genoito. The verb ptaiō (dictionary) is “trip” or “stumble,” and it is not the verb of 9:32–33, where Israel “stumbled on the stone of stumbling” (proskoptō, proskomma); this page prints “trip” so that the two stay two. It stands in four verses of the New Testament (ptaiō, searched): here, James 2:10 and 3:2 (“for we all trip in many ways”; James 2 and 3 not yet on these pages) and 2 Peter 1:10 (“you will never stumble”). Paul asks about the purpose of the tripping, hina pesōsin, “so that they might fall,” with the verb piptō, which comes back at verse 22 as the participle pesontas, “those who fell,” and the shelf reads the clause three ways. ASV “Did they stumble that they might fall?” and KJV “Have they stumbled that they should fall?” read it as purpose; NWT 1984 “Did they stumble so that they fell completely?” reads result and adds “completely”; NIV “Did they stumble so as to fall beyond recovery?” adds “beyond recovery.” The Greek has no word for “completely” or for “recovery.” Paul’s own answer is another purpose clause, eis to parazēlōsai autous, “to make them jealous,” the verb of the Moses quotation at 10:19 (dictionary): what Moses threatened in Deuteronomy 32:21 is here the stated aim. The same verb is used in 1 Corinthians 10:22 (not yet on these pages) with the Lord as its object (“Or are we making the Lord jealous?”). The sequence of the sentence, a people who do not obey and a salvation that goes to the nations, is the one Luke narrates in Acts 13:46 and 28:28.

“Their trespass … their diminishment … their fullness” (to paraptōma autōn … to hēttēma autōn … to plērōma autōn, Romans 11:12). The three nouns are a ladder. Paraptōma (dictionary) is the noun of the trespasses for which Jesus “was handed over” (4:25) and of Adam’s trespass in 5:15–18 and 20; it stands in seventeen verses of the New Testament (paraptōma, searched). Hēttēma (dictionary) stands in two verses (hēttēma, searched): here and 1 Corinthians 6:7, where lawsuits among believers are already “altogether a defeat for you.” Its range is defeat, loss and lessening, and Isaiah 31:8 in the Greek has it for the Hebrew lamas, “forced labor” (reported, not checked in a lexicon): where the Hebrew has the young men of Assyria becoming lamas, the Greek has hoi de neaniskoi esontai eis hēttēma (checked; Isaiah 31 is not yet on these pages). This page prints “diminishment” because the next noun is a fullness and the ladder needs a smaller thing before it. The shelf: KJV “the diminishing of them”, Geneva “the diminishing of them” and Douay “the diminution of them” are close; ASV “their loss” and NIV “their loss” take the second sense; NWT 1984 “their decrease.” Plērōma (dictionary) is what fills something, and the shelf splits on what is filled: KJV “their fulness”, ASV “their fulness” and Douay “the fulness of them” keep the noun; Geneva “their abundance”; NWT 1984 “the full number of them” reads it as a count; NIV “their full inclusion” reads it as an event; and the TLB “when the Jews, too, come to Christ.” The noun comes back at verse 25 for the nations, and the verse closes on posō mallon, “how much more,” which stands in eight verses of the New Testament (posō mallon, searched), two in Romans, here and verse 24; chapter 5’s “much more” is a different Greek phrase, pollō mallon. The Greek ends the verse with a full stop and no verb. KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva end it with a question mark; NIV and NWT 1984 end it with an exclamation mark, as this page does. “Riches” is the noun of 2:4 (“the riches of his kindness”) and 9:23 (“the riches of his glory”), and the verb “being rich” is said of the Lord in 10:12.

“But I am speaking to you, the nations. So then, as long as I am an apostle of nations, I glorify my ministry” (Hymin de legō tois ethnesin. eph’ hoson men oun eimi egō ethnōn apostolos, tēn diakonian mou doxazō, Romans 11:13). The letter has said at its opening that Paul wanted fruit “among the rest of the nations” also (1:13), and here he turns from the people he has been speaking about to the nations in the congregation in Rome. The apparatus has two notes. The Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont has gar, “for,” where the SBLGNT has de, “but,” at the start of the verse, and leaves out oun, “so then”: KJV “For I speak to you Gentiles, inasmuch as I am the apostle of the Gentiles” has both of the Byzantine readings, ASV “But I speak to you that are Gentiles. Inasmuch then as I am an apostle of Gentiles” has both of the other. The words eph’ hoson stand in eight verses of the New Testament (eph’ hoson, searched). In five of the other seven this library prints “as long as”: Matthew 9:15 (“as long as the bridegroom is with them”), Romans 7:1 (“for as long as he lives”), 1 Corinthians 7:39 and Galatians 4:1 (those two with chronon, “time,” after the words; not yet on these pages) and 2 Peter 1:13 (“as long as I am in this tent,” with eimi as here and no chronon). In the other two, Matthew 25:40 and 25:45, it is “in so far as.” This page prints “as long as” for the same words with the same verb as Matthew 9:15 and 2 Peter 1:13; the other reading, “inasmuch as,” is the shelf’s: KJV “inasmuch as”, ASV “Inasmuch then as”, NIV “Inasmuch as” and NWT 1984 “Forasmuch as”; only Douay “as long indeed as” takes the time sense. The genitive ethnōn is printed “of nations” with no article, as ASV “an apostle of Gentiles”; KJV and Douay “the apostle of the Gentiles” add “the,” and NIV “the apostle to the Gentiles” adds it and changes the preposition.

“If somehow I might make jealous those who are my flesh, and save some from among them” (ei pōs parazēlōsō mou tēn sarka kai sōsō tinas ex autōn, Romans 11:14). The sentence is Paul’s own use of the verb he has just put in God’s purpose: the aim of the nations’ salvation is jealousy (verse 11), and the aim of Paul’s ministry to them is the same. “My flesh” is his kin, those he called “my relatives according to the flesh” in 9:3; KJV “them which are my flesh”, ASV “them that are my flesh” and NWT 1984 “[those who are] my own flesh” keep the noun, and NIV “my own people” does not. “Save some” is the sentence of 1 Corinthians 9:22 (hina pantōs tinas sōsō, “so that by all means I might save some”; not yet on these pages), in the same words in a different order. Ei pōs, “if somehow,” is the hope of a man who does not know the result, as in Philippians 3:11 (ei pōs katantēsō, “if somehow I might arrive”; not yet on these pages).

“For if their casting away is the reconciliation of the world, what is the receiving, if not life from the dead?” (ei gar hē apobolē autōn katallagē kosmou, tis hē proslēmpsis ei mē zōē ek nekrōn, Romans 11:15). Apobolē stands in two verses of the New Testament (apobolē, searched), here and Acts 27:22, where it is the “loss” of life that Paul says no one on the ship will suffer; proslēmpsis stands in one (proslēmpsis, searched); and katallagē (5:10 has the verb) stands in four verses (katallagē, searched): 5:11 (“through whom we have now received the reconciliation”), this one, and 2 Corinthians 5:18 and 5:19 (not yet on these pages). Paul says “the reconciliation of the world,” as 5:10 said “we were reconciled to God” of those who were enemies. “Life from the dead” (zōē ek nekrōn) is a phrase this letter uses of two things: of Christ, raised “from the dead” (10:7, 10:9), and of the readers, who are to present themselves “as alive from the dead” (6:13). Whether the reception of Israel is the resurrection at the end, a revival among the nations, or a figure for either, the verse does not say. The TLB “It will be like dead people coming back to life” chooses the figure. The other six versions on the shelf print the phrase the same way: KJV “life from the dead”, ASV “life from the dead”, Douay “life from the dead”, Geneva “life from the dead”, NIV “life from the dead” and NWT 1984 “life from the dead.” For the first noun KJV “the casting away of them”, ASV “the casting away of them” and NWT 1984 “the casting of them away” keep the noun, Douay “the loss of them” takes the Acts 27 sense, and NIV “their rejection” takes the sense of verse 1; for the second, KJV and ASV “the receiving of them” and NIV “their acceptance.”

Verses 16–18 · εἰ δὲ ἡ ἀπαρχὴ ἁγία, καὶ τὸ φύραμα … ἀγριέλαιος ὢν ἐνεκεντρίσθης ἐν αὐτοῖς … μὴ κατακαυχῶ τῶν κλάδων … οὐ σὺ τὴν ῥίζαν βαστάζεις ἀλλὰ ἡ ῥίζα σέ ei de hē aparchē hagia, kai to phyrama … agriélaios ōn enekentristhēs en autois … mē katakauchō tōn kladōn … ou sy tēn rhizan bastazeis alla hē rhiza se — Romans 11:16–18: “if the firstfruit is holy, the lump also; and if the root is holy, the branches also.” A portion of dough from Numbers, an olive tree from the prophets, and a branch told not to boast

“And if the firstfruit is holy, the lump also; and if the root is holy, the branches also” (ei de hē aparchē hagia, kai to phyrama; kai ei hē rhiza hagia, kai hoi kladoi, Romans 11:16). Two pictures, with no verbs in either. The first comes from the law of the dough. Numbers 15:19–21 has Israel, when it eats the bread of the land, set apart a portion, which this site prints “The first of your dough — a loaf — you shall lift out as a contribution” (15:20) and “From the first of your dough you shall give to Jehovah a contribution, throughout your generations” (15:21). The Hebrew is reshit arisoteichem, “the first of your dough,” and the Greek Old Testament has aparchēn phyramatos hymōn, “a firstfruit of your lump” (Numbers 15:21, in Brenton’s printed text, checked): Paul’s two nouns, aparchē and phyrama, are the two nouns of that verse in Greek. Aparchē (dictionary) stands in nine verses of the New Testament (aparchē, searched): the Spirit as firstfruits at Romans 8:23 (“having the firstfruits of the Spirit”), Christ at 1 Corinthians 15:20 and 15:23, this verse, and five more (Romans 16:5, 1 Corinthians 16:15, 2 Thessalonians 2:13, James 1:18 and Revelation 14:4; those seven not yet on these pages). Phyrama (dictionary) stands in five verses (phyrama, searched): the lump of clay at Romans 9:21, this lump of dough, and the lump that leaven works through at 1 Corinthians 5:6, 5:7 and Galatians 5:9. Numbers does not say what the portion does for the rest of the dough; Paul’s sentence says that if the portion is holy, so is the lump. It does not say who the firstfruit and the root are. Three answers are read (reported, no vote): the patriarchs, since verse 28 says the people are “beloved on account of the fathers” and 9:5 says “whose are the fathers”; the remnant of verse 5, the first Jews to believe; and Christ, who is called a firstfruit at 1 Corinthians 15:20 and 15:23. The shelf: KJV “if the firstfruit be holy, the lump is also holy”, Douay “if the firstfruit be holy, so is the lump also” and NWT 1984 “if the [part taken as] firstfruits is holy, the lump is also” keep the pair of nouns; NIV “the part of the dough offered as firstfruits is holy, then the whole batch is holy” explains the picture; and the TLB “since Abraham and the prophets are God’s people, their children will be too” answers the question this note leaves open.

“Some of the branches were broken off, and you, being a wild olive, were grafted in among them” (tines tōn kladōn exeklasthēsan, sy de agriélaios ōn enekentristhēs en autois, Romans 11:17). The olive is a picture the Hebrew Bible already uses of the people. Jeremiah 11:16 (not yet on these pages) has zayit ra’anan yefeh peri-to’ar qara YHVH shemekh, “A leafy olive tree, fair with goodly fruit, Jehovah called your name” (my translation), and goes on to say that fire has been kindled on it and vera’u daliyyotav, “its branches are broken”; the Greek Old Testament has the verb ēchreiōthēsan, “were made useless,” and not Paul’s “broken off” (checked). And Hosea 14:7 (14:6 in English) promises, “his branches shall spread, and his beauty shall be as the olive tree” (not yet on these pages). The richness of the next phrase is also the olive’s own: the noun piotēs, “fatness, richness,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (piotēs, searched), and in the fable of the trees in Judges 9:9 the olive says, hechadalti et-dishni asher-bi yekabbedu Elohim va’anashim, “Should I leave my fatness, by which God and men are honored?” (Hebrew checked; the Greek there has tēn piotēta mou; Judges 9 is not yet on these pages). The fatness the olive will not leave is read as its oil (reported). The verbs of the graft are all in this chapter. Ekklaō, “break off,” stands in three verses of the New Testament (ekklaō, searched), 17, 19 and 20 of this chapter; enkentrizō (dictionary), “graft in,” in four (enkentrizō, searched), 17, 19, 23 and 24; and agriélaios (dictionary), “wild olive,” in two, 17 and 24 (agriélaios, searched). The noun klados, “branch,” stands in eleven verses of the New Testament (klados, searched), six in the Gospels, where the mustard seed becomes a tree whose branches shelter the birds (Matthew 13:32, Mark 4:32, Luke 13:19), the crowd cuts branches for the road (Matthew 21:8) and the fig tree’s branch becomes tender (Matthew 24:32, Mark 13:28); the other five are verses 16, 17, 18, 19 and 21 here. The phrase en autois is “among them” (or “in them”), and the Greek for “in their place” would be anti autōn. The shelf: KJV “wert graffed in among them”, ASV “wast grafted in among them”, NWT 1984 “were grafted in among them” and NIV “grafted in among the others” keep “among”; Douay “art ingrafted in them”; Geneva “wast grafted in for them” and the TLB “put in to replace” (verse 18) turn it into replacement. The next phrase has an apparatus note: the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition read tēs rhizēs tēs piotētos tēs elaias, “of the root of the richness of the olive,” and Tregelles and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont have kai between the first two nouns, “the root and the richness.” KJV “the root and fatness of the olive tree” and Geneva “the root and fatness of the Olive tree” carry the “and”; ASV “the root of the fatness of the olive tree” and NWT 1984 “the olive’s root of fatness” do not; NIV “the nourishing sap from the olive root” turns the genitive into a flow.

“Do not boast over the branches” (mē katakauchō tōn kladōn, Romans 11:18). The boasting is an old target in this letter: the one who “boasts in God” (kauchasai en theō, 2:17) and “boasts in the law” (kauchasai, 2:23), and the question “Where, then, is the boasting? It has been shut out” (3:27). The form here is the same second-person kauchasai with kata- in front, “down on,” and the verb stands in three verses of the New Testament (katakauchaomai, searched): this one, where it is said twice, and James 2:13 and 3:14 (not yet on these pages). The ones told not to do it have changed: at 2:17 and 2:23 it is the Jew boasting in his privileges, and here it is the branch that came from the wild olive. The second sentence reverses the picture of a tree: ou sy tēn rhizan bastazeis alla hē rhiza se. Bastazō, “bear,” is the verb of a load, as in Acts 15:10, where Peter calls the yoke on the disciples one “that neither our fathers nor we have been able to bear” (Acts 15:10). The shelf: KJV “Boast not against the branches” and Douay “Boast not against the branches”; ASV “glory not over the branches”; Geneva “Boast not thyself against the branches”; NWT 1984 “do not be exulting over the branches”; NIV “do not consider yourself to be superior to those other branches” changes the verb to a judgment; and the TLB “you must be careful not to brag about being put in to replace the branches that were broken off.” For the second sentence ASV “it is not thou that bearest the root, but the root thee”, NWT 1984 “it is not you that bear the root, but the root [bears] you” and KJV “thou bearest not the root, but the root thee” keep the verb; NIV “You do not support the root, but the root supports you.”

Verses 19–24 · ἐρεῖς οὖν· Ἐξεκλάσθησαν κλάδοι … εἰ γὰρ ὁ θεὸς τῶν κατὰ φύσιν κλάδων οὐκ ἐφείσατο … ἴδε οὖν χρηστότητα καὶ ἀποτομίαν θεοῦ … παρὰ φύσιν ἐνεκεντρίσθης εἰς καλλιέλαιον ereis oun: Exeklasthēsan kladoi … ei gar ho theos tōn kata physin kladōn ouk epheisato … ide oun chrēstotēta kai apotomian theou … para physin enekentristhēs eis kallielaion — Romans 11:19–24: “God’s kindness and severity.” An objector, a verb used of the Son, kindness that depends on remaining, and a graft “contrary to nature”

“You will say, then, ‘Branches were broken off so that I might be grafted in’” (ereis oun: Exeklasthēsan kladoi hina egō enkentristhō, Romans 11:19). The objector of 9:19 (Ereis moi oun, “You will say to me, then”) has come back, and this time the objection is the nations’ boast. Paul’s answer is one word, kalōs, and the shelf has four of them: KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva “Well”, NIV “Granted”, NWT 1984 “All right!”, and the TLB “Watch out!” This page prints “Well said,” with a verb the Greek leaves out. He grants the fact and changes its cause: not “so that I might be grafted in” but “by unbelief they were broken off, but you stand by faith.” The pair is apistia and pistis, the pair printed “unfaithfulness” and “faithfulness” in 3:3, where the subject is God’s, and “unbelief” and “faith” in 4:20, where Abraham “did not doubt through unbelief.” Hestēkas, “you stand,” is the perfect of the verb at 5:2 (“this grace in which we stand,” hestēkamen), and the standing is set against the falling of verses 11 and 22. The verse ends on an order: mē hypsēla phronei, alla phobou, “Do not set your mind on high things, but fear.” The verb phroneō is the verb of 8:5 (“set their minds on the things of the flesh”), and ta hypsēla phronountes, “setting their minds on the high things,” is the phrase of Romans 12:16, with the compound hypsēlophronein at 1 Timothy 6:17 (the three verses, hypsēla phron-, searched; 1 Timothy 6:17 not yet on these pages). The apparatus has one note: Robinson and Pierpont read the compound here, hypsēlophronei, and the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition read the two words. KJV “Be not highminded, but fear”, ASV “Be not highminded, but fear”, Douay “be not highminded, but fear” and Geneva “be not high-minded, but fear” have the one-word sense; NWT 1984 “Quit having lofty ideas, but be in fear” keeps the phrase; NIV “Do not be arrogant, but tremble”; the TLB “Do not be proud; be humble and grateful—and careful.” The objector in the TLB is made to add a conclusion the Greek does not give him: “so I must be pretty good.”

“For if God did not spare the branches according to nature, neither will he spare you” (ei gar ho theos tōn kata physin kladōn ouk epheisato, oude sou pheisetai, Romans 11:21). Ouk epheisato is the clause of 8:32, “He who did not spare his own Son but handed him over for us all.” The exact words stand in four verses of the New Testament (ouk epheisato, searched): Romans 8:32, this verse, and 2 Peter 2:4 and 2:5, where God “did not spare angels who sinned” and “did not spare the ancient world” (those two not yet on these pages). The apparatus has a note on the end: the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort and Tregelles read oude sou pheisetai, “neither will he spare you,” and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and the Byzantine text of Robinson and Pierpont read mēpōs oude sou pheisetai, “lest perhaps he will not spare you either.” The shelf follows the split. ASV “neither will he spare thee”, NIV “he will not spare you either” and NWT 1984 “neither will he spare you” read the first; KJV “take heed lest he also spare not thee”, Geneva “take heed, lest he also spare not thee” and Douay “fear lest perhaps he also spare not thee” read the second. The words kata physin, “according to nature,” are printed here as the Greek has them (the shelf’s “natural branches” hides the phrase), because the stem of physis stands in six verses of Romans (physis, searched in Romans): 1:26 (twice, “natural use” and “contrary to nature”), 1:27, 2:14 (“by nature”), 2:27 (“from nature”), and verses 21 and 24 here, where it stands four times.

“See, then, God’s kindness and severity” (ide oun chrēstotēta kai apotomian theou, Romans 11:22). Chrēstotēs (dictionary), “kindness,” stands in eight verses of the New Testament (chrēstotēs, searched), three in Romans: 2:4, where the riches of God’s kindness are what the judge despises and “the kindness of God is leading you to repentance”; 3:12, “there is no one who does kindness”; and this verse, where it is said three times. Its pair is apotomia (dictionary), which stands in this verse twice and nowhere else in the New Testament as a noun; the root stands in three verses (apotom-, searched), the other two being the adverb apotomōs, “severely,” at 2 Corinthians 13:10 (not yet on these pages) and Titus 1:13. It is built, reference works say, from the verb “cut off” (reported, not checked in a lexicon), and the sentence ends with the verb ekkoptō, “cut off”: the severity is a cutting. The verb stands in the Gospels in John the Baptist’s warning to those who say “We have Abraham as father” (Matthew 3:9): “the axe lies at the root of the trees; so every tree not producing good fruit is cut down and thrown into fire” (3:10; also Luke 3:9). The kindness is conditional: ean epimenēs tē chrēstotēti, “if you remain in the kindness.” Epimenō, “remain,” is the verb of 6:1 (“Shall we remain in sin?”), and it has three objects in this letter: sin (6:1), kindness (here) and unbelief (verse 23). The apparatus has three notes at the verse, none of which changes the English except one: Robinson and Pierpont read chrēstotēta, “kindness,” and the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition read chrēstotēs theou, “God’s kindness,” for the second kindness. ASV “toward thee, God’s goodness”, Douay “towards thee, the goodness of God” and NWT 1984 “toward you there is God’s kindness” have “God”; KJV “but toward thee, goodness”, Geneva “but toward thee, bountifulness” and NIV “kindness to you” do not. The other two notes are of spelling and form (apotomia against apotomian, epimenēs against epimeinēs). The shelf for the pair: KJV and ASV “goodness and severity”, Douay “the goodness and the severity”, Geneva “the bountifulness, and severity”, NIV “the kindness and sternness”, NWT 1984 “God’s kindness and severity”, and the TLB “God is both kind and severe.”

“God is able to graft them in again” (dynatos gar estin ho theos palin enkentrisai autous, Romans 11:23–24). “Able” is the word of Abraham’s conviction at 4:21 (“what he has promised he is also able to do”), and the sentence turns on the direction of the graft. The phrase para physin, “contrary to nature,” stands in two verses of the New Testament (para physin, searched), Romans 1:26 and here. At 1:26 the phrase names the end of an exchange made by people whom God has handed over (1:24–26); here it names what the grafter does, and the sentence does not treat the graft as a fault: it is the thing Paul sets before a how-much-more. A wild shoot set into a cultivated tree is the opposite of the usual practice, as modern writers on this verse report from ancient farming handbooks (reported, not checked here), and Paul does not argue from farming; he says himself that the graft is “contrary to nature,” and the point is the argument from the harder to the easier, posō mallon, “how much more” (the second of the two in Romans; verse 12 has the first). Kallielaios, “cultivated olive,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (kallielaios, searched), and the wild olive is its pair. The apparatus has two notes in verse 23, the spelling of “remain” (epimenōsi against epimeinōsin) and the order of “is God” (estin ho theos against ho theos estin); neither changes the English. The shelf: KJV “a good olive tree”, ASV “a good olive tree” and Douay “the good olive tree”; Geneva “a right Olive tree”; NIV “a cultivated olive tree”; NWT 1984 “the garden olive tree.” The last word of the verse, tē idia elaia, “their own olive,” has the adjective of 10:3, where Israel sought to establish tēn idian, “their own” righteousness; the “own” of this verse is the olive that was theirs from the start. For the end of the verse KJV “be graffed into their own olive tree” and ASV “be grafted into their own olive tree” keep it; and the TLB “don’t you see that he will be far more ready to put the Jews back again, who were there in the first place?” says the argument out.

Verses 25–27 · Οὐ γὰρ θέλω ὑμᾶς ἀγνοεῖν, ἀδελφοί, τὸ μυστήριον τοῦτο … πώρωσις ἀπὸ μέρους τῷ Ἰσραὴλ γέγονεν ἄχρι οὗ τὸ πλήρωμα τῶν ἐθνῶν εἰσέλθῃ, καὶ οὕτως πᾶς Ἰσραὴλ σωθήσεται … Ἥξει ἐκ Σιὼν ὁ ῥυόμενος Ou gar thelō hymas agnoein, adelphoi, to mystērion touto … pōrōsis apo merous tō Israēl gegonen achri hou to plērōma tōn ethnōn eiselthē, kai houtōs pas Israēl sōthēsetai … Hēxei ek Siōn ho rhyomenos — Romans 11:25–27: “and thus all Israel will be saved.” A mystery with a limit built into it, the word “thus,” an “all Israel” the letter has already divided once, and a quotation put together from two chapters of Isaiah

“For I do not want you to be ignorant of this mystery, brothers” (Ou gar thelō hymas agnoein, adelphoi, to mystērion touto, Romans 11:25). The formula “I do not want you to be ignorant, brothers” stands in six verses of the New Testament (agnoein, searched): Romans 1:13 (“that I often intended to come to you”), this verse, 1 Corinthians 10:1 and 12:1, 2 Corinthians 1:8 and 1 Thessalonians 4:13 (the others not yet on these pages; the last two are in the plural, “we”). In each it opens a piece of information the writer wants his readers to have. Mystērion (dictionary) in Paul is an open secret, something long hidden and now stated, and this one is stated in the clause that follows. The reason given is hina mē ēte heautois phronimoi, “so that you may not be wise in yourselves,” and the phrase has a source: al-tehi chakham be’einekha, “Do not be wise in your own eyes” (Proverbs 3:7; Hebrew checked), which the Greek has as Mē isthi phronimos par’ seautō, and hoi synetoi en heautois, “the understanding in themselves,” in the woe of Isaiah 5:21 (Hebrew chakhamim be’eineihem; neither chapter is yet on these pages). Paul says it again, nearly in these words, at Romans 12:16: mē ginesthe phronimoi par’ heautois (Greek checked). The apparatus has one note on the phrase: the SBLGNT (as Holmes prints it) has the dative alone, heautois phronimoi; Westcott–Hort and Tregelles put en before it; the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and Robinson–Pierpont put par’, the preposition of Proverbs 3:7 and Romans 12:16. KJV “wise in your own conceits”, ASV “wise in your own conceits” and Douay “wise in your own conceits” are one reading; Geneva “arrogant in yourselves”; NIV “conceited”; NWT 1984 “discreet in your own eyes.” For the noun itself KJV, ASV, Douay and NIV have “mystery”, Geneva “secret”, NWT 1984 “sacred secret”, and the TLB “truth from God.”

The mystery has three parts in one sentence: pōrōsis apo merous tō Israēl gegonen, “a hardening in part has happened to Israel”; achri hou, “until”; and to plērōma tōn ethnōn eiselthē, “the fullness of the nations comes in.” Pōrōsis is the noun that goes with the verb of verse 7 (dictionary). Apo merous, “in part,” stands in five verses of the New Testament (apo merous, searched): here, Romans 15:15 (“I wrote to you more boldly, in part”), 15:24 (“if I am first satisfied with you in part”), 2 Corinthians 1:14 (“you have understood us in part”) and 2 Corinthians 2:5 (Romans 15 and 2 Corinthians 2 not yet on these pages; the Greek is checked). In the other four it limits an amount, “partly,” and here the Greek can mean that the hardening is partial in who (the “rest” of verse 7) or partial in degree; the time limit is not in these two words but in the clause after them. The same opening words, achri hou, with “the nations” and a verb of filling, are in Luke 21:24: “Jerusalem will be trampled underfoot by the nations, until the times of the nations are fulfilled” (achri hou plērōthōsin kairoi ethnōn). I report the likeness without calling either verse a quotation of the other. The shelf: KJV “blindness in part is happened to Israel, until the fulness of the Gentiles be come in”, Douay “blindness in part has happened in Israel” and Geneva “partly obstinacy is come to Israel” name the hardening something else; ASV “a hardening in part hath befallen Israel” and NIV “a hardening in part” keep it; NWT 1984 “a dulling of sensibilities has happened in part to Israel until the full number of people of the nations has come in” moves the “in part” and reads plērōma as a count; and the TLB “some of the Jews have set themselves against the Gospel now, but this will last only until all of you Gentiles have come to Christ.”

“And thus all Israel will be saved” (kai houtōs pas Israēl sōthēsetai, Romans 11:26). Two words carry the verse, and the library reports the readings and does not vote. The first is houtōs, “thus, in this way.” Read as manner, the verse says that all Israel will be saved by the route the previous verses have laid out: a hardening for a time, the nations coming in, jealousy. Read as sequence, it says “and then.” The shelf is divided in the same way: KJV “And so all Israel shall be saved”, ASV “and so all Israel shall be saved”, Douay “And so all Israel should be saved” and Geneva “And so all Israel shall be saved” print “and so,” which in English can be either; NIV “and in this way all Israel will be saved” and NWT 1984 “and in this manner all Israel will be saved” choose manner; the TLB “And then all Israel will be saved” chooses sequence. This page prints “thus.” The second is pas Israēl, which stands in one verse of the New Testament (pas Israēl, searched). The letter has already said, of the name, that “not all who are out of Israel — these are Israel” (9:6), and in this chapter “Israel” has been the people that sought and did not obtain (verse 7), the people to whom a hardening has happened (verse 25) and now this “all.” The Hebrew phrase kol Yisrael stands in 149 verses of the Hebrew Bible (searched; the letters, not the lemma); in Joshua 7:25, now on these pages, it is the people as one actor: “all Israel pelted him with stones.” Four readings are held (reported): the nation as a whole at the end; the nation as a body, without every person in it; the chosen of Israel in every age, the remnant of verse 5 drawn out; and Israel and the nations together as one people. The verb is a future passive, sōthēsetai, and the verse does not say when, by what, or whether the sentence is a promise to a generation or to a people.

“The one who rescues will come out of Zion; he will turn ungodliness away from Jacob” (Hēxei ek Siōn ho rhyomenos, apostrepsei asebeias apo Iakōb, Romans 11:26). The quotation is Isaiah 59:20, with a change in its first line. The Hebrew is uva letsiyon goel, ulshavei fesha beYaakov, “And a redeemer will come to Zion, and to those who turn from transgression in Jacob” (my translation; Isaiah 59 is not yet on these pages). The Greek Old Testament has Kai hēxei heneken Siōn ho rhyomenos, kai apostrepsei asebeias apo Iakōb, “And for Zion’s sake the one who rescues will come, and he will turn ungodliness away from Jacob” (in Brenton’s printed text, checked). So the Hebrew has “to Zion,” the Greek Old Testament “for Zion’s sake,” and Paul ek Siōn, “out of Zion”; and the Hebrew’s “those who turn” has become, in the Greek and in Paul, a deliverer who turns ungodliness away. Rhyomai is the verb of 7:24, “Who will rescue me from the body of this death?”, and the participle ho rhyomenos, “the one who rescues,” stands in two verses of the New Testament (ho rhyomenos, searched; the roaring lion of 1 Peter 5:8 is a different word): here and 1 Thessalonians 1:10, where it is said of Jesus, “who rescues us from the coming wrath.” “Ungodliness” is the noun that opens the charge at 1:18, “against all ungodliness and unrighteousness of people,” here in the plural. The apparatus has one note: Robinson and Pierpont add kai before apostrepsei, “and he will turn away.” KJV “the Deliverer, and shall turn away ungodliness from Jacob” has the “and”; ASV “the Deliverer; He shall turn away ungodliness from Jacob”, Douay “he that shall deliver, and shall turn away ungodliness”, Geneva “The deliverer shall come out of Zion, and shall turn away the ungodliness from Jacob”, NIV “The deliverer will come from Zion; he will turn godlessness away from Jacob”, NWT 1984 “The deliverer will come out of Zion and turn away ungodly practices from Jacob” and the TLB “There shall come out of Zion a Deliverer, and he shall turn the Jews from all ungodliness.”

“And this is the covenant from me for them, when I take away their sins” (kai autē autois hē par’ emou diathēkē, hotan aphelōmai tas hamartias autōn, Romans 11:27). The first clause is the first clause of Isaiah 59:21 in the Greek, word for word: Kai autē autois hē par’ emou diathēkē, eipe Kyrios, “And this is the covenant from me for them, said the Lord” (checked; the Hebrew is va’ani zot briti otam, “And as for me, this is my covenant with them”). The second clause is a different verse: hotan aphelōmai tēn hamartian autou, “when I take away his sin,” of Isaiah 27:9 in the Greek (checked), where the Hebrew has zeh kol-peri hasir chatato, “and this is all the fruit of the removal of his sin” (my translation); Paul has the plural “their sins.” The nearest sentence in the Hebrew Bible about a covenant with forgiveness in it is Jeremiah 31:31–34 in English Bibles (31:30–33 in the Hebrew numbering this site follows): “I will forgive their guilt, and remember their sin no more” (31:34 in English), in a passage the Greek calls diathēkēn kainēn, “a new covenant” (31:31 in English, Greek 38:31, checked). Paul’s sentence does not call the covenant new; the adjective is the one on the cup in Luke 22:20, “the new covenant in my blood,” and in 1 Corinthians 11:25 (not yet on these pages). Diathēkē is in the dictionary (entry). KJV “For this is my covenant unto them, when I shall take away their sins”, ASV “And this is my covenant unto them, When I shall take away their sins”, Douay “And this is to them my covenant”, NIV “And this is my covenant with them when I take away their sins” and NWT 1984 “And this is the covenant on my part with them, when I take their sins away” are one sentence; the TLB “At that time I will take away their sins, just as I promised” turns the covenant into a promise.

Verses 28–32 · κατὰ μὲν τὸ εὐαγγέλιον ἐχθροὶ διʼ ὑμᾶς, κατὰ δὲ τὴν ἐκλογὴν ἀγαπητοὶ διὰ τοὺς πατέρας … ἀμεταμέλητα γὰρ τὰ χαρίσματα … συνέκλεισεν γὰρ ὁ θεὸς τοὺς πάντας εἰς ἀπείθειαν ἵνα τοὺς πάντας ἐλεήσῃ kata men to euangelion echthroi di’ hymas, kata de tēn eklogēn agapētoi dia tous pateras … ametameleta gar ta charismata … synekleisen gar ho theos tous pantas eis apeitheian hina tous pantas eleēsē — Romans 11:28–32: “the gifts and the calling of God are not things he regrets.” Enemies and beloved at once, a word about God that has no regret in it, a mercy that goes round the circle, and “all” shut up so that “all” may be shown mercy

“According to the good news, they are enemies on account of you; but according to the choosing, they are beloved on account of the fathers” (kata men to euangelion echthroi di’ hymas, kata de tēn eklogēn agapētoi dia tous pateras, Romans 11:28). The sentence is two kata clauses, “according to,” each with its dia, “on account of.” The first noun, euangelion, is the good news the letter began with; the second, eklogē, “choosing” (dictionary), is the noun of verses 5 and 7. Echthroi, “enemies,” is the word of 5:10 (“while we were enemies, we were reconciled to God”), and as there it can be read two ways: enemies of God, or enemies whom God is against; the sentence does not say whose enemies, and the shelf does not agree. The TLB “enemies of the Gospel. They hate it” makes them enemies of the good news; the ASV “enemies for your sake”, the NIV “enemies for your sake”, the KJV “enemies for your sakes” and the NWT 1984 “enemies for YOUR sakes” say nothing about whose. “The fathers” are the fathers of 9:5 (“whose are the fathers”), and NIV “the patriarchs” and NWT 1984 “their forefathers” name them differently from KJV and Geneva “the fathers’ sakes”, ASV “the fathers’ sake” and Douay “the sake of the fathers.” The verse also bears on verse 16: the people are “beloved on account of the fathers” as the branches are holy because of the root.

“For the gifts and the calling of God are not things he regrets” (ametameleta gar ta charismata kai hē klēsis tou theou, Romans 11:29). Ametamelētos (dictionary) is a- plus the verbal adjective of metamelomai, “feel regret afterwards” (dictionary). It stands in two verses of the New Testament (ametamelēt-, searched), this one and 2 Corinthians 7:10, which says that grief “according to God” works “a repentance to salvation not to be regretted” (not yet on these pages). Whose regret it is, the verse does not say beyond “of God,” and this page prints it as the regret of the giver. Charisma, “gift,” stands in six verses of Romans (charisma, searched): 1:11, 5:15–16, 6:23 (“the gracious gift of God is eternal life”), this verse, and 12:6. The shelf: KJV “are without repentance”, Douay “are without repentance” and Geneva “are without repentance” use the old English for regret; ASV “are not repented of”; NWT 1984 “are not things he will regret” (this page prints the same words in the present, “he regrets”); NIV “irrevocable” names the consequence; and the TLB “can never be withdrawn; he will never go back on his promises.”

“So these also have now been disobedient, by your mercy, so that they themselves also may now be shown mercy” (houtōs kai houtoi nyn ēpeithēsan tō hymeterō eleei hina kai autoi nyn eleēthōsin, Romans 11:30–31). The two verses are a mirror. Apeitheō (dictionary), “disobey,” is the verb of 10:21’s “disobedient and contradicting people,” and the noun and verb stand four times in verses 30–32 (ēpeithēsate, apeitheia, ēpeithēsan, apeitheian); the root of eleos, “mercy,” and its verb stand four times (ēleēthēte, eleei, eleēthōsin, eleēsē). The sentence of 9:15–18, “I will have mercy on whomever I have mercy,” “on whom he wishes he has mercy,” and the “vessels of mercy” of 9:23, comes round to its widest form in verse 32. The dative tō hymeterō eleei, “your mercy,” can attach to three places, and the shelf shows the three. With “may be shown mercy” (“by your mercy they may be shown mercy”): KJV “that through your mercy they also may obtain mercy”, ASV “that by the mercy shown to you they also may now obtain mercy” and NIV “as a result of God’s mercy to you.” With “disobedient” (“disobedient with mercy resulting to you”): NWT 1984 “have been disobedient with mercy resulting to YOU”, Geneva “have they not believed by the mercy showed unto you” and Douay “have not believed, for your mercy.” And with the verb itself as its object, “disobedient to your mercy,” which none of the seven versions on the shelf prints. This page prints “by your mercy” between commas, so that it can go either way. The verb of verse 30 is the aorist ēpeithēsate, “you disobeyed,” and the shelf splits it in two: ASV “were disobedient to God”, NIV “were at one time disobedient to God” and NWT 1984 “were once disobedient to God” print “disobedient”; KJV “have not believed God”, Douay “did not believe God” and Geneva “have not believed God” print “believed.” The apparatus has two notes. In verse 30 Robinson and Pierpont read gar kai, “for also,” and the others gar; Douay “For as you also in times past” has the “also.” In verse 31 the second nyn, “now,” is read by the SBLGNT, Westcott–Hort and the Nestle–Aland 28th edition and left out by Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont: ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 “now” have it; KJV “that through your mercy they also may obtain mercy” and Douay “that they also may obtain mercy” do not.

“For God shut up all together into disobedience, so that he might show mercy to all” (synekleisen gar ho theos tous pantas eis apeitheian hina tous pantas eleēsē, Romans 11:32). The verb synkleiō (dictionary) stands in four verses of the New Testament (synkleiō, searched): Luke 5:6, where the nets enclose “a great number of fish” (this library prints “they caught”); this verse; and Galatians 3:22 and 3:23 (not yet on these pages), where the subject of the first is not God but the scripture: “the scripture shut up all things under sin.” The shape is the same, a shutting up and then a hina, a purpose. The two tous pantas, “the all,” are identical, and the verse does not say whether they mean each person or the two groups of the last verses, “you” and “they”; 3:9 had charged “that Jews and Greeks alike are all under sin,” and 5:20 had said that where sin increased, grace abounded far more. The shelf: KJV “concluded them all in unbelief” and Douay “concluded all in unbelief”; ASV “shut up all unto disobedience” and Geneva “shut up all in unbelief”; NIV “bound everyone over to disobedience”; NWT 1984 “shut them all up together in disobedience” keeps the prefix syn-; and the TLB “God has given them all up to sin.”

Verses 33–36 · Ὦ βάθος πλούτου καὶ σοφίας καὶ γνώσεως θεοῦ … Τίς γὰρ ἔγνω νοῦν κυρίου … ἢ τίς προέδωκεν αὐτῷ … ὅτι ἐξ αὐτοῦ καὶ διʼ αὐτοῦ καὶ εἰς αὐτὸν τὰ πάντα Ō bathos ploutou kai sophias kai gnōseōs theou … Tis gar egnō noun kyriou … ē tis proedōken autō … hoti ex autou kai di’ autou kai eis auton ta panta — Romans 11:33–36: “O the depth of riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!” A sentence that cannot be parsed one way, ways without footprints, two quotations that stop short, and three prepositions

“O the depth of riches and wisdom and knowledge of God!” (Ō bathos ploutou kai sophias kai gnōseōs theou, Romans 11:33). The Greek is a depth followed by three nouns in the genitive joined by “and,” with “of God” at the end, and English cannot hold all its readings at once. Is it the depth of one thing, riches, which is of wisdom and knowledge (“the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God”)? Or the depth of three things, riches and wisdom and knowledge? The shelf is split exactly there. KJV “the riches both of the wisdom and knowledge of God”, ASV “the riches both of the wisdom and the knowledge of God”, Douay “the riches of the wisdom and of the knowledge of God”, Geneva “the riches, both of the wisdom, and knowledge of God” and NIV “the riches of the wisdom and knowledge of God” make the depth that of the riches; NWT 1984 “the depth of God’s riches and wisdom and knowledge” makes it three; the TLB “How great are his wisdom and knowledge and riches!” drops the depth. This page prints the Greek order and its three “and”s. Bathos stands in six verses of the New Testament (bathos, searched), two in Romans: 8:39, where “depth” is one of the things that will not be able to separate us from the love of God, and this one. The related plural bathē is at 1 Corinthians 2:10 (“the depths of God”; not yet on these pages). Ploutos, “riches,” has been the letter’s word for what God has in kindness (2:4), in glory (9:23), and in what the world and the nations receive (verse 12).

“How unsearchable his judgments, and how untraceable his ways!” (hōs anexeraunēta ta krimata autou kai anexichniastoi hai hodoi autou, Romans 11:33). Two negative adjectives. Anexeraunētos stands in one verse of the New Testament (anexeraunēta, searched), and anexichniastos (dictionary) in two (anexichniast-, searched), this one and Ephesians 3:8, “the unsearchable riches of the Christ” (not yet on these pages). The second is built, reference works say, on ichnos, “footprint” (reported, not checked in a lexicon), and the noun is in the letter once before, at 4:12, where believers are those who “walk in the footsteps of the faith that our father Abraham had.” (The word stands in three verses of the New Testament, ichn-, searched: that one, 2 Corinthians 12:18 and 1 Peter 2:21.) The letter has one set of footprints it tells people to follow and a set of ways with none. The adjective is the Greek Job’s: ho poiōn megala kai anexichniasta, “who does great and untraceable things,” in Job 5:9 and 9:10 (checked; the Hebrew has ein cheqer, “there is no searching”; neither chapter is yet on these pages). The sentence also has the sound of Isaiah 55:8–9: “your ways are not my ways … so are my ways higher than your ways” (Hebrew checked; the Greek has hai hodoi mou), though Paul does not quote it. KJV “how unsearchable are his judgments, and his ways past finding out”, ASV “his ways past tracing out”, NIV “his paths beyond tracing out” and NWT 1984 “past tracing out his ways” keep the second adjective as a different word from the first; Douay “How incomprehensible are his judgments, and how unsearchable his ways!” uses “unsearchable” for the ways, the word the others use for the judgments; and the TLB “How impossible it is for us to understand his decisions and his methods!”

“For who has known the mind of Jehovah? Or who became his counselor?” (Tis gar egnō noun kyriou? ē tis symboulos autou egeneto?, Romans 11:34). The first question is Isaiah 40:13, which this site prints “Who has directed the spirit of Jehovah, or has instructed him as his counselor?” The Greek Isaiah has Tis egnō noun Kyriou? kai tis autou symboulos egeneto, hos symbibā auton? (checked): noun, “mind,” for the Hebrew ruach, “spirit,” and Paul has the Greek almost word for word (his “or who” for “and who”), ending before “who will instruct him.” Paul quotes the whole Greek line, with the last clause and a comment, in 1 Corinthians 2:16: tis gar egnō noun kyriou, hos symbibasei auton? hēmeis de noun Christou echomen, “but we have the mind of Christ” (checked; not yet on these pages). Symboulos stands in one verse of the New Testament (symboulos, searched). This page prints “Jehovah” for kyriou, as the Hebrew of Isaiah 40:13 names him, and as NWT 1984 does, “who has come to know Jehovah’s mind, or who has become his counselor?”, against KJV “who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor?”, ASV “who hath known the mind of the Lord? or who hath been his counsellor?”, NIV “Who has known the mind of the Lord? Or who has been his counselor?” and the TLB “who among us can know the mind of the Lord?”

“Or who has given to him beforehand, and it will be repaid to him?” (ē tis proedōken autō, kai antapodothēsetai autō, Romans 11:35). The second question is Job 41:3 in the Hebrew numbering (41:11 in English): mi hiqdimani va’ashallem, “Who has come before me, that I should repay? Whatever is under the whole heaven is mine,” and the speaker is Jehovah, answering Job out of the whirlwind (Job 38:1, 40:6; Hebrew checked; Job 41 is not yet on these pages). The Greek Job does not have the sentence: it reads Ē tis antistēsetai moi kai hypomenei?, “Or who will stand against me and endure?” (checked), so Paul is closer to the Hebrew than to the Greek here. Prodidōmi, “give beforehand,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (proedōken, searched), and the verb “repay” is the family of antapodoma in verse 9 (the table that becomes a repayment); it comes again at Romans 12:19, egō antapodōsō, “I will repay” (Greek checked). The shelf: KJV “Or who hath first given to him, and it shall be recompensed unto him again?”, ASV “or who hath first given to him”, Douay “Or who hath first given to him, and recompense shall be made him?”, Geneva “Or who hath given unto him first, and he shall be recompensed?”, NWT 1984 “Who has first given to him, so that it must be repaid to him?”, NIV “Who has ever given to God, that God should repay them?” and the TLB “who could ever offer to the Lord enough to induce him to act?”

“Because from him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory forever. Amen” (hoti ex autou kai di’ autou kai eis auton ta panta; autō hē doxa eis tous aiōnas, amēn, Romans 11:36). Three prepositions, ex, dia and eis, “from, through, to,” and the pronoun “him” is the God of verse 33 and the Jehovah of verse 34. The same three small words, in the same style, are in 1 Corinthians 8:6 (not yet on these pages; Greek checked), “one God, the Father, from whom are all things and we to him, and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things and we through him,” in Colossians 1:16 (“all things have been created through him and for him,” di’ autou kai eis auton, of the Son), and in Hebrews 2:10 (“for whom are all things and through whom are all things,” of the one who is bringing many sons to glory; Greek checked; not yet on these pages). The sentences distribute the formula differently, and this page reports where each attaches it. The doxology ends the way two earlier sentences of the letter end, eis tous aiōnas, amēn, “forever. Amen,” at 1:25 (“the Creator, who is blessed forever”) and 9:5 (“God blessed forever”): one at the end of the charge against the nations, one after Israel’s privileges, and this one at the end of the argument that began at chapter 9. The next sentence of the letter is Parakalō oun hymas, adelphoi, dia tōn oiktirmōn tou theou, “I urge you, then, brothers, by the mercies of God” (Romans 12:1; Greek checked). The shelf: KJV “For of him, and through him, and to him, are all things: to whom be glory for ever”, ASV “For of him, and through him, and unto him, are all things”, Douay “For of him, and by him, and in him, are all things”, Geneva “For of him, and through him, and for him are all things”, NIV “For from him and through him and for him are all things”, NWT 1984 “Because from him and by him and for him are all things” and the TLB “For everything comes from God alone. Everything lives by his power, and everything is for his glory.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “pushed away” for apōtheō (vv. 1–2), as at Acts 7:27, 7:39 and 13:46; “knew beforehand” for proginōskō (v. 2), as at 8:29 and Acts 26:5; “pleads” for entynchanō (v. 2), as at 8:27 and 8:34; “Lord” for the vocative the Hebrew does not have (v. 3), as at 10:16; “remnant” for leimma (v. 5), as for hypoleimma at 9:27; “choosing” for eklogē (vv. 5, 7, 28), as at 9:11; “hardened” for pōroō (v. 7), kept apart from the “hardens” of sklērynō at 9:18; “stupor” for katanyxis (v. 8); “repayment” for antapodoma (v. 9) and “repaid” for its verb (v. 35); “trip” for ptaiō (v. 11), kept apart from “stumbled” for proskoptō at 9:32; “trespass” for paraptōma (vv. 11–12), as at 4:25 and 5:15–20; “diminishment” for hēttēma and “fullness” for plērōma (vv. 12, 25); “as long as” for eph’ hoson (v. 13), as at Matthew 9:15 and Romans 7:1; “casting away” for apobolē (v. 15); “firstfruit” for aparchē (v. 16), as at 8:23; “lump” for phyrama (v. 16), as at 9:21; “wild olive,” “cultivated olive” and “grafted” (vv. 17, 24); “boast over” for katakauchaomai (v. 18), from the “boast” of 2:17; “according to nature” and “contrary to nature” for kata physin and para physin (vv. 21, 24), as at 1:26; “kindness” for chrēstotēs (v. 22), as at 2:4, and “severity” for apotomia; “remain” for epimenō (vv. 22–23), as at 6:1; “mystery” for mystērion (v. 25); “thus” for houtōs (v. 26); “the one who rescues” for ho rhyomenos (v. 26), from the “rescue” of 7:24; “disobedient” for apeitheō (vv. 30–32), as at 10:21; “shown mercy” for eleeō (vv. 30–32); “shut up” for synkleiō (v. 32); “unsearchable” for anexeraunētos and “untraceable” for anexichniastos (v. 33); and “Jehovah” for kyrios in the Isaiah question (v. 34), as in the Isaiah quotations of 9:28–29. Six pairs are split on purpose: ptaiō and proskoptō; pōroō and sklērynō; kata physin and para physin; chrēstotēs and apotomia; anexeraunētos and anexichniastos; and aparchē (a first portion) and phyrama (the lump it stands for).
