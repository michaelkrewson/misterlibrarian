Warned in dreams, Joseph flees with Mary and Jesus to Egypt, then later settles the family in Nazareth rather than returning to Judea.
c. 6-4 BC, before Herod the Great's death in 4 BC, which the chapter's own narrative requires since Herod is alive and reigning throughout it — the same infancy period chapter 1 opens on, continuing directly from it.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · magi from the east, and a king who was born one

⚠ “MAGI” — not wise men, not kings, and Matthew never says how many. Magoi was a technical word long before it was a legend: the Magi were a hereditary priestly caste of the Medes and Persians, keepers of ritual and readers of the sky. The Greek translations of Daniel use this very word for one of the classes of Babylonian diviners a king summons — the guild our Daniel 2:2 lists as “the magicians, the enchanters, the sorcerers, and the Chaldeans,” and over which Daniel himself was set as “chief prefect over all the wise men of Babylon” (Daniel 2:48). By the first century the word could also slide downhill into plain sorcerer, which is how Acts uses it of Bar-Jesus. ⚠ So these men are neither philosophers nor monarchs: they are foreign astrologer-priests, and they are the only people in this chapter who act on the answer. KJV and Geneva “wise men”; Douay “wise men”; ASV “Wise-men”; NIV “Magi”; NWT “astrologers” — the bluntest of the shelf about the actual trade, though it also passes a judgment the Greek itself does not. Mister keeps magi, the word itself, and lets the note carry the trade. ⚠ And the count: there are three gifts, never three men. The names Caspar, Melchior and Balthasar first surface in a Latin list of about the sixth century, and “kings” grew out of Psalm 72:10–11 and Isaiah 60:3–6 — tradition, and worth labelling as tradition.

⚠ “At its RISING” — or “in the east”? Anatolē means a rising — of the sun, and so, by extension, the east. Verse 1 has the plural, “from the risings,” which is plainly geography: they came from the east. But verse 2 has the singular with the article, en tē anatolē, and in Greek astronomical writing that is the technical phrase for a star’s heliacal rising — its first reappearance in the dawn sky after weeks of invisibility. So the magi may not be telling Herod where they were, but what the star did. The shelf divides three ways: KJV and Douay “in the east”; ASV “in the east,” with a margin reading “at its rising”; NIV “when it rose”; NWT “when we were in the east,” a third possibility taking the phrase of the observers’ own position. Mister reads “at its rising” and prints the alternatives, because the choice decides whether the sentence is geography or astronomy.

⚠ A king who was BORN one — standing in front of a king who was appointed. The question is sharper in Greek than in English: “where is the one born king of the Jews?” Herod was not born a king of anything. An Idumean, son of Antipater, he was voted “king of the Jews” by the Roman Senate in 40 BC and had to take Jerusalem by siege three years later to collect the title. Foreigners walk into his capital and ask after a king with a better claim than his, and they do it without knowing they have done it. ⚠ The title is also a bracket: “king of the Jews” is used of Jesus at only two moments in this Gospel — here, by Gentiles who have come to kneel, and at the cross, where Gentiles nail it over his head as a joke (27:37). The first strangers to say the words bow; the last ones laugh.

⚠ “To BOW DOWN before him” — the most fought-over verb in the chapter. Proskyneō is a body-word: to fall forward, to prostrate oneself, to do homage. Greek used it of gods and of kings alike, and Matthew himself uses it both ways — of a slave on his face before his master (18:26) and of the disciples in the boat saying “truly you are the Son of God” (14:33). KJV, ASV and NIV read “worship” — defensible, but note that the KJV also has the slave “worship” his lord in 18:26, because in 1611 the English word still covered homage to a human superior, which it no longer does. NWT reads “do obeisance”: ⚠ lexically available, and closer to the gesture — but the NWT applies it to Jesus while keeping “worship” for God, a distinction the Greek word does not itself make, so it is a doctrinal decision and is named here as one. Mister prints the gesture, “bow down before,” in every mouth that uses it — including Herod’s in verse 8 — and leaves what the gesture means to the reader, which is where the argument actually lives.

Verses 3–6 · a shaken city, and an answer stitched out of two prophets

“He was SHAKEN — and all Jerusalem with him.” Etarachthē is the verb for water stirred up and for a soul in turmoil. The detail that should stop a reader is the second half. Jerusalem is the city of the promise; word that its king has been born ought to be the best news it ever had. Instead the city is shaken with Herod — the first quiet notice in this Gospel that the Messiah’s own capital will not want him. KJV and ASV “he was troubled”; NIV “he was disturbed”; NWT “he was agitated.”

“Where the Christ WAS TO BE BORN” — and the Greek is a present tense. Gennātai, “is born”: not a question about a coming event but about a standing fact of Scripture — where does the Messiah get born? Herod assembles “all the chief priests and the scribes of the people” (their first appearance in Matthew; their last is at the trial), and they answer at once, from memory, and correctly — and then not one of them walks the six miles to look. ⚠ The chapter’s hardest irony is that the machinery of Israel’s learning works perfectly. The men with a star and no Scripture travel a thousand miles; the men with the Scripture and no star stay home.

⚠ The quotation is not quite Micah’s wording — and its last clause is not Micah at all. Micah 5:2 in English Bibles (5:1 in the Hebrew) says of Bethlehem that it is “too little to be among the thousands of Judah.” Matthew’s Greek says something close to the opposite: “by no means least among the rulers of Judah.” Three things are going on, and this translation prints Matthew as Matthew wrote him rather than harmonizing him back to the Hebrew:

— “thousands” becomes “rulers.” The Hebrew is alfei, thousands — and so clans, since a “thousand” was a clan-sized unit of a tribe. Its near relation is alluf, “chief” — the word this library met among the chiefs of Edom (Genesis 36) — from the same root: a clan, and the man at the head of it. Matthew’s “rulers” reads that root the other way. It is a live reading of the Hebrew, not a slip.

— “too little” becomes “by no means least.” Either Micah’s clause is being read as a rhetorical question (are you too little?), or it is being read in the light of what has now happened there: the town that was least is least no longer. Readings differ, and both are old.

— ⚠ The last clause comes out of a different book. “Who will shepherd my people Israel” is not in Micah. It is what was said to David when the tribes came to Hebron to crown him: “you shall shepherd my people Israel” (2 Samuel 5:2, repeated at 1 Chronicles 11:2). So the answer handed to Herod is Micah’s town with David’s job description stitched onto the end — the right birthplace fused to the right commission, which is exactly the claim chapter 1 spent seventeen verses of genealogy making. KJV “that shall rule my people Israel”; ASV “shall be shepherd of my people Israel”; NIV and NWT “who will shepherd.” The Greek poimanei is a shepherd’s word that also means to rule, and the KJV keeps the ruling while losing the flock.

Verses 7–8 · secretly, and exactly

⚠ “SECRETLY” — the same word as one chapter ago, pointing the opposite way. Lathra occurs twice in Matthew’s opening chapters and nowhere else in his Gospel. In 1:19 Joseph, “being righteous and unwilling to expose her to disgrace, resolved to divorce her lathra” — quietly, to shield a woman from a public penalty. Here Herod is “lathra calling the magi” — secretly, to fix the location of a child he intends to kill. One adverb, two chapters, mercy and murder, and Matthew never comments. It is the cleanest instance in this Gospel of a single word doing the work of a character sketch.

“EXACTLY” — and the exactness turns into a body count. Akriboō is a rare verb meaning to know a thing to the last detail, and in the New Testament it appears only in this chapter — twice — with its adverb standing between. Herod “ascertained exactly the time” (v7); he tells the magi to “search out exactly concerning the child” (v8); and then he kills every boy “from two years old and under, according to the time he had ascertained exactly from the magi” (v16). The precision he extracts in verse 7 is the width of the slaughter in verse 16, and the repetition is how the chapter says so without saying it. KJV “enquired of them diligently” and “search diligently”; ASV “learned of them carefully”; NIV “found out from them the exact time”; NWT “carefully ascertained.” Mister uses one English word in all three places so the chain stays audible.

“So that I too may come and bow down before him.” The liar borrows the pilgrims’ own verb (see the note on verse 2), and his sentence is true of every other person in the chapter and false of himself. ⚠ Worth noticing what he does not do: he sends no soldier, no servant, no informer. A king who has just convened the chief priests cannot spare one man to walk six miles to Bethlehem — because the whole plan rests on the secrecy of verse 7, and it is the secrecy, not the arithmetic, that fails.

Verses 9–11 · the star that stood, the house, and three gifts

⚠ The star “went before them… and STOOD over where the child was.” No object in the sky can single out a house, and the honest place to begin is by saying so. Two of the chapter’s astronomical verbs, though, are ordinary technical language: a star “rising” (vv2, 9) is the vocabulary of star-watchers, and a planet genuinely does appear to halt — its “station” — where its apparent motion reverses at the turn of retrograde. What has been proposed, with pedigrees: Kepler (1603) reasoned from a great conjunction to the triple Jupiter–Saturn conjunction in Pisces of 7 BC; a Jupiter–Venus conjunction of 2 BC has its advocates; Chinese records note a comet in 5 BC (Halley’s, in 12 BC, is too early); and one modern line reads the sky by the rules Roman astrology actually used, making the sign Jupiter’s occultation by the moon in Aries in 6 BC. Others hold that the light was never an astronomical body at all. ⚠ The library does not vote. What can be said flatly is this: the chapter’s language is the language of the trade, and Matthew reports the sign without ever endorsing the trade — which the Law forbids to Israel (Deuteronomy 18:10–14) and Isaiah mocks in Babylon (Isaiah 47:13). God is shown here speaking to these men in the only language they could read.

⚠ “Into the HOUSE” — not a stable, and perhaps not a newborn. Oikia, a house. Matthew has no inn, no manger and no shepherds — those are Luke’s, and Luke has no magi; the two accounts never overlap. By the time these visitors arrive the family is under a roof, and Herod’s decree “from two years old and under,” calculated from the star, allows a gap of as much as two years since the birth. The Christmas card that gathers magi and shepherds around one manger is a harmony of two stories, not a scene either evangelist wrote. Every version on the shelf reads “house” here — KJV, ASV, NIV and NWT alike. The stable is in the pictures, not the text.

“They rejoiced with a very great JOY.” A cognate accusative — literally “they rejoiced a joy” — a Hebrew way of intensifying that Greek writes only when it is following a Semitic idiom. Kept here rather than smoothed away, as this translation keeps “swarm with swarms” in Genesis. It is also the only unmixed happiness in the chapter.

Gold and frankincense and myrrh — three gifts, and the arithmetic that made three kings. Frankincense and myrrh are aromatic resins tapped from desert trees of Arabia and East Africa, carried north by caravan and priced accordingly; both belonged to worship and to burial. ⚠ The gifts are the seed of the whole legend: three gifts became three givers, and Psalm 72:10–11 (“the kings of Sheba and Seba shall bring gifts… all kings shall bow down”) together with Isaiah 60:6 (“gold and frankincense… they shall bring”) turned the givers into kings and gave them camels. Matthew says neither three, nor kings, nor camels. Later readers assigned each gift a meaning — gold for a king, incense for God, myrrh for a burial — and the myrrh at least does return at the end of the story (John 19:39), though Matthew himself never draws the line.

Verses 12–15 · four withdrawals, a second Joseph, and a son called out of Egypt

⚠ The chapter is built on one verb: WITHDRAW. Anachōreō means to give ground, to retire, to get out of the way. The magi “withdrew to their own country” (v12); “when they had withdrawn” (v13); Joseph “withdrew to Egypt” (v14); and at the close he “withdrew into the district of Galilee” (v22). Four retreats in twelve verses. Matthew keeps the word for the rest of the Gospel as the thing Jesus does when a threat is real — he withdraws when John is arrested (4:12), when the Pharisees take counsel against him (12:15), when John is beheaded (14:13). The Messiah’s first recorded movement is a retreat, and the retreat becomes a habit.

“An angel of the Lord APPEARS” — the tense changes. At 1:20 the angel “appeared,” a past tense. At 2:13 and 2:19 Matthew shifts into the present, phainetai, “appears” — the historic present, the tense a storyteller drops into to put the reader in the room. Kept here rather than levelled to the past. The versions all normalize it: KJV “appeareth” at v13 but “appeared” at v19; ASV, NIV and NWT read a past tense in both. The Greek does not.

⚠ A Joseph guided by dreams, going down into Egypt. Chapter 1 planted this and here it pays: Joseph the carpenter is given four dreams (1:20; 2:13, 19, 22) and takes his household down into Egypt to survive a death sentence — a second Joseph the dreamer, whose namesake also went down to Egypt, also because of men who wanted him dead, and also ended by keeping his family alive there (Genesis 37; Genesis 46). The difference is exact and worth hearing: the first Joseph interpreted dreams — for a cupbearer, a baker and a Pharaoh. This one is only ever told, and obeys without a recorded word; he does not speak once in the whole Gospel.

⚠ “OUT OF EGYPT I CALLED MY SON” — the clearest case in the New Testament of “fulfilled” not meaning “predicted.” Hosea 11:1 is not a prophecy. It is a memory: “when Israel was a child I loved him, and out of Egypt I called my son” — Hosea looking back at the exodus, and the son is the nation. The readings, with their pedigrees:

— Typology, or corporate solidarity. Matthew’s whole opening is Israel’s story run again through one child: down into Egypt, out of Egypt, through the water at the Jordan, forty days in the wilderness, up a mountain for the law. On this reading Israel’s history is prophetic of him because he is what Israel was called to be, so a verse about the nation lands on him without being wrenched. This is the mainstream Christian reading, and it is plainly what Matthew is doing structurally, whatever a reader makes of it.

— A recognized Jewish way of reading. In the commentaries found at Qumran a text is applied to a present event rather than mined for a prediction; “this is what that text is for” is a normal first-century use of Scripture, and “fulfil” carries that sense easily.

— The critical reading. That Matthew has lifted a sentence out of its context and manufactured a prophecy from it. Stated here because it is stated everywhere else, and because a reader is owed it.

⚠ One detail belongs to whoever is arguing, on either side: Hosea’s very next line is “and they kept sacrificing to the Baals.” Anyone quoting Hosea 11:1 is quoting a passage about a son who failed — which may be exactly why Matthew wants it standing over the head of a Son who does not. On the shelf no version can soften the difficulty; ASV and NWT print Hosea’s plain past tense, “out of Egypt I called my son,” and leave the reader to notice.

“Until the death of Herod” — the date the calendar got wrong. Josephus places Herod’s death after a lunar eclipse and before a Passover, which is usually reckoned as 4 BC (a minority argues for 1 BC). If Jesus was born before Herod died, then he was born “BC” — the era named after him starts a few years too late, the arithmetic of a sixth-century monk, Dionysius Exiguus, who devised the Anno Domini count. ⚠ The stay in Egypt is given no length in the text; the traditional two years is a guess run backwards from the two-year-olds of verse 16. Later legend filled those months with miracles (the Arabic and Coptic infancy gospels are full of them); Matthew reports only that they went, stayed, and came back.

Verses 16–18 · the killing, and a mother who refuses to be comforted

“He had been OUTWITTED” — and the verb comes back at the end of the book. Enepaichthē: to be mocked, made a fool of. ⚠ Matthew uses this verb again for what is done to Jesus — the soldiers “mocked him” (27:29, 31) and so do the chief priests (27:41). The first man mocked in this Gospel is Herod, and he is mocked by three foreigners who did nothing more aggressive than go home by another road.

“All the BOYS… from two years old and under.” Paidas is masculine, and the age-limit settles it: boys. KJV, ASV and Douay read “children”; NIV and NWT read “boys.” ⚠ And the numbers, since they are always inflated: Bethlehem was a village of a few hundred people, which puts the boys under two at perhaps a dozen or twenty — a horror, and not the “fourteen thousand innocents” of the later liturgical calendars, a figure with no basis whatever in the text.

⚠ Did it happen? Josephus does not mention it. Said plainly, because it is the standard objection and belongs at the front of the note rather than the back. What can be set beside it: Josephus records at length that Herod executed his favourite wife Mariamne, her brother, her grandfather and her mother; that he had two of his own sons strangled; that he had a third, Antipater, killed five days before his own death; and that as he lay dying he ordered the leading men of Judea shut in the hippodrome to be slaughtered the moment he died, so the nation would be seen to mourn (his sister quietly cancelled it). A small atrocity in a village six miles from Jerusalem is not out of character for that reign, and would not have been the most newsworthy thing in it. Against that: Matthew’s account is shaped very closely on the story of Moses — a tyrant killing infant boys (Exodus 1:16, 22), the one child who escapes, the return when the tyrant dies — which leads some to read it as theological narrative rather than report. Both readings are given here, and the library does not vote. ⚠ One late witness gets quoted in this argument and should be handled carefully: Macrobius, writing about AD 400, has Augustus joke that “it is better to be Herod’s pig than his son,” and attaches it to the killing of boys — but the pun turns on Herod’s murdered sons, and Macrobius is four centuries downstream. It is evidence of a tradition, not confirmation of an event.

⚠ RACHEL — and the word Jacob used at the pit. Jeremiah 31:15 (31:14 in the Hebrew) is a picture of the exile: Rachel, centuries dead, weeping over the deported children of the north. Matthew brings it to Bethlehem, and the geography is not arbitrary — Genesis buries Rachel “on the way to Ephrath, that is, Bethlehem” (Genesis 35:19), though Jeremiah’s own “in Ramah” and 1 Samuel 10:2 put her tomb north of Jerusalem in Benjamin. The two traditions are ancient and unresolved; Matthew’s citation works with the Bethlehem one. ⚠ Then the vocabulary, which is the real payoff. The Hebrew says Rachel “refused to be comforted” — me’anah lehinnachem, the same pair of roots as Jacob at the loss of Joseph, who “refused to be comforted” (Genesis 37:35). And the Hebrew’s last clause is singular: ki einennu, “because he is not” — the very word cried over the empty pit at Dothan, “the boy is not” (Genesis 37:30), and again by Jacob over his sons, “Joseph is not, and Simeon is not” (Genesis 42:36). Rachel is Joseph’s mother. The phrase that began at a pit in Dothan is spoken at last over the children of the town where she was buried — and Matthew’s Greek turns the Hebrew singular into a plural: “because they are not.”

The shelf splits on how many kinds of crying there were — and the manuscripts are why. The critical text has two nouns, “weeping and much lamentation.” A large part of the later Greek tradition adds a third, thrēnos, which is where the KJV gets its famous triple: “lamentation, and weeping, and great mourning.” ⚠ The Hebrew of Jeremiah also has a three-part cry (wailing, bitter weeping), so the longer Greek reading either preserves the fuller quotation or is a scribe’s correction toward the Hebrew — both are argued, and the manuscripts alone cannot settle it. Douay, following the Vulgate, has two; ASV, NIV and NWT follow the shorter Greek. Mister prints the shorter text and tells you what the longer one says.

Verses 19–21 · the words that were spoken to Moses

⚠ The angel’s sentence is a quotation, and Matthew does not say so. “Rise, take the child and his mother and go into the land of Israel, for those who were seeking the child’s life are dead.” Set beside it what was said to Moses in Midian: “Go, return to Egypt, for all the men who sought your life are dead” (Exodus 4:19). Matthew’s Greek stands almost word for word on the Greek of Exodus, down to psychē for the Hebrew nefesh, “life.” ⚠ Matthew flags four quotations in this chapter with a formula (“so that what was spoken… might be fulfilled”). This one he does not flag at all — he simply puts Moses’ marching orders into the angel’s mouth and lets whoever knows Exodus hear them. It is the single strongest piece of evidence that the Moses pattern running through this chapter is deliberate rather than imagined by later readers.

“THOSE who were seeking” — a plural for one dead king. Herod alone has died, and the angel says “those.” The plural comes straight out of Exodus (“all the men who sought your life”), which is one more sign the line is quoted rather than composed. KJV “they are dead which sought the young child’s life”; NIV “those who were trying to take the child’s life are dead”; NWT keeps “soul” for psychē — its standing practice, and here the more transparent of the two renderings, since the Hebrew behind Exodus is nefesh.

“The child and his mother” — four times, and never “his parents.” The phrase at vv13, 14, 20 and 21 is always the child and his mother (and at v11, “the child with Mary his mother”) — a carefulness the reader of chapter 1 can hear, and one Luke has no reason to keep, since he can write “his parents” without difficulty (Luke 2:41). And the word for him is paidion, a little child, used nine times in these sixteen verses. ⚠ Matthew names him “Jesus” once, in verse 1, and then not again in the chapter: for twenty-two verses the person everyone is travelling toward or hunting for is simply “the child,” who never speaks, never acts, and is carried everywhere by other people.

Verses 22–23 · Archelaus, and a prophecy nobody can find

“ARCHELAUS was reigning” — and strictly speaking he was not. Herod’s will split the kingdom between three sons. Augustus confirmed the division but withheld the title of king from Archelaus, making him ethnarch of Judea, Samaria and Idumea, while Galilee and Perea went to his brother Antipas as tetrarch. Matthew’s verb is the ordinary “is king over” — how people spoke, rather than how the paperwork read. ⚠ Joseph’s fear is well founded: Josephus records that Archelaus opened his rule by sending troops into the temple at Passover and killing about three thousand, and that Rome deposed him for cruelty in AD 6 and banished him to Gaul. So the family avoids Judea and settles under the other brother — which is why Jesus grows up a Galilean, and why Galilee, not Bethlehem, becomes the name that sticks to him.

⚠ “He shall be called a NAZARENE” — the one quotation in this Gospel that cannot be found. No such sentence exists in the Old Testament, and Matthew’s formula is unusual in two ways: he writes “through the prophets,” plural, and he introduces the words with hoti (“that”) instead of quoting them. Both may be signals that he is summarizing a theme rather than citing a verse. The proposals, with pedigrees:

— Netser, “shoot” or “branch.” Isaiah 11:1: “a shoot shall come out of the stump of Jesse.” A messianic branch-text, and its consonants n-ts-r are the consonants of Natsrat, Nazareth. Wordplay on a name is exactly Matthew’s habit — he has just built a genealogy on the numerical value of David’s — and this is the oldest and strongest of the readings; Jerome already argues it.

— Nazir, “consecrated one.” Judges 13:5, of Samson: “the boy shall be a nazir to God” — attractive because it is an angelic birth-announcement about a deliverer. Against it: the sibilant does not match as neatly, and Jesus pointedly was not a Nazirite, as he says himself when they call him a drinker (11:19).

— The theme of contempt. “Nazarene” was a slur — “can anything good come out of Nazareth?” (John 1:46) — and the prophets said the servant would be despised and rejected. On this reading Matthew means: the prophets said he would be scorned, and the village he was raised in supplied the word they scorned him with. Acts 24:5 shows it already in use against the church, “the sect of the Nazarenes.”

— A source we no longer have. Possible; unprovable; listed for completeness.

The library prints all four and chooses none. KJV, ASV, NIV and NWT all read “Nazarene” or “Nazaraean,” and the footnotes of the modern editions point the reader to Isaiah 11:1 or simply to the theme — which is itself an honest admission that there is no verse to cite.

NAZARETH itself. A farming village in the hills of lower Galilee, unmentioned in the Old Testament, unmentioned by Josephus (who knew Galilee well and names dozens of its towns), and unmentioned in the Talmud — a silence once used to argue that it did not exist in the first century. It did: first-century tombs, wine-presses, cisterns and a first-century dwelling have been excavated there, and a marble fragment found at Caesarea Maritima in 1962 — a list of the twenty-four priestly courses and the places they settled after the war — names Nazareth, the earliest mention of the village outside the New Testament. ⚠ Its obscurity is the point of the verse. The chapter opened in Bethlehem, the city of David, with foreign dignitaries on their faces and gold on the floor; it ends in a village so small that nobody had bothered to write its name down.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “magi” kept as the word magoi, neither “wise men” nor “kings”; “at its rising” for en tē anatolē, with “in the east” and “when we were in the east” printed as the live alternatives; “bow down before” for proskyneō in every mouth that uses it, Herod’s included — the gesture translated, its meaning left to the reader; “was shaken” (etarachthē); “secretly” (lathra) matched to the “quietly” of 1:19; “exactly” (akriboō) in all three places, so verse 7’s precision is audible in verse 16; “withdrew” (anachōreō) four times; the historic present “appears” for the two Egyptian dreams; “the boys” (paidas) rather than “children”; “the child” (paidion) nine times; “weeping and much lamentation,” the shorter reading, with the KJV’s third noun explained rather than adopted; “those who were seeking” left in Exodus’s plural; and “a Nazarene” left unglossed, because nobody can gloss it.

Echoes paid. The dreams of Joseph promised in chapter 1’s closing note (1:20 → 2:13, 19, 22); lathra from 1:19, mercy turned into ambush; “refused to be comforted” and einennu, “he is not,” from the pit at Dothan (Genesis 37:30, 35) and Jacob’s bereavement (Genesis 42:36) — the family’s oldest words of loss handed back to Rachel at the town where she was buried (Genesis 35:19); the descent into Egypt of Genesis 46; Pharaoh’s killing of the Hebrew boys (Exodus 1:16, 22); the words spoken to Moses in Midian (Exodus 4:19), quoted without a footnote; and alluf, the chief, from the Edomite lists of Genesis 36, standing behind Matthew’s “rulers of Judah.”

Echoes planted. “King of the Jews,” which returns only at the cross (27:37), said there by Gentiles who are not kneeling; empaizō, the mocking of Herod, which returns as the mocking of Jesus (27:29, 31, 41); the chief priests and scribes first assembled here and last assembled at the trial; anachōreō, the withdrawal that becomes Jesus’ habit under threat (4:12; 12:15; 14:13); Galilee, the name that attaches to him instead of Bethlehem; and the exodus pattern this chapter sets going — Egypt, then the water, then the wilderness, then the mountain — which chapters 3 to 5 walk through in that order.

Next in this book: chapter 3 — John the Baptist in the wilderness of Judea, camel’s hair and locusts, “prepare the way”; the axe at the root and the winnowing fork; and the baptism at the Jordan, where the heavens open and a voice names the Son.
