Moses argues that the land is not being given for Israel's righteousness — stated three times in three verses — but for the wickedness of the nations being dispossessed and an oath sworn to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob. To prove it he retells the golden calf from his own side: forty days on the mountain receiving the two stone tablets written with the finger of God, the order to go down because the people had already made a molten image, the tablets shattered in front of them, a second forty days prostrate without bread or water, and a prayer for Aaron. He then lists Taberah, Massah, Kibroth-hattaavah and Kadesh-barnea as further evidence, and the chapter ends mid-prayer, arguing that destroying Israel would let the land say Jehovah lacked the power, or hated them.
Still the plains of Moab in the fortieth year (1:3); no new narrated event in Moab — the chapter continues Moses' second address without a break from chapter 8. The CONTENT reported, however, is Horeb roughly thirty-eight years earlier: the same episode as Exodus 32 (already on these pages), told from Moses' own vantage and differing from it in several details, which are set out in the chapter's notes rather than reconciled. The three place-names of v22 and the Kadesh-barnea refusal of v23 range across the whole forty years in between, so the chapter is a survey rather than a sequence — it is arranged as an argument, not as an itinerary.

Deuteronomy 9 — The Mister Translation

1
שְׁמַע יִשְׂרָאֵל, אַתָּה עֹבֵר הַיּוֹם אֶת-הַיַּרְדֵּן, לָבֹא לָרֶשֶׁת גּוֹיִם, גְּדֹלִים וַעֲצֻמִים מִמֶּךָּ--עָרִים גְּדֹלֹת וּבְצֻרֹת, בַּשָּׁמָיִם.
Hear, O Israel: you are crossing the Jordan today, to go in and dispossess nations greater and mightier than you — cities great and fortified up to the heavens,note
Oye, Israel: hoy vas a cruzar el Jordán, para entrar a desposeer a naciones más grandes y más fuertes que tú —ciudades grandes y fortificadas hasta los cielos,
2
עַם-גָּדוֹל וָרָם, בְּנֵי עֲנָקִים: אֲשֶׁר אַתָּה יָדַעְתָּ, וְאַתָּה שָׁמַעְתָּ--מִי יִתְיַצֵּב, לִפְנֵי בְּנֵי עֲנָק.
a people great and tall, the sons of the Anakim — whom you yourself know, and of whom you yourself have heard it said: ‘Who can take his stand before the sons of Anak?’note
un pueblo grande y alto, los hijos de los anaquitas —a quienes tú mismo conoces, y de quienes tú mismo has oído decir: «¿Quién puede mantenerse en pie delante de los hijos de Anac?»
3
וְיָדַעְתָּ הַיּוֹם, כִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ הוּא-הָעֹבֵר לְפָנֶיךָ אֵשׁ אֹכְלָה--הוּא יַשְׁמִידֵם וְהוּא יַכְנִיעֵם, לְפָנֶיךָ; וְהוֹרַשְׁתָּם וְהַאֲבַדְתָּם מַהֵר, כַּאֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר יְהוָה לָךְ.
And you shall know today that Jehovah your God — he is the one crossing over before you, a devouring fire. He himself will destroy them, and he himself will bring them low before you; and you shall dispossess them and make them perish quickly, as Jehovah has spoken to you.note
Y sabrás hoy que Jehová tu Dios —él es el que cruza delante de ti, fuego devorador. Él mismo los aniquilará, y él mismo los humillará delante de ti; y tú los desposeerás y los harás perecer pronto, tal como Jehová te ha dicho.
4
אַל-תֹּאמַר בִּלְבָבְךָ, בַּהֲדֹף יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ אֹתָם מִלְּפָנֶיךָ לֵאמֹר, בְּצִדְקָתִי הֱבִיאַנִי יְהוָה, לָרֶשֶׁת אֶת-הָאָרֶץ הַזֹּאת: וּבְרִשְׁעַת הַגּוֹיִם הָאֵלֶּה, יְהוָה מוֹרִישָׁם מִפָּנֶיךָ.
Do not say in your heart, when Jehovah your God thrusts them out from before you: ‘It is for my righteousness that Jehovah brought me in to possess this land’ — when it is for the wickedness of these nations that Jehovah is dispossessing them from before you.note
No digas en tu corazón, cuando Jehová tu Dios los eche de delante de ti: «Por mi justicia me trajo Jehová a tomar posesión de esta tierra» —cuando es por la maldad de estas naciones que Jehová las desposee de delante de ti.
5
לֹא בְצִדְקָתְךָ, וּבְיֹשֶׁר לְבָבְךָ, אַתָּה בָא, לָרֶשֶׁת אֶת-אַרְצָם: כִּי בְּרִשְׁעַת הַגּוֹיִם הָאֵלֶּה, יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ מוֹרִישָׁם מִפָּנֶיךָ, וּלְמַעַן הָקִים אֶת-הַדָּבָר אֲשֶׁר נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה לַאֲבֹתֶיךָ, לְאַבְרָהָם לְיִצְחָק וּלְיַעֲקֹב.
Not for your righteousness, and not for the uprightness of your heart, are you going in to possess their land — but for the wickedness of these nations Jehovah your God is dispossessing them from before you, and in order to establish the word that Jehovah swore to your fathers, to Abraham, to Isaac, and to Jacob.note
No por tu justicia, ni por la rectitud de tu corazón, entras tú a tomar posesión de su tierra —sino por la maldad de estas naciones Jehová tu Dios las desposee de delante de ti, y para confirmar la palabra que Jehová juró a tus padres, a Abraham, a Isaac y a Jacob.
6
וְיָדַעְתָּ, כִּי לֹא בְצִדְקָתְךָ יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ נֹתֵן לְךָ אֶת-הָאָרֶץ הַטּוֹבָה הַזֹּאת--לְרִשְׁתָּהּ: כִּי עַם-קְשֵׁה-עֹרֶף, אָתָּה.
And you shall know that it is not for your righteousness that Jehovah your God is giving you this good land to possess it — for you are a stiffnecked people.note
Y sabrás que no es por tu justicia que Jehová tu Dios te da esta buena tierra para tomarla en posesión —porque eres un pueblo de dura cerviz.
7
זְכֹר, אַל-תִּשְׁכַּח, אֵת אֲשֶׁר-הִקְצַפְתָּ אֶת-יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ, בַּמִּדְבָּר: לְמִן-הַיּוֹם אֲשֶׁר-יָצָאתָ מֵאֶרֶץ מִצְרַיִם, עַד-בֹּאֲכֶם עַד-הַמָּקוֹם הַזֶּה, מַמְרִים הֱיִיתֶם, עִם-יְהוָה.
Remember! Do not forget how you provoked Jehovah your God to wrath in the wilderness. From the day you came out of the land of Egypt until your coming to this place, you have been rebellious with Jehovah.note
¡Acuérdate! No olvides cómo provocaste a ira a Jehová tu Dios en el desierto. Desde el día en que saliste de la tierra de Egipto hasta que ustedes llegaron a este lugar, rebeldes han sido ustedes con Jehová.
8
וּבְחֹרֵב הִקְצַפְתֶּם, אֶת-יְהוָה; וַיִּתְאַנַּף יְהוָה בָּכֶם, לְהַשְׁמִיד אֶתְכֶם.
And at Horeb you provoked Jehovah to wrath, and Jehovah was incensed with you enough to destroy you.note
Y en Horeb ustedes provocaron a ira a Jehová, y Jehová se indignó contra ustedes hasta querer aniquilarlos.
9
בַּעֲלֹתִי הָהָרָה, לָקַחַת לוּחֹת הָאֲבָנִים לוּחֹת הַבְּרִית, אֲשֶׁר-כָּרַת יְהוָה, עִמָּכֶם; וָאֵשֵׁב בָּהָר, אַרְבָּעִים יוֹם וְאַרְבָּעִים לַיְלָה--לֶחֶם לֹא אָכַלְתִּי, וּמַיִם לֹא שָׁתִיתִי.
When I went up the mountain to receive the tablets of stone, the tablets of the covenant that Jehovah cut with you, I stayed on the mountain forty days and forty nights — bread I did not eat, and water I did not drink.note
Cuando subí al monte a recibir las tablas de piedra, las tablas del pacto que Jehová cortó con ustedes, me quedé en el monte cuarenta días y cuarenta noches —pan no comí, y agua no bebí.
10
וַיִּתֵּן יְהוָה אֵלַי, אֶת-שְׁנֵי לוּחֹת הָאֲבָנִים--כְּתֻבִים, בְּאֶצְבַּע אֱלֹהִים; וַעֲלֵיהֶם, כְּכָל-הַדְּבָרִים אֲשֶׁר דִּבֶּר יְהוָה עִמָּכֶם בָּהָר מִתּוֹךְ הָאֵשׁ--בְּיוֹם הַקָּהָל.
And Jehovah gave me the two tablets of stone, written with the finger of God; and on them was written according to all the words that Jehovah spoke with you on the mountain, out of the midst of the fire, on the day of the assembly.note
Y Jehová me dio las dos tablas de piedra, escritas con el dedo de Dios; y en ellas estaba escrito conforme a todas las palabras que Jehová habló con ustedes en el monte, de en medio del fuego, el día de la asamblea.
11
וַיְהִי, מִקֵּץ אַרְבָּעִים יוֹם, וְאַרְבָּעִים, לָיְלָה; נָתַן יְהוָה אֵלַי, אֶת-שְׁנֵי לֻחֹת הָאֲבָנִים--לֻחוֹת הַבְּרִית.
And it was at the end of forty days and forty nights that Jehovah gave me the two tablets of stone, the tablets of the covenant.note
Y sucedió que al cabo de cuarenta días y cuarenta noches Jehová me dio las dos tablas de piedra, las tablas del pacto.
12
וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה אֵלַי, קוּם רֵד מַהֵר מִזֶּה--כִּי שִׁחֵת עַמְּךָ, אֲשֶׁר הוֹצֵאתָ מִמִּצְרָיִם: סָרוּ מַהֵר, מִן-הַדֶּרֶךְ אֲשֶׁר צִוִּיתִם--עָשׂוּ לָהֶם, מַסֵּכָה.
And Jehovah said to me: ‘Get up, go down quickly from here, for your people, whom you brought out of Egypt, have acted corruptly. They have turned aside quickly from the way I commanded them; they have made themselves a molten image.’note
Y Jehová me dijo: «Levántate, desciende pronto de aquí, porque tu pueblo, que sacaste de Egipto, se ha corrompido. Pronto se han apartado del camino que les mandé; se han hecho una imagen de fundición».
13
וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה, אֵלַי לֵאמֹר: רָאִיתִי אֶת-הָעָם הַזֶּה, וְהִנֵּה עַם-קְשֵׁה-עֹרֶף הוּא.
And Jehovah said to me, saying: ‘I have seen this people, and behold, it is a stiffnecked people.note
Y Jehová me dijo, diciendo: «Yo he visto a este pueblo, y he aquí que es pueblo de dura cerviz.
14
הֶרֶף מִמֶּנִּי, וְאַשְׁמִידֵם, וְאֶמְחֶה אֶת-שְׁמָם, מִתַּחַת הַשָּׁמָיִם; וְאֶעֱשֶׂה, אוֹתְךָ, לְגוֹי-עָצוּם וָרָב, מִמֶּנּוּ.
Let go of me, that I may destroy them and blot out their name from under the heavens — and let me make you into a nation mightier and more numerous than they.’note
Suéltame, para que los aniquile y borre su nombre de debajo de los cielos —y déjame hacer de ti una nación más fuerte y más numerosa que ellos».
15
וָאֵפֶן, וָאֵרֵד מִן-הָהָר, וְהָהָר, בֹּעֵר בָּאֵשׁ; וּשְׁנֵי לוּחֹת הַבְּרִית, עַל שְׁתֵּי יָדָי.
So I turned and came down from the mountain, and the mountain was burning with fire; and the two tablets of the covenant were on my two hands.note
Así que me volví y descendí del monte, y el monte ardía en fuego; y las dos tablas del pacto estaban sobre mis dos manos.
16
וָאֵרֶא, וְהִנֵּה חֲטָאתֶם לַיהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם--עֲשִׂיתֶם לָכֶם, עֵגֶל מַסֵּכָה: סַרְתֶּם מַהֵר--מִן-הַדֶּרֶךְ, אֲשֶׁר-צִוָּה יְהוָה אֶתְכֶם.
And I looked, and behold, you had sinned against Jehovah your God: you had made yourselves a molten calf. You had turned aside quickly from the way that Jehovah had commanded you.note
Y miré, y he aquí que ustedes habían pecado contra Jehová su Dios: se habían hecho un becerro de fundición. Pronto se habían apartado del camino que Jehová les había mandado.
17
וָאֶתְפֹּשׂ, בִּשְׁנֵי הַלֻּחֹת, וָאַשְׁלִכֵם, מֵעַל שְׁתֵּי יָדָי; וָאֲשַׁבְּרֵם, לְעֵינֵיכֶם.
And I seized the two tablets and flung them from off my two hands, and shattered them before your eyes.note
Y agarré las dos tablas y las arrojé de sobre mis dos manos, y las hice pedazos delante de los ojos de ustedes.
18
וָאֶתְנַפַּל לִפְנֵי יְהוָה כָּרִאשֹׁנָה, אַרְבָּעִים יוֹם וְאַרְבָּעִים לַיְלָה--לֶחֶם לֹא אָכַלְתִּי, וּמַיִם לֹא שָׁתִיתִי: עַל כָּל-חַטַּאתְכֶם אֲשֶׁר חֲטָאתֶם, לַעֲשׂוֹת הָרַע בְּעֵינֵי יְהוָה לְהַכְעִיסוֹ.
And I fell prostrate before Jehovah as at the first, forty days and forty nights — bread I did not eat, and water I did not drink — because of all your sin that you had sinned, in doing what was evil in the eyes of Jehovah, to provoke him.note
Y me postré delante de Jehová como la primera vez, cuarenta días y cuarenta noches —pan no comí, y agua no bebí— por todo el pecado de ustedes que habían cometido, haciendo lo malo a los ojos de Jehová, para provocarlo.
19
כִּי יָגֹרְתִּי, מִפְּנֵי הָאַף וְהַחֵמָה, אֲשֶׁר קָצַף יְהוָה עֲלֵיכֶם, לְהַשְׁמִיד אֶתְכֶם; וַיִּשְׁמַע יְהוָה אֵלַי, גַּם בַּפַּעַם הַהִוא.
For I was in dread of the anger and the burning fury with which Jehovah raged against you, to destroy you. And Jehovah listened to me that time also.note
Porque tuve pavor de la ira y del furor ardiente con que Jehová se enfureció contra ustedes, hasta querer aniquilarlos. Y Jehová me escuchó también aquella vez.
20
וּבְאַהֲרֹן, הִתְאַנַּף יְהוָה מְאֹד--לְהַשְׁמִידוֹ; וָאֶתְפַּלֵּל גַּם-בְּעַד אַהֲרֹן, בָּעֵת הַהִוא.
And with Aaron Jehovah was very incensed, enough to destroy him; and I prayed for Aaron also, at that time.note
Y contra Aarón se indignó Jehová en gran manera, hasta querer aniquilarlo; y oré también por Aarón en aquel tiempo.
21
וְאֶת-חַטַּאתְכֶם אֲשֶׁר-עֲשִׂיתֶם אֶת-הָעֵגֶל, לָקַחְתִּי וָאֶשְׂרֹף אֹתוֹ בָּאֵשׁ, וָאֶכֹּת אֹתוֹ טָחוֹן הֵיטֵב, עַד אֲשֶׁר-דַּק לְעָפָר; וָאַשְׁלִךְ, אֶת-עֲפָרוֹ, אֶל-הַנַּחַל, הַיֹּרֵד מִן-הָהָר.
And your sin that you had made — the calf — I took, and I burned it with fire and beat it, grinding it well until it was as fine as dust; and I threw its dust into the brook that comes down from the mountain.note
Y el pecado de ustedes que habían hecho —el becerro— lo tomé, y lo quemé en el fuego y lo trituré, moliéndolo bien hasta que quedó fino como polvo; y eché su polvo en el arroyo que desciende del monte.
22
וּבְתַבְעֵרָה, וּבְמַסָּה, וּבְקִבְרֹת, הַתַּאֲוָה--מַקְצִפִים הֱיִיתֶם, אֶת-יְהוָה.
And at Taberah, and at Massah, and at Kibroth-hattaavah, you were provoking Jehovah to wrath.note
Y en Tabera, y en Masá, y en Kibrot-hataavá, ustedes estuvieron provocando a ira a Jehová.
23
וּבִשְׁלֹחַ יְהוָה אֶתְכֶם, מִקָּדֵשׁ בַּרְנֵעַ לֵאמֹר, עֲלוּ וּרְשׁוּ אֶת-הָאָרֶץ, אֲשֶׁר נָתַתִּי לָכֶם; וַתַּמְרוּ, אֶת-פִּי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם, וְלֹא הֶאֱמַנְתֶּם לוֹ, וְלֹא שְׁמַעְתֶּם בְּקֹלוֹ.
And when Jehovah sent you from Kadesh-barnea, saying, ‘Go up and possess the land that I have given you,’ you rebelled against the mouth of Jehovah your God, and you did not trust him, and you did not listen to his voice.note
Y cuando Jehová los envió desde Cades-barnea, diciendo: «Suban y tomen posesión de la tierra que les he dado», ustedes se rebelaron contra la boca de Jehová su Dios, y no le creyeron, y no escucharon su voz.
24
מַמְרִים הֱיִיתֶם, עִם-יְהוָה, מִיּוֹם, דַּעְתִּי אֶתְכֶם.
You have been rebellious with Jehovah from the day I knew you.note
Rebeldes han sido ustedes con Jehová desde el día en que los conocí.
25
וָאֶתְנַפַּל לִפְנֵי יְהוָה, אֵת אַרְבָּעִים הַיּוֹם וְאֶת-אַרְבָּעִים הַלַּיְלָה--אֲשֶׁר הִתְנַפָּלְתִּי: כִּי-אָמַר יְהוָה, לְהַשְׁמִיד אֶתְכֶם.
So I fell prostrate before Jehovah the forty days and the forty nights that I fell prostrate — because Jehovah had said he would destroy you.note
Así que me postré delante de Jehová los cuarenta días y las cuarenta noches que me postré —porque Jehová había dicho que los aniquilaría.

Verses 1–3 · ‘Hear, O Israel’ opens a war speech — and the spies’ terror report is quoted back word for word, and conceded

★ The chapter opens on the Shema’s own two words, and it is not the Shema. The bare two-word address Shema Yisrael stands four times in the Hebrew Bible and all four are in this book, checked across the whole archived Hebrew: 5:1, introducing the Ten Words; 6:4, the sentence that became the creed; here; and once more not yet on these pages — 20:3, where a priest says it to troops about to fight. (A fifth verse, 27:9, has the same imperative under a different shape, hasket u-shma Yisrael, ‘Keep silence and hear, O Israel’, telling them they have become a people that day.) So the formula is not reserved for the creed at all. Two of its four uses open a BATTLE address, and the two are built the same way: ‘Hear, O Israel: you are crossing the Jordan today’ here, ‘Hear, O Israel: you are drawing near today to the battle’ there. A reader who knows only Deuteronomy 6 will hear a creed starting. What starts is a war briefing.

★ Every frightening thing the spies said is repeated here — and not one word of it is denied. Set the two side by side. Deuteronomy 1:28 (already on these pages) is the report that broke the first generation: ‘A people greater and taller than we, cities great and fortified to the heavens — and we saw the sons of the Anakim there too.’ Verses 1–2 here run the same three items in the same words — arim gedolot uvtzurot ba-shamayim, am gadol va-ram, bnei anakim — merely reordered, cities first. At Deuteronomy 1:29 Moses answered that report by telling them not to dread. Here he does not answer it. He repeats it, adds a proverb about it (‘Who can take his stand before the sons of Anak?’), grants that they know it and have heard it — the pronoun is doubled for emphasis, you yourself know, you yourself have heard — and then changes the subject of the sentence from Israel to God. The fear was never the error. Concluding from it was.

⚠ ‘A devouring fire’ is a title this book has already used against Israel. The participial phrase esh okhlah stands five times in the Hebrew Bible once BOTH spellings are searched: twice defectively — Deuteronomy 4:24 (already on these pages) and this verse — and three times written full, all in Isaiah (29:6, 30:30 and 33:14, none yet on these pages), where it is the flame of an approaching theophany and, at 33:14, the thing Isaiah asks who among us could live beside. ⚠ A consonantal search on the defective spelling alone also returns Joel 1:19, 1:20 and Job 15:34, and those are a different construction altogether: there the word is a finite verb — fire HAS DEVOURED the pastures — not a participle standing as a description. Deuteronomy’s two are the places where the phrase sits in apposition to the divine name itself. At 4:24 the fire is a warning to Israel about images. Here the identical two words go out ahead of Israel against somebody else. Same title, reversed direction, five chapters apart. ⚠ The Hebrew puts the phrase in apposition — ‘he is the one crossing over before you, a devouring fire’ — with no particle of comparison anywhere in the clause, and the shelf mostly supplies one. ASV and Geneva read ‘as a devouring fire’/‘as a consuming fire’; RV60 reads «como fuego consumidor» where RV 1909 had kept the bare apposition, «fuego consumidor». NWT 1984 is the tightest witness on the shelf here, breaking it out as its own flat sentence — ‘A consuming fire he is.’ — matched by TNM 1987, «Un fuego consumidor es él.» And KJV alone moves the fire across a semicolon and out of God’s title entirely, into the manner of the killing: ‘goeth over before thee; as a consuming fire he shall destroy them’. Mister keeps the apposition.

⚠ Three times in one verse the Hebrew adds the free-standing pronoun hu where the verb already carries its subject — he is the one crossing, he will destroy them, he will bring them low — a stress the language uses only when it wants the actor named against some other candidate. The other candidate is in the sentence: two clauses later, Israel does the dispossessing. NWT 1984 catches one of the three (‘he himself will subdue them’), as does TNM 1987 («él mismo los sojuzgará»); both of their own later revisions drop it, the first of five places in this chapter where that happens.

Verses 4–6 · ‘Not for your righteousness’ — three times in three verses, and the third time it says what Israel is instead

★ The denial is made three times, and each time it is put differently. Verse 4 forbids the thought (‘do not say in your heart…’); verse 5 states the fact flatly and adds a second ground; verse 6 turns it into something Israel must actively KNOW. The Hebrew noun is tzedaqah, and this project has now met it travelling three different roads: at Genesis 15:6 it is RECKONED to Abram for trusting; at Deuteronomy 6:25 (already on these pages) it is PROMISED to Israel for doing; and here, three times over, it is DENIED as a ground of anything. One noun, and the Hebrew Bible has already put it on all three sides of its own argument before the New Testament arrives to take it up.

★ Two reasons are given, and neither of them is Israel. The land changes hands because of the rish‘ah of the nations, and because of an oath sworn to three dead men. The second is the older claim — the promise to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob — and the effect of pairing them is that the conquest is grounded entirely outside the generation carrying it out: partly in what somebody else did wrong, partly in what God said to somebody else’s great-grandfather. ⚠ It is worth being plain about the shape of this argument, because it has been quoted in support of conquests that had nothing to do with it: the text is not saying the winners are better. It is at pains, three times in three verses, to say they are not.

⚠ ‘A stiffnecked people’ is the golden calf’s own epithet, handed to the new generation ten verses before the calf is named. The phrase am qesheh-oref stands six times in the Hebrew Bible: four in the calf’s own aftermath (Exodus 32:9; 33:3, 5; 34:9, all already on these pages), and two here, at v6 and at v13. Proverbs 29:1 has the image without the phrase — a MAN who stiffens his neck, maqsheh-oref, with no ‘people’ in it. So the insult arrives here with a history the hearers have not yet been told, and the rest of the chapter is Moses explaining where he got it. ⚠ NWT 2013 and TNM 2019 both give up the neck — ‘an obstinate people’, «un pueblo terco» — where NWT 1984 and TNM 1987 keep it (‘stiff-necked’, «de dura cerviz»), which costs the reader the link to the four Exodus verses. Douay-Rheims intensifies instead, ‘a VERY stiffnecked people’, supplying an intensifier the Hebrew does not have at this verse (though it does at v20, of Aaron), and following the Vulgate in doing so. ⚠ And TLB does something none of the others do: it calls Israel ‘a wicked, stubborn people’ — importing the very word, wickedness, that vv4 and 5 reserve twice over for the NATIONS. The paraphrase does it again seven verses later at vv13–14, ‘this evil, stubborn people’. Twice is a habit, not a slip, and it quietly answers the question the chapter is built to leave open.

Verses 7–8 · ‘Remember! Do not forget’ — the last chapter’s verb, turned around on its object; and the moment the sermon changes number

★ Deuteronomy 8 was built on shakach, to forget, and its object was God. Three of that chapter’s twenty verses warn against forgetting Jehovah — four times over, since 8:19 doubles the verb — and the danger there was prosperity: houses built, herds multiplied, silver increased, and only then the forgetting. This chapter opens its retelling with the same verb — eight verses after Deuteronomy 8’s last use of it — and swaps the object. Zekhor, al-tishkach — do not forget what you did. Chapter 8: do not forget who fed you. Chapter 9: do not forget who you were while he was feeding you. The pair is the sermon’s whole argument in two verbs.

⚠ The two imperatives sit side by side with nothing between them, which most of the shelf will not leave alone. KJV, Geneva and Douay-Rheims all insert a conjunction — ‘Remember, and forget not’ — and so does TNM 2019, «Acuérdate y nunca te olvides»; NIV supplies an object the Hebrew does not have (‘Remember this’), and so does NVI («Recuerda esto»); TLB converts the command into a question, ‘Don’t you remember’. Six witnesses keep the two imperatives bare, with nothing joining them: ASV ‘Remember, forget thou not’, NWT 1984 ‘Remember: Do not forget’ and NWT 2013 ‘Remember—never forget—’, TNM 1987 «Acuérdate: No olvides», RV 1909 «Acuérdate, no te olvides» and RV60 «Acuérdate, no olvides». Mister keeps it too, and marks the join with a full stop rather than a comma, since the second imperative is not a softening of the first.

★ Mid-verse, without warning, ‘you’ stops being one person and becomes a crowd — and it stays a crowd for the rest of the chapter. Verified against the archived Hebrew, verse by verse: there is not one second-person plural form anywhere in vv1–6. Every pronoun and every verb is singular — you are crossing, you know, you have heard, you shall know, do not say in your heart, you are a stiffnecked people. The first plural in the chapter arrives in the second half of this verse, at ad-bo’akhem, ‘until YOUR coming to this place’, and from there to v25 every address to Israel is plural. The one singular ‘you’ left inside the retelling belongs to somebody else: at vv12–14 it is God speaking to Moses. So the future is put to Israel as though to a single man; the past is put to them as a mob. English cannot show it and does not try. ⚠ The Spanish edition of this chapter CAN and does — tú through v7a, ustedes from v7b — so the two columns of this page deliberately diverge here, and the Spanish reader simply sees what the English reader has to be told. Most of the shelf shows some of it by accident of its own age: ASV keeps ‘thou wentest forth…until ye came unto this place, ye have been rebellious’ and KJV the same shift in its own words, ‘thou didst depart…until ye came unto this place’, and RV 1909 and RV60 keep it in the verbs («saliste…entrasteis…habéis sido»). NIV and NVI, having only one modern ‘you’ between them, lose it entirely.

★ ‘You have been rebellious with Jehovah’ — three Hebrew words that occur exactly twice in the whole Bible, and both times in this chapter. Mamrim heyitem im-YHVH closes this verse and opens v24, seventeen verses later, and the search of all 929 archived chapters of the Hebrew Bible returns those two verses and nothing else. Everything between them is the evidence for the sentence they bracket, and the only thing that changes on the second pass is the clock: here the rebellion is measured from Israel’s own exodus, there from the day Moses personally met them. Deuteronomy 8’s closing note asked whether a chapter quoting its own opening at its close would turn out to be a habit of this book or a one-off. It is a habit — and here it is tighter than it was there, because this time the repeated words are unique to the pair.

⚠ Hit’annaf at v8, ‘was incensed’, is a rare verb: six occurrences in the Hebrew Bible, four of them in this book (1:37 and 4:21, both of Moses himself and both already on these pages; then v8 and v20 here), and two outside it, of Solomon and of the northern kingdom. It is not the ordinary word for God being angry, and three of its four Deuteronomy uses aim it at a named leader rather than at the nation — twice at Moses, once at Aaron. This verse is the exception that keeps it from being a rule: here it lands on the people.

Verses 9–11 · Forty days without bread or water — the previous chapter’s own bread verse, met on the mountain; and the day of the QAHAL

★ ‘Bread I did not eat, and water I did not drink’ — one chapter after the sentence about bread. Deuteronomy 8:3 (already on these pages) is the famous one: not by bread alone does the human live, but by every utterance that comes from the mouth of Jehovah. Twenty-six verses later the man who said it reports doing exactly that, twice, for forty days each time (v9 and v18), on a mountain, with the utterances in his hands. Deuteronomy makes no comment on the join and this translation adds none; the two sentences are simply set close enough together to be read in one breath. ⚠ That the same forty-day fast is what Matthew puts Jesus into before quoting 8:3 back at the tempter (Matthew 4:4, already on these pages) is a further step again, and one the Hebrew text itself does not take.

⚠ The tablets get two names in one sentence and keep both: luchot ha-avanim, the tablets of the STONE, and luchot ha-berit, the tablets of the COVENANT — material and function, side by side, and the second pair is repeated alone at v11 and v15. What Moses carries down the mountain and smashes is never called ‘the law’; it is called the covenant. That is why breaking it is an event and not an accident.

★ ‘On the day of the qahal’ — the day of the ASSEMBLY, and the first of only three uses of the phrase. Be-yom ha-qahal occurs three times in the Hebrew Bible, all in this book: here, then at 10:4 and 18:16, neither yet on these pages. It names the day Israel stood at the mountain and heard the voice out of the fire — a single date, treated as the founding assembly. ⚠ The word is worth watching because of what happens to it in Greek: qahal is regularly rendered ekklēsia in the Greek Old Testament — reported here rather than verified, since this project’s archive carries the Masoretic Hebrew and the critical Greek New Testament but no Septuagint, and the rendering is in any case not uniform (synagōgē also translates it) — the noun the New Testament will use for a congregation of believers — which this project renders ‘congregation’ and never ‘church’. The first congregation, on that vocabulary, is this one: everybody, at the foot of a burning mountain, before there is a tabernacle or a priesthood or a page of law written down.

⚠ ‘Written with the finger of God’ is left exactly as it stands. It is an anthropomorphism the Hebrew is content with, and twelve of the thirteen witnesses keep the finger — eleven of them reading ‘the finger of God’ or «el dedo de Dios» outright, and NVI rephrasing it as God writing «con su dedo» himself. Only TLB drops it, leaving tablets ‘on which he had written’ and no hand at all. The temptation to soften it into ‘by God’s own hand’ would be smoothing an image the text chose.

Verses 12–14 · ‘Let go of me’ — a different verb from the one Exodus used; and an offer Deuteronomy quietly raises

★ ‘YOUR people, whom YOU brought out of Egypt.’ Two possessives in one clause, and both of them have just changed owner. Everywhere else in this chapter Israel is God’s people; at the moment of the calf God hands them to Moses. Exodus 32:7 (already on these pages) does the same thing, though not in quite the same words — ‘your people, whom you brought UP out of the land of Egypt’, he‘elah where Deuteronomy has hotzi, brought OUT. Moses hands them straight back four verses later, and there he uses Deuteronomy’s verb (Exodus 32:11, ‘your people, whom you brought out of the land of Egypt’). Deuteronomy makes the reader wait fourteen verses for the return, and then does it twice: v26, ‘do not bring YOUR people to ruin, YOUR inheritance’, and v29, the chapter’s last sentence, ‘and they are YOUR people and YOUR inheritance’. The whole prayer at the end of the chapter is an argument about a pronoun.

★ Verse 13 is a quotation, and this translation prints it as one. Word for word, ra’iti et-ha‘am ha-zeh ve-hineh am-qesheh-oref hu is Exodus 32:9, which is already on these pages and reads there: ‘I have seen this people, and behold, it is a stiffnecked people.’ What Deuteronomy changes is the frame and not the sentence: Exodus has Jehovah say it TO MOSES, Deuteronomy has him say it TO ME, and adds le’mor, ‘saying’. The quoted clause inside is untouched, because the speaker has changed and the verdict has not. So the English here is deliberately identical to the English already printed at Exodus 32:9, down to keeping ‘behold’ where this project often prefers ‘Look —’ for hineh: a quotation that is reworded stops being visible as a quotation.

★ ‘Let go of me’ is NOT the phrase Exodus uses, and the shelf makes them identical. At Exodus 32:10 the Hebrew is hanichah li, from nuach, to set down or leave be — rendered on these pages ‘let me alone’. Here it is heref mimeni, from raphah, to slacken, to let a grip go loose; the same verb tells a man to STOP in the middle of doing something. Two different images — being left in peace, and being released from a hold — and almost every witness on both shelves prints the same English or Spanish for both (‘let me alone’, «déjame»). NWT 1984 is the one that separates them, reading ‘Let me alone’ here against its own ‘So now let me be’ at Exodus 32:10. Its NWT 2013 revision reads ‘Let me be’ here and the same words there, losing the distinction. Mister renders it ‘let go of me’ and keeps ‘let me alone’ where it already stands at Exodus 32:10, so that the two verses read differently on this site because they read differently in Hebrew.

★ The offer to Moses is bigger here than it was in Exodus, and it is measured against the people it would replace. Exodus 32:10: ‘I will make of you a GREAT nation’ — goy gadol, the same two words promised to Abram at Genesis 12:2 (already on these pages), with no comparison in it. Deuteronomy 9:14: goy atzum va-rav mimenu, a nation MIGHTIER AND MORE NUMEROUS THAN THEY. The comparative is the addition, and it points backwards: verse 1 opened this chapter by describing the Canaanite nations as gedolim va-atzumim mimeka, greater and mightier than YOU. Thirteen verses later God offers to build, out of one man, exactly the kind of nation the chapter began by holding up as terrifying. ⚠ Exodus has ‘blot out’ in its telling too, twice over and in two mouths: Moses offers his own name out of God’s book (Exodus 32:32), and God answers with the same verb aimed at the individual sinner — ‘Whoever has sinned against me, him will I blot out of my book’ (Exodus 32:33, already on these pages). So what Deuteronomy changes is not whose verb it is but what it is aimed at: not a name in a book but a name under the heavens, and not one man but a nation.

⚠ Three witnesses read the offer as a promotion rather than a replacement. Douay-Rheims reads ‘set thee over a nation’, and both Spanish Reina-Valeras agree — RV 1909 «yo te pondré sobre gente fuerte», RV60 «te pondré sobre una nación fuerte». That is a different offer altogether: Moses made ruler of a great nation, rather than made INTO one. The Hebrew verb is asah with the preposition le-, the ordinary construction for making something into something, and every other witness on both shelves reads it that way — including NVI, which breaks with the Reina-Valera tradition here («De ti, en cambio, haré una nación»). The Douay-Rheims translates the Vulgate, and its Spanish company on this reading is the reason to name it rather than pass over it: an old Latin-shaped reading that survived four centuries in the Spanish shelf and was corrected only by the newest version on it. ⚠ NVI makes an addition of its own in the same verse, «borre hasta el recuerdo de su nombre» — blot out even the MEMORY of their name — where the Hebrew says only the name. ⚠ And the cohortative ‘let me make you’ (NWT 1984, TNM 1987 «déjame hacer de ti») flattens to a plain future in TNM 2019 alone, «y haré de ti» — NWT 2013 keeps it. The third of this chapter’s five, and the only one of the five that happens on the Spanish shelf and not the English.

Verses 15–17 · The tablets shattered BEFORE YOUR EYES — where Exodus broke them at the foot of the mountain, and where Moses’ own anger has gone

★ Two hands, twice, and the preposition turns over between them. At v15 the tablets are al shtei yadai, ON my two hands — carried, balanced, the posture of someone bringing something down carefully. At v17 they go me‘al shtei yadai, from off my two hands. One letter of difference, and it is the whole gesture. Most of the shelf keeps the two hands (ASV ‘cast them out of my two hands’, Geneva, RV 1909 and RV60 «de mis dos manos»); Douay-Rheims drops the count and the first verb together, ‘I cast the tables out of my hands’, losing the seizing; NIV keeps the seizing and drops the count, and turns the shattering into a participle, ‘breaking them to pieces’, which subordinates the one action the sentence exists to report. ⚠ NWT 1984 reads ‘from both my hands’ and NWT 2013 ‘with both my hands’ — source changed to instrument, the fourth of the five 1984/1987-versus-2013/2019 shifts in this chapter, and on the Spanish side TNM 2019 goes further again, adding a floor to throw them at that the Hebrew does not mention.

★ Exodus breaks them ‘at the foot of the mountain’; Deuteronomy breaks them ‘before your eyes’. Exodus 32:19 (already on these pages) locates the act — tachat ha-har — and gives its cause: he came near the camp, he SAW the calf and the dancing, and his anger burned. Deuteronomy 9:16–17 keeps the seeing and drops both of the others. There is no dancing in this account, and there is no anger of Moses anywhere in this chapter — the only person angry in Deuteronomy’s telling is God. What Deuteronomy puts in their place is an audience: le-eineikhem, before YOUR eyes, addressed to the very people whose parents were watching. The retelling turns a private loss of temper into a public act performed at them.

⚠ TLB supplies the missing drama and then some: ‘I lifted the tablets high above my head and dashed them to the ground!’ The Hebrew has neither the head nor the ground. It is the paraphrase’s standing habit — filling in a physical picture nothing else on the shelf offers — and here it restores exactly the heat that Deuteronomy went to the trouble of removing.

Verses 18–20 · ‘And with Aaron Jehovah was very incensed’ — the one verse in the Bible that says God meant to destroy him, and the only place Moses is recorded praying for his brother

★ Verse 20 reports two things that Exodus, telling the same story at length, never mentions. Read Exodus 32 straight through, as this project has (it is already on these pages): Moses questions Aaron and gets the famous excuse about the fire and the calf (32:21–24); the chapter ends with a plague on the PEOPLE for the calf ‘which Aaron made’ (32:35). Nowhere does it say God intended to destroy Aaron, and nowhere does Moses pray for him. Both of those come only from this verse, forty years later, in the mouth of the man who was there. ⚠ Two honest limits on how far that can be pushed. God IS angry with Aaron elsewhere — at Numbers 12:9 (already on these pages) his anger burns against Aaron and Miriam together — so the rarity here is not the anger but its stated aim, to destroy him. And Numbers 12 is the near-miss on the second half too, and an instructive one: Aaron asks Moses to intercede, and the prayer Moses actually prays is for Miriam (‘Heal her now, O God’). In Numbers 12 he asks and his sister is prayed for; here he does not ask, and he is.

⚠ The chapter records the prayer and does not record an answer. Verse 19 ends the people’s case with a result — ‘and Jehovah listened to me that time also’ — and then verse 20 gives Aaron the prayer and stops. Aaron lives, so the reader may draw the obvious inference; the text declines to state it. TLB is the one witness that closes the gap, ending the verse with the outcome supplied outright. Every other version on both shelves stops where the Hebrew stops. It is a small addition and a real one: the difference between a text that reports a prayer and a text that reports it working.

⚠ ‘That time ALSO’ (v19) and ‘ALSO for Aaron’ (v20) are the same little word gam twice over, and together they quietly say that this was not the first such rescue and Aaron was not the only beneficiary. The chapter is counting intercessions without ever listing them.

⚠ Yagorti, ‘I was in dread’, is the only emotion the chapter gives Moses, and it is fear rather than anger — fear of God, not of the mob. Set beside Exodus 32:19, where Moses’ anger burns and the tablets go down, Deuteronomy’s retelling has swapped his one recorded feeling for a different one.

Verses 21–24 · ‘Your sin…the calf’ — the sin IS the object of the sentence; three place-names in one line; and ‘the MOUTH of Jehovah’, which no version on either shelf prints

★ The grammar of verse 21 identifies the calf with the sin, and the modern shelf is uncomfortable with it. Hebrew word order: ve-et chattatkhem asher-asitem et-ha‘egel laqachti — ‘And your sin, which you made — the calf — I took’. What Moses takes and burns is not an object associated with a sin; the direct object of the verb is the sin, with ‘the calf’ standing in apposition to it. The older witnesses keep it: KJV and ASV both read ‘I took your sin, the calf’, and RV 1909 «vuestro pecado, el becerro». Geneva keeps it and marks it, reading ‘I took your sin, I mean the calf’ — an editorial nudge telling the reader the two nouns are the same thing rather than two. And so, unexpectedly for a paraphrase, does TLB: ‘your sin—the calf you had made’. Five witnesses soften it, in three different ways. Two keep the apposition but demote the head noun from a sin to a thing: NWT 2013 ‘the sinful thing you made, the calf’ where NWT 1984 had ‘YOUR sin that YOU had made, the calf’, and NIV makes the identical move, ‘that sinful thing of yours, the calf you had made’. RV60 instead inserts a noun between them, «el objeto de vuestro pecado». TNM 2019 dissolves the apposition outright into a relative clause. And NVI reverses the direction of cause altogether, «ese ídolo que los hizo pecar» — the idol that MADE them sin, rather than the idol that WAS the sin. That is the fifth of this chapter’s 1984/1987-versus-2013/2019 shifts, and the second of three places in this chapter where RV60 moves away from a literalism RV 1909 had kept — the other two are the bare apposition at v3 and the «dicho» at v23 below.

⚠ What Deuteronomy does with the dust is not what Exodus does with it. Exodus 32:20 (already on these pages) has three verbs and an ending: he burned it, ground it, scattered it on the water — and made the Israelites DRINK it. Deuteronomy has three verbs (burned, beat, ground fine) and a destination: he threw the dust into ha-nachal ha-yored min ha-har, the brook coming down from the mountain, and says nothing about anyone drinking. Two details worth noticing rather than harmonising: Deuteronomy adds a running stream at the mountain that Exodus does not mention, and drops the one action in Exodus’s account that made the people swallow their own idol. This translation prints each account as it stands; readings differ, and neither is adjusted toward the other.

⚠ Verse 22 names three places in one breath and explains none of them, because the audience is assumed to know: Taberah, ‘Burning’, named at Numbers 11:3 (already on these pages) and mentioned in only one other verse in the entire Hebrew Bible — this one; Massah, ‘Testing’, whose only other appearance on these pages is Deuteronomy 6:16 (the place-name stands three times in the book — 6:16, here, and 33:8); and Kibroth-hattaavah, ‘the Graves of Craving’, from Numbers 11:34. Two of the three are burial-or-burning names given after the event they commemorate, which is what makes the list work as an argument: the map itself is the evidence.

★ ‘You rebelled against the MOUTH of Jehovah’ — and not one of the thirteen witnesses on either shelf prints the mouth. The Hebrew at v23 is pi YHVH, two words, the second of them the divine name and the first of them a body part. Eleven of the thirteen render it as an order or a command: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay-Rheims all read ‘the commandment’; NIV ‘the command’; NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 ‘the order’, with TNM 1987, TNM 2019 and NVI reading «la orden»; and RV60 «al mandato». Only TLB drops the noun altogether, reporting simply that Israel ‘refused to obey him’. The closest any of them comes to the image is RV 1909’s «al dicho de Jehová» — the SAYING — which at least keeps it a thing that was spoken. ★ The reason to keep the mouth is one chapter back. Deuteronomy 8:3 (already on these pages) is built on the same two words: the human lives ‘by every utterance that comes from the MOUTH of Jehovah’. Print ‘commandment’ here and the two verses stop rhyming; keep the mouth, and Deuteronomy 9:23 is the exact negative of Deuteronomy 8:3 — what you live by is the thing you rebelled against. Mister keeps it.

⚠ Verse 24 closes the bracket opened at v7 (see that note): the same three words, and the only two places in the Hebrew Bible they occur. What changes is the starting date — v7 counts from the exodus, v24 from ‘the day I knew you’, which is Moses putting his own testimony behind the charge instead of the nation’s history.

Verses 25–29 · ‘Lest the land say’ — the slander Moses fears is the accusation Israel already made in Deuteronomy 1

★ The prayer’s worst sentence is a quotation of Israel’s own words, put into the mouths of strangers. At Deuteronomy 1:27 (already on these pages) the exodus generation grumbles in its tents: be-sin’at YHVH otanu hotzi’anu me-eretz mitzrayim — ‘because Jehovah HATED us he brought us out of the land of Egypt’ — to destroy them. Here at v28 Moses argues that God must not destroy Israel, because if he does, the watching world will say u-mi-sin’ato otam hotzi’am, ‘and out of his hatred of them he brought them out’, to kill them in the wilderness. The same noun for the hatred, the same verb for the bringing-out, the same shape of sentence — only the killing-word differs. The blasphemy Moses is trying to prevent the nations from uttering is a sentence his own people said out loud eight chapters ago. It is not presented as a clever argument, and the text does not comment; the two verses simply stand in the same book.

⚠ Two charges, and four of the thirteen witnesses make them alternatives rather than a pair. The Hebrew joins them with a plain waw: God was not ABLE, AND he HATED them. Geneva reads ‘or because he hated them’, and both Reina-Valeras do the same (RV 1909 «ó porque los aborrecía», RV60 «o porque los aborrecía»), and so, in its own way, does TLB, which splits the sentence into two quoted alternatives joined by ‘or’. That turns a compound accusation — he was weak AND he was malicious — into a choice between two explanations. NVI restores the conjunction («Y como los aborrecía»). Mister keeps the pair, because the pair is the point: the two charges cover the whole ground between impotence and cruelty, and leave God no third character to be.

⚠ ‘Lest the LAND say’ — and the Hebrew’s own grammar is already uneasy about it. The subject ha-aretz is a feminine singular noun, and the verb attached to it, yomru, is masculine PLURAL: strictly, ‘lest the land, they say’. So the versions that supply people to do the speaking are not inventing — they are following the verb. Six of the thirteen do: Douay-Rheims ‘the inhabitants of the land’, NWT 2013 ‘the people of the land’, TNM 2019 «la gente del país», RV 1909 and RV60 both «digan los de la tierra», and NVI, which puts the speaking inside the country («en el país…digan»). KJV, ASV, NIV and NWT 1984 keep the land itself as the speaker, and this translation does too: a personified country is a figure the Hebrew Bible uses often enough, and the strangeness is worth keeping when it is the text’s own. ⚠ Geneva makes a different change in the same clause, ‘the country whence thou broughtest THEM’ — where the Hebrew has hotzetanu, brought US out, with Moses standing inside the group he is pleading for. ⚠ TLB replaces the land with ‘the Egyptians’, which is Exodus 32:12’s wording imported into Deuteronomy’s sentence — and Exodus 32:12 is a real and different reading: there it is Egypt who would speak, and the killing would be ‘in the mountains’, not in the wilderness. Two accounts, two witnesses to the slander; harmonising them costs the reader the difference.

★ The prayer ends by giving the pronouns back. God said ‘YOUR people’ to Moses at v12; Moses says ‘YOUR people and YOUR inheritance’ at v26 and again as the chapter’s last words at v29, and in between he asks God to look at Abraham, Isaac and Jacob instead of at the people in front of him — the same three names the chapter’s opening argument used at v5. He offers no defence of Israel at all. Verse 27 asks God to look AWAY from their hardness, their wickedness and their sin, naming all three; the case is not that they are better than the charge, but that they belong to him. ⚠ Nachalah, inheritance, is where one family of editions parts company with the other nine witnesses: NWT 1984 ‘your private property’ and TNM 1987 «tu propiedad particular», with NWT 2013 ‘your personal possession’ and TNM 2019 «tu propiedad» keeping the same idea in plainer words. Everything else on both shelves reads inheritance or heredad. It is a defensible gloss of a word whose root is about allotted possession, and it is worth naming as the outlier it is: the Hebrew noun is the same one that names Israel’s share of the land, and the metaphor of the chapter is family, not title.

⚠ Adonai YHVH at v26 is the double form, and this project renders it ‘Lord Jehovah’ — the same pairing ASV uses, ‘O Lord Jehovah’, and, on the Spanish shelf, RV 1909 and RV60 alike, «Oh Señor Jehová». It is the only place in the chapter where Moses addresses God by anything other than the name alone, and it comes at the exact moment he starts arguing.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew, English and Spanish all number this chapter identically at 29 verses — checked against every fetched witness on both shelves, with no splits and no renumbering anywhere; the only departure is presentational, TLB printing 26 units for the 29 verses by folding vv1–2, vv10–11 and vv13–14 into merged blocks, the same habit already noted in that paraphrase at Deuteronomy 8. Four renderings are departures worth naming in their own notes: ‘let go of me’ at v14, deliberately NOT the ‘let me alone’ already standing at Exodus 32:10, because the two verses use two different Hebrew verbs (n9-12); ‘the mouth of Jehovah’ at v23, where not one of the thirteen witnesses prints the mouth (n9-21); ‘your sin…the calf’ at v21, keeping the Hebrew’s apposition rather than inserting a noun to separate the object from the offence; and ‘a devouring fire’ at v3, printed as apposition with no particle of comparison, since the Hebrew has none. Verse 13 is rendered word-for-word identically to the already-shipped Exodus 32:9 — including ‘behold’ for hineh where this project often prefers ‘Look —’ — because a quotation that is reworded stops being visible as one. ‘Jehovah’ for the divine name, ‘Lord Jehovah’ for Adonai YHVH (matching Amos on these pages), ‘stiffnecked’ for qesheh-oref (matching Exodus 32–34), ‘cut a covenant’ for karat berit and ‘the brook’ for nachal all hold from earlier chapters unchanged. Verse and note anchors follow the canonical v9-N/n9-N form.

Library. Two new dictionary entries and one new encyclopedia entry, each in both languages: shamad, the annihilation verb this chapter runs six times — about a fifth of the book’s twenty-nine occurrences, and the heaviest concentration in Deuteronomy after chapter 28 — which completes the destruction-word family begun with cherem at Deuteronomy 7 and abad at Deuteronomy 8; hitnapel, to throw oneself down, the posture the whole intercession happens in (v18, v25 twice); and Taberah, ‘Burning’, a place named in only two verses in the entire Hebrew Bible, with atlas coordinates marked approximate because it is not securely located. Six existing entries extended: marah, which now carries the two-verse bracket unique to this chapter; qesheh-oref, whose Exodus entry had promised these two verses as ‘not yet on these pages’ and can now link them; tzedaqah, which now carries its third route — reckoned at Genesis 15:6, promised at Deuteronomy 6:25, denied three times here; shakach, whose Deuteronomy 8 entry now carries this chapter’s reversal of its object; qahal, which gains ‘the day of the assembly’; and machah, which gains v14 beside the Exodus 32 scene it already cited. VIDEO_QUEUE was checked at the moment the Taberah entry was written, per the standing rule: its four queued items are Ai, Shechem, Bethel and Caesarea, none of which touch this chapter, so nothing was placed.

Echoes paid. Deuteronomy 8:3’s pi YHVH, the mouth of Jehovah, answered at v23 with the preposition reversed — what you live BY becomes what you rebelled AGAINST (n9-21). Deuteronomy 8’s shakach, its central verb, returned at v7 with its object swapped from God to Israel itself (n9-7). Deuteronomy 8:3’s bread, met again at vv9 and 18 as forty days of literally not eating it (n9-9). Deuteronomy 1:28’s spy report, quoted back at vv1–2 in its own words and conceded rather than refuted (n9-1). Deuteronomy 1:27’s ‘because he hated us’, returning at v28 as the slander Moses fears the nations will speak (n9-25). Deuteronomy 4:24’s ‘devouring fire’, turned outward at v3 (n9-1). Deuteronomy 6:4’s Shema Yisrael, opening a war briefing instead of a creed (n9-1). Deuteronomy 6:25’s tzedaqah, denied three times over at vv4–6 (n9-4). Exodus 32:9, quoted verbatim at v13, and 32:7’s disowning pronoun, handed back at vv26 and 29 (n9-12, n9-25). Exodus 33:3, 5 and 34:9’s qesheh-oref, given to the new generation at v6 before the story that earned it (n9-4). Numbers 11:3’s Taberah and 11:34’s Kibroth-hattaavah, both collected in one line at v22 (n9-21). And Numbers 12:9, which is the near-miss that keeps v20’s claim about Aaron honest (n9-18).

Patterns worth carrying forward. ★ Deuteronomy 8’s open question is answered, and the answer is stronger than the case that raised it. That chapter found itself quoting its own opening at its close and asked whether a chapter bracketing itself would turn out to be a habit of this book or a one-off. It is a habit — and this chapter’s instance is tighter, because Deuteronomy 8’s bracket reused ordinary words while this one reuses three words that occur in no other verse of the Hebrew Bible: mamrim heyitem im-YHVH at v7 and again at v24, and nowhere else in all 929 archived chapters. Everything between them is the evidence for the sentence they bracket. ⭐ And the bracket is not only verbal: the grammatical NUMBER changes at exactly the same seam — singular ‘you’ through vv1–6 and the first half of v7, plural from the second half of v7 to v25 — so two independent features of the Hebrew open at the identical seam, mid-verse, and close within one verse of each other (the bracket at v24, the plural running one verse further into v25). Worth watching in Deuteronomy 10 and after (not yet on these pages) whether the number-shift keeps tracking the seam between what Israel is told to do and what Israel is reminded it did. ⚠ The shelf’s 1984/1987-versus-2013/2019 split added FIVE more instances here, all of them the older edition being the tighter one: the emphatic pronoun at v3; ‘stiff-necked’ becoming ‘obstinate’/«terco» at v6 (both shelves); the cohortative ‘let me make you’ flattening to a plain future at v14 (the Spanish shelf only — NWT 2013 keeps it); ‘from both my hands’ becoming ‘with both my hands’ at v17; and the identification ‘your sin…the calf’ softened at v21 (both shelves — dissolved outright by TNM 2019, its head noun demoted from a sin to a thing by NWT 2013). Running total across the four chapters that have counted this deliberately: twenty places — Deuteronomy 6’s four, 7’s seven, 8’s four, and this chapter’s five. Deuteronomy 8 called it a tendency rather than a rule, on the strength of two counter-examples; this chapter adds no counter-example and five more instances, which sharpens the tendency without changing the verdict — and the honest way to read a run like that is still case by case, since the two clean counter-examples already on the books did not stop being counter-examples. ★ A NEW pattern worth naming, because it is the shelf split’s Spanish-only twin. The Reina-Valera does the same thing across its own 1909–1960 gap, three times in this chapter alone: the bare apposition «fuego consumidor» gaining a «como» at v3, «vuestro pecado» gaining «el objeto de» at v21, and «al dicho» hardening into «al mandato» at v23. But it does NOT run one way: at v2 the 1909’s «hijos de gigantes» becomes the 1960’s «hijos de los anaceos», which is the more literal reading, transliterating a name where the older edition had translated it into a monster. Three toward smoothness, one toward the Hebrew — the same mixed picture the English pattern shows, arrived at independently, fifty years earlier, in another language. ★ And a third pattern, which is really a method: what Deuteronomy CHANGES when it retells Exodus is where its argument lives. Six changes are visible in this chapter alone — a different verb for ‘let me alone’ (v14), a bigger and now comparative offer to Moses (v14), the tablets broken before witnesses instead of beneath a mountain (v17), Moses’ own anger removed entirely, the calf’s dust thrown into a stream instead of made into a drink (v21), and two facts about Aaron that Exodus never reports at all (v20). None of them is a correction and none is presented as one. Worth carrying into Deuteronomy 10, which retells the tablets again.

Next installment in this book: Deuteronomy 10 — the second pair of tablets, cut by Moses this time and carried up in his own hand to a God who writes the same words again; the ark made to hold them; Aaron’s death and his son in his place; the tribe of Levi set apart to carry, to stand, and to bless. Then the sermon’s turn from the neck to the heart — circumcise the foreskin of your heart, and stiffen your neck no more — and the reason given is not power but partiality reversed: the God of gods takes no bribe, and loves the stranger, giving him bread and a coat.
