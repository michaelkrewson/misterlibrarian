Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–8 · ἀργύριον καὶ χρυσίον οὐχ ὑπάρχει μοι argyrion kai chrysion ouch hyparchei moi — silver and gold I do not have — and a verse the manuscripts can’t agree on either

The Beautiful Gate is not named anywhere else in the New Testament, and its exact identity among the temple’s several gates is not certain from the text alone; the strongest ancient candidate is the great bronze Nicanor Gate on the temple’s eastern side, which Josephus rated more valuable than the gold- and silver-plated gates around it.

⚠ V6’s command genuinely divides the manuscripts, not just the translations. The oldest single witness (Westcott-Hort’s base text) reads simply ‘walk’; the Byzantine majority text and the modern critical standard (NA28) both add ‘rise up and’ before it — so this is not a simple old-text-versus-new-text split, but real disagreement among the critical witnesses themselves. KJV and ESV print ‘rise up and walk’; ASV and NIV print the bare ‘walk.’ RV60 and NVI both keep ‘levántate y anda.’ This translation follows NA28.

Verses 9–11 · κρατοῦντος δὲ αὐτοῦ τὸν Πέτρον καὶ τὸν Ἰωάννην kratountos de autou ton Petron kai ton Iōannēn — he held on — and a portico named for a king who never saw it

The healed man clings to Peter and John (v11) rather than simply accompanying them — the Greek pictures a man still gripping on, not yet willing to test his own legs too far from the ones who fixed them. The crowd’s astonishment gathers at Solomon’s Portico, a covered colonnade along the temple’s eastern wall that Jewish tradition traced back to Solomon’s original temple, though the structure standing in this chapter is Herod’s, built a thousand years later on what was believed to be the same footprint. It recurs later in Acts (5:12) and, earlier still, is the place Jesus himself walked and was questioned in winter (John 10:23).

Verses 12–16 · τὸν παῖδα αὐτοῦ Ἰησοῦν ton paida autou Iēsoun — his servant, or his Son — a single Greek word and a real fork in the shelf

Peter’s first move is a denial of credit: not our own power, not our piety. What follows names Jesus with a Greek word, pais, that carries two entirely different weights in English and Spanish, and translators have never agreed which one it means here.

⚠ KJV renders pais ‘Son’ at both its occurrences in this chapter (v13, v26); ASV, NIV and ESV all render it ‘servant’ at both. The same fork appears in Spanish: RV60 reads ‘Hijo’ both times, NVI reads ‘siervo’ both times. The word itself can mean either a child/son or a servant depending on context; ‘servant’ connects Jesus to the Suffering Servant of Isaiah (the Greek Old Testament uses this identical word, pais theou, for Isaiah’s servant figure), while ‘Son’ reads the same Greek through a later, more explicitly filial theology. This translation follows the servant reading, for the connection to Isaiah the Greek itself invites — but the shelf splits cleanly on exactly which reading a single word should carry, and neither side is simply wrong.

‘The Author of life’ (v15, archegos) is a striking title in its own right — a word for a founder, originator, or pioneer, the one who goes first and opens a way others then follow. The same word returns at Hebrews 2:10 and 12:2, of Jesus as the ‘pioneer’ of salvation and of faith.

Verses 17–21 · χρόνων ἀποκαταστάσεως πάντων chronōn apokatastaseōs pantōn — times of restoration — a word Origen would later stretch further than Peter meant it

“Ignorance” (v17, agnoia) is offered almost as mitigation — Peter does not excuse the crowd, but he does not treat their guilt as the last word either; what follows is an invitation, not only a charge.

⚠ “Times of restoration” (v21) translates a single Greek noun, apokatastasis, that occurs nowhere else in the New Testament as a noun and has carried an outsized theological weight ever since — it is the word later taken up by Origen and others to argue for the eventual restoration of all things, including the damned, a doctrine (apocatastasis) the wider church never adopted but never fully stopped arguing about either. KJV stands alone on this shelf with ‘restitution,’ an older and more legal-sounding word; ASV, NIV and ESV all use some form of ‘restore.’ RV60 and NVI both read ‘restauración.’ Whatever precise scope Peter intended, the word itself named something enormous: not a repair, but everything set back the way it was meant to be.

Verses 22–23 · προφήτην ὑμῖν ἀναστήσει κύριος prophētēn hymin anastēsei kyrios — a prophet promised, quoted short

Peter’s citation compresses Deuteronomy 18:15-19, where Moses promises Israel a prophet like himself whom they must heed — a passage Judaism has read messianically at various points and that Peter here applies to Jesus directly, without argument, as though the identification needs no defending to this audience.

Verses 24–26 · ὑμῖν πρῶτον hymin prōton — the blessing, and who gets it first

The citation of ‘in your offspring shall all the families of the earth be blessed’ (v25) draws on the Abrahamic covenant (Genesis 22:18, itself echoing 12:3 and 18:18) — the promise this whole sermon has been building toward, now handed to a Jewish crowd in a Jewish courtyard as a Jewish inheritance, before it is ever offered to anyone else.

⚠ “To you first” (v26) is doing real work: not to you only, but to you first — a sequencing Paul will repeat as a settled pattern (‘to the Jew first, and also to the Greek,’ Romans 1:16), planted here at what may be the earliest recorded apostolic sermon after Pentecost itself.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: "servant" for pais at both v13 and v26, rather than "Son"; "Author of life" for archegos, matching its other New Testament occurrences (Hebrews 2:10, 12:2) rather than varied for local flavor; "rise up and walk" at v6, following NA28 over Westcott-Hort's bare "walk"; "ignorance" for agnoia, kept concrete rather than softened; and "times of restoration" for the single noun apokatastasis, not paraphrased into a verb.

Echoes paid. Restoration — chapter 1's note on Jesus declining to date "the restoration of the kingdom to Israel" said plainly that Peter would preach it in this chapter; v21's apokatastasis is that promise cashed, in Peter's own mouth. Witnesses (v15) — martys, the same note's word for testimony to what was seen, now attached specifically to the resurrection. Signs and wonders through the apostles — chapter 2's closing note called this "the engine of the next several chapters," and this healing is its first cashing.

Echoes planted. "You acted in ignorance" (v17), a plea that will not survive contact with the same crowd's rulers in the very next chapter, once the apostles are arrested for saying it twice. The prophet like Moses (v22), a citation Stephen will pick up and turn into an argument, at length, in chapter 7. And "to you first" (v26), the sequencing this whole book will spend its second half complicating.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether pais (vv13, 26) means "Son" or "servant" — the shelf splits cleanly and this translation states its reason for choosing servant without pretending the other reading is unreasonable. And v6's "rise up and walk," where the earliest single witness and the standing critical text actually disagree with each other, not just with the Byzantine tradition.
