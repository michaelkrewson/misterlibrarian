Hebron: Ish-bosheth’s hands grow slack at Abner’s death; Jonathan’s son Mephibosheth is lame from a fall at five; Rechab and Baanah of Beeroth, chiefs of Ish-bosheth’s raiding bands, strike him in the belly at noon and carry his head to David, who answers with the oath that he has been redeemed from all distress, reminds them of the bringer of good news killed at Ziklag, and has them put to death, their hands and feet cut off and their bodies hanged by the pool at Hebron, while Ish-bosheth’s head is buried in Abner’s grave.
Undated: within David’s seven years and six months at Hebron (2 Samuel 2:11), after Abner’s death (3:27) and within Ish-bosheth’s two years (2:10).

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · “His hands grew slack” — Ish-bosheth hears of Abner’s death, and the two chiefs of raiding bands from Beeroth

Slack hands. “And the son of Saul heard that Abner had died in Hebron, and his hands grew slack; and all Israel was terrified”: “and his hands grew slack” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one. It is the opposite of what David said to Jabesh, “let your hands be strong” (2 Samuel 2:7, already on these pages; see chazaq and raphah), and of the strengthening of Abner himself in 3:6 (already on these pages). The Hebrew calls him only “the son of Saul” here and in verse 2, and names him Ish-bosheth in verse 5. “Terrified” is nivhalu, the word of Saul before Gilboa, “greatly terrified” (1 Samuel 28:21, already on these pages). The Greek calls him Memphibosthe. The ASV has “his hands became feeble, and all the Israelites were troubled”; the KJV “his hands were feeble”; the NIV “he lost courage, and all Israel became alarmed”; the NWT 1984 “his hands became feeble and all the Israelites themselves were disturbed”; the Douay “his hands were weakened, and all Israel was troubled”; the Geneva “his hands were feeble, and all Israel was afraid”.

Two chiefs of raiding bands. “And the son of Saul had two men, chiefs of raiding bands; the name of the one was Baanah, and the name of the other Rechab, sons of Rimmon the Beerothite, of the sons of Benjamin”: gedudim, the raiding bands of David’s pursuit at Ziklag (1 Samuel 30:8, already on these pages); these men lead Ish-bosheth’s. “Sons of Rimmon” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in this chapter, verses 2, 5 and 9. The Hebrew explains, “for Beeroth also is reckoned to Benjamin”: Beeroth was one of the four cities of the Gibeonites (Joshua 9:17) and stands in Benjamin’s list (Joshua 18:25, both already on these pages). The ASV has “captains of bands”; the NIV “leaders of raiding bands”; the NWT 1984 “chiefs of the marauding bands”; the Douay “captains of his bands”; the TLB “captains of King Ish-bosheth’s raiding bands”.

Gittaim. “And the Beerothites fled to Gittaim, and have been sojourners there to this day”: “to Gittaim” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The Hebrew gives no reason for the flight, and “to this day” is the narrator’s, writing from a later time. Saul’s killing of Gibeonites in his zeal for Israel is told in 2 Samuel 21:1–2; the text here does not connect the two. The Greek has “Gethaim”. The ASV has “the Beerothites fled to Gittaim, and have been sojourners there until this day”; the NIV “resided there as foreigners to this day”; the NWT 1984 “alien residents there down to this day”; the Douay “were sojourners there until that time”.

Verse 4 · Mephibosheth — Jonathan’s son, five years old at Gilboa, dropped by his nurse and lame in his feet

The lame son. “And Jonathan son of Saul had a son, lame in his feet. He was five years old when the report of Saul and Jonathan came from Jezreel; and his nurse took him up and fled; and it happened, as she hurried to flee, that he fell and became lame; and his name was Mephibosheth”: the verse stands apart, between the death of Ish-bosheth’s support and Ish-bosheth’s own death, and tells what is left of Saul’s line in the one boy who is not a king’s son but a king’s grandson. “Lame in his feet” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and the verse where Ziba tells David “Jonathan has yet a son, who is lame in his feet” (2 Samuel 9:3, now on these pages). His age is given at the moment of Gilboa, when the report came “from Jezreel” (1 Samuel 31:7, already on these pages). “His nurse” is omanto, the root of Naomi, who “became his nurse” (Ruth 4:16, already on these pages). The name Mephibosheth is the Samuel books’ form of the Merib-baal of Chronicles (1 Chronicles 8:34), set beside Ish-bosheth and Eshbaal in the note at Romans 11 (already on these pages). The Greek calls him Memphibosthe, the form it also gives to Ish-bosheth in verses 1 and 2. The ASV has “a son that was lame of his feet … as she made haste to flee, that he fell, and became lame”; the NIV “who was lame in both feet”; the NWT 1984 “a son lame in the feet”; the Douay “a son that was lame of his feet”; the Geneva “had a son that was lame on his feet”.

Verses 5–8 · At noon — Rechab and Baanah enter the house “as fetchers of wheat”, strike Ish-bosheth in the belly, and carry his head to Hebron

The heat of the day. “And the sons of Rimmon the Beerothite, Rechab and Baanah, went, and came about the heat of the day to the house of Ish-bosheth; and he was lying down for the midday rest”: “about the heat of the day” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and Abraham at the door of his tent (Genesis 18:1, already on these pages); Saul’s first victory had run “until the heat of the day” (1 Samuel 11:11, already on these pages). “The midday rest” stands in one verse. The ASV has “came about the heat of the day to the house of Ish-bosheth, as he took his rest at noon”; the NIV “while he was taking his noonday rest”; the NWT 1984 “as he was taking his noonday siesta”; the KJV “who lay on a bed at noon”; the Geneva “who slept on a bed at noon”.

As fetchers of wheat. “And they came into the midst of the house, as fetchers of wheat; and they struck him in the belly; and Rechab and Baanah his brother escaped”: “fetchers of wheat” stands in one verse. The Greek has no such pretext: it has a woman at the door, “the doorkeeper of the house was cleaning wheat, and dozed and slept”, and the brothers “slipped by”; the Douay follows it, “the doorkeeper of the house, who was cleansing wheat, was fallen asleep”. “In the belly” is ha-chomesh, the third of the four verses of 2 Samuel that have the noun, after Abner’s blow to Asahel and Joab’s to Abner (2:23, 3:27, already on these pages) and before Joab’s to Amasa (20:10, now on these pages); see chomesh. The ASV has “as though they would have fetched wheat; and they smote him in the body”; the KJV “smote him under the fifth rib”; the NIV “as if to get some wheat, and they stabbed him in the stomach”; the NWT 1984 “as men fetching wheat, and then struck him in the abdomen”; the Douay “stabbed him in the groin”.

The head. “And they came into the house, as he lay on his bed in his bedchamber, and struck him, and put him to death, and removed his head; and they took his head and went by the way of the Arabah all night”: “in his bedchamber” stands in one verse, and “and removed his head” stands in one verse; the Philistines at Gilboa “cut off” Saul’s head with another verb (1 Samuel 31:9, already on these pages). The Arabah and “all night” are the way and the time of Abner’s flight from Gibeon (2 Samuel 2:29, already on these pages). The Greek has “the way toward the west”. The ASV has “they smote him, and slew him, and beheaded him, and took his head, and went by the way of the Arabah all night”; the NIV “Taking it with them, they traveled all night by way of the Arabah”; the NWT 1984 “they removed his head and took his head and walked on the road to the Arʹa·bah all night long”; the Douay “taking away his head they went off by the way of the wilderness”.

Vengeance. “And they brought the head of Ish-bosheth to David at Hebron, and said to the king: ‘Look — the head of Ish-bosheth son of Saul, your enemy, who sought your soul! And Jehovah has given my lord the king vengeance this day on Saul and on his seed’”: “who sought your soul” is Abiathar’s word for Saul (1 Samuel 22:23) and Abigail’s (25:29, both already on these pages), here said of a man whom the earlier chapters do not show seeking David’s life. Neqamot is plural, “vengeances”, and stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, among them Jephthah’s daughter, “Jehovah has avenged you on your enemies” (Judges 11:36, already on these pages), and three more (Ezekiel 25:17; Psalm 18:48; 94:1). David once brought a head to Jerusalem (1 Samuel 17:54, already on these pages); now a head is brought to him. The ASV has “Jehovah hath avenged my lord the king this day of Saul, and of his seed”; the NIV “This day the Lord has avenged my lord the king against Saul and his offspring”; the NWT 1984 “Jehovah gives to my lord the king revenge this day upon Saul and his offspring”; the Douay “the Lord hath revenged my lord the king this day of Saul, and of his seed”; the TLB “Today the Lord has given you revenge upon Saul”.

Verses 9–12 · David’s answer — “who has redeemed my soul out of all distress”, the bringer of good news at Ziklag, “burn you out of the land”, and the hands and feet by the pool

Redeemed out of all distress. “And David answered Rechab and Baanah his brother, the sons of Rimmon the Beerothite, and said to them: ‘As Jehovah lives, who has redeemed my soul out of all distress’”: “who has redeemed my soul out of all distress” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this oath and David’s in his old age, “out of every distress” (1 Kings 1:29, already on these pages). It is the prayer of Saul’s sparing, “may he deliver me out of all distress” (1 Samuel 26:24, already on these pages), turned into a thanksgiving; padah is the verb of buying back. The ASV has “As Jehovah liveth, who hath redeemed my soul out of all adversity”; the KJV “As the LORD liveth, who hath redeemed my soul out of all adversity”; the NIV “who has delivered me out of every trouble”; the NWT 1984 “As Jehovah who redeemed my soul out of all distress is living”; the Douay “who hath delivered my soul out of all distress”; the TLB “I swear by the Lord who saved me from my enemies”.

A bringer of good news. “When one told me, saying: ‘Look — Saul is dead’, and he was in his own eyes as a bringer of good news, I took hold of him and killed him at Ziklag — to whom I should have given a reward for his news”: “as a bringer of good news in his own eyes” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible; the root is that of mevasser (1 Samuel 31:9, already on these pages) and of the Amalekite whom David had struck down (2 Samuel 1:15, already on these pages; his claim is at 1:10). The last clause is ambiguous, and the versions split: the ASV has “which was the reward I gave him for his tidings”; the KJV “who thought that I would have given him a reward for his tidings”; the NIV “That was the reward I gave him for his news!”; the NWT 1984 “when it was due [for me] to give him the messenger’s fee”; the Douay “who should have been rewarded for his news”. The Greek has “to whom I ought to have given good-news gifts”.

Burn you out of the land. “How much more, when wicked men have killed a righteous man in his own house, upon his bed — shall I not now require his blood from your hand, and burn you out of the land?”: “wicked men” in this form stands in one verse. The “righteous man” is Ish-bosheth, and the word was Saul’s of David, “You are more righteous than I” (1 Samuel 24:17, already on these pages). “Require his blood from your hand” is Judah’s “from my hand you shall require him” (Genesis 43:9, already on these pages). “Burn you out” is bi’er, the verb of the law’s “you shall burn out the evil from your midst” (Deuteronomy 17:7, already on these pages), said here in the first person. The ASV has “shall I not now require his blood of your hand, and take you away from the earth?”; the NIV “should I not now demand his blood from your hand and rid the earth of you!”; the NWT 1984 “must I not clear YOU out of the earth?”; the Douay “take you away from the earth”; the TLB “Shall I not demand your lives?”.

Hands and feet. “And David commanded the young men, and they killed them, and cut off their hands and their feet, and hanged them by the pool in Hebron; and the head of Ish-bosheth they took, and buried in the grave of Abner in Hebron”: “and they cut off” (vayeqatsetsu) stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and Adoni-bezek’s thumbs and big toes (Judges 1:6, already on these pages; see the note at Deuteronomy 25:11–12). The bodies are hanged “by the pool”; Joshua’s five kings hung “until evening” (Joshua 10:26), and the law said a hanged body “shall not stay the night on the tree” (Deuteronomy 21:23, all already on these pages); the Hebrew does not say when these were taken down. The head goes to the grave of the man who made Ish-bosheth king (2 Samuel 3:32, already on these pages). The Greek has “the spring in Hebron”. The ASV has “cut off their hands and their feet, and hanged them up beside the pool in Hebron”; the KJV “hanged them up over the pool in Hebron”; the NIV “hung the bodies by the pool in Hebron”; the NWT 1984 “hanged them by the pool in Heʹbron”; the Douay “hanged them up over the pool in Hebron”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “his hands grew slack” for raphah, against “let your hands be strong” in 2 Samuel 2:7; “terrified” for nivhal, as in 1 Samuel 28:21; “chiefs of raiding bands” for gedudim; “sojourners”; “lame in his feet” for nekheh raglayim; “nurse”, as in Ruth 4:16; “the heat of the day”, as in Genesis 18:1; “the belly” for chomesh, as in 2:23 and 3:27; “removed his head”; “all night”, as in 2:29; “sought your soul”, as in 1 Samuel 22:23; “vengeance”; “redeemed my soul out of all distress”, as in 1 Kings 1:29; “a bringer of good news”; “require his blood from your hand”, as in Genesis 43:9; “burn you out”, as in Deuteronomy 17:7; “cut off”, as in Judges 1:6; “hanged”; and “Ish-bosheth” and “Hebron” in the forms of the last chapter.

Echoes paid. “Let your hands be strong” and Abner making himself strong (2 Samuel 2:7; 3:6); Saul’s terror (1 Samuel 28:21); David’s raiding bands (1 Samuel 30:8); Beeroth among the Gibeonite cities and in Benjamin (Joshua 9:17; 18:25); the report from Jezreel (1 Samuel 31:7); Naomi the nurse (Ruth 4:16); Merib-baal, Eshbaal and Ish-bosheth (Romans 11); Abraham’s noon and Saul’s victory at the heat of the day (Genesis 18:1; 1 Samuel 11:11); Asahel’s and Abner’s bellies (2 Samuel 2:23; 3:27); Saul’s head (1 Samuel 31:9); Abner’s night in the Arabah (2 Samuel 2:29); Abiathar’s and Abigail’s “seeks your soul” (1 Samuel 22:23; 25:29); Jephthah’s avenging (Judges 11:36); Goliath’s head (1 Samuel 17:54); the oath at its second place (1 Kings 1:29) and its prayer (1 Samuel 26:24); the bringer of news and the Amalekite (1 Samuel 31:9; 2 Samuel 1:10, 15); the righteous man (1 Samuel 24:17); Judah’s surety (Genesis 43:9); the burning out of evil (Deuteronomy 17:7); Adoni-bezek’s thumbs (Judges 1:6); the hanged kings and the law of the tree (Joshua 10:26; Deuteronomy 21:23); and Abner’s grave (2 Samuel 3:32).

Echoes planted. Saul’s killing of the Gibeonites (2 Samuel 21:1–2); Ziba’s “lame in his feet” and David’s kindness to Mephibosheth (2 Samuel 9:1–13, now on these pages); Mephibosheth with Ziba and at Absalom’s revolt (2 Samuel 16:1–4, now on these pages; 19:25–31, now on these pages); Sheba’s head thrown to Joab (2 Samuel 20:22, now on these pages) and Amasa in the belly (2 Samuel 20:10, now on these pages); Saul’s body hanged by the Philistines (2 Samuel 21:12); the tribes’ coming to David at Hebron (2 Samuel 5:1–3, now on these pages); and the Chronicler’s Merib-baal (1 Chronicles 8:34). None of the others is translated here yet.
