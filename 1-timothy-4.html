Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · “In later times some will fall away” — deceiving spirits, and a conscience branded

“The Spirit says expressly that in later times some will fall away from the faith.” Rhētōs, “expressly, in so many words,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). “Later times” is hysterois kairois, not the “last days”; the KJV reads “in the latter times,” and the Douay, after the Vulgate’s novissimis, “in the last times.” “Fall away” is the verb whose noun is “the apostasy” of 2 Thessalonians 2:3 (dictionary). “Paying attention to” is the participle of Titus 1:14, “not paying attention to Jewish myths,” and the same verb returns, for good reading, in verse 13. “Deceiving” is planos (dictionary). ⚠ The NWT 1984 reads “the inspired utterance says definitely,” and in the same verse “misleading inspired utterances,” where the Greek has pneuma, “Spirit,” and pneumasi, “spirits.”

“Through the hypocrisy of liars, branded in their own conscience.” Pseudologos, “a speaker of lies,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). Kaustēriazō, from kaustērion, a hot iron, stands in one verse too (dictionary); the Byzantine copies spell it kautēriazō, the word behind “cauterize.” ⚠ Readers take the image two ways: seared, a conscience burned until it feels nothing, as the KJV reads, “having their conscience seared with a hot iron”; or branded, marked with a hot iron as a runaway slave or a criminal was, as the ASV reads, “branded in their own conscience as with a hot iron.” The conscience is a thread of this letter: “a good conscience” at 1 Timothy 1:5 and 1 Timothy 1:19, “a pure conscience” at 1 Timothy 3:9.

Verses 3–5 · “Forbidding marriage” and foods — “everything created by God is good”

“Forbidding marriage and ordering abstinence from foods.” The Greek has one verb for two objects: “forbidding to marry, to abstain from foods,” and the second must be heard as a command. The Douay keeps the bare form, “Forbidding to marry, to abstain from meats”; the KJV supplies the verb, “Forbidding to marry, and commanding to abstain from meats,” where “meats” means foods. Metalēmpsis, “partaking,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). “Have come to know the truth” is the verb of “the knowledge of the truth” at 1 Timothy 2:4. Paul’s own letters meet the same rules: “Do not handle, do not taste, do not touch” at Colossians 2:21, and the slogan he quotes at 1 Corinthians 7:1, “it is good for a man not to touch a woman.”

⚠ Who forbade marriage, and the verse in later quarrels. The letter does not name the teachers. In the second century Irenaeus describes the Encratites, the “self-controlled,” who “preached against marriage” and among whom some “introduced abstinence from animal food” (Against Heresies 1.28.1). At the Reformation the verse was turned on the rule of clerical celibacy: the Augsburg Confession of 1530, Article 23, says, “Paul, in 1 Tim. 4:3, calls that a doctrine of devils which forbids marriage.” The Catholic answer is that marriage is not forbidden but celibacy is accepted by those who are ordained: the Catechism of the Catholic Church says the ordained ministers of the Latin Church, except permanent deacons, are normally chosen from men who intend to remain celibate (paragraph 1579), and that the Eastern Churches ordain married men as deacons and priests (paragraph 1580). This page records the uses and does not choose.

“For everything created by God is good, and nothing is to be rejected if it is received with thanksgiving.” Ktisma, “a created thing,” stands in four verses: this one, “firstfruits of his creatures” at James 1:18, and Revelation 5:13 and Revelation 8:9 (dictionary). “Good” is the verdict of creation, “God saw everything that he had made — and look, it was very good” at Genesis 1:31; Jesus “declared all foods clean” at Mark 7:19; and Paul eats “for he gives thanks to God” at Romans 14:6 and asks, “If I partake with thanks, why am I slandered over what I give thanks for?” at 1 Corinthians 10:30. Apoblētos, “to be thrown away,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. The KJV reads “every creature of God is good”; the NIV reads “everything God created is good”; the NWT 1984 reads “every creation of God is fine.”

“For it is sanctified through the word of God and prayer.” Enteuxis, “prayer,” stands in two verses, both in this letter: here and the “intercessions” of 1 Timothy 2:1 (dictionary). “Sanctified” is hagiazō, as at 1 Thessalonians 5:23 (dictionary). ⚠ Readers take “the word of God” as God’s own verdict in Genesis that what he made is good, or as words of Scripture spoken in the prayer at table. The NWT 1984 reads “through God’s word and prayer over” the food; the NIV reads “consecrated by the word of God and prayer.”

Verses 6–8 · “Train yourself for godliness” — old wives’ myths, and bodily training “for a little”

“You will be a good servant of Christ Jesus.” “Servant” is diakonos, the word of the servants at 1 Timothy 3:8; Timothy is held to the same standard (dictionary). The KJV and NIV read “minister”; the Byzantine text reads “Jesus Christ.” Entrephō, “to nourish, to rear,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). Parakoloutheō, “to follow closely,” stands in four verses: the signs that will “accompany” believers at Mark 16:17, Luke’s “having followed all things closely” at Luke 1:3, this one, and 2 Timothy 3:10 (dictionary). The NWT 1984 reads “the fine teaching which you have followed closely.”

“But refuse the profane myths that old women tell.” “Refuse” is the verb of Paul’s “I do not beg off from dying” at Acts 25:11. “Profane” is bebēlos, as at 1 Timothy 1:9; the “myths” are those of 1 Timothy 1:4 and the “Jewish myths” of Titus 1:14. Graōdēs, “old-womanish,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); it is a sneer at tales fit only for the nursery, not a verdict on old women. The KJV reads “refuse profane and old wives’ fables”; the NIV reads “godless myths and old wives’ tales”; the NWT 1984 reads “the false stories which violate what is holy and which old women tell.”

“Train yourself rather for godliness. For bodily training is of profit for a little.” “Train” is gymnazō, the verb of the gymnasium, where men trained naked, gymnos. Gymnasia, “training,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary); “bodily” is the adjective of the Spirit’s “bodily form” at Luke 3:22 (dictionary). Paul uses the athlete elsewhere: “Everyone who competes exercises self-control in all things” at 1 Corinthians 9:25. ⚠ The phrase pros oligon stands in two verses, here and James 4:14, a mist that appears “for a little”, and readers take it of extent, of little profit, or of time, profit for a short while; this translation keeps “for a little,” which holds both. The KJV reads “bodily exercise profiteth little”; the ASV reads “bodily exercise is profitable for a little”; the NIV reads “physical training is of some value.” “The life that is now and the life to come” sets the two side by side; “godliness” is eusebeia (dictionary).

Verses 9–10 · “Reproached” or “strive”? — “the Savior of all people, especially of believers”

“Faithful is the saying, and worthy of full acceptance.” The third of the letter’s “faithful sayings,” in the words of 1 Timothy 1:15, after 1 Timothy 3:1. ⚠ Readers differ on which saying it vouches for: the line about godliness in verse 8 before it, or the line about hope in verse 10 after it.

⚠ “We labor and are reproached” — or “strive”? The manuscripts read two ways. Oneidizometha, “we are reproached,” is the reading of the Byzantine copies and of the Greek text printed on this page, which this translation follows; so the KJV, “we both labour and suffer reproach,” the Geneva, “we labor and are rebuked,” and the Douay, after the Vulgate, “we labor and are reviled.” It is the verb of Romans 15:3, “The reproaches of those who reproached you have fallen on me.” Agōnizometha, “we strive,” the athlete’s verb of Colossians 1:29, “struggling,” is printed by Westcott and Hort and by the Nestle-Aland edition; so the ASV, “we labor and strive,” and the NWT 1984, “we are working hard and exerting ourselves.” The readings differ, and the page sets them out.

⚠ “The living God, who is the Savior of all people, especially of believers.” “The living God” is the title of 1 Timothy 3:15; “Savior” the title of 1 Timothy 1:1 and 1 Timothy 2:3. “All people” is pantōn anthrōpōn, as at 1 Timothy 2:4; “especially” is malista, as in “especially toward the members of the household of the faith” at Galatians 6:10. Readers take the verse four ways: Savior of all as the one who keeps and provides for every life, and Savior of believers in the full sense; Savior of all in offer, of believers in fact; “especially” as “that is,” so that the two groups are one; or a promise that all will in the end be saved. This page sets out the readings and does not choose. The NWT 1984 reads “a Savior of all sorts of men,” the same narrowing of “all” as at 2:4; the KJV reads “the Saviour of all men, specially of those that believe.”

Verses 11–13 · “Let no one look down on your youth” — and “the reading”

“Your youth.” Neotēs, “youth,” stands in four verses: the rich man’s “from my youth” at Mark 10:20 and Luke 18:21, Paul’s “from youth” at Acts 26:4, and this one (dictionary). Paul gave the same warning about Timothy to Corinth at 1 Corinthians 16:11, telling them not to despise him; Timothy had joined Paul as a young disciple at Lystra, “well spoken of by the brothers,” at Acts 16:2. The KJV reads “Let no man despise thy youth”; the NIV reads “Don’t let anyone look down on you because you are young.”

“Be an example to the believers in speech, in conduct, in love, in faith, in purity.” “Example” is typos, the “pattern” the Thessalonians became at 1 Thessalonians 1:7 (dictionary). “Conduct” is anastrophē, Paul’s “former way of life” at Galatians 1:13. Hagneia, “purity,” stands in two verses, both in this letter: here and 5:2 (yet to come in this translation; dictionary). The Byzantine text adds “in spirit,” and so the KJV: “in word, in conversation, in charity, in spirit, in faith, in purity.” The NWT 1984 reads “in speaking, in conduct, in love, in faith, in chasteness.”

“Until I come, pay attention to the reading, to exhortation, to teaching.” “Until I come” answers “hoping to come to you soon” at 1 Timothy 3:14; “pay attention” is the verb of the false teachers’ hearers in verse 1. Anagnōsis, “the reading,” with the article, stands in three verses: the synagogue’s “reading of the Law and the Prophets” at Acts 13:15, the reading of the old covenant at 2 Corinthians 3:14, and this one (dictionary). It is reading aloud to the gathered congregation, as Paul asks for his own letter at 1 Thessalonians 5:27. In the middle of the second century Justin Martyr describes the same order on Sunday: the writings of the apostles or the prophets “are read, as long as time permits,” and “when the reader has ceased, the president verbally instructs, and exhorts” (First Apology 67). The NIV reads “the public reading of Scripture”; the NWT 1984 reads “public reading”; the KJV reads “give attendance to reading.”

Verses 14–16 · “The laying on of the hands of the body of elders” — and “you will save both yourself and those who hear you”

“Do not neglect the gift that is in you, which was given you through prophecy.” “Gift” is charisma, as in “gifts that differ according to the grace given to us” at Romans 12:6 (dictionary). “Through prophecy” looks back to “the prophecies previously made about you” at 1 Timothy 1:18. The NWT 1984 reads “given you through a prediction”; the Douay, after the Vulgate’s gratiam, reads “Neglect not the grace that is in thee.”

⚠ “With the laying on of the hands of the body of elders.” Presbyterion, “body of elders,” stands in three verses: the Jewish “council of elders of the people” at Luke 22:66, “the whole body of elders” at Acts 22:5, and this one, the only one where the elders are the congregation’s (dictionary). Hands were laid on the seven at Acts 6:6 and on Barnabas and Saul at Acts 13:3. At 2 Timothy 1:6 the gift comes “through the laying on of my hands”: readers take the two verses as one occasion with Paul among the elders, or as two occasions. The KJV and ASV read “the presbytery”; the NIV reads “the body of elders”; the NWT 1984 reads “the body of older men”; the Douay, from the Vulgate’s presbyterii, reads “the hands of the priesthood.”

“Work at these things; give yourself wholly to them, so that your progress may be evident to all.” Meletaō, “to work at, to give careful thought to,” is the verb of “the peoples plot empty things” at Acts 4:25, quoting Psalm 2:1 (dictionary). “Give yourself wholly to them” is, word for word, “be in them.” Prokopē, “progress,” stands in three verses: “the advance of the gospel” at Philippians 1:12, the Philippians’ progress at Philippians 1:25, and Timothy’s here (dictionary). The KJV reads “Meditate upon these things; give thyself wholly to them”; the ASV reads “Be diligent in these things”; the NWT 1984 reads “Ponder over these things; be absorbed in them.”

“Keep watch over yourself and over the teaching; persist in them, for by doing this you will save both yourself and those who hear you.” “Persist” is epimenō, the “if indeed you remain in the faith” of Colossians 1:23. ⚠ “Save” is the verb behind “Savior” in verse 10, here put in Timothy’s hands. Paul speaks so of himself: “so that I might by all means save some” at 1 Corinthians 9:22, and “save some from among them” at Romans 11:14. Readers take it of Timothy as God’s instrument for others and of his own perseverance in the faith that saves him. The KJV reads “thou shalt both save thyself, and them that hear thee”; the NIV reads “Watch your life and doctrine closely.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “later times,” not “last days”; “fall away,” the verb of 2 Thessalonians 2:3’s “apostasy”; “paying attention” and “pay attention” for one verb in verses 1 and 13, as at Titus 1:14; “branded,” with “seared” in the note; “good,” the word of Genesis 1:31; “servant,” as at 3:8; “train” and “training” for one root; “for a little,” which keeps both readings; “reproached,” with “strive” in the note; “all people,” as at 2:4; “the reading,” with the article; “body of elders,” as at Acts 22:5; and “save” in verse 16, the verb behind “Savior” in verse 10.

Echoes paid. The faithful saying of 1 Timothy 1:15 and 1 Timothy 3:1; the myths of 1 Timothy 1:4 and Titus 1:14; the conscience of 1 Timothy 1:19; the prophecies about Timothy at 1 Timothy 1:18; the servant of 1 Timothy 3:8; the living God of 1 Timothy 3:15; all people from 1 Timothy 2:4; the creation very good of Genesis 1:31; “let no one despise him” from 1 Corinthians 16:11; and the laying on of hands of 2 Timothy 1:6.

Echoes planted. Purity again at 5:2; the elders who lead well and the laying on of hands at 5:17 and 5:22; and godliness with contentment at 6:6.

Next in this book: 1 Timothy 5, already on these pages. Older and younger men and women, the list of widows, double honor for elders who lead well, the ox that is not to be muzzled, and a little wine for the stomach.
