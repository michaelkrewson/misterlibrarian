Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · “Appeal to him as to a father” — older and younger men and women

“Do not rebuke an older man sharply, but appeal to him as to a father.” “An older man” is presbyteros, the word that names an office in verses 17 and 19, “elder”; here it stands beside “younger men,” “older women” and “younger women,” and means age (dictionary). Epiplēssō, “to strike at, to rebuke sharply,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). The whole congregation is a family: fathers, brothers, mothers, sisters. “In all purity” is the hagneia of 1 Timothy 4:12 (dictionary). The KJV and ASV read “Rebuke not an elder”; the NWT 1984 reads “Do not severely criticize an older man”; the Douay reads “An ancient man rebuke not.”

Verses 3–8 · “Honor widows who are truly widows” — and “worse than an unbeliever”

“Honor widows who are truly widows.” To “honor” includes support: the commandment “Honor your father and your mother” is set against keeping back help from them at Mark 7:10 and Mark 7:12, and the elders’ “double honor” in verse 17 is followed by the worker’s wages. The Law gives God as the one “doing justice for the orphan and the widow” at Deuteronomy 10:18; the first quarrel in the congregation at Jerusalem was over widows “overlooked in the daily service” at Acts 6:1; and pure religion is “to look in on orphans and widows in their affliction” at James 1:27. The NIV reads “Give proper recognition to those widows who are really in need”; the NWT 1984 reads “Honor widows that are actually widows.”

“Let them learn first to show godliness toward their own household and to make a return to their forebears.” “Show godliness” is the verb of eusebeia (dictionary): care for one’s own parents is counted as religion. Ekgonos, “grandchild,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does amoibē, “a return, a repayment” (dictionary). Progonos, “forebear,” stands in two verses: here and Paul’s “as my forefathers did” at 2 Timothy 1:3. “Acceptable before God” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 2:3; the Byzantine text adds “good and,” and so the KJV, “for that is good and acceptable before God.” ⚠ The Greek “let them learn” is plural, the children; the Vulgate makes it singular, the widow herself, and so the Douay reads “let her learn first to govern her own house.”

“The one who is truly a widow, and left alone, has set her hope on God and continues in petitions and prayers night and day.” Monoō, “to be left alone,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. “Has set her hope” is the perfect of 1 Timothy 4:10, “we have set our hope on the living God”; “petitions and prayers” are the first two words of 1 Timothy 2:1. The picture is Anna’s at Luke 2:37, a widow who “never left the temple, serving night and day with fastings and prayers.” The KJV reads “trusteth in God, and continueth in supplications and prayers night and day.”

“The one who lives in self-indulgence is dead while she lives.” Spatalaō, “to live in luxury,” stands in two verses: here and James 5:5 (yet to come in this translation; dictionary). The paradox is that of the letter to Sardis at Revelation 3:1, “you have a name that you are alive, and you are dead.” The KJV reads “she that liveth in pleasure is dead while she liveth”; the NWT 1984 reads “the one that goes in for sensual gratification.”

“If anyone does not provide for his own, and especially for the members of his household, he has denied the faith and is worse than an unbeliever.” “Above reproach” in verse 7 is the word of the overseer at 1 Timothy 3:2. “Especially” and “members of the household” are the words of Galatians 6:10, “especially toward the members of the household of the faith.” The faith is denied here not in words but in neglect, and an unbeliever who keeps the ordinary duty to family is judged the better. The KJV reads “is worse than an infidel”; the NWT 1984 reads “is worse than a person without faith.”

Verses 9–10 · “Put on the list” — sixty years old, “the wife of one husband,” and feet washed

⚠ “Let a widow be put on the list who is not less than sixty years old, the wife of one husband.” Katalegoō, “to enroll, to put on a list,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). Readers take the list two ways: a roll of widows supported by the congregation, or an order of widows with duties, praying “night and day” (verse 5) and bound by a pledge (verse 12). The early second century knows such widows: Ignatius greets “the virgins who are called widows” (To the Smyrnaeans 13), and Polycarp tells the widows of Philippi to remember that “they are the altar of God” (To the Philippians 4). “The wife of one husband” mirrors “the husband of one wife” at 1 Timothy 3:2, with the same readings. The KJV reads “Let not a widow be taken into the number under threescore years old”; the NWT 1984 reads “Let a widow be put on the list”; the Douay reads “Let a widow be chosen.”

“If she has brought up children, if she has shown hospitality to strangers, if she has washed the feet of the holy ones.” Teknotropheō, “to bring up children,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does xenodocheō, “to receive strangers,” a near neighbor of the overseer’s philoxenos at 1 Timothy 3:2. Washing feet is the command of John 13:14, “you also ought to wash one another’s feet.” Eparkeō, “to help, to relieve,” stands in two verses, both in this chapter: here and verse 16 (dictionary). Epakoloutheō, “to follow after,” stands in four verses: the signs that “accompanied” the word at Mark 16:20, this one, verse 24 below, and 1 Peter 2:21 (the last yet to come in this translation). The KJV reads “if she have lodged strangers, if she have washed the saints’ feet”; the NIV reads “washing the feet of the Lord’s people.”

Verses 11–16 · “Their first faith” — younger widows, gossips and busybodies, and “I want the younger ones to marry”

“But refuse younger widows; for when their desires turn them against Christ, they want to marry.” “Refuse” is the verb of 1 Timothy 4:7. Katastrēniaō, from strēniaō, “to run riot, to live wantonly,” with kata, “against,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). The KJV reads “when they have begun to wax wanton against Christ”; the NIV reads “when their sensual desires overcome their dedication to Christ”; the NWT 1984 reads “when their sexual impulses have come between them and the Christ.”

⚠ “Because they have set aside their first faith.” Pistis is the letter’s word for “faith” (verse 8, “denied the faith”), and it can also mean a pledge given. Readers take “their first faith” two ways: a pledge, made when a widow was enrolled, to remain unmarried and serve; or their faith in Christ itself, abandoned. The ASV reads “because they have rejected their first pledge,” and the NIV “broken their first pledge”; the KJV reads “because they have cast off their first faith,” and the Douay “made void their first faith.” “Set aside” is atheteō, to treat as void.

“Not only idle, but also gossips and busybodies.” Phlyaros, “a babbler, a gossip,” stands in one verse of the New Testament; its verb is Diotrephes’ “prating against us” at 3 John 1:10 (dictionary). The adjective periergos stands in two verses: here, “busybodies,” and the “curious arts” of the magicians at Acts 19:19; its verb is the “busybodies” of 2 Thessalonians 3:11 (dictionary). The KJV reads “tattlers also and busybodies, speaking things which they ought not”; the NWT 1984 reads “gossipers and meddlers in other people’s affairs.”

⚠ “So I want the younger ones to marry, to bear children, to manage their households.” The Greek says only “the younger,” feminine: the younger widows of verse 11, or younger women generally. The KJV reads “I will therefore that the younger women marry”; the ASV reads “I desire therefore that the younger widows marry.” Teknogoneō, “to bear children,” stands in one verse, the verb of “the childbearing” at 1 Timothy 2:15 (dictionary); oikodespoteō, “to be master of a house,” stands in one verse too (dictionary). The counsel sits beside Paul’s at 1 Corinthians 7:8, “To the unmarried and the widows I say: it is good for them to remain as I am,” and at 1 Corinthians 7:39 and 1 Corinthians 7:40, where a widow “is free to marry whom she wishes, only in the Lord,” but “she is happier if she remains as she is.” Readers explain the difference by different circumstances, by a later stage of Paul’s life, or by the question of authorship set out at 1 Timothy 1; this page records them and does not choose.

“To give the adversary no occasion for reviling; for some have already wandered off after Satan.” ⚠ “The adversary” is ho antikeimenos, “the one who opposes”: readers take it of a human opponent, as in “there are many who oppose” at 1 Corinthians 16:9, or of Satan, named in the next verse; the same participle is the man of lawlessness, “who opposes,” at 2 Thessalonians 2:4. Loidoria, “reviling,” stands in two verses: here and 1 Peter 3:9. “Wandered off” is the verb of 1 Timothy 1:6; Satan is named at 1 Timothy 1:20. The Byzantine text of verse 16 reads “any believing man or woman,” and so the KJV: “If any man or woman that believeth have widows.” The NWT 1984 reads “If any believing woman has widows.”

Verses 17–18 · “Double honor” — “You shall not muzzle an ox,” and “The worker is worthy of his wages”

⚠ “Let the elders who lead well be counted worthy of double honor.” “Lead” is the proistēmi of the overseer at 1 Timothy 3:4 and of 1 Thessalonians 5:12 (dictionary). Words built on diplous, “double,” stand in three verses: the convert made “twice as much a son of Gehenna” at Matthew 23:15, this one, and Revelation 18:6. Readers take “double honor” as honor twice that of the widows of verse 3, as honor and pay together, or as a double portion. John Calvin, commenting on the verse, reports that Chrysostom took it as “support and reverence” and himself compares the elders with the widows. The Douay, from the Vulgate’s presbyteri, reads “Let the priests that rule well, be esteemed worthy of double honour”; the English “priest” descends from presbyter. The TLB reads “should be paid well.”

⚠ “Especially those who labor in the word and in teaching.” Readers take the verse as naming two kinds of elders, those who govern and those who also teach, or as singling out the elders who labor hardest at a teaching all of them share, since every overseer must be “able to teach” at 1 Timothy 3:2. Calvin writes, “We may learn from this, that there were at that time two kinds of elders; for all were not ordained to teach,” and Presbyterian churches distinguish ruling and teaching elders. This page records the readings and does not choose. The NIV reads “especially those whose work is preaching and teaching”; the NWT 1984 reads “especially those who work hard in speaking and teaching.”

⚠ “For the Scripture says, ‘You shall not muzzle an ox while it treads out the grain,’ and, ‘The worker is worthy of his wages.’” The first line is the Law at Deuteronomy 25:4, “You shall not muzzle an ox while it threshes,” which Paul also turns to the support of apostles at 1 Corinthians 9:9, with another verb for “muzzle,” and at 1 Corinthians 9:14, “the Lord commanded that those who proclaim the good news should live from the good news.” “Muzzle” here is phimoō, Jesus’ “Be muzzled” to the unclean spirit at Mark 1:25. The second line is word for word Jesus’ saying at Luke 10:7, “the worker is worthy of his wages”; at Matthew 10:10 it is “of his food.” Readers take “the Scripture says” as covering both lines, so that a saying of Jesus, or Luke’s Gospel, is already counted as Scripture, or as covering only the first, with the second a proverb; Calvin writes, “He does not quote this as a passage of Scripture, but as a proverbial saying.” The NWT 1984 reads “You must not muzzle a bull when it threshes out the grain”; the KJV reads “The labourer is worthy of his reward.”

Verses 19–21 · “Two or three witnesses” — reproof in front of everyone, and “the chosen angels”

“Do not accept an accusation against an elder, except on the basis of two or three witnesses.” The rule is the Law’s at Deuteronomy 19:15, “At the mouth of two witnesses, or at the mouth of three witnesses, a matter shall stand,” used by Jesus at Matthew 18:16 and by Paul at 2 Corinthians 13:1. Here it shields the elder from a single accuser. The Douay reads “Against a priest receive not an accusation”; the NIV reads “unless it is brought by two or three witnesses.”

⚠ “Those who go on sinning, reprove in front of everyone.” The participle is present, of sin that continues. Readers take it of elders, the subject of verse 19, as the NIV makes explicit, “But those elders who are sinning you are to reprove before everyone,” or of anyone in the congregation. “Reprove” is elenchō, to expose and convict. The KJV reads “Them that sin rebuke before all, that others also may fear.”

⚠ “Before God and Christ Jesus and the chosen angels.” “I solemnly charge you” is diamartyromai, Paul’s “testifying solemnly” at Acts 20:21. “Chosen angels” stands only here; readers take it of the angels who did not fall, set against those “who did not keep their own domain” at Jude 1:6, or of the angels who attend the judgment, as in “when he comes in the glory of his Father with the holy angels” at Mark 8:38. Prokrima, “a judgment made beforehand,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does prosklisis, “a leaning toward one side” (dictionary). The Byzantine text reads “the Lord Jesus Christ,” and so the KJV, “I charge thee before God, and the Lord Jesus Christ.” The Douay reads “doing nothing by declining to either side”; the NWT 1984 reads “doing nothing according to a biased leaning.”

Verses 22–25 · “Lay hands on no one hastily” — “a little wine for the sake of your stomach”

⚠ The laying on of hands, “hastily,” and “the sins of others.” Readers take the laying on of hands two ways: the appointing of elders, as with Timothy’s own gift at 1 Timothy 4:14 and the seven at Acts 6:6, so that a hasty appointment makes Timothy a sharer in the man’s sins; or the receiving back of a penitent, a practice of the later church, of which Eusebius says, “the ancient custom prevailed in regard to such, that they should receive only the laying on of hands with prayers” (Church History 7.2). “Keep yourself pure” uses hagnos, the adjective of the “purity” of verse 2. The KJV reads “Lay hands suddenly on no man”; the NIV reads “Do not be hasty in the laying on of hands.”

⚠ “No longer be a water-drinker, but use a little wine for the sake of your stomach and your frequent illnesses.” Hydropoteō, “to drink water,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does stomachos, “stomach” (dictionary). Pyknos, “frequent,” stands in three verses: John’s disciples who “fast often” at Luke 5:33, Felix sending for Paul “more often” at Acts 24:26, and this one. Readers note that the advice follows straight on from “keep yourself pure,” and take it as a guard against counting abstinence as purity, perhaps against the teachers of 1 Timothy 4:3. Wine was a common medicine in the ancient world. The KJV reads “use a little wine for thy stomach’s sake and thine often infirmities”; the TLB adds its own comment, “this doesn’t mean you should completely give up drinking wine.”

“The sins of some people are evident, going ahead of them to judgment; but for others they follow after.” Prodēlos, “evident, plain beforehand,” stands in three verses: verses 24 and 25 here, and Hebrews 7:14 (dictionary). “Follow after” is the verb of the widow who “followed after every good work” in verse 10. The two verses close the matter of verse 22: neither hidden sins nor hidden good works stay hidden, so haste in judging either way is unneeded. The KJV reads “Some men’s sins are open beforehand, going before to judgment”; the NIV reads “the sins of others trail behind them.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “an older man” in verse 1 and “elder” in verses 17 and 19 for one word, as the context sets it; “truly widows”; “show godliness,” the verb of eusebeia; “acceptable before God,” as at 2:3; “has set her hope,” as at 4:10; “the wife of one husband,” matching 3:2; “followed after” in verses 10 and 24 for one verb; “refuse,” as at 4:7; “their first faith,” with “pledge” in the note; “the younger ones,” as the Greek leaves it; “wandered off,” as at 1:6; “lead,” as at 3:4; the ox and the worker in the words of 1 Corinthians 9:9 and Luke 10:7; “the chosen angels”; and “water-drinker.”

Echoes paid. Purity from 1 Timothy 4:12; petitions and prayers from 1 Timothy 2:1; above reproach and one wife from 1 Timothy 3:2; the childbearing of 1 Timothy 2:15; the busybodies of 2 Thessalonians 3:11; those who lead well, from 1 Timothy 3:4 and 1 Thessalonians 5:12; refuse, from 1 Timothy 4:7; the laying on of hands of 1 Timothy 4:14; the ox of Deuteronomy 25:4 and 1 Corinthians 9:9; the worker’s wages of Luke 10:7; and the two or three witnesses of Deuteronomy 19:15.

Echoes planted. Slaves and masters at 6:1–2; “godliness with contentment” and the love of money at 6:6–10; and “the good confession” before Pontius Pilate at 6:13.

Next in this book: 1 Timothy 6, already on these pages. Slaves and masters, godliness with contentment, the love of money as a root of all kinds of evils, the good fight of the faith, and a charge to the rich.
