David goes up to the room over the gate and weeps, ‘My son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would that I had died, I, in your place’, and the salvation of the day is turned into mourning, the people stealing into the city as men put to shame. Joab tells him he has put to shame the faces of his servants and must speak to their heart, and the king sits in the gate. The people contend throughout the tribes about bringing the king back; David sends to Zadok and Abiathar to the elders of Judah, offers Amasa the army in place of Joab, and bends the heart of Judah as one man, and the king returns to the Jordan, where Judah comes to Gilgal. Shimei, with a thousand men of Benjamin and Ziba, meets him at the crossing, and the king swears to Shimei that he shall not die; Mephibosheth, who has not tended himself since the king left, says his servant deceived him, and the king says that he and Ziba divide the field; Barzillai the Gileadite crosses the Jordan with the king, declines to go to Jerusalem, and sends Chimham in his place, and the king kisses him and blesses him. At Gilgal the man of Israel and the man of Judah quarrel over the king, and the word of Judah is the harder.
The days after the battle in the forest of Ephraim: from the gate at Mahanaim to the crossing of the Jordan and Gilgal, with Judah and half of Israel bringing the king back.

23

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–5 · 2 Samuel 19 The king’s cry — “Would that I had died, I, in your place”, a salvation turned to mourning, and the people who steal into the city

Shaken. “And the king was shaken, and went up to the room over the gate, and wept; and as he went, he said thus: ‘My son Absalom, my son, my son Absalom! Would that I had died, I, in your place — Absalom, my son, my son!’”: the English versions number this verse 2 Samuel 18:33, and in the Hebrew it is 19:1. The verb is vayyirgaz (see ragaz). The ASV has “was much moved”. The NIV has “The king was shaken”. The NWT 1984 has “the king became disturbed”. The TLB has “the king broke into tears”. Rahlfs’ Greek has etarachthē, “was troubled”. The wish is mi yitten moti ani tachtekha, “who will give my dying, I, in your place”. The ASV has “would I had died for thee”. The NIV has “If only I had died instead of you”. The NWT 1984 has “O that I might have died, I myself, instead of you”. The TLB has “If only I could have died for you!”. The word tachat, “in place of”, is the word of Judah’s offer to Joseph, “let your servant remain instead of the boy” (Genesis 44:33, now on these pages): a man of Judah’s tribe offers himself for a son, and in David’s mouth it comes too late. “My son” stands five times in this cry, with Absalom’s name three times, and in verse 5 three times and twice.

The room over the gate. “The room over the gate”: the Hebrew is aliyat hasha‘ar, an upper room. The ASV has “the chamber over the gate”. The NIV has “the room over the gateway”. The NWT 1984 has “the roof chamber over the gateway”. Rahlfs’ Greek has to hyperōon tēs pylēs. The gate has carried the last two chapters: the king stood “by the side of the gate” as his people went out (2 Samuel 18:4), sat “between the two gates” to wait for news (18:24), goes up over the gate to weep (19:1), and in verse 9 will come down and sit in it.

The salvation of that day. “And the salvation of that day was turned into mourning for all the people; for the people heard it said that day: ‘The king is grieved for his son’”: the noun is teshu‘ah (see teshuah), the word of Samson’s “great salvation” (Judges 15:18) and of Saul’s “today Jehovah has worked salvation in Israel” (1 Samuel 11:13, both now on these pages). The ASV has “the victory that day”. The NIV has “the victory that day”. The NWT 1984 has “the salvation on that day”. The TLB has “the joy of that day’s wonderful victory”. Rahlfs’ Greek has hē sōtēria. The people hear that the king is “grieved”, ne‘etsav, and the deliverance they brought him becomes a day they would rather not have won.

Stealing in. “And the people stole in that day to come into the city, as the people steal in who are put to shame when they flee in the battle”: the verb is hitgannev, and the verb ganav, to steal, stands in four verses of 2 Samuel: 15:6, where Absalom “stole the heart of the men of Israel” (now on these pages), this one, 19:42, and 21:12. The ASV has “gat them by stealth”. The NIV has “stole into the city”. The NWT 1984 has “began to steal away”. The Douay has “shunned the going into the city”. “Put to shame” is nikhlam, the word of David’s envoys who were “greatly humiliated” (2 Samuel 10:5, now on these pages; see kalam). “And the king covered his face, and the king cried out with a loud voice”: at the Mount of Olives he had gone up weeping “with his head covered” and the people had covered theirs (2 Samuel 15:30, now on these pages). The ASV has “the king covered his face”. The NIV has “The king covered his face and cried aloud”. The Douay has “the king covered his head”.

Verses 6–9 · 2 Samuel 19 Joab’s rebuke — “you have put to shame today the faces of your servants”, “speak to the heart”, and the king in the gate

You have put to shame today. “You have put to shame today the faces of all your servants, who today have saved your life, and the lives of your sons and your daughters, and the lives of your wives and the lives of your concubines”: hevoshta hayyom, with the root of shame that the army had put on in verse 4. The ASV has “Thou hast shamed this day the faces of all thy servants”. The NIV has “Today you have humiliated all your men”. The NWT 1984 has “You have today put to shame the face of all your servants”. The TLB has “making us feel ashamed”. The Hebrew says nefesh four times, “the life of” you, your sons and daughters, your wives, your concubines. The NWT 1984 keeps it as “the soul of your wives and the soul of your concubines”. The concubines are the ten whom David left to keep the house and whom Absalom went in to (2 Samuel 16:22, now on these pages), and what the king will do with them is in the next chapter.

Loving those who hate you. “In loving those who hate you, and hating those who love you”: the ASV has “in that thou lovest them that hate thee, and hatest them that love thee”. The NIV has “You love those who hate you and hate those who love you”. The NWT 1984 has “by loving those hating you and by hating those loving you”. “For you have declared today that commanders and servants are nothing to you”: the ASV has “princes and servants are nought unto thee” and the NIV has “the commanders and their men mean nothing to you”. Joab ends with the sentence that the Hebrew puts in one breath, “if Absalom were alive, and all of us dead today, then it would be right in your eyes”. The ASV has “then it had pleased thee well”. The NWT 1984 has “it would be right in your eyes”. The Douay has “it would have pleased thee”. “Right in the eyes” is the verb of the word that was “right in the eyes of Absalom” when Ahithophel counseled him (2 Samuel 17:4, now on these pages), and Joab uses it of the king.

Speak to the heart. “Arise, go out, and speak to the heart of your servants”: “speak to the heart” in this form stands in five verses: Genesis 34:3, where Shechem “spoke to the girl’s heart”, 50:21, where Joseph “comforted them, and spoke to their heart”, Judges 19:3, where the Levite goes “to speak to her heart and bring her back” (all now on these pages), this one, and 2 Chronicles 32:6. The ASV has “speak comfortably unto thy servants”. The NIV has “encourage your men”. The NWT 1984 has “speak straight to the heart of your servants”. The Douay has “speak to the satisfaction of thy servants”. The TLB has “congratulate the troops”. The oath is “by Jehovah”, and the threat is that “not a man will stay the night with you”: the verb is lun, the one Hushai used of David in 17:8, “he will not lodge with the people”. The ASV has “there will not tarry a man with thee this night”. The NWT 1984 has “not a man will lodge with you tonight”.

The king sat in the gate. “And the king arose and sat in the gate. And they told all the people, saying: ‘Look, the king is sitting in the gate.’ And all the people came before the king. And Israel had fled, each to his tent”: the last sentence is the one of 18:17, said now of the other side. The ASV has “Now Israel had fled every man to his tent”. The NIV has “the Israelites had fled to their homes”. The NWT 1984 has “they had fled each one to his home”. The Douay has “Israel fled to their own dwellings”. Joab’s speech has done what a king’s own comfort could not, and the page gives the king’s answer as an act and not a word: he goes down and sits where the people can see him.

Verses 10–16 · 2 Samuel 19 Bringing the king back — “whom we anointed”, “my bone and my flesh”, Amasa in place of Joab, and Judah’s heart bent as one man

At strife throughout the tribes. “And all the people were contending throughout all the tribes of Israel”: nadon stands only here. The ASV has “were at strife”. The NIV has “were arguing among themselves”. The NWT 1984 has “came to be involved in dispute”. What they say is about David’s past: “The king saved us from the hand of our enemies, and he delivered us from the hand of the Philistines; and now he has fled from the land because of Absalom”. The Hebrew has kaf, the palm, where this translation has “hand”, and the NWT 1984 keeps it as “out of the palm of our enemies”. “Whom we anointed over us”: the form mashachnu, “we anointed”, stands only here. The ASV has “Absalom, whom we anointed over us”. The NIV has “Absalom, whom we anointed to rule over us”. The NWT 1984 has “whom we anointed over us”. For “why are you silent”, the ASV has “why speak ye not a word”. The NIV has “why do you say nothing”. The NWT 1984 has “why are YOU doing nothing”. Rahlfs’ Greek has kōpheuete, “are you dumb”.

To bring the king back. “To bring the king back”, lehashiv et-hammelekh, stands in three verses, verses 11, 12 and 13 of this chapter: said by Israel, then by David to the elders of Judah, and again in the same words. David sends to “Zadok and Abiathar the priests” and asks why Judah, his own tribe, will be “the last”. The ASV has “Why are ye the last to bring the king back to his house?”. The NIV has “Why should you be the last to bring the king back to his palace”. The NWT 1984 has “Why should YOU become the last ones to bring the king back to his house”. In the Hebrew the clause “for the word of all Israel has come to the king, to his house” ends verse 12, and Rahlfs’ Greek has it at the end of verse 11 as well, as the last words of Israel’s own speech. The ASV has “seeing the speech of all Israel is come to the king, to bring him to his house”. The NWT 1984 has “when the word of all Israel itself has come to the king at his house”.

My bone and my flesh. “You are my brothers, you are my bone and my flesh”: “my bone and my flesh”, atsmi uvesari, stands in three verses: Genesis 29:14, where Laban says it to Jacob (now on these pages), and verses 13 and 14 here. At Hebron the tribes had said it to David, “we are your bone and your flesh” (2 Samuel 5:1, now on these pages), and now David says it to Judah and to Amasa. The ASV has “Ye are my brethren, ye are my bone and my flesh”. The NIV has “You are my relatives, my own flesh and blood”. The NWT 1984 has “My brothers YOU are; my bone and my flesh YOU are”. “And to Amasa you shall say”: the commander whom Absalom had set over the army (2 Samuel 17:25, now on these pages) is offered Joab’s place by the king, and “in place of Joab”, tachat Yo‘av, stands in two verses, that one and this. The oath is the one David swore of Abner, “So may God do to me, and so may he add” (2 Samuel 3:9, now on these pages). The ASV has “God do so to me, and more also”. The NIV has “May God deal with me, be it ever so severely”. The NWT 1984 has “So may God do to me and so may he add to it”.

He bent the heart. “And he bent the heart of every man of Judah as one man; and they sent to the king: ‘Return, you and all your servants’”: the Hebrew names no subject, “he bent”. The ASV has “he bowed the heart”. The NWT 1984 has “he proceeded to bend the heart”. The NIV has “He won over the hearts of the men of Judah”. The TLB has “Amasa convinced all the leaders of Judah”, which supplies the man. “As one man”, ke’ish echad, stands in nine verses, among them Judges 20:1, 8 and 11 and 1 Samuel 11:7 (all now on these pages), where Israel comes out together to war; here Judah turns as one man to bring a king home. The heart is the one that Absalom stole (2 Samuel 15:6, now on these pages). “And Judah came to Gilgal”: the place where Israel first camped after crossing the Jordan (Joshua 4:19) and where Samuel said “let us go to Gilgal, and renew the kingship there” (1 Samuel 11:14, both now on these pages); the king is brought back over the Jordan at a place where a kingship has been renewed before.

Verses 17–24 · 2 Samuel 19 Shimei at the Jordan — Ziba, the ferry, “take it to heart”, and “Shall a man be put to death today in Israel?”

Shimei hurried. “And Shimei son of Gera, the Benjaminite, who was from Bahurim, hurried and came down with the man of Judah to meet King David”: the man who came out of Bahurim to curse (2 Samuel 16:5, now on these pages) comes down to the Jordan first, with a thousand men of Benjamin and “Ziba, the young man of the house of Saul, and his fifteen sons and his twenty servants”. The ASV has “Ziba the servant of the house of Saul”. The NIV has “Ziba, the steward of Saul’s household”. The NWT 1984 has “Ziba the attendant of the house of Saul”. The word is na‘ar, as in 16:1. “They rushed over the Jordan before the king”: tsalchu. The ASV has “they went through the Jordan in the presence of the king”. The NIV has “They rushed to the Jordan”. The NWT 1984 has “they made it successfully to the Jordan”.

The ferry. “And the ferry crossed over to bring over the king’s household, and to do what was good in his eyes”: the noun ha‘avarah stands only here. The ASV has “there went over a ferry-boat”. The NIV has “They crossed at the ford”. The NWT 1984 has “he crossed the ford”. “What was good in his eyes” is a phrase that comes back four times in this chapter, in verses 19, 28, 38 and 39, always about what someone will do for the king or the king for someone. Shimei “fell down before the king as he was crossing the Jordan”.

Take it to heart. “Let not my lord count iniquity against me, and do not remember what your servant did perversely on the day my lord the king went out from Jerusalem, that the king should take it to heart”: the Hebrew avon (see avon) is what he asks the king not to count, and the last clause is the one the versions disagree about. The ASV has “that the king should take it to his heart”. The NWT 1984 has “so that the king should lay it to his heart”. The NIV has “May the king put it out of his mind”, which reverses the clause into the wish that it implies. “The first of all the house of Joseph”: Shimei, a Benjaminite, counts himself with the northern tribes, and “first” is also what Israel will claim for its own word in verse 44. The ASV has “the first of all the house of Joseph”. The NIV has “the first from the tribes of Joseph”.

Today. “Shall Shimei not be put to death for this, because he cursed Jehovah’s anointed?”: “Jehovah’s anointed”, mashiach YHWH, stands in ten verses, nine of them now on these pages (1 Samuel 24:6, 10, 26:9, 11, 16, 23, 2 Samuel 1:14, 16, and this) and the tenth in Lamentations 4:20; in the nine before it the anointed is Saul, and here it is David. The ASV has “Jehovah’s anointed”. The NIV has “Shouldn’t Shimei be put to death for this?”. The NWT 1984 has “the anointed of Jehovah”. David answers “What to me and to you, sons of Zeruiah”: these words stand in two verses, 2 Samuel 16:10 and this one. “That you should be to me today an adversary”: “as an adversary”, lesatan, stands in four verses, Numbers 22:22 and 22:32 (of the angel of Jehovah in the road), 1 Samuel 29:4, where the Philistines fear David will become one, and this one (see satan). The ASV has “adversaries”. The NWT 1984 has “a resister of me”. The NIV has “What right do you have to interfere?”. Rahlfs’ Greek has eis epiboulon, “a plotter”. “Shall a man be put to death today in Israel? For do I not know that today I am king over Israel?” is Saul’s sentence after the victory over Ammon, “Not a man shall be put to death this day, for today Jehovah has worked salvation in Israel” (1 Samuel 11:13, now on these pages). “You shall not die”, said to a man who fears death, is Jehovah’s to Gideon (Judges 6:23), Jonathan’s to David (1 Samuel 20:2) and Nathan’s to David himself (2 Samuel 12:13, all now on these pages). The ASV has “Thou shalt not die” and the NIV has “You shall not die”. “And the king swore to him”: what Solomon is told about this oath is in 1 Kings 2:8–9.

Verses 25–31 · 2 Samuel 19 Mephibosheth — feet untended, “when he came to Jerusalem”, “my servant deceived me”, and the field divided

He had not tended his feet. “And Mephibosheth son of Saul came down to meet the king; and he had not tended his feet, and had not trimmed his mustache, and had not washed his clothes, from the day the king went until the day he came in peace”: the Hebrew says ben-Sha’ul, “son of Saul”, for the grandson, and Rahlfs’ Greek has “son of Jonathan, son of Saul”. The ASV has “neither dressed his feet, nor trimmed his beard”. The NIV has “had not taken care of his feet or trimmed his mustache”. The NWT 1984 has “had not attended to his feet nor had he attended to his mustache”. The Hebrew sapham is the mustache, and the Greek adds “nor pared his nails”, oude ōnychisato. These are the signs of a man in mourning, and the page gives no reason for them beyond the king’s absence.

When he came to Jerusalem. “And it was, when he came to Jerusalem to meet the king, that the king said to him: ‘Why did you not go with me, Mephibosheth?’”: the king is at the Jordan and Gilgal (verses 16 and 41), so the page does not say where the two meet. The ASV has “when he was come to Jerusalem to meet the king”. The NWT 1984 has “when he came to Jerusalem to meet the king”. The NIV has “When he came from Jerusalem to meet the king”. The Douay has “when he met the king at Jerusalem”. The question is the one that Ziba’s report had raised, that Mephibosheth “is staying in Jerusalem” hoping that Israel will give him his father’s kingdom (2 Samuel 16:3, now on these pages), and the king had answered it with “yours is all that is Mephibosheth’s” (16:4).

My servant deceived me. “My servant deceived me; for your servant said: ‘I will saddle the donkey for myself, and ride on it and go with the king,’ for your servant is lame”: the Hebrew has avdi rimmani, “my servant deceived me”, and does not name Ziba. The verb is the one of Laban’s “Why then have you deceived me?” (Genesis 29:25) and Saul’s to Michal (1 Samuel 19:17, both now on these pages). The ASV has “I will saddle me an ass”. The NWT 1984 has “Let me saddle the female ass for me”. The NIV has “I will have my donkey saddled”, and names “Ziba my servant”. The TLB has “Saddle my donkey so that I can go with the king”, an order to the servant, and Rahlfs’ Greek has the same, episaxon moi tēn onon, “saddle the donkey for me”; the Hebrew echbeshah is a first-person “I will saddle”, which a lame man could not do, and this translation prints it. The verb chavash, “to saddle”, is the one of Ahithophel’s last ride, “he saddled the donkey” (2 Samuel 17:23, now on these pages): one man saddles a donkey to go home and die, and another says he would have saddled one to follow the king.

Like an angel of God. “And he slandered your servant to my lord the king; but my lord the king is like an angel of God; so do what is good in your eyes”: “like an angel of God”, kemal’akh ha’Elohim, stands in two verses, here and 2 Samuel 14:17, where the woman of Tekoa says it of the king (now on these pages); Achish said “as an angel of God” of David without the article (1 Samuel 29:9). The ASV has “he hath slandered thy servant”. The NIV has “he has slandered your servant”. The NWT 1984 has “he slandered your servant”. “For all my father’s house were nothing but men of death to my lord the king; and you set your servant among those who eat at your table”: the table is the one of 9:7, “you yourself shall eat bread at my table continually” (2 Samuel 9:7, now on these pages). The ASV has “dead men”. The NIV has “deserved nothing but death”. The NWT 1984 has “nothing but doomed to death”. “Why do you speak any more of your matters? I have said: you and Ziba divide the field”: the ASV has “divide the land” and the NIV has “I order you and Ziba to divide the land”. The king had given Ziba all of it, and the half he now gives back is not argued. Mephibosheth says “Let him take it all, since my lord the king has come in peace to his house”, and the word shalom that stood on the lips of Ahimaaz (2 Samuel 18:28) is in his: the king has come back to his house, and he asks for nothing else.

Verses 32–40 · 2 Samuel 19 Barzillai — “a very great man”, “How many are the days of the years of my life”, and the kiss at the Jordan

A very great man. “And Barzillai the Gileadite came down from Rogelim; and he crossed the Jordan with the king, to send him on at the Jordan”: the written form is bayarden, “in the Jordan”, and the reading is et-hayyarden, and this translation follows the pointed reading. “And Barzillai was very old, a son of eighty years; and he had sustained the king while he stayed at Mahanaim, for he was a very great man”: the verb is kilkal (see kilkal), and the gifts are those of 2 Samuel 17:27–29 (now on these pages). The ASV has “provided the king with sustenance” and “a very great man”. The NIV has “provided for the king” and “a very wealthy man”. The NWT 1984 has “supplied the king with food” and “a very great man”. “A very great man” are the words that said of Nabal “the man was very great” (1 Samuel 25:2, now on these pages), with a Hebrew gadol and me’od in each.

I will sustain you. “You cross over with me, and I will sustain you with me in Jerusalem”: the king uses the same verb, vekhilkalti, “and I will sustain you”, so that the man who sustained the king at Mahanaim is offered the same in Jerusalem. The ASV has “I will sustain thee with me in Jerusalem”. The NIV has “I will provide for you”. The NWT 1984 has “I shall certainly supply you with food with me in Jerusalem”.

How many are the days. “How many are the days of the years of my life, that I should go up with the king to Jerusalem?”: “How many are the days of the years of my life”, kammah yemei shenei chayyai, stands in two verses, here and Genesis 47:8, where Pharaoh asks Jacob the same with “your life” (Genesis 47:8, now on these pages). The ASV has “How many are the days of the years of my life”. The NIV has “How many more years will I live”. The NWT 1984 has “What are the days of the years of my life like”. “Can I discern between good and bad?”: “between good and bad” stands in three verses, Leviticus 27:33 (now on these pages), this one, and 1 Kings 3:9, where Solomon asks for a heart “to discern between good and bad”. The ASV has “can I discern between good and bad?”. The NWT 1984 has “Could I discern between good and bad”. The NIV has “Can I tell the difference between what is enjoyable and what is not?”. “The voice of singing men and singing women” is in the ASV as “the voice of singing men and singing women” and in the NIV as “the voices of male and female singers”, and the man who asks “why should your servant be still a burden” is the one who has carried the king.

By the grave of my father and my mother. “Let your servant return, please, and let me die in my city, by the grave of my father and my mother”: the Hebrew is im qever avi ve’immi. Ahithophel went home to the tomb of his father (2 Samuel 17:23, now on these pages), and Barzillai asks for what he went home to find, in the other order of time. “And look, your servant Chimham”: the name is written Kimham in verses 38 and 39 and Kimhan in verse 41. The ASV has “Chimham”. The NIV has “Kimham”. “And all the people crossed the Jordan, and the king crossed; and the king kissed Barzillai and blessed him, and he returned to his place”: “and the king kissed” stands in two verses, here and 14:33, where it was Absalom (2 Samuel 14:33, now on these pages). The ASV has “the king kissed Barzillai, and blessed him”. The NIV has “The king kissed Barzillai and bid him farewell”. The NWT 1984 has “the king kissed Barzillai and blessed him”.

Verses 41–44 · 2 Samuel 19 Israel against Judah — “stolen you”, “near to me”, “ten hands”, and a word that was harder

Half the people of Israel. “And the king crossed over to Gilgal, and Chimham crossed with him; and all the people of Judah brought the king over, and also half of the people of Israel”: the written form is ya‘avru, “crossed”, and the reading is he‘eviru, “brought over”, and this translation follows the pointed reading. The ASV has “all the people of Judah brought the king over, and also half the people of Israel”. The NIV has “All the troops of Judah and half the troops of Israel had taken the king over”. The NWT 1984 has “also half the people of Israel, that they might bring the king across”. The verb avar runs through the chapter, with the ferry in verse 19 and the crossings of verses 32 to 41, as it ran through the last two (17:24; 18:9, 23).

Two men. “And look, every man of Israel came to the king; and they said to the king: ‘Why have our brothers, the man of Judah, stolen you’”: the Hebrew speaks of two single men, ish Yisra’el and ish Yehudah, brothers; the ASV, the NIV and the NWT 1984 all print “the men of Israel” and “the men of Judah”, and Rahlfs’ Greek has the singular, anēr Iouda and andra Israēl. This translation keeps “the man of Israel” as it did in 15:13 and 16:15. The verb is ganav, the one of the army’s stealing into the city in verse 4 and Absalom’s stealing the heart (15:6). The ASV has “Why have our brethren the men of Judah stolen thee away”. The NIV has “Why did our brothers, the men of Judah, steal the king away”. The NWT 1984 has “Why did our brothers the men of Judah steal you”.

Near to me. “Because the king is near to me. And why is your anger hot over this matter? Have we eaten at all from the king? Or has a gift been carried to us?”: the Hebrew has elai, “to me”, where all three English versions print “us”, and Rahlfs’ Greek has pros me. The ASV has “Because the king is near of kin to us”. The NIV has “the king is closely related to us”. The NWT 1984 has “Because the king is closely related to us”. For the gift, the ASV has “or hath he given us any gift?” and the NWT 1984 has “has a gift been carried to us?”. Judah answers with the kinship that David himself had named in verse 13, and the answer is about food and gifts, which the question had not asked.

Ten hands. “I have ten hands in the king, and in David also I am more than you; and why did you make light of me? And was not my word first, to bring back my king?”: the Hebrew has eser yadot, “ten hands”, and Rahlfs’ Greek has the same, deka cheires, and adds “and I am the firstborn rather than you”, prōtotokos. The ASV has “We have ten parts in the king”. The NIV has “We have ten shares in the king”. The NWT 1984 has “We have ten parts in the king”. The word yad is the one that ran through the death of Absalom (see yad). “Make light of me” is hekelotani, from the root of Shimei’s “cursing” (see qalal). The ASV has “why then did ye despise us”. The NIV has “Why then do you treat us with contempt?”. The NWT 1984 has “have you treated us with contempt”. “And the word of the man of Judah was harder than the word of the man of Israel”: vayyiqshe, “was hard”, the root of the sons of Zeruiah who were “too hard for me” (2 Samuel 3:39, now on these pages). The chapter that began with a king who could not speak to his people ends with brothers who cannot speak to each other, and the next one begins with a horn.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “Would that I had died, I, in your place”; “my son” said five times and three times; “the room over the gate”; “the salvation of that day”; “stole in”; “put to shame”; “speak to the heart”; “right in your eyes”; “at strife”; “whom we anointed”; “to bring the king back”; “my bone and my flesh”; “in place of Joab”; “as one man”; “the ferry”; “take it to heart”; “Jehovah’s anointed”; “an adversary”; “You shall not die”; “my servant deceived me”; “like an angel of God”; “men of death”; “a very great man”; “sustain”; “How many are the days of the years of my life”; “between good and bad”; “the man of Judah” and “the man of Israel”; “ten hands”; “make light of me”; and “harder”; with the pointed readings “to bring over” and “the Jordan” and the written forms in the notes.

Echoes paid. Judah’s offer in place of the boy (Genesis 44:33); Samson’s and Saul’s salvation (Judges 15:18; 1 Samuel 11:13); Saul’s sparing of his enemies and Gilgal where the kingship was renewed (1 Samuel 11:13–14); David’s covered head on the Mount of Olives (2 Samuel 15:30); the stolen heart (2 Samuel 15:6); the humiliated envoys (2 Samuel 10:5); the ten concubines (2 Samuel 16:22); Hushai’s “lodge” (2 Samuel 17:8); Absalom’s “right in the eyes” (2 Samuel 17:4); the gate (2 Samuel 18:4, 24); Israel’s flight to the tents (2 Samuel 18:17); Shechem, Joseph and the Levite speaking to the heart (Genesis 34:3; 50:21; Judges 19:3); Laban’s bone and flesh and the tribes’ at Hebron (Genesis 29:14; 2 Samuel 5:1); Amasa in place of Joab and the oath on Abner (2 Samuel 17:25; 3:9); “as one man” (Judges 20:1, 8, 11; 1 Samuel 11:7); Joshua’s Gilgal (Joshua 4:19); Jehovah’s anointed (1 Samuel 24:6, 10; 26:9, 11, 16, 23; 2 Samuel 1:14, 16); “What to me and to you” and the adversary (2 Samuel 16:10; Numbers 22:22, 32; 1 Samuel 29:4); “You shall not die” (Judges 6:23; 1 Samuel 20:2; 2 Samuel 12:13); Laban’s and Saul’s deception (Genesis 29:25; 1 Samuel 19:17); Ahithophel’s donkey and tomb (2 Samuel 17:23); the angel of God (2 Samuel 14:17; 1 Samuel 29:9); the king’s table (2 Samuel 9:7); Nabal’s greatness (1 Samuel 25:2); Pharaoh’s question to Jacob (Genesis 47:8); the goods of Mahanaim (2 Samuel 17:27–29); the kiss for Absalom (2 Samuel 14:33); and the sons of Zeruiah too hard (2 Samuel 3:39).

Echoes planted. Sheba son of Bichri’s horn and “We have no portion in David” (2 Samuel 20:1); the ten concubines put in ward (2 Samuel 20:3); Amasa told to call Judah in three days, and Joab’s sword (2 Samuel 20:4–10); Shimei’s oath and Solomon’s charge about him (1 Kings 2:8–9, 36–46); Barzillai’s sons at the king’s table (1 Kings 2:7); Chimham’s lodging by Bethlehem (Jeremiah 41:17); Solomon’s asking to “discern between good and bad” (1 Kings 3:9); and “speak to the heart” in 2 Chronicles 32:6 and the Prophets. None of these is translated here yet.

Next in this book: 2 Samuel 20 — Sheba son of Bichri, a Benjaminite, blows the horn and says “We have no portion in David”, and every man of Israel goes up after him while Judah holds to the king from the Jordan to Jerusalem; David comes to his house and puts the ten concubines in ward; Amasa is told to call Judah together in three days and is late, and David sends Abishai after Sheba; at the great stone in Gibeon Joab takes Amasa by the beard to kiss him and strikes him in the belly; Joab besieges Abel of Beth-maacah, a wise woman speaks from the wall, and Sheba’s head is thrown over to Joab; and the list of David’s officers.
