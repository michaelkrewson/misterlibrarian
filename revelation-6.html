Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–8 · four riders, and a question the text never answers · ἵππος λευκός… ἵππος πυρρός… ἵππος μέλας… ἵππος χλωρός hippos leukos… hippos pyrros… hippos melas… hippos chlōros

⚠ Who rides the white horse? The text never says, and the library reports two live readings rather than choosing one. The white horse, crown, and bow could echo Parthian mounted archers — Rome's one persistent military anxiety — making this rider a figure of conquest grouped with the next three woes: war, famine, death. Or the white horse and crown could deliberately anticipate the rider on the white horse at 19:11, making this first seal Christ or the gospel going out to conquer. Both readings are old, both are argued by serious commentators on both sides, and this chapter alone does not settle which.

⚠ The third seal's price list is a famine, priced exactly. A choinix of wheat was roughly one adult's daily bread ration; a denarius was a laborer's daily wage (compare Matthew 20:2). The voice prices one day's food at one full day's pay — enough for one person, nothing left for a household — while ordering the oil and wine spared. KJV renders the measure a ‘measure’ and the wage a ‘penny’; ESV keeps the Greek terms directly, ‘a quart’ for a ‘denarius’; NIV converts both to modern units, ‘two pounds of wheat… for a day's wages.’ The detail sparing oil and wine has a real, documented echo: Suetonius records that Domitian, ruling when this book was likely written, ordered vineyards across the empire halved to make room for grain — then abandoned the edict without enforcing it. Whether John's audience heard that controversy in this verse is not stated; the resonance is only noted here, not claimed as the verse's source.

⚠ ‘Pale’ or ‘yellow’? The fourth horse is chlōros, the same word used of green grass elsewhere in the New Testament (Mark 6:39). Every English version compared here — KJV, ASV, NIV, ESV — reads ‘pale,’ with no disagreement; both Spanish versions compared, RV60 and NVI, read ‘amarillo’ or ‘amarillento,’ yellow or yellowish — the two language-shelves splitting from each other rather than within themselves. Both are legitimate: chlōros spans the whole sickly range from green to yellow to the color of a corpse.

Verses 9–11 · a question the martyrs ask, and an answer that asks them to wait · Ἕως πότε, ὁ δεσπότης ὁ ἅγιος καὶ ἀληθινός heōs pote, ho despotēs ho hagios kai alēthinos

The souls are seen under the altar — the place, in the temple's own practice, where a sacrificial animal's blood was poured out at the base (Leviticus 4:7). Their death is pictured as the very offering the altar was built for.

⚠ ‘How long’ is not a new cry; it is the Psalter's own recurring question, asked of God by name in the same words (Psalm 6:3, 13:1, 79:5) long before this scene. The martyrs are not improvising a complaint — they are praying the oldest complaint in Israel's own hymnbook.

⚠ The answer is real, and it is also a delay. White robes, the mark of vindication elsewhere in this book (3:5, 7:9), are given at once — but the text also tells them to rest a little longer, until a fixed number of ‘fellow servants and brothers’ still to be killed is complete. The chapter does not soften this: the wait has a stated cause, and the cause is more killing still to come. The library reports the verse as written rather than explaining away its hardest edge.

Verses 12–14 · two prophets' skies, borrowed and combined · ὁ ἥλιος ἐγένετο μέλας… ὁ οὐρανὸς ἀπεχωρίσθη ὡς βιβλίον ἑλισσόμενον ho hēlios egeneto melas… ho ouranos apechōristhē hōs biblion helissomenon

⚠ Every image in these three verses has an Old Testament address. The sun turning black and the moon turning to blood is Joel's own language for ‘the day of Jehovah’ (Joel 2:31) — the same verse Peter quotes at Pentecost to explain what the crowd was witnessing (Acts 2:20). The stars falling like a fig tree shedding unripe fruit, and the sky rolling up like a scroll, both come from a single verse of Isaiah's own oracle against the nations (Isaiah 34:4) — two separate images there, fused into one continuous description here. John is not inventing apocalyptic weather; he is quoting the prophets' own vocabulary for divine judgment and letting their images do the work.

Verses 15–17 · a plea Jesus already quoted, on his own way to die · Πέσετε ἐφʼ ἡμᾶς καὶ κρύψατε ἡμᾶς pesete eph' hēmas kai krypsate hēmas

The seven classes named in v15 — kings, nobles, commanders, the rich, the mighty, slave and free — span every rank a first-century reader could name, the same totalizing instinct behind chapter 5's ‘tribe and tongue and people and nation’: no one's standing exempts them.

⚠ ‘Fall on us’ is not a new cry either. It is Hosea's own words for judgment day (Hosea 10:8) — and Jesus quotes that exact line to the women of Jerusalem weeping for him on the road to his own execution: ‘they will begin to say to the mountains, “Fall on us,” and to the hills, “Cover us”’ (Luke 23:30). What Jesus said would be said, this chapter now shows being said — the same words, the same appeal to the landscape itself for the mercy of being crushed rather than seen.

The chapter's last line is a question, not an answer: who is able to stand? Nothing in chapter 6 responds to it. Chapter 7 opens by answering it, but not yet.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation reports rather than resolves: the identity of the white horse's rider (v2), left as a genuine, live interpretive split rather than assigned to either reading; and the martyrs' told-to-wait (v11), kept exactly as hard as the Greek makes it rather than softened.

Echoes paid. The Lamb (v1) opening the scroll is the same Lamb chapter 5 found worthy to open it — the promise of that chapter's closing note, cashed seal by seal starting here. The white robes (v11) anticipate the great multitude's robes at 7:9, made white in the same book's own later image (7:14).

Echoes planted. The seven-class list of v15 (kings to slaves), a totalizing formula matching chapter 5's ‘tribe and tongue and people and nation.’ The martyrs' ‘how long’ (v10), left open until it is finally answered, chapters from now. And the fifth and sixth seals' pairing of a question asked and a world in terror — both still waiting, at this chapter's end, for the seventh seal chapter 8 will finally break.

⚠ Left open on purpose. Whether the first rider is conquest itself or Christ going out to conquer. Whether the fixed number of martyrs still to come is meant to console or unsettle. The chapter asks ‘who is able to stand’ and does not answer it — the library leaves the question standing exactly where the text leaves it.

Next installment in this book, whenever it is translated: Revelation 7, where four angels hold back the four winds of the earth, and a number is finally given — a hundred and forty-four thousand, sealed.
