Five days later (the verse does not say from when) the high priest Ananias comes down with some elders and an orator, Tertullus, and they lay information against Paul before the governor Felix. Tertullus begins with praise for the peace and reforms under Felix, then calls Paul a plague, a stirrer-up of dissensions among all the Jews throughout the inhabited world, and a ringleader of the party of the Nazarenes who tried to profane the temple; the Jews join in. The Greek printed here has no verse 7 and only the first half of 6 and the second half of 8; the longer text found in some Bibles is left out and the gap is shown. With the governor’s nod, Paul answers: it is not more than twelve days since he went up to Jerusalem to worship; they did not find him reasoning with anyone or stirring up a crowd; he confesses that according to the Way, which they call a party, he serves the God of his fathers, believing the law and the Prophets, with a hope that there will be a resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous; he came after several years to bring alms and offerings and was found purified in the temple; the Jews from Asia who accused him are not here, and his one cry before the Sanhedrin was about resurrection of the dead. Felix, knowing more accurately the things concerning the Way, puts them off until Lysias the tribune comes down, and orders Paul kept with some freedom. After some days Felix comes with his wife Drusilla and hears him about faith in Christ Jesus; as Paul reasons about righteousness, self-control and the judgment to come, Felix becomes terrified and sends him away for now, hoping at the same time for money and sending for him often. When two years are completed Felix is succeeded by Porcius Festus, and, wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews, leaves Paul bound.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are ‘after five days’ (v1), ‘not more than twelve days’ since Paul went up to Jerusalem (v11), ‘after several years’ since his earlier visit with alms and offerings (v17), ‘after some days’ (v24) and ‘when two years had been completed’ (v27). The text gives no date for Felix’s recall and this library dates the events no more closely than the book does. It follows Acts 23, where Paul is sent by night to Felix at Caesarea; Acts 25 follows.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–4 · κατέβη ὁ ἀρχιερεὺς Ἁνανίας … ῥήτορος Τερτύλλου … Πολλῆς εἰρήνης … κράτιστε Φῆλιξ … τῇ σῇ ἐπιεικείᾳ katebē ho archiereus Hananias … rhētoros Tertyllou … Pollēs eirēnēs … kratiste Phēlix … tē sē epieikeia — Acts 24:1–4: who is Tertullus, the “orator” (rhētōr) who accuses Paul before Felix, and was there really “much peace” under Felix?

“Came down” with “some elders” (Acts 24:1). Katebē, “came down,” is the verb Luke uses for the trip from Judea to the coast: Herod, “going down from Judea to Caesarea, spent time there” (12:19), and the prophet Agabus “came down from Judea” to the house where Paul was staying (21:10). The high priest is the Ananias who at 23:2 ordered those standing beside him to strike Paul on the mouth (see Ananias the high priest), and the Greek says he came in person. Meta de pente hēmeras, “after five days,” names no starting point. The nearest event is the delivery of the letter and of Paul to the governor at Caesarea (23:33), and the Greek does not say that the five days run from there. The elders are the second group in the plot that Luke has just told: the forty men went to “the chief priests and the elders” (23:14). Whether these are the same elders, or members of the Sanhedrin at all, the verse does not say. It says only presbyterōn tinōn, “some elders.” The apparatus has a note here: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print presbyterōn tinōn, and Robinson–Pierpont reads tōn presbyterōn, “the elders,” with the article and without “some.” The shelf divides the same way. KJV “the elders” and Geneva “the Elders” print the article; ASV “certain elders,” NWT 1984 “some older men” and Douay “some of the ancients” print an indefinite group, and so do NIV “some of the elders” and TLB “some of the Jewish leaders,” so five of the seven keep “some” or “certain.” TLB also replaces the title with “leaders,” which the Greek word does not say. This translation follows the printed text with “some elders.”

“Orator” (Acts 24:1). Rhētōr stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The name Tertullus stands in two verses, 24:1 and 24:2, and both are in this chapter. The word names a trained public speaker, and the Greek does not call him a lawyer, an advocate or a hired man. Luke does not say why the delegation brought one, who paid him, or whether he was a Jew; in his speech he says “we” and “us” (“we are enjoying,” “we welcome,” “we accuse,” 24:2, 3, 8) and speaks as the spokesman of the accusers. The shelf divides over his job. KJV “a certain orator,” Geneva “a certain orator,” ASV “an orator” and Douay “an orator” keep the word, four of the seven; NWT 1984 “a public speaker” keeps the sense; NIV “a lawyer” and TLB “the lawyer” name a profession, which turns the speaker into a counsel at law. The word the verse does print is katēgorein, “to accuse” (v2), and he is already named there as one who accuses, not as one who pleads for a client. The tribune at 22:30 had wanted to learn “the certainty” of what Paul “was accused” of by the Jews, with the same verb in the passive (katēgoreitai). Five days after the letter, the accusation is at last spoken by a man whose profession is words.

“Gave … information” (Acts 24:1). Enephanisan is the aorist of emphanizō, which stands in ten verses of the New Testament: Matthew 27:53, John 14:21, 22, five verses of Acts (23:15, 23:22, here, 25:2 and 25:15), Hebrews 11:14 and Hebrews 9:24 (not yet on these pages). All five Acts verses lie within chapters 23 to 25, and the verb is the plotters’ at 23:15, where the Sanhedrin is to “make it clear” to the tribune, and the tribune’s at 23:22, where the young man is told to keep silent about having “made these things known” to him. This translation prints three English phrases for one verb because the sentences differ. Here the verb takes the governor as its hearer and has the phrase kata tou Paulou, “against Paul,” and it has the same phrase at 25:2, where the chief priests and the leading men “gave … information” to Festus against Paul. The shelf prints the force of the phrase in different ways: NWT 1984 “gave information to the governor against Paul,” KJV “informed the governor” and ASV “informed the governor” keep the idea of information, three of the seven; NIV “brought their charges against Paul before the governor” and TLB “make their accusations against Paul” make it a charge; Douay “went to the governor against Paul” and Geneva “appeared before the governor against Paul” print a movement toward the governor and no verb of telling.

“When he was called” (Acts 24:2). Klēthentos de autou is a genitive absolute, and its “him” is not named. The usual pattern puts a different person in such a phrase than the subject of the main verb, which here is Tertullus, and so the “him” points to Paul; the verse that follows then reads “began to accuse him.” The shelf names the person differently. KJV “when he was called forth,” Geneva “when he was called forth,” ASV “when he was called” and NWT 1984 “When he was called” leave the pronoun, four of the seven; NIV “When Paul was called in” and Douay “Paul being called for” name Paul; TLB “When Tertullus was called forward” names the speaker. This translation keeps “he” so that the Greek’s own reading stays open, and this note reports the question rather than deciding it.

“Much peace” (Acts 24:2). Pollēs eirēnēs tynchanontes di’ sou is “obtaining much peace through you,” and the word eirēnē is the Greek “peace” of eirēnē. The shelf prints “peace” in five of its seven: ASV “much peace,” Douay “much peace,” NWT 1984 “great peace,” NIV “a long period of peace” and TLB “quietness and peace”; KJV “great quietness” and Geneva “great quietness” print the other word. What Tertullus claims, in front of the man he is praising, is that the Jews have had peace through Felix. Josephus, read here in Niese’s Greek, tells Felix’s time as both calm and disorder. The emperor (Nero, in his telling) put Felix over the rest of Judea as epitropos (War 2.252); Felix seized the arch-robber Eleazar, who had plundered the country for twenty years, and sent him to Rome, and the robbers he crucified and the townsmen he punished as their accomplices were past counting (2.253). Then, kathartheisēs de tēs chōras, “the land having been cleansed,” another kind of robber, the sikarioi, grew up in Jerusalem, killing in daylight in the middle of the city, and the high priest Jonathan was the first they cut down (2.254–256). Felix sent horse and foot against crowds led into the wilderness by deceivers (2.258–260), and he met the Egyptian false prophet, with about thirty thousand followers in Josephus’s figure, on the Mount of Olives (2.261–263). That Egyptian is the one the tribune asked Paul about at 21:38, whose followers Luke counts as four thousand; the two numbers differ, and which is right is not decided here. Tertullus’s “peace” takes the first half of Josephus’s account and leaves the second; Luke gives it as the speaker’s words and does not comment.

“Reforms” and “foresight” (Acts 24:2). Diorthōma stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and a lexicon would give it as something set straight, a correction or improvement; that is reported here, not checked on this page. Its root stands in three verses (diorth-): here; Titus 1:5, where Titus is to “set right” what was left undone (the verb with a prefix, epidiorthōsēs); and Hebrews 9:10 (not yet on these pages), where the noun diorthōsis names the time of “setting right.” The apparatus has a note: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print diorthōmatōn and Robinson–Pierpont prints katorthōmatōn. That second noun stands in no printed verse of the Greek New Testament, so here it is a variant only; lexicons give it as a success or a right action, reported here, not examined. The shelf spans both senses. NWT 1984 “reforms are taking place” and NIV “reforms in this nation” print “reforms,” two of the seven; ASV “evils are corrected” and Douay “many things are rectified” read a setting right; KJV “very worthy deeds are done” and Geneva “many worthy things are done” read achievements, and TLB “greatly reduced the discrimination against us” names a specific grievance that the Greek, which says only “this nation,” does not name. The nation is singular, tō ethnei toutō, where the plural ethnē prints as “nations” on these pages; the singular is Israel as one people. Then pronoia, “foresight,” stands in two verses: here and Romans 13:14 (not yet on these pages), where it is “make no provision.” KJV, Geneva and Douay “thy providence” and ASV “the providence” print “providence,” four of the seven, an English word that now sounds like a divine one; NWT 1984 “forethought” and NIV “foresight” print the plain word. This translation prints “foresight” so that an English reader hears a governor’s planning and nothing more.

“Most excellent Felix” and “welcome” (Acts 24:3). Kratiste, “most excellent,” stands in four verses: Luke 1:3, the letter at 23:26 (the note at 23:23–30), here, and Paul’s “most excellent Festus” at 26:25. At 23:26 it sat inside a letter as the governor’s written title; here it is spoken to his face, and Paul, in his own answer at 24:10, uses no title at all. The shelf prints three titles. ASV “most excellent Felix,” Douay “most excellent Felix” and NIV “most excellent Felix” keep the title as at 23:26, three of the seven; KJV “most noble Felix” and Geneva “most noble Felix” print “noble”; NWT 1984 “Your Excellency Felix” and TLB “Your Excellency” print an address in the style of a modern court (TLB drops the name here). The verb is apodechometha, “we welcome,” and the verb stands in seven verses, all in Luke–Acts: Luke 8:40, Luke 9:11, Acts 2:41, 18:27, 21:17, here and 28:30. The nearest is 21:17, where the brothers in Jerusalem “received us gladly,” the welcome Paul’s own party met. Tertullus uses it for what the nation does with Felix’s rule. The shelf has KJV “accept,” ASV “accept” and Douay “accept” (three of seven), NIV “acknowledge” and Geneva “acknowledge” (two), NWT 1984 “receive,” and TLB has no verb, only “very, very grateful.” The pair pantē te kai pantachou is “in every way and everywhere,” and pantachou stands in seven verses (Mark 1:28, Mark 16:20, Luke 9:6, Acts 17:30, here, 28:22 and 1 Corinthians 4:17, the last not yet on these pages). The Asian Jews at 21:28 said Paul was teaching everyone pantachē, “everywhere,” against the people, the law and the place, a different spelling of the same idea; Tertullus’s “everywhere” is the praise, theirs the charge. The Greek of the “in every way” half, pantē, reads as manner here, and KJV “always,” Douay “always” and NWT 1984 “at all times” read it as time, three of the seven; ASV “in all ways” and NIV “in every way” read it as manner. The apparatus has no note at verse 3.

“Detain,” “briefly” and “forbearance” (Acts 24:4). Enkoptō, “detain,” literally “cut in on,” stands in two verses in this spelling: here and 1 Peter 3:7 (not yet on these pages), where prayers are not to be “hindered.” The augmented forms in Romans 15:22, Galatians 5:7 and 1 Thessalonians 2:18 (none yet on these pages) are spelled enekopt- and enekops- and are reported here as the same verb, not examined. KJV “tedious,” Geneva “tedious,” ASV “tedious” and Douay “tedious” print the speaker’s fear of boring the governor, four of the seven; NWT 1984 “hinder” keeps the picture of an interruption, and NIV “weary” and TLB “bore” print the fear of tiring him. Syntomōs, “briefly,” stands in two verses, here and Mark 16:8, where it is inside the bracketed text of the shorter ending. The Greek here is the single adverb; KJV “a few words,” Geneva “a few words,” ASV “a few words” and Douay “in few words” print a phrase, four of the seven, and NWT 1984 “briefly,” NIV “briefly” and TLB “briefly” print the adverb. The last noun is epieikeia, “forbearance” (epieikēs), which stands in two verses in the noun form (epieikeia): here and 2 Corinthians 10:1 (not yet on these pages), where it is the “gentleness” of Christ. The adjective epieikes is at Philippians 4:5, printed “reasonableness” on these pages, and in 1 Timothy 3:3, Titus 3:2, James 3:17 and 1 Peter 2:18 (the last four not yet on these pages). KJV “clemency,” ASV “clemency” and Douay “clemency” print the word of a judge who forgoes the full penalty, three of the seven; Geneva “courtesy,” NWT 1984 “kindliness,” NIV “be kind enough” and TLB “kindly” print kindness. This translation keeps “forbearance” as the quality of not pressing a point, and the speaker uses it to ask for a hearing, with the most favorable opening he can think of, before any charge is named. Paul’s own opening at 24:10 makes no such request. The apparatus has no note at verse 4, so the Greek of verses 3 and 4 stands as printed by Westcott–Hort, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont alike.

Verses 5–9 · λοιμὸν … κινοῦντα στάσεις … πρωτοστάτην … τῆς τῶν Ναζωραίων αἱρέσεως … βεβηλῶσαι … παρʼ οὗ … συνεπέθεντο loimon … kinounta staseis … prōtostatēn … tēs tōn Nazōraiōn haireseōs … bebēlōsai … par’ hou … synepethento — Ringleader of the sect of the Nazarenes (Acts 24:5–9): what did Tertullus charge Paul with, why is there no Acts 24:7, and who is the “whom” Felix is told to examine?

A “plague” (Acts 24:5). The charge opens heurontes gar, “for having found,” and in the printed Greek it never reaches a main verb: from here to the end of verse 8 every finite verb sits inside a relative clause (“who even tried,” “whom we seized,” “from whom you will be able,” “of which we accuse”), so the English “we have found this man a plague” tidies a sentence that runs on. Loimos stands in two verses of the New Testament, and the other is Luke 21:11, where the plural loimoi is the disease itself, “famines and pestilences in place after place.” Only here is it a man: the noun for an epidemic turned into a label for one person. That the word is used of a person as a term of abuse is what the lexicons report; it is reported here, not checked on this page. This translation prints “plague” so that an English reader hears the noun, not the archaic adjective. The shelf splits: KJV, ASV, Geneva and NWT 1984 “a pestilent fellow” and Douay “a pestilent man” keep the disease word (five of the seven), while NIV and TLB “a troublemaker” give the meaning without the metaphor. The participle “having found” is also the first of the chapter’s finding-words: Paul will answer that they did not find him reasoning or stirring up a crowd (heuron me, 24:12), did find him purified (24:18), and asks what wrongdoing they found (24:20).

“Stirring up dissensions” (Acts 24:5). Kinounta staseis, literally “moving dissensions.” The verb is the one Luke used of the city at 21:30, ekinēthē, “was stirred,” when the Asian Jews’ cry in the temple set Jerusalem running; Tertullus puts into Paul’s hands what the crowd did. The noun stasis is the word of 23:7 and 23:10, where the Pharisees and Sadducees split over the resurrection and this translation prints “dissension”; it is also the “insurrection” for which Barabbas lay in prison (Luke 23:19) and of which the town clerk at Ephesus feared being charged (19:40); see stasis. The noun means a rising or a party-strife, and which the English should print depends on the scene. At 23:7 the dissension arose as Paul was speaking; here it is charged as Paul’s business among “all the Jews throughout the inhabited world” (see oikoumenē), a charge about disorder in place of the “questions of their law” that the tribune had found (23:29). Luke does not say why Tertullus chose that ground. The apparatus records that Robinson–Pierpont prints the singular stasin, “a dissension,” where Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print the plural. The shelf: KJV and Geneva “a mover of sedition” use a singular, ASV “a mover of insurrections” and Douay “raising seditions” and NWT 1984 “stirring up seditions” the plural; NIV “stirring up riots” is a plural and a different noun; TLB “riots and rebellions against the Roman government” adds an object that the Greek does not have, since the Greek says only that the disturbances are among the Jews. For the world, KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “throughout the world” print the plain English, and NWT 1984 “the inhabited earth” keeps the participle of oikoumenē, as this translation does.

“Ringleader” (Acts 24:5). Prōtostatēs stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so it is a hapax in the New Testament, and no second use in it can show the sense. Lexicons build it from prōtos, “first,” and the root of histēmi, “to stand,” a man who stands in the front rank, and report it as a word of military and political leadership; that is reported here, not examined on this page. The English “ringleader” keeps the bad sense of someone leading a disturbance. The shelf: KJV, ASV, NIV and TLB “a ringleader of the” agree with it; NWT 1984 “a spearhead of the sect” keeps the front-rank picture, Geneva “a chief maintainer of the sect” makes him a defender, and Douay “author of the sedition of the sect” turns the word into the founder of the sedition, which the Greek does not say. The word is reported to share its root with stasis, the noun just before it in the verse; that is reported here, not tested.

“The party of the Nazarenes” (Acts 24:5). Tēs tōn Nazōraiōn haireseōs. The plural Nazōraioi stands in one verse of the thirteen in which the title occurs, this one. Everywhere else the title is the singular “the Nazarene,” of Jesus: as Peter says it at 2:22 and 4:10, as Jesus says it of himself at 22:8 (see Nazōraios). Here it becomes the name of a group. Whether Tertullus coins the plural, or the label was already in use for Jesus’s followers, the text does not say. Hairesis is the word of 5:17, “the party of the Sadducees,” and 15:5, the Pharisees, and in Acts the Greek by itself carries no accusation: it names a body defined by the position it chose (see hairesis). Paul will meet the word again at 24:14, “which they call a party,” and it comes back at 26:5, and at 28:22. The English shelf prints “sect” here in seven of the seven versions: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “the sect of the Nazarenes,” NIV “the Nazarene sect,” TLB “the sect known as the Nazarenes,” and NWT 1984 “sect.” Since the English “sect” suggests a break-away, “party” keeps the word the Greek shares with the Sadducees and the Pharisees; in Tertullus’s mouth the disciples are one more group within the nation, with Paul at its head.

“Profane the temple” and “whom we seized” (Acts 24:6). Hos kai to hieron epeirasen bebēlōsai. The verb bebēloō stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Matthew 12:5, where “the priests in the temple profane the sabbath and are guiltless.” The related adjective bebēlos stands in five verses of the letters, one on these pages (1 Timothy 1:9) and four not (1 Timothy 4:7 and 6:20, 2 Timothy 2:16 and Hebrews 12:16, not yet on these pages), and is not counted here. The shelf: KJV and Douay “gone about to profane the temple,” ASV “assayed to profane the temple” and NWT 1984 “tried to profane the temple” keep “profane” (four of the seven); NIV “even tried to desecrate the temple,” TLB “trying to defile the Temple” and Geneva “gone about to pollute the Temple” use another verb. The charge is smaller than the one the Asian Jews cried at 21:28, that Paul “has even brought Greeks into the temple and has made common this holy place” (the verb there is koinoō; see koinoō), on the strength of a supposition about Trophimus the Ephesian (21:29). Epeirasen is an attempt, “tried,” the verb that governs an infinitive in 16:7, where they “were trying to go on into Bithynia,” and again at 26:21; Tertullus names neither Greeks nor Trophimus and claims only an attempt. The tribune’s own letter describes the seizure from the other side: the man was “seized by the Jews and about to be killed by them” and the tribune “rescued” him (23:27); Luke’s narrative has the crowd “seizing Paul” and dragging him out (21:30). Tertullus’s ekratēsamen, “we seized,” uses a different verb from either, and its “we” is the speaker’s group, the high priest Ananias and the elders of 24:1 (see Ananias the high priest), though the Jews who laid hands on Paul in the temple are named at 21:27 as “the Jews from Asia,” and Paul will say at 24:19 that they “ought to be here before you.” Whether the “we” claims their act as its own, the Greek does not say. The shelf’s verbs for the seizing: NWT 1984 “whom we seized,” KJV “whom we took,” ASV “we laid hold” and TLB “we arrested him.”

The gap at 6b–8a: no verse 7 (Acts 24:6–8). The apparatus note on ekratēsamen reads: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont all end the clause there, and a longer text, tagged TR, adds three pieces: kai kata ton hēmeteron nomon ēthelēsamen krinein, “and we wished to judge according to our law”; a verse 7, parelthōn de Lysias ho chiliarchos meta pollēs bias ek tōn cheirōn hēmōn apēgage, “but Lysias the tribune coming up, with great violence led him away out of our hands”; and the start of verse 8, keleusas tous katēgorous autou erchesthai epi se, “ordering his accusers to come to you.” The printed text this translation follows is therefore the shorter one, with verse 6 ending at “whom we seized,” no verse 7 and verse 8 beginning at “from whom,” and the verse numbers are left with the gap showing, as at 8:37 and 15:34. The apparatus gives no reason for the difference and none is guessed here. The shelf prints the longer text in five of the seven versions: KJV “Lysias came upon us, and with great violence took him away out of our hands,” ASV “Lysias came, and with great violence took him away out of our hands,” Geneva “Lysias came upon us, and with great violence took him out of our hands,” Douay “Lysias the tribune coming upon us, with great violence took him away out of our hands” and TLB “Lysias, the commander of the garrison, came and took him violently away from us.” For the third piece, Geneva and Douay “Commanding his accusers to come to thee” and ASV “commanding his accusers to come before thee” are close to the Greek, while TLB “demanding that he be tried by Roman law” is a paraphrase that changes what is demanded. The two that do not print it are NIV “so we seized him,” whose text runs from verse 6 to verse 8 with no verse 7, and NWT 1984 “and whom we seized,” which prints the number 7 followed by a dash and a footnote; the footnote, as reported here and not checked against the manuscripts, names the papyrus P74 and the codices Sinaiticus, Alexandrinus and Vaticanus, with the Vulgate in one form, as lacking the words, and a further form of the Vulgate and Syriac and Armenian witnesses as having them. NWT 2013 is a different witness and also prints a dash at verse 7. Two things change in the sentence. First, Lysias: in the shorter text Tertullus never names the tribune, and the name comes into this chapter only from Felix at 24:22, “When Lysias the tribune comes down” (the name Lysias stands in two verses of Acts, 23:26 and 24:22; see Claudius Lysias); the longer text has the accusers tell the rescue as an outrage, “with great violence,” where the letter at 23:27 says “I rescued, coming upon them with the troop.” Second, the instruction to the accusers: the longer text has Lysias order them to come to Felix, as the letter itself says at 23:30, “instructing his accusers also to speak against him before you,” in different Greek (paraggeilas there, keleusas in the addition). In the shorter text Tertullus says nothing of the order, and accusers who ought to be present are raised only by Paul, at 24:19.

“From whom you yourself, by examining” (Acts 24:8). Par’ hou dynēsē autos anakrinas peri pantōn toutōn epignōnai hōn hēmeis katēgoroumen autou. The relative hou is a singular, so it cannot be “the accusers,” and which one man it names depends on the text. In the shorter text the only singular in reach is the man of v6, “whom we seized”: Felix will learn it from Paul, by examining him. In the longer text the singular nearest is Lysias, and the sentence can be read as sending Felix to the tribune, with the examining then falling on Paul. The shelf shows both: NWT 1984 “From him you yourself can by examination find out,” NIV “examining him yourself” and TLB “examining him yourself” point at Paul, and KJV “by examining of whom thyself” and ASV “from whom thou wilt be able, by examining him thyself” keep a “whom” that, with the longer text before it, can be Lysias. The Greek verb has no object of its own, so the English “him” is supplied. Anakrinō stands in fourteen verses of the New Testament, six of them in Luke–Acts: Pilate’s “having examined him before you” at Luke 23:14, Peter and John “being examined” at 4:9, Herod examining the guards at 12:19, the Bereans “examining the Scriptures” at 17:11, this verse, and 28:18; see anakrinō. The noun anakrisis stands at 25:26. The verb is the one Luke gives Pilate, and Tertullus asks Felix for the same kind of inquiry by the one holding the bench. The infinitive epignōnai is Lysias’s own at 23:28, “wishing to find out the charge,” and Paul uses it back to Felix at 24:11, “since you are able to ascertain,” with the same verb of ability (dynēsē here, dynamenou there): both sides appeal to what Felix can find out. Felix’s answer is the postponement at 24:22.

“Joined in the attack” (Acts 24:9). Synepethento de kai hoi Ioudaioi phaskontes tauta houtōs echein. The verb synepethento stands in one verse of the New Testament, so the compound is a hapax; the simple verb epitithēmi, the verb of “set upon you to harm you” at 18:10, is its base. The “attack” here is verbal, and NWT 1984 “joined in the attack” and this translation print the same English; ASV “joined in the charge,” NIV “joined in the accusation” and TLB “chimed in” make it a joining of voices, KJV “assented,” Geneva “affirmed” and Douay “added” drop the notion of attack. The participle phaskontes, “asserting,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Romans 1:22, and 25:19, where Festus reports of a dead Jesus “whom Paul asserted to be alive.” The phrase tauta houtōs echein, “these things were so,” stands in two verses: the high priest’s question to Stephen at 7:1, “Are these things so?,” and this one, where the answer comes from the other side as an assertion. The Bereans of 17:11 had set the question and the verb of v8 together, “examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so” (anakrinontes, ei echoi tauta houtōs, in another word order). Luke does not tie the three passages together. “The Jews also” is the Greek hoi Ioudaioi, and the delegation of v1 are Jews already, so the word may mean the same group speaking for themselves; NWT 1984, ASV, KJV and Douay “the Jews also” keep the Greek, while NIV “The other Jews” and TLB “all the other Jews” add “other.” Who these Jews are is not said; the apparatus notes nothing on this verse beyond the gap at 6, so the Greek of 9 stands as printed by Westcott–Hort, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont alike. The prosecution rests here, and the governor nods to Paul at 24:10.

Verses 10–13 · νεύσαντος … κριτὴν … εὐθύμως … δώδεκα … ἐπιγνῶναι … ἐπίστασιν … παραστῆσαι neusantos … kritēn … euthymōs … dōdeka … epignōnai … epistasin … parastēsai — Acts 24:10–13: how long had Paul been in Jerusalem (“twelve days”), what does the governor’s “nod” allow, and what must the accusers “prove”?

“Nodded” (Acts 24:10). Neusantos autō tou hēgemonos legein is “the governor having nodded to him to speak.” The verb is neuō, a sign made with a movement of the head; that is how lexicons give it, reported here, not examined on this page. The simple verb stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and John 13:24, where Simon Peter “motioned” to the disciple beside Jesus to ask whom he meant. This translation prints “motioned” at John 13:24 for the same verb and “nodded” here, so the English does not mark the one verb the same way twice; the narrower “nodded” keeps the gesture that a governor can make to a prisoner without a word. Compounds of the verb stand elsewhere (for example eneneuon at Luke 1:62 and epeneusen at Acts 18:20, where Paul “did not consent” to stay longer in Ephesus), and they are not counted here. It is not the verb that Luke uses for a hand raised to a crowd. Kataseiō, “motioning with the hand,” stands in four verses of Acts: 12:17 (Peter), 13:16 (Paul), 19:33 (Alexander) and 21:40 (Paul on the steps, after the tribune had “allowed” it, epitrepsantos). At 21:40 the one who gives leave is the tribune and the one who gestures is Paul; here the leave and the gesture are both the governor’s, and Paul waits for the nod before he speaks. The shelf has four wordings for the one verb. NWT 1984 “nodded to him to speak” keeps the head movement. KJV “beckoned unto him to speak,” ASV “beckoned unto him to speak” and Geneva “beckoned unto him that he should speak” print “beckoned,” three of the seven. NIV “motioned for him to speak” and TLB “motioned for him to rise and speak” print “motioned,” two of the seven, and TLB adds “rise,” which the Greek does not say. Douay “making a sign to him to speak” is the seventh. The sentence opens apekrithē te, “and he answered,” where the printed Greek of Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 has te and the Robinson–Pierpont text has de; the two are both connectives, and the sense of “and Paul answered” is the same.

“A judge over this nation” (Acts 24:10). Ek pollōn etōn onta se kritēn tō ethnei toutō, “from many years being a judge to this nation.” Paul’s opening has no vocative: there is no “most excellent Felix” (kratiste Phēlix), the address Tertullus used at 24:3 and Lysias had written in the letter at 23:26. The adjective kratistos stands in four verses of the New Testament, three of them on these pages (Luke 1:3, 23:26 and 24:3) and the fourth at 26:25, where Paul himself uses it to Festus (kratiste Phēste). Paul has the word to hand, then, and does not use it here. Nor does he pick up Tertullus’s “much peace” or the “reforms” of 24:2; he says only what Felix has been, and for how long. The two words “this nation” (tō ethnei toutō) are the very words of 24:2 in Tertullus’s mouth. The noun kritēs, “judge,” stands in four verses of Acts: 10:42, of the one “determined by God as judge of the living and the dead”; 13:20, the judges God gave Israel until Samuel; 18:15, where Gallio says “I do not wish to be a judge of these things” when the Jews bring Paul to him over “a word and names and your own law”; and here. Gallio refuses the office, and Paul names it for Felix in a clause. What the clause does not say is how many years. Paul’s “many years” is his own claim, and this page does not fix the dates of Felix’s term; it reports what two ancient historians say. Josephus, Antiquities 20.137 (fetched, Perseus Greek), says Claudius sent “Claudius Felix, brother of Pallas, to take charge of the affairs of Judea” (Pempei de kai Klaudion Phēlika Pallantos adelphon tōn kata tēn Ioudaian prostēsomenon pragmatōn), and 20.182 says that Porcius Festus was sent by Nero as Felix’s successor (diadochou). Neither sentence gives a number of years. Tacitus, Annals 12.54 (fetched, Perseus Latin), calls him Felix, “long since set over Judea” (iam pridem Iudaeae impositus), and says that Judea was then split with Ventidius Cumanus, with the Galileans under Cumanus and the Samaritans under Felix. The same passage says that Felix “kept kindling crimes with ill-timed remedies” (intempestivis remediis delicta accendebat), a different report from Tertullus’s “peace.” The library has entries for Felix and Claudius, and for Josephus and Tacitus. On the shelf, “many years” is the common rendering: KJV “of many years,” ASV “of many years,” Geneva “oft many years,” Douay “for many years,” NWT 1984 “for many years” and TLB “for many years” make six of the seven, and NIV “a number of years” is the seventh and the vaguer. TLB “a judge of Jewish affairs” turns the people (ethnos) into a subject, and NWT 1984 “this nation has had you as judge” recasts the participle as a clause; both keep “judge.”

“Cheerfully” (Acts 24:10). Euthymōs ta peri emautou apologoumai, “cheerfully I make my defense about my own affairs.” The adverb stands in this verse alone, and the stem is a family: euthym- stands in five verses of the New Testament, the adverb here, euthymein at Acts 27:22 and euthymeite at 27:25, euthymoi at 27:36, and euthymei at James 5:13 (not yet on these pages): “be of good cheer” in a storm, and “is anyone cheerful?” The apparatus has a note on the word: the printed text of Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 is euthymōs, where Robinson–Pierpont prints euthymoteron, a comparative, “more cheerfully.” Two shelf versions print a comparative: KJV “the more cheerfully” and Geneva “the more gladly.” That is the English the comparative would give; whether it rests on that reading is not examined here. The others have the plain adverb or a phrase: ASV “I cheerfully make my defense,” NIV “gladly,” NWT 1984 “readily,” Douay “with good courage,” and TLB “this gives me confidence,” which turns the mood into a result of the judge’s long service. The verb apologoumai is the present, “I am making my defense,” as the speech is under way; its noun apologia opened Paul’s speech to the crowd at 22:1, and the verb has its earlier use at 19:33, where Alexander “wished to make a defense” and was shouted down. Here nobody shouts. Ta peri emautou, “the things about myself,” limits the speech to the matter in hand.

“Twelve days” (Acts 24:11). Ou pleious eisin moi hēmerai dōdeka aph’ hēs anebēn proskynēsōn eis Ierousalēm is literally “there are not more to me than twelve days from which I went up, going to worship, to Jerusalem.” The apparatus has one note on the number: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print dōdeka, and Robinson–Pierpont print dekadyo, “ten-two”; the number is the same, and only the spelling of it differs. What the chapters on these pages give for a count is this. Paul comes to Jerusalem and the brothers receive him (21:17); the next day he goes in to James and the elders (21:18); “on the next day” he takes the men, is purified with them and goes into the temple to announce the days of purification (21:26); the Asian Jews seize him “as the seven days were about to be completed” (21:27); “on the next day” the tribune brings him before the Sanhedrin (22:30); “when it was day” the plot is made (23:12); the soldiers leave at the third hour of the night (23:23), reach Antipatris in the night (23:31) and enter Caesarea the day after (23:32–33). Then comes “after five days” (24:1). Counted from 21:17, the day of the seizure falls about the eighth or ninth day if the seven days of purification begin on the third; the Sanhedrin and the plot follow it by a day each, and Caesarea is reached about the eleventh or twelfth. That is twelve days or near it from the arrival to the delivery at Caesarea, and the hearing before Felix comes five days after that, about the sixteenth or seventeenth day, if the five days are counted from the arrival at Caesarea, which the verse at 24:1 does not say. So “twelve” fits the interval up to the transfer and not the hearing, on this count. Luke does not say how Paul counts, and the Greek is ou pleious, “not more than,” a ceiling and not a tally, so a count that ends at the seizure or at the Sanhedrin also stays under it. The road up from Caesarea came before the arrival (21:15–16, anebainomen, “we were going up”), the same verb that Paul uses here; whether his count begins on the road or at the arrival is not said. This page reports the count and does not make it fit. On the shelf the ceiling is stated by ASV “not more than twelve days,” NWT 1984 “not been more than twelve days,” NIV “no more than twelve days ago” and TLB “no more than twelve days ago,” four of the seven; KJV “yet but twelve days,” Geneva “but twelve days” and Douay “yet but twelve days” say “but,” the other three, a milder word for the same limit.

“Able to ascertain” (Acts 24:11). Dynamenou sou epignōnai, “you being able to ascertain.” The infinitive epignōnai stands in four verses of the New Testament: Luke 24:16, where the disciples’ eyes “were kept from recognizing him,” and three in Acts that run together: Lysias, “wishing to find out the charge” (23:28), Tertullus, saying Felix will be able “to learn about all these things” by examining Paul (24:8), and Paul, here. All three men invite the governor to look into the matter, and they say it with the same word. The English of this translation has three verbs for the three (“find out,” “learn,” “ascertain”) where the Greek has one. Apparatus 905: Robinson–Pierpont prints the shorter gnōnai, “to know,” where the others print epignōnai. The shelf has a spread of verbs: NWT 1984 “in a position to find out,” NIV “easily verify,” TLB “quickly discover,” ASV “take knowledge,” Geneva “mayest know,” and KJV “mayest understand” and Douay “mayest understand.” The adverbs “easily” and “quickly” in the second and third of those renderings are not in the Greek.

“To worship” (Acts 24:11). Proskynēsōn is the future participle of purpose, “going to worship,” the same form and the same preposition as at 8:27, where the Ethiopian official “had come to Jerusalem to worship” (Ethiopian eunuch). Proskyneō stands in four verses of Acts: 7:43 (figures “that you made to worship them”), 8:27, 10:25 (Cornelius “bowed down” to Peter) and here. Paul states his purpose in Jerusalem as a pilgrim’s, in a single word, and the longer account of it follows at 24:17–18. The apparatus has a note on the preposition after the participle: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print eis, “to,” and Robinson–Pierpont print en, “in,” so that the place is either where he went or where he worshiped. On the shelf NIV “went up to Jerusalem to worship” and KJV “to Jerusalem for to worship” put the place first; ASV “to worship at Jerusalem” and NWT 1984 “to worship in Jerusalem” put it last. Douay “to adore in Jerusalem” has the verb “adore,” and TLB “to worship at the Temple” names the temple, which this verse does not; the next verse does.

“Reasoning” and “stirring up a crowd” (Acts 24:12). Oute en tō hierō heuron me pros tina dialegomenon ē epistasin poiounta ochlou, “neither in the temple did they find me reasoning with anyone or making an epistasis of a crowd.” Dialegomai stands in ten verses of Acts, and eight of the others are printed “reasoned” or “reasoning” on earlier pages: 17:2, 17:17, 18:4, 18:19, 19:8, 19:9, 20:7, 20:9, and this verse is the ninth; the tenth is 24:25, where the same verb is printed “reasoning” as well. So the chapter has Paul deny the very practice that Acts has shown him in synagogue after synagogue. The denial is limited by place: not in the temple, not in the synagogues, not “throughout the city.” The chapters on these pages show no such reasoning in Jerusalem between his arrival at 21:17 and the seizure at 21:30; no use of the verb stands in Acts 21, 22 or 23. The noun epistasis is rarer: it stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 2 Corinthians 11:28 (not yet on these pages), where it is the “daily” weight of Paul’s concern for the congregations, a sense lexicons commonly give as “pressure” and set apart from the sense here, a crowd rushing together (reported here, not checked on this page). The same apparatus note stands at both places: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print epistasis and Robinson–Pierpont print episystasis. Paul’s word answers the charge of kinounta staseis, “stirring up dissensions” (24:5; stasis was printed “dissension” at 23:7); he uses a different noun, and Luke has a nearby echo in the noun systrophē, which stands in two verses of the New Testament: 19:40, the town clerk’s “turbulent crowd” (the speaker is named at 19:35), and 23:12, where the Jews “formed a conspiracy” against Paul. The apparatus has oude (Westcott–Hort, Nestle–Aland 28) or oute (Tregelles, Robinson–Pierpont) at the start of v13, which decides whether v13 continues the “neither … nor” series of v12; the English “nor” fits either. The shelf has four wordings for the first verb. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “disputing with any man” print “disputing” (four of the seven); NIV and NWT 1984 “arguing with anyone” print “arguing” (two); TLB “never incited a riot” has no verb for the first charge at all. None of the seven prints “reasoning,” so that the shelf’s English hides the repetition across Acts that the site’s “reasoned” shows. For the crowd, ASV and NIV “stirring up a crowd” agree; KJV “raising up the people,” Geneva “making uproar among the people,” Douay “causing any concourse of the people,” NWT 1984 “causing a mob to rush together” and TLB “incited a riot” each choose a different phrase.

“Prove” (Acts 24:13). Parastēsai is the aorist infinitive of paristēmi, “to set beside, to present,” and in a court the thing presented is evidence: “nor can they prove to you the things of which they now accuse me.” The same infinitive stands two chapters back, at 23:24, where Lysias orders animals “provided” (parastēsai) to bring Paul to Felix, and the same verb stands at 23:33, where the soldiers “presented Paul to him also” (parestēsan). So Felix has already had Paul “presented” to him, and Paul now asks that the accusers “present” the charges. The Greek verb says “present”; “prove” is the English that a courtroom sense of presenting evidence calls for, and the shelf takes it. The same stem presents a child at the temple (Luke 2:22), and presents bodies, a bride and a worker to God (Romans 12:1, 2 Corinthians 11:2 and 2 Timothy 2:15, none of them on these pages). The apparatus has three small readings in the verse. Robinson–Pierpont adds me after the infinitive (“to prove me”), and omits soi, “to you,” which the other three editions print; and Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have nyni, “now,” where Robinson–Pierpont has nyn, the same word in a shorter form. Nyni is also the word in Paul’s opening at 22:1, “my defense before you now.” The shelf agrees on the verb: NWT 1984 “Nor can they prove to you,” NIV “cannot prove to you,” TLB “cannot prove,” ASV “Neither can they prove,” KJV “Neither can they prove,” Geneva “Neither can they prove” and Douay “Neither can they prove” print “prove,” seven of the seven. The verse names no rule of procedure; Festus speaks of the custom of accuser and accused meeting face to face at 25:16.

Verses 14–16 · ὁμολογῶ … τὴν ὁδὸν ἣν λέγουσιν αἵρεσιν … λατρεύω τῷ πατρῴῳ θεῷ … ἀνάστασιν μέλλειν ἔσεσθαι δικαίων τε καὶ ἀδίκων … ἀσκῶ ἀπρόσκοπον συνείδησιν homologō … tēn hodon hēn legousin hairesin … latreuō tō patrōiō theō … anastasin mellein esesthai dikaiōn te kai adikōn … askō aproskopon syneidēsin — Acts 24:14–16: is “the Way” a “sect” or a “party” (hairesis), what is “a resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous,” and what is a conscience “without offense”?

“But this I confess to you” (Acts 24:14). Homologō is, as the dictionary has it, to “say the same thing” as another: an admission, an acknowledgement, a promise given (homologeō). The de after it answers the verse before: the accusers cannot prove what they charge (24:13), “but” there is one thing Paul grants. The forms homologō, homologousin and hōmologēsen stand in three verses of Acts: 7:17, where God “had declared” the promise to Abraham; 23:8, where the Pharisees “acknowledge” resurrection, angel and spirit; and here. So the verb that names the Pharisees’ belief at 23:8 names Paul’s statement a chapter later, and what he grants is in the next breath the Pharisees’ own article (v15). The shelf prints two English verbs. KJV “I confess unto thee,” ASV “I confess unto thee,” Geneva “I confess unto thee” and Douay “I confess to thee” print “confess,” and so does TLB “I do confess,” five of the seven; NWT 1984 “I do admit” and NIV “I admit” print “admit.” Both English verbs carry a faint sound of guilt that the Greek verb does not need; what follows is a statement of loyalty, not of fault. This translation keeps “confess,” the dictionary’s word for the verb.

“The Way, which they call a party” (Acts 24:14). Kata tēn hodon hēn legousin hairesin, literally “according to the road which they call a hairesis.” Tertullus had used the word of the Nazarenes at 24:5, “party of the Nazarenes,” and Paul now takes it up as other people’s label (legousin, “they say, they call”) and sets his own name for the movement beside it. “The Way” is the name Luke gave it at 9:2, 19:9 and 19:23, and Paul himself used it of what he once persecuted at 22:4; it comes once more at 24:22 (hodos). The noun hairesis is the word the dictionary renders “party”: Luke uses it of the Sadducees (5:17), of the Pharisees (15:5, and 26:5) and of the Christians, here and at 24:5 and at 28:22. The noun stands in nine verses of the New Testament, six of them in Acts; the other three are 1 Corinthians 11:19, Galatians 5:20 and 2 Peter 2:1 (none yet on these pages). The reading “party” rests on the Acts uses, where Luke applies the same noun to Sadducees and Pharisees without laying any charge by it. The English word “heresy” comes from this Greek by way of Latin, and the Douay, which translates the Latin, keeps it. At 24:5 seven of the seven shelf versions print “sect”; at 24:14 the shelf splits. KJV “which they call heresy,” Geneva “which they call heresy” and Douay “which they call a heresy” keep the old word, three of the seven; ASV “which they call a sect,” NIV “which they call a sect,” TLB “which they refer to as a sect” and NWT 1984 “they call a ‘sect,’” print “sect,” four of the seven; none prints “party,” the word this translation uses at 5:17 and 15:5 and here. What changes is how an English reader hears Paul. “Heresy” makes his clause an answer to a charge of false teaching, which the Greek of 24:5 does not put in so many words; “sect” and “party” name a company of people, which he did. The name of the Way is also capitalized differently: ASV “after the Way” and NIV “a follower of the Way” print it as a name, KJV “after the way” and NWT 1984 “according to the way” as a common noun, and TLB “the way of salvation” adds a gloss that is not in the Greek.

“The God of my fathers” (Acts 24:14). Houtōs latreuō tō patrōiō theō, “thus I serve the ancestral God.” The adjective patrōios stands in three verses of the New Testament, all of them Paul’s own words in Acts: 22:3, where he was educated “according to the strictness of the ancestral law”; here; and 28:17. It is one word in both places. At 22:3 it modifies “law” and is printed “ancestral”; here it modifies “God,” and “ancestral God” is not English a reader would take in, so the phrase is printed “the God of my fathers,” which is what “ancestral” means. KJV “the God of my fathers,” Geneva “the God of my fathers” and ASV “the God of our fathers” keep “fathers”; NWT 1984 “the God of my forefathers,” NIV “the God of our ancestors” and TLB “the God of our ancestors” modernize it, six of the seven. Douay “the Father and my God” is different in kind: it has two nouns where the Greek printed here has one adjective, and since the Douay translates the Latin, the divergence is that of the Latin it follows (reported here, not checked on this page). The verb latreuō, the verb of service paid to a god (latreuō), stands in five verses of Acts: 7:7, where Israel will “serve” God in this place, and 7:42, where God gives them over “to serve the host of heaven”; here; 26:7; and 27:23. Paul uses it of himself outside Acts too, in Romans 1:9 (“whom I serve in my spirit”) and 2 Timothy 1:3 (“whom I serve, as my forefathers did, with a clean conscience”), the second a near neighbor of this verse, since it joins service, forefathers and conscience, the three things of vv14–16. For the verb the shelf prints “serve” (ASV “so serve I”) or “worship” (KJV “so worship I” and Geneva “so worship I”), and NWT 1984 “rendering sacred service” uses a phrase where the others use a verb; NIV “I worship” and TLB “serving the God of our ancestors” recast the clause. The point of the sentence is the claim itself: serving the God of Israel’s fathers is exactly what he says “the Way” is.

“All the things according to the law and written in the Prophets” (Acts 24:14). Pisteuōn pasi tois kata ton nomon kai tois en tois prophētais gegrammenois has two phrases under one “all,” “the things according to the law” and “the things written in the Prophets”; the law is not said to be “written,” and the preposition before it is kata, “according to.” The apparatus marks the second phrase: the printed text (Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28) has tois en, “the ones in,” and Robinson–Pierpont omits those two words, leaving tois prophētais gegrammenois; the English is the same either way. The shelf mostly folds the two into one clause. ASV “which are according to the law” and NIV “in accordance with the Law” keep kata; KJV “written in the law and in the prophets,” Geneva “written in the Law and the Prophets” and Douay “written in the law and the prophets” make both of them “written,” three of the seven; NWT 1984 “set forth in the Law” and TLB “the Jewish law” take a third course, with the TLB adding “Jewish,” which is not in the Greek. This translation keeps the two phrases apart.

“Which these men themselves also accept” (Acts 24:15). Elpida echōn eis ton theon, hēn kai autoi houtoi prosdechontai. “Hope” is the word of 23:6 (“concerning hope and resurrection of the dead I am being judged,” 23:6); the noun is elpis. The verb prosdechomai is “receive toward oneself,” and it means both “accept” and “wait for.” At 23:21 it is the plotters “waiting for the promise from you,” with the tribune; at Luke 2:25 it is Simeon “waiting for the consolation of Israel”; at Mark 15:43 it is Joseph of Arimathea, “who himself was also waiting for the kingdom of God,” the same shape as “which these men themselves also” here; and Titus 2:13 (not yet on these pages) has it with the “blessed hope.” The shelf divides over the sense. ASV “look for,” Geneva “look for” and Douay “look for” take “wait for,” three of the seven; KJV “allow” is closer to “accept”; NWT 1984 “entertain” takes it as holding a belief; and NIV “the same hope in God as these men themselves have” and TLB “just as these men do” leave the verb out and print the shared hope. The harder question is who “these men” are. The Sadducees, as the encyclopedia says, rejected resurrection (Sadducees), and 23:8 says so; the Pharisees accept it (Pharisees), and the delegation before Felix is the high priest, some elders and an orator (24:1; Ananias the high priest). The Greek does not say which of them Paul means, and Luke does not say how any answered. Whether Paul speaks of those among the accusers who share the hope, or of the Jews as a people, the text leaves open.

“A resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous” (Acts 24:15). Anastasin mellein esesthai dikaiōn te kai adikōn is, word for word, “a resurrection about to be of righteous both and unrighteous”; mellein esesthai, literally “to be about to be,” is printed “is going to be,” the English idiom for something that is coming. The pair dikaiōn te kai adikōn, with te kai binding the two groups into one, stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Neighbors differ: Luke 14:14 speaks of “the resurrection of the righteous” (one group); John 5:29 has “a resurrection of life” for those who did good and “a resurrection of judgment” for those who “practiced vile things” (two nouns, and neither is dikaios or adikos); and Daniel 12:2 has “many of those sleeping in the ground of dust shall wake” to “everlasting life” or to “reproaches,” with no word for righteous either. Acts 24:15 has a single anastasin for both groups. Readers divide on whether this describes one event or two, and what follows for each group; the verse itself says that there is going to be a resurrection of both, and it names no outcome. The apparatus has one reading: Robinson–Pierpont adds nekrōn after esesthai, “a resurrection of the dead, of the righteous and the unrighteous.” On the shelf, KJV “a resurrection of the dead, both of the just and unjust” and Geneva “the resurrection of the dead” print “the dead,” two of the seven, the reading Robinson–Pierpont prints; the other five do not. For the pair, ASV “both of the just and unjust” and Douay “of the just and unjust” keep “just” and “unjust,” so with the two that print “the dead” four of the seven; NWT 1984 “both the righteous and the unrighteous” keeps “righteous” and “unrighteous,” as this translation does; NIV “both the righteous and the wicked” and TLB “both the righteous and ungodly” change the second word to “wicked” and “ungodly,” which names a worse state than adikos, “not just.” Paul returns to the topic at 24:21, “concerning resurrection of the dead I am being judged,” and again at 26:6.

“I exercise myself to have a conscience without offense” (Acts 24:16). Askō aproskopon syneidēsin echein pros ton theon kai tous anthrōpous dia pantos. The verb askō stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so there is no second use to compare. It means to work at something, to train; English “ascetic” comes from the same Greek family (reported here, not checked on this page). “Exercise myself” keeps the sense of regular practice, and the phrase dia pantos adds “always.” The adjective aproskopos stands in three verses: here; Philippians 1:10, printed “without stumbling,” “sincere and without stumbling for the day of Christ”; and 1 Corinthians 10:32 (not yet on these pages), where the Greek has the Corinthians become aproskopoi to Jews, to Greeks and to the congregation of God. It is a- with the root of proskoptō, “strike against”: no stumbling, whether one stumbles or causes another to. The English “without offense” leans to causing offense, and “without stumbling” at Philippians 1:10 to not stumbling oneself; the Greek adjective is the same, and which sense it carries is settled by each sentence (reported from the lexicons, not checked on this page). The phrase “toward God and men” makes this conscience one that looks in two directions. Acts 23:1 has the first syneidēsis in Acts, “in all good conscience up to this day,” and the noun stands in two verses of Acts, that one and this (syneidēsis); the “up to this day” of 23:1 becomes “always” here. For the verb, KJV “exercise myself,” ASV “exercise myself” and NWT 1984 “exercising myself continually” keep it, three of the seven; Douay “endeavour” and Geneva “endeavor myself” weaken it to effort; NIV “I strive” and TLB “I try” make it a plain wish. For the adjective, KJV “void of offence” and ASV “void of offence” and Douay “without offence” keep the idea of offense; NWT 1984 “committing no offense” makes it an act; and Geneva “a clear conscience,” NIV “conscience clear” and TLB “a clear conscience” print “clear,” three of the seven, which is the English idiom but a different idea: a clear conscience is about what one feels inside; aproskopos is about what one does to others. The apparatus has two small notes here. The printed text has kai autos, “I also,” echoing kai autoi, “they also,” in v15, and Robinson–Pierpont has de autos, “but I myself.” And the printed echein, “to have,” is in Robinson–Pierpont the participle echōn, “having”; on that reading the conscience is no longer the aim of the exercise but what accompanies it.

Verses 17–21 · ἐλεημοσύνας … εἰς τὸ ἔθνος μου … προσφοράς … ἡγνισμένον … οὓς ἔδει … ἀδίκημα … μιᾶς ταύτης φωνῆς eleēmosynas … eis to ethnos mou … prosphoras … hēgnismenon … hous edei … adikēma … mias tautēs phōnēs — Acts 24:17–21: were Paul’s “alms and offerings” the collection for Jerusalem, what does “found me purified” answer, and why does his defense end on one cry about the resurrection?

“Alms to my nation” (Acts 24:17). Eleēmosynas poiēsōn eis to ethnos mou paregenomēn kai prosphoras is “I arrived to do alms to my nation, and offerings.” The participle poiēsōn is future, a participle of purpose, and it matches proskynēsōn, “to worship,” at 24:11: Paul gives the governor two purposes for the journey, and this is the second. “Doing alms” is also how Cornelius is described, poiōn eleēmosynas pollas, “doing many alms” (10:2), and the word is the lame man’s request at 3:2 and the thing that “went up as a memorial before God” at 10:4 (see eleēmosynē). The noun eleēmosynē stands in thirteen verses of the New Testament, and every one is in Matthew, Luke or Acts; it is not used in any of Paul’s letters. The Greek of 24:17 names no city: paregenomēn is “I arrived,” and the destination comes from 24:11, “to Jerusalem.” NIV “I came to Jerusalem” and TLB “I returned to Jerusalem” print the city here. “My nation” is the singular ethnos, and the phrase ethnos mou in its three case forms stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, 26:4, and 28:19. The singular also names Israel at 10:22 (“the whole nation of the Jews”), 24:2 and 24:10. The plural is the one this translation prints “nations”: Paul reported to James “the things that God had done among the nations” (21:19). So across these chapters Paul has Israel as his ethnos and the other peoples as ethnē. The shelf divides on the alms. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “alms to my nation” keep the English that descends from the Greek word; NWT 1984 “gifts of mercy” keeps the eleos (mercy) root in the word; NIV “gifts for the poor” names recipients the Greek does not name here; TLB “money to aid the Jews” names money and a recipient, and replaces “my nation” by “the Jews.” Douay “offerings, and vows” adds a third noun that the Greek printed here does not have; that version translates the Vulgate, and what the Latin reads is not checked on this page.

“After several years” (Acts 24:17). Di’ etōn de pleionōn is the preposition dia with the genitive of years, “after an interval of more years.” The exact phrase di’ etōn stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Galatians 2:1 (not yet on these pages) has the same preposition with a number, dia dekatessarōn etōn, “after fourteen years,” and that sense of dia is reported here, not checked in a grammar on this page. Pleionōn is the comparative “more”: at 4:22 the same word stands in etōn gar ēn pleionōn tesserakonta, “more than forty years old.” Paul has just said of Felix ek pollōn etōn, “for many years” (24:10), so the speech counts years twice with two different words. The shelf prints “many” for the second: KJV, Geneva and Douay “after many years” give 24:17 the word of 24:10, ASV “after some years” and NWT 1984 “after quite a number of years” stay indefinite, and NIV and TLB “several years” are the two that agree with this translation. Which visit is being counted from is not stated. The last time Luke has Paul “go up” is 18:22, “having gone up and greeted the congregation, he went down to Antioch,” and that verse does not name a city either; that it is Jerusalem is the usual reading and is an inference. Between there and 21:17 the book gives these figures: “two years” of the word spreading from Ephesus (19:10), three months in Greece (20:3), and Paul’s own “three years” of admonishing at Ephesus (20:31). Luke adds no total and does not say whether those figures overlap.

Alms and the collection (Acts 24:17). Paul’s letters name a gift for Jerusalem, and the note on Acts 21:17–19 has already said that no verse of Acts 21 mentions one. This verse is the nearest Acts comes, and the vocabulary on the two sides does not overlap. In the letters the gift is a diakonia, “ministry” (Romans 15:25, 15:31; 2 Corinthians 8:4, 9:1, 9:12), a koinōnia, “fellowship,” made by Macedonia and Achaia for “the poor of the saints who are in Jerusalem” (Romans 15:26), a logeia “for the saints” (1 Corinthians 16:1–2) and a charis carried “to Jerusalem” by men the Corinthians approve (1 Corinthians 16:3); Galatians 2:10 has the “poor” whom Paul says he was eager to remember (all of these not yet on these pages). The noun logeia stands in two verses, 1 Corinthians 16:1 and 16:2, and the root of “the poor,” ptōch-, stands in no verse of Acts. Acts has an earlier relief gift, in the word diakonia: the disciples sent it “to the brothers living in Judea,” by Barnabas and Saul to the elders (11:29–30), and the two “fulfilled the service” at Jerusalem (12:25). And Romans 15:27 says the nations (ta ethnē) are in debt to the saints, because they shared in “their spiritual things” (not yet on these pages): in the letters the gift comes from the nations toward the saints, while here it goes “to my nation.” Two readings are held. One takes 24:17 as Luke’s only mention of the collection, in the words a Roman governor would understand, with “my nation” and “alms” being the speaker’s own terms for what the letters call a ministry to the saints; some readers take the list of companions at 20:4 as the bearers of it (see Acts 20:1–6). The other keeps the verse to what it says, charity for Israel together with the temple offerings of the next verse, and does not connect it with the letters. Neither Luke nor Paul links the two in so many words, and it is reported here, not decided. What the verse does is give Paul’s purposes, worship (24:11) and giving, in a speech answering a charge of stirring up dissension (24:5).

“Offerings” and “found me purified” (Acts 24:17–18). Prosphoras is the plural of prosphora, “offering,” the noun of prospherō, “to bring to.” In Acts the noun stands twice, here and at 21:26, where “the offering was presented for each one of them”; the nine New Testament verses also include Romans 15:16, “the offering of the nations” (not yet on these pages). The singular at 21:26 and the plural here do not say that they are the same sacrifices, and the verse does not either (see prosphora). NIV “to present offerings” and TLB “to offer a sacrifice to God” narrow the plural in different directions. Verse 18 then begins en hais, “in which,” a feminine plural relative with no noun beside it. The nearest feminine plurals are eleēmosynas and prosphoras, so the clause can mean “in the midst of which [gifts]”; and the “days” of purification and the seven days of 21:27, also feminine plural, are not in this verse. The apparatus gives hais to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and hois, neuter, “in which things,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The shelf divides on how to carry the clause. ASV “amidst which they found me purified in the temple” keeps the plural relative; NWT 1984 “While I was at these matters” reads it as the matters; KJV “Whereupon certain Jews from Asia found me” turns it into a connective and gives the Asian Jews the verb; Geneva “At what time, certain Jews of Asia found me” does the same; Douay “In which I was found purified” makes the verb passive; NIV “when they found me in the temple courts doing this” reads “this” as the offering and adds “courts”; and TLB “My accusers saw me in the Temple” and “I had shaved my head as their laws required” takes the head-shaving of 21:24 into the verse, which is not in these Greek words. The participle hēgnismenon is perfect passive of hagnizō, “purified,” and the form hēgnism- stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; the earlier forms are the command hagnisthēti said to Paul (21:24) and the participle hagnistheis said of him (21:26), see the note on Acts 21:23–26 and hagnizō. The perfect describes a state: the Greek says he was found in the condition of a man already purified. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “purified” keep the word (four of the seven); NWT 1984 “ceremonially cleansed” and NIV “ceremonially clean” add the adverb, and the seventh version, quoted above, paraphrases the whole clause. The verse then denies a crowd and noise: ou meta ochlou oude meta thorybou. KJV, Geneva and Douay “neither with multitude, nor with tumult” and ASV “nor yet with tumult” and NWT 1984 “with a tumult” print “tumult” (five of the seven); NIV “any disturbance” and TLB “no rioting” use other words. This translation prints “uproar,” the word of 21:34, where the tribune “could not learn the certainty because of the uproar,” and of 20:1: the noise Paul says was absent when he was found is the noise that the next verses of Acts 21 report.

“Some Jews from Asia” (Acts 24:19). Tines de apo tēs Asias Ioudaioi opens a clause that never gets a verb. The nominative “some Jews from Asia” is followed by a relative clause, hous edei epi sou parein kai katēgorein ei ti echoien pros eme, and then the sentence is dropped: verse 20 begins ē autoi houtoi eipatōsan, “or let these men themselves say.” The Greek simply breaks off (an anacoluthon, reported here as the usual name for it), and the English verse keeps the break with two dashes. The shelf mends it in four ways. ASV “certain Jews from Asia” ends verse 18 with a dash and leaves the break; NWT 1984 “there were certain Jews” and NIV “there are some Jews” and TLB “Jews from Turkey were there” supply a verb; KJV and Geneva make them the subject of the verb of 18, “found me,” so that 19 begins a new sentence with “Who ought”; and Douay “But certain Jews of Asia, who ought to be present” keeps the Greek order. TLB’s “Turkey” is a modern country’s name; the Greek says Asia, the Roman province of western Asia Minor (see Asia). These are the Jews who “having seen him in the temple” threw the crowd into confusion (21:27; see Acts 21:27–30). Their absence is the point of the clause: hous edei, “whom it was necessary” (the imperfect of dei, see dei), “to be present before you and to accuse.” An imperfect of dei is regularly an obligation not met, as in Edei men, ō andres, “Men, you ought to have listened to me” at 27:21; that use is reported here, not checked in a grammar on this page. The apparatus gives edei to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the present dei, “it is necessary,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The shelf splits on the tense: KJV and ASV “ought to have been here” and Geneva “ought to have been present” take the unmet obligation (three of the seven); NWT 1984 and Douay “ought to be present” and NIV and TLB “ought to be here” take the present (four of the seven). Epi sou, “before you,” is the phrase of Lysias’s letter, where he told the accusers “to speak against him before you” (23:30), and Felix had himself promised, “I will hear you out when your accusers also arrive” (23:35). Festus will later say that it is not the custom of Romans to give a man up before the accused has had his accusers face to face and a place for a defense (25:16); whether a codified rule stands behind that custom, this page has not checked. Two small points: katēgorein, “to accuse,” is KJV “and object,” where ASV “to make accusation,” Geneva “accuse me,” Douay “and to accuse,” NWT 1984 “to accuse me” and NIV “bring charges” keep the sense of the verb; and echoien is an optative, “if they might have anything,” which NWT 1984 “if they might have anything” alone keeps, the others printing plain “had” or “have.”

“What wrongdoing they found” (Acts 24:20). Ē autoi houtoi eipatōsan ti heuron adikēma stantos mou epi tou synedriou hands the answer to the men standing there. The noun adikēma stands in three verses of the New Testament: Gallio’s “some wrong or wicked villainy” at 18:14, here, and Revelation 18:5, in the plural (not yet on these pages). Gallio’s word is printed “wrong” and this one “wrongdoing”: these are the two places where a Roman official hears the word, Gallio from the accusers and Felix from the accused (see Gallio and adikēma). The verb is heuron, “found,” the same verb as heuron me in verse 18, so the two clauses answer each other: they found a purified man, and what wrong did they find? The Pharisee scribes had said ouden kakon heuriskomen en tō anthrōpō toutō, “We find nothing evil in this man” (23:9). “The Sanhedrin” is the council before which Paul “stood” at 22:30 and 23:1 (see Sanhedrin); the high priest Ananias was there (23:2) and has now come down with “some elders” (24:1), so “these men themselves” includes at least him (see Ananias), though the Greek names no one. KJV, ASV and Douay “before the council” and Geneva “in the Council” and TLB “their Council” print “council”; NWT 1984 “what wrong they found” and NIV “when I stood before the Sanhedrin” print the name (two of the seven). The apparatus gives the clause without “in me” to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 and with en emoi to Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont. Five of the seven print it: KJV “any evil doing in me,” Geneva “any unjust thing in me,” Douay “in me any iniquity,” NIV “what crime they found in me” and TLB “what wrongdoing their Council found in me.” Two do not: ASV “what wrong-doing they found” and NWT 1984 “what wrong they found.” “Crime” is a narrower word than adikēma, which names a wrongful act and does not say it is statutory.

“This one statement” (Acts 24:21). Ē peri mias tautēs phōnēs hēs ekekraxa en autois hestōs hoti Peri anastaseōs nekrōn egō krinomai sēmeron eph’ hymōn is the last clause of the defense. Phōnē is “voice” or “sound,” and the shelf prints it three ways: KJV, Geneva and Douay “Except it be for this one voice” and ASV “except it be for this one voice” keep it (four of the seven); NWT 1984 “this one utterance” names the act; NIV “this one thing I shouted” and TLB “I said one thing” turn it into an object. This translation prints “statement,” since “voice” in English reads as a faculty and not as something said, and keeps ekekraxa as “cried out,” the verb of 23:6, where Paul “cried out in the Sanhedrin.” The form ekekraxa is a different form of that verb from the ekrazen of 23:6, and it stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Two things the verse does with the conjunction. The Greek has ē, “or,” at the start of both verse 20 and verse 21. At 20 the shelf prints “or” six times (KJV “Or else”; ASV “Or else”; Geneva “Or”; Douay “Or”; NIV “Or”; NWT 1984 “Or,”), and at 21 all seven change it to an exception, “except” or “unless,” so that the cry becomes the one possible fault, where this translation’s “or about this one statement” makes it a third thing to be named. Whether ē can carry “unless” here is reported as a question for the grammars and not checked on this page. And Paul does not quote 23:6 word for word. There he cried peri elpidos kai anastaseōs nekrōn egō krinomai, “concerning hope and resurrection of the dead I am being judged”; here the clause is peri anastaseōs nekrōn, without “hope,” and with “today” and “before you” added. The “you” is plural (hymōn) and sits inside the quotation, so it is the Sanhedrin he was addressing, and the “today” is the day of that hearing; Luke does not say whether the cry had more words than 23:6 gives. This translation prints “resurrection of the dead” with no article, as the Greek has none and as 23:6 prints it, and the shelf prints “the resurrection of the dead” in six of the seven (KJV “Touching the resurrection of the dead”; ASV “Touching the resurrection of the dead”; Douay “Concerning the resurrection of the dead”; Geneva “Of the resurrection of the dead”; NWT 1984 “Over the resurrection of the dead”; NIV “the resurrection of the dead”), while TLB “for believing that the dead will rise again” rewrites the clause. The last word, eph’ hymōn, is “before you.” The apparatus prints ephʼ (“before,” “in the presence of”) in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and hyph’ (“by”) in Robinson–Pierpont, and three versions have the agent: KJV “by you,” Geneva “accused of you” and Douay “judged this day by you” read as Robinson–Pierpont does; ASV “before you this day,” NWT 1984 “being judged before YOU” and NIV “on trial before you today” read “before,” and TLB “before the Council” names the court. The charge Tertullus laid, kinounta staseis, “stirring up dissensions” (24:5), had its origin in what followed that cry: “there arose a dissension of the Pharisees and Sadducees” (23:7). Paul does not say so here, and Luke does not comment on it. Resurrection is the one subject he names (see 24:15), and it is where Festus will later place the dispute: a certain Jesus who had died, whom Paul said was alive (25:19).

Verses 22–23 · Ἀνεβάλετο … ἀκριβέστερον εἰδὼς τὰ περὶ τῆς ὁδοῦ … διαγνώσομαι … τηρεῖσθαι … ἄνεσιν … τῶν ἰδίων … ὑπηρετεῖν Anebaleto … akribesteron eidōs ta peri tēs hodou … diagnōsomai … tēreisthai … anesin … tōn idiōn … hypēretein — Why does Felix “put off” Paul’s trial (anebaleto), what does he know “more accurately” about the Way, and what freedom does Paul have in custody?

“Put them off” (Acts 24:22). Anebaleto is the whole of Felix’s ruling: no verdict, no acquittal, a postponement. The verb anaballō stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The noun built on the same stem, anabolē, “delay,” stands in one verse as well, 25:17, where Festus says he made no delay in seating himself on the tribunal the next day. The verb is formed from ana-, “up,” and ballō, “throw”; that is how lexicons build it, and it is reported here, not examined on this page. The shelf splits three ways on how much to say. KJV “he deferred them,” and so ASV “deferred them” and Geneva “deferred them.” Douay “put them off,” the wording this page’s verse also uses. NWT 1984 “began to put the [men] off,” with “[men]” supplied in brackets and an inchoative “began to” for a plain aorist. NIV “adjourned the proceedings,” which drops the object “them” and reads the verb as a court term; and TLB “told the Jews to wait,” which names the people put off as the accusers alone. The Greek has only autous, “them,” and the plural covers everyone Felix is facing at the hearing that 24:1 opened: the high priest, the elders, Tertullus and Paul. Which of them the verb is aimed at is not stated.

“Knowing more accurately the things concerning the Way” (Acts 24:22). Akribesteron eidōs is a comparative, and a comparative needs something to be more than. The adverb akribesteron stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 18:26, where Priscilla and Aquila “set out to him the way of God more accurately”; 23:15 and 23:20, where the Sanhedrin and the plotters want the tribune to bring Paul down “as though” they were going to determine or inquire into his case “more exactly”; and here. The first of these describes teaching, and the other two an inquiry still to be made, a thing not yet known. Here the knowledge is already Felix’s. More accurately than what is not said: more than the accusers have just told him, more than he knew before, or simply “quite accurately,” since a Greek comparative can carry that elative sense, as grammars note (reported here, not checked on this page). The participle eidōs, “knowing,” does not say whether this knowledge is the reason for delay or in spite of it. Where the knowledge came from, Luke does not say either. The shelf takes the comparative differently. ASV “more exact knowledge concerning the Way” and KJV “more perfect knowledge of that way” keep the comparative. NWT 1984 “knowing quite accurately” reads it as an elative, and NIV “well acquainted with the Way” does the same. Douay “most certain knowledge of this way” is a superlative, which is the Latin’s wording and is not checked here against the Vulgate. Geneva turns the participle into a promise: “When I shall more perfectly know the things which concern this way.” In that wording it is the arrival of Lysias that will supply the knowledge, so Felix has less of it than the Greek gives him; this is a different sentence, not a different shade of the same one. TLB “knew Christians didn’t go around starting riots” is a paraphrase that answers the charge of 24:5 inside the translation, which the Greek does not do; the Greek only says Felix knew things “concerning the Way.” “The Way” is the name of the movement at 9:2, 19:9, 19:23, 22:4 and 24:14 (see hodos); Tertullus had called its followers a “party” (24:5) and Paul had answered at 24:14, and Luke’s own sentence here says only “the Way.”

“When Lysias the tribune comes down” (Acts 24:22). The name Lysias stands in two verses of the New Testament: 23:26, where the tribune signs the letter “Claudius Lysias to the most excellent governor Felix” (see Claudius Lysias), and here. The two verses are one man; the letter gave Felix the tribune’s account at 23:27–30, including the rescue and the discovery that Paul was a Roman, and Felix now wants it in person. The verb is katabē, “comes down,” the same direction as the high priest’s trip at 24:1, and the one the Sanhedrin wanted Paul to make when it asked the tribune to “bring him down” at 23:15. This is a second condition after the first. At 23:35 Felix had said, “I will hear you out when your accusers also arrive.” They have now arrived and spoken, and a new person must come before anything is decided. Neither the name Lysias nor the singular chiliarchos, “tribune,” recurs in Acts after this verse: Lysias stands in no later verse, and the one later verse with chiliarch-, 25:23, has the plural, officers who enter the audience room with Agrippa and Berenice. So the book records no arrival. Whether the promise was meant, or was a way of closing the hearing without a ruling, the Greek does not decide; the chapter’s last verse (24:27) says only that two years were completed, that Festus succeeded Felix, and that Felix left Paul bound.

“I will decide your case” (Acts 24:22). Diagnōsomai is the future of diaginōskō, “to decide, to determine thoroughly” (dia- intensifies ginōskō, “know,” as lexicons give it; reported here, not examined on this page). The verb stands in two verses of the New Testament, diaginōskein at 23:15, where the Sanhedrin’s plan wears this word as its pretext, and diagnōsomai here; the noun diagnōsis stands in one verse, 25:21, where Paul appeals to be kept for the “decision” of the Emperor. The plotters used the word as a cover for an ambush, and Felix uses it of a hearing he does not hold. The phrase ta kath’ hymas is literally “the things according to you,” with a plural “you”: whether Paul is inside that plural or only the accusers is not settled by the grammar. The shelf renders the verb as NIV “decide your case,” Geneva “I will decide your matter,” NWT 1984 “I shall decide upon these matters,” and ASV “I will determine your matter.” KJV has “I will know the uttermost of your matter,” which reads dia- as thoroughness, and Douay has “I will hear you,” which is not a verb of deciding at all; its wording rests on the Latin and is not checked here.

“That Paul be kept, yet have some freedom” (Acts 24:23). Tēreisthai is the present passive infinitive of tēreō, “kept,” custody that goes on; and the verb the verse then pairs with it, echein anesin, “to have anesis,” sets the limit on it. The passive forms tēreisthai, etēreito, tērēthēnai stand in four verses of the New Testament: 12:5, Peter “kept in the prison”; here; and 25:4 and 25:21, where Festus answers that Paul is to be kept at Caesarea and then says he ordered him kept for the Emperor. At 23:35 the verb was a different one, phylassesthai, “to be guarded,” “under guard in Herod’s praetorium” (see praitōrion); this verse does not say whether the place changes, only the terms. Anesis, from the verb for loosening, is a word for relief or relaxation. The noun anesis stands in five verses of the New Testament: here; 2 Corinthians 2:13 and 7:5, where Paul says first that his spirit had no relief and then that, once in Macedonia, his flesh had none; 2 Corinthians 8:13, “relief” for others and affliction for the Corinthians (all three not yet on these pages); and 2 Thessalonians 1:7, “to give relief to you who are afflicted.” In the four other verses it is relief from pressure or burden; here the pressure is custody, and the freedom it buys is not defined. The shelf puts it differently. NIV “some freedom” and KJV “liberty” read it as a measure of movement; ASV “indulgence” and Geneva “have ease” as a favor or a comfort; Douay “should be easy” the same; NWT 1984 “some relaxation [of custody]” supplies in brackets what is being relaxed; and TLB “treat him gently” makes it a rule for the guards. On the first verb, TLB says “to prison” and NIV says “under guard”; the Greek says “kept,” and the verb at 12:5 and 25:4 shows it can mean custody, but neither a prison nor a guard is named in this verse.

“None of his own people” (Acts 24:23). Tōn idiōn is “of his own,” with the article making it a group: “his own people.” In Acts these two forms, idiōn and idious, stand in two verses: 4:23, where Peter and John, released, “went to their own people,” and here. The word does not say whether they are relatives, companions or fellow believers; it says only that they belong to him. The English shelf mostly turns it into friendship, a word the Greek does not use here. ASV “any of his friends,” Douay “any of his friends,” NIV “his friends,” and TLB “any of his friends” make four of the seven; KJV “his acquaintance” and Geneva “his acquaintance” make two; NWT 1984 “no one of his people” stays with the Greek. The word for friends, philoi, is what Luke uses at 27:3, when the centurion Julius lets Paul go to “the friends” at Sidon and receive care (see philos). What these people may do is hypēretein, “to serve.” The verb stands in three verses of the New Testament: hypēretein here; 13:36, David “having served in his own generation the counsel of God”; and 20:34, where Paul says to the Ephesian elders that “these hands ministered to my needs and to those who were with me.” The man whose hands served the needs of others at Miletus now has others serve his; the noun hypēretēs (see hypēretēs) is a different part of the family. NWT 1984 “wait upon him” and KJV “minister” keep a servant’s sense; TLB “visiting him or bringing him gifts” narrows it to two named acts. Later, at 28:16, Paul in Rome is allowed “to stay by himself” with a soldier guarding him, and the shape of the arrangement there is the same: kept, yet with room around him.

Where the manuscripts differ (Acts 24:22–23). The apparatus shows five readings, and none of them changes the sense of the two verses. At 24:22 the clause printed here, Anebaleto de autous ho Phēlix, is read by the Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 editions; the Robinson–Pierpont text instead has Akousas de tauta ho Phēlix anebaleto autous, “But Felix, having heard these things, put them off.” KJV “And when Felix heard these things” and Geneva “Now when Felix heard these things” print the longer opening and the other five shelf versions do not. A second variant at 24:22 is eipas against eipōn, two forms of the same participle “saying.” At 24:23 the Robinson–Pierpont text adds te, “and,” to the first participle; has ton Paulon, “Paul,” where the printed text has auton, “him”; and adds ē proserchesthai, “or to come to him,” after “serve.” KJV “to keep Paul” and Geneva “to keep Paul” name Paul, and KJV “minister or come unto him” carries the added words, and so does Geneva “or to come unto him.” The verse above supplies “Paul” on its own, as its English needs a subject for “be kept,” and the added “or to come to him” would widen what the visitors may do from serving to arriving. The text this page prints is the shorter one.

Verses 24–26 · Δρουσίλλῃ τῇ ἰδίᾳ γυναικὶ οὔσῃ Ἰουδαίᾳ … δικαιοσύνης καὶ ἐγκρατείας καὶ τοῦ κρίματος τοῦ μέλλοντος … ἔμφοβος γενόμενος … Τὸ νῦν ἔχον πορεύου, καιρὸν δὲ μεταλαβών … χρήματα … πυκνότερον Drousillē tē idia gynaikí ousē Ioudaia … dikaiosynēs kai enkrateias kai tou krimatos tou mellontos … emphobos genomenos … To nyn echon poreuou, kairon de metalabōn … chrēmata … pyknoteron — Acts 24:24–26: why is Felix “terrified” when Paul talks about righteousness, self-control and the judgment to come, who is Drusilla, and is the “money” he hopes for a bribe?

“Drusilla his own wife, who was a Jewish woman” (Acts 24:24). The name Drousilla (Drusilla) stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and Luke says nothing about her except that she is Felix’s wife and Jewish. Felix has been in the story since 23:24. The Greek is Drousillē tē idia gynaiki ousē Ioudaia, literally “Drusilla, the own wife, being Jewish.” The adjective is the feminine of Ioudaios, and it has the same form as the name of the land, Ioudaia, “Judea” (9:31), so the sentence, where ousē, “being,” agrees with “wife,” is what shows it is an adjective and not the country. At 16:1 the genitive gynaikos Ioudaias is printed “a believing Jewish woman,” Timothy’s mother, beside a Greek father; here the same feminine adjective is printed “Jewish woman” too. The shelf splits on the word. NWT 1984 and ASV “who was a Jewess,” KJV and Geneva “which was a Jewess,” and TLB “a Jewess” print it (five of the seven); NIV “who was Jewish” uses the adjective, and Douay “who was a Jew” uses the masculine noun for a woman. The Greek idia, “own,” is absent from the Robinson–Pierpont text (apparatus 929: it is printed by Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and omitted by RP). Six of the seven shelf versions print plain “his wife”; only TLB “his legal wife” renders the word, and it renders it as a legal status, which the Greek does not say. The same word idios returns at 24:23, tōn idiōn autou, “his own people.”

Drusilla in Josephus (Acts 24:24). What else is known of Drusilla comes from Josephus, whose Greek is read here, not from Luke. In the Antiquities (18.132 in the Perseus numbering) she is one of three daughters born to Agrippa and Cypros; at 19.354 she is six years old when her father dies, already promised to Epiphanes, son of Antiochus of Commagene. That father is the king of Acts 12:1, Herod Agrippa I, on the identification the library entry reports. At 20.139 her brother Agrippa gives “Drusilla his sister” in marriage to Azizus, king of the Emesenes, “who was willing to be circumcised” (peritemnesthai thelēsanti), because Epiphanes had refused to take on the Jewish customs; at 20.141 that marriage is dissolved “after no long time.” At 20.142 Felix, who “was procurator of Judea” (epetropeue), saw her, for she “surpassed all in beauty,” and conceived a desire for her; he sent a friend of his named Atomus, a Jew and a Cypriot by birth, “pretending to be a magician,” to persuade her to leave her husband and marry him. At 20.143 she is persuaded “to transgress the ancestral customs” (parabēnai ta patria nomima) and marry Felix, and at 20.144 she bears him a son named Agrippa. Josephus’s adjective patria is from the same family as Luke’s patrōos, the “ancestral” word of 22:3 and the “of my fathers” of 24:14. Luke draws no line from this story to the scene he tells. Suetonius, Claudius 28.1, read here in the Latin, names among the freedmen Claudius esteemed a Felicem, whom he set over “the cohorts and cavalry wings and the province of Judea,” and calls him trium reginarum maritum, “the husband of three queens.” Suetonius gives no second name and names none of the queens; that this Felix is Luke’s Felix, and Drusilla one of the three, is the reference works’ identification, reported here and not decided.

“Faith in Christ Jesus” (Acts 24:24). Felix “sent for Paul and heard him concerning faith in Christ Jesus”: ēkousen autou peri tēs eis Christon Iēsoun pisteōs. The sequence tēs eis Christon … pisteōs, “the faith in Christ,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Colossians 2:5 (not yet on these pages; Colossians 1 is). At 20:21 Paul’s own summary of his preaching is “repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus,” so faith in Jesus is already how Paul sums up his preaching. The apparatus (930) marks one reading: Iēsoun, “Jesus,” is printed by Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 and omitted by Tregelles and RP. The shelf follows both. KJV and Geneva “the faith in Christ” have no “Jesus”; ASV “the faith in Christ Jesus,” NWT 1984 “the belief in Christ Jesus,” NIV and TLB “faith in Christ Jesus” and Douay “the faith, that is in Christ Jesus” keep it (five of the seven). NWT 1984 prints “belief” for pistis, where this translation, like the other six, prints “faith.” The verb is a single one: Felix “sent for” (metepempsato) and “heard,” and the subject of both is Felix. TLB “they listened” makes the pair, Felix and Drusilla, the hearers, which the Greek does not say; whether she was in the room, Luke does not report. He also does not say what Paul said about faith in Christ. Felix already knew “more accurately the things concerning the Way” (24:22), and Luke gives the hearing as about the Way’s own faith, not about the charges.

“Righteousness and self-control and the judgment to come” (Acts 24:25). The three topics are joined by “and,” and the Greek does not say how they arose from the talk of faith. Dikaiosynē, “righteousness” (dictionary), stands in four verses of Acts, 10:35, 13:10, 17:31 and here, in the stem dikaiosyn-. Two of the others have a Roman official or officer as a listener. At 10:35 Peter speaks in the house of the centurion Cornelius, and at 13:7 the proconsul Sergius Paulus has summoned Barnabas and Saul, and Paul calls the magician at his side “enemy of all righteousness” (13:10). At 17:31 the Athenians hear that God “is going to judge the inhabited world in righteousness” by a man he appointed; there the verb is mellei krinein and here the noun is to krima to mellon, “the judgment, the coming one.” The noun krima stands in one verse of Acts, this one. The apparatus (931) notes that RP adds esesthai, “to be,” after mellontos. Neither the Greek nor the verse names who judges. Peter has said Jesus is “the one determined by God as judge of living and dead” (10:42), and Paul has told this same court of a coming “resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous” (24:15) and said he is judged “concerning resurrection of the dead” (24:21); whether the “judgment to come” here is that judgment, spoken of to a governor, is for the reader to connect, not something Luke says. Enkrateia, “self-control” (dictionary), stands in three verses of the New Testament, enkrateia here, in Galatians 5:23 (not yet on these pages), and at 2 Peter 1:6, “and in knowledge self-control.” Its relatives are the adjective in Titus 1:8, “self-controlled,” and the verb at 1 Corinthians 7:9 and 9:25 (neither yet on these pages). This is the only verse of Luke–Acts with the stem. Josephus, read in the first paragraph, tells how Felix won Drusilla (Antiquities 20.142–143); Luke does not connect that to the topic, and the Greek says only that these were the subjects. The verb for the speaking is dialegomenou, from the verb that 24:12 has as dialegomenon; both verses print “reasoning.” The shelf names the three things differently. For the first, six of the seven print “righteousness” (NWT 1984 “righteousness and self-control and the judgment to come”); Douay “justice, and chastity” prints “justice” for dikaiosynē and “chastity” for enkrateia, the one version to narrow the second word to one sphere of conduct. KJV and Geneva “temperance” make it moderation, and ASV, NIV and TLB join NWT 1984 in “self-control” (four of the seven). For the verb: KJV and ASV “reasoned,” TLB “reasoned with them,” NWT 1984 and NIV “talked,” Geneva “disputed” and Douay “treated of,” so that “disputed” makes it an argument and “treated of” a lecture; neither is in dialegomai alone.

“Terrified” (Acts 24:25). Emphobos genomenos is “having become in fear”: emphobos is the adjective “fearful,” and the verb is the ordinary “become.” The adjective stands in five verses of the New Testament, emphobos here, at Luke 24:5 and 24:37, at Acts 10:4 and at Revelation 11:13. At Luke 24:5 and 24:37 (the plural) this translation prints “frightened”; at Acts 10:4 it prints “terrified” and at Revelation 11:13 “terrified” too. The exact phrase emphobos genomenos, in the singular, stands in two verses: here and 10:4. There Cornelius, a centurion in the same city of Caesarea (10:24), gazes at the angel, becomes emphobos and asks “What is it, Lord?”; here a Roman governor becomes emphobos and sends the speaker away. Luke says that the fear came; he does not say why, or whether Drusilla shared it. The shelf has three English readings of the same Greek word. ASV and TLB “was terrified” and Douay “being terrified” match this translation; NWT 1984 “became frightened” and NIV “was afraid” are milder; KJV and Geneva “trembled” name a bodily reaction, where the Greek names the state.

“For the present, go” (Acts 24:25). To nyn echon poreuou is literally “the now having, go,” an idiom for “as things now stand.” The string to nyn echon stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The imperative poreuou is the verb the Lord uses to Paul in 22:10, “Rise, go on into Damascus,” and again at 22:21, “Go, because I will send you out far away to the nations”; here the one told to go is Paul, and the speaker is a governor. The shelf has the same dismissal in two shapes. NWT 1984 “For the present go your way” and Douay “For this time, go thy way” keep the two parts, and KJV, ASV and Geneva “Go thy way for this time” do the same, so that five of the seven print “way,” which is not in the Greek; TLB “Go away for now” is further from it, and NIV “That’s enough for now” adds a curtness the Greek does not have. The second clause is kairon de metalabōn metakalesomai se, “and having got an opportunity, I will call you over.” Kairos is the fitting moment (dictionary), and metalabōn is the participle of metalambanō, “take a share of,” which stands in seven verses of the New Testament (Acts 2:46, here, Acts 27:33 and 27:34, 2 Timothy 2:6, Hebrews 6:7 and Hebrews 12:10). In the other six the thing shared is in the genitive: food at 2:46, 27:33 and 27:34, “the fruits,” “blessing” and “his holiness”; here kairon is in the accusative, and the sense is “get.” Josephus has the verb with the accusative too, of Epiphanes, who gave up the marriage rather than “take on” the Jewish customs (ta Ioudaiōn ethē metalabein, Antiquities 20.139). The verb in the main clause, metakaleō, “call over,” stands in four verses, all in Acts: 7:14 (Joseph summoning Jacob), 10:32 (“call for Simon”), 20:17 (Paul “called over the elders”) and here, the future middle. On kairos the shelf prints “convenient”: KJV and ASV “a convenient season,” Geneva “convenient time,” Douay “a convenient time,” TLB “a more convenient time” and NIV “When I find it convenient” (six of the seven), which makes the reason Felix’s comfort; NWT 1984 “an opportune time” is the one version to keep the fitting-moment sense. Two versions add “again,” which has no Greek behind it: NWT 1984 “send for you again” and TLB “call for you again.” Luke does not say whether the “opportunity” ever came: the next verse says only that he sent for Paul often, and 24:27 ends with Paul still bound.

“Money” and “hoping” (Acts 24:26). Hama kai elpizōn hoti chrēmata dothēsetai autō hypo tou Paulou: “at the same time also hoping that money will be given him by Paul.” The plural chrēmata (dictionary), the spellings chrēmata, chrēmatōn, chrēmasin, stands in five verses of the New Testament: Mark 10:23 and Luke 18:24, “those who have wealth”; Acts 8:18, where Simon “offered them money” for the laying on of hands, and 8:20, “through money”; and here. In these verses the plural is used of wealth and money, and none of them uses a word for a bribe. Luke’s sentence has the passive “will be given” with “by Paul,” and does not say what the money would be for. The shelf splits there. NIV “would offer him a bribe” and TLB “would bribe him” print a bribe (two of the seven); NWT 1984 “hoping for money,” ASV “money would be given him of Paul” and Douay “money should be given him by Paul” keep the plain word. KJV and Geneva “that he might loose him” carry a purpose clause, Greek hopōs lysē auton, “so that he might release him”; the apparatus (932) lists it as a reading of RP, and the Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 texts do not have it. It makes the money a payment for release; without it, the sentence says only that he hoped for money. Luke does not say why Felix thought Paul might have it. The same chapter has Paul speaking of “alms” for his nation (24:17) and of friends who may serve him (24:23), and Luke makes no link between these and the hope. Elpizōn shares its stem with the hope Paul states at 24:15, “having a hope in God”; the dictionary entry on hope says the Greek word is neutral expectation, and the word is the same in both verses though the hopes are not. The verb in the second half of the sentence, pyknoteron, is the comparative of pyknos, “frequent,” a stem in three verses of the New Testament: Luke 5:33, “John’s disciples fast often” (5:33), 1 Timothy 5:23 (not yet on these pages) and here. Felix “kept sending for him more often” (pyknoteron metapempomenos, present participle) “and conversing with him” (hōmilei, imperfect). The verb homileō stands in four verses: here, Luke 24:14 and 24:15 (the two walking to Emmaus “talking with each other,” 24:14) and Acts 20:11, where Paul “talked for a considerable time until daybreak.” It is talk in company, with no accusation in it. The comparative is the shelf’s test: KJV, ASV and Geneva “the oftener” and NWT 1984 “even more frequently” keep it; NIV “frequently,” Douay “oftentimes” and TLB “from time to time” do not, so that three of the seven drop the comparative. “More often” than what, Luke does not say, whether than before the hearing or than the custody of 24:23 had allowed. Festus will later tell the king of a man “left” by Felix, “a prisoner” (25:14), and the chapter’s last verse, 24:27, says Felix left Paul bound.

Verse 27 · Διετίας … διάδοχον … Πόρκιον Φῆστον … χάριτα καταθέσθαι … δεδεμένον Dietias … diadochon … Porkion Phēston … charita katathesthai … dedemenon — Felix and Festus in Acts 24:27: why did Felix leave Paul bound after two years, and what does it mean to “lay up a favor” with the Jews?

“When two years had been completed” (Acts 24:27). The verse opens with a genitive absolute, Dietias de plērōtheisēs, literally “a two-year span having been filled.” The noun dietia stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 28:30, where the Greek reads dietian holēn, “a whole two years.” Time is “filled” with the same verb at 7:30, “when forty years were completed” (plērōthentōn etōn tesserakonta). The verse does not say what the two years are counted from. The nearest moments in the story are Paul’s arrival in Caesarea (23:33) and Felix’s order that he be kept (24:23), and the Greek goes no further. The calendar years in which the two fell, and so the date of Felix’s replacement, are disputed among historians; that is reported here, not decided, and neither this verse nor the sentences of Josephus quoted below names a year. The shelf mostly reads the participle as a simple lapse of time: NWT 1984 “when two years had elapsed,” NIV “When two years had passed,” Geneva “When two years were expired” and Douay “when two years were ended.” ASV “when two years were fulfilled” is the one that keeps the “filled” of the verb. KJV “after two years” drops the participle for a plain preposition, and TLB “Two years went by in this way” adds a phrase, “in this way,” that the Greek does not have.

“Felix received as successor Porcius Festus” (Acts 24:27). Elaben diadochon ho Phēlix Porkion Phēston is literally “Felix took a successor, Porcius Festus.” The noun diadochos stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The verb from the same root, diadexamenoi, “having received in turn,” also stands in one verse, 7:45, where Stephen says the fathers, “having received it in turn,” brought it in with Joshua. Both words are about something handed down from one holder to the next. In the Greek the grammatical subject is Felix, and the verb is active. The shelf mostly turns that around. NWT 1984 “was succeeded by Porcius Festus,” ASV “was succeeded by Porcius Festus,” NIV “was succeeded by Porcius Festus” and TLB “then Felix was succeeded by Porcius Festus” make Felix the one acted upon, four of the seven; KJV “Porcius Festus came into Felix’ room” and Geneva “came into Felix’s room” make Festus the subject, two of the seven; Douay “Felix had for successor Portius Festus” is the one of the seven that keeps Felix as the subject, as the Greek does. The name Phēstos stands in thirteen verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: here, then 25:1, 25:4, 25:9, 25:12, 25:13, 25:14, 25:22, 25:23, 25:24, and 26:24, 26:25 and 26:32. This is its first appearance. The Greek Porkion is the Latin family name Porcius in Greek letters; that Roman naming, family name first and then the surname, is reported here, not checked on this page. Josephus gives the same pair of names with the same noun for the office passed on. In the Antiquities (20.182) he writes Porkiou de Phēstou diadochou Phēliki pemphthentos hypo Nerōnos, “Porcius Festus having been sent by Nero as successor to Felix,” and goes on to say that the leading Jews of Caesarea went up to Rome to accuse Felix, and that he would not have escaped punishment had Nero not yielded to the pleas of Felix’s brother Pallas. In the Jewish War (2.270–271) the verb is the one of Stephen’s sentence, diadexamenos, and the office is epitropē, “procuratorship”: Felix has sent leading men from both sides of a dispute to Nero, and Festus, taking over the procuratorship from him, goes after the robbers. Luke’s word for Felix’s office is hēgemōn, “governor” (23:26). Luke does not mention the Caesarean complaint at Rome, and the sentences of Josephus quoted here do not mention Paul; the two accounts are set side by side, not joined. See also Festus, Felix and diadochos; Caesarea, where all this happens, is in the library.

“Lay up a favor” (Acts 24:27). Thelōn te charita katathesthai tois Ioudaiois: the participle is “wishing,” the verb is the middle of katatithēmi, “to put down for oneself,” and the object is charis, “favor.” Lexicons treat the whole phrase as an idiom for doing someone a kindness so as to have a claim on his goodwill, as one lays up money with a banker; that is reported here, not checked on this page, and “lay up” keeps the picture of a deposit (see charin katatithemi). The infinitive katathesthai stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and 25:9, where the Greek says of Festus the very same thing, thelōn tois Ioudaiois charin katathesthai, “wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews.” Luke gives both governors the same motive in nearly the same words. A ruler who weighs the approval of the Jews is not new in Acts. Herod, Herod Agrippa I, “seeing that it was pleasing to the Jews” (areston estin tois Ioudaiois), “proceeded to arrest Peter also” (12:3), and Peter was then “bound with two chains” (12:6). The verse before this one gave Felix a different reason for keeping Paul near him, the hope of money (24:26). Neither reason is a finding about Paul. The shelf reads the favor in two directions. KJV “willing to shew the Jews a pleasure,” Douay “willing to shew the Jews a pleasure” and NIV “wanted to grant a favor to the Jews” keep the direction of the Greek dative, a favor done to the Jews, three of the seven; “pleasure” there is the older English for a kindness. NWT 1984 “desired to gain favor with the Jews,” ASV “desiring to gain favor with the Jews,” TLB “wanted to gain favor with the Jews” and Geneva “willing to get favor of the Jews” name what Felix was to get, four of the seven, which is the point of the deposit but not what the verb says he did. The apparatus has one note on the verse (933): charita, singular, is the reading of Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 as well as the printed text; Robinson–Pierpont reads charitas, plural, “favors.” The difference is one of number, and the seven shelf versions print the singular. The form charita stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Jude 4 (“the grace of our God”); 25:9 has the form charin in the same idiom.

“Left Paul bound” (Acts 24:27). Katelipe ton Paulon dedemenon. The verb is the aorist of kataleipō, “leave behind,” and Festus will use its perfect participle of Paul when he tells King Agrippa, anēr tis estin kataleleimmenos hypo Phēlikos desmios, “There is a certain man left a prisoner by Felix” (25:14; see desmios). Dedemenon is a perfect passive participle, “having been bound,” a state, and it names no chain, cord or cell. The form with this stem, dedemen-, stands in six verses of Acts, 9:2, 9:21, 12:6, 20:22, 22:5 and here. Three of those six are Saul’s errand to Damascus told three ways, to bring believers “bound” to Jerusalem (9:2, 9:21, 22:5), the last in Paul’s own mouth; one is Peter in Herod’s prison (12:6); and one is Paul’s “bound in the spirit” on the way to Jerusalem (20:22). Luke does not draw the line from the first group to the last verse, and the verse does not say Paul had that errand in mind. Earlier verses about this prisoner use other words. Lysias’s letter, which Felix read (23:34), says Lysias rescued Paul “having learned that he is a Roman” (23:27) and found no accusation “worthy of death or bonds” (23:29), and the tribune at 22:29 was afraid “that he had bound him.” The same Felix had ordered Paul kept in Herod’s praetorium and, at 24:23, kept yet with some anesis, “freedom.” What the text does not do is reconcile that “freedom” with this “bound”: it does not say whether the bonds are literal, whether the freedom of 24:23 has ended, or whether “bound” sums up the whole custody. The shelf: NWT 1984 “left Paul bound,” KJV “left Paul bound,” Geneva “left Paul bound” and Douay “left Paul bound” keep the participle, four of the seven. ASV “left Paul in bonds” turns it into a noun, and NIV “left Paul in prison” and TLB “left Paul in chains” supply a place or a means that the Greek does not name. NWT 2013 is a different witness and reads “in custody.”

What the chapter leaves open (Acts 24:27). The Greek of the chapter ends on dedemenon, “bound,” and on no verdict. Felix had said at 24:22, “When Lysias the tribune comes down, I will decide your case,” and the name Lysias stands in two verses of the New Testament, 23:26 and 24:22. The verse does not say that Lysias came, that Felix decided, or that Paul said anything. The chapter began with Ananias coming down from Jerusalem to accuse (24:1) and ends with the accused held by a governor who is leaving. Josephus says the Jews of Caesarea went up to Rome to accuse Felix; Luke says Felix wished to lay up a favor with the Jews. Neither says what Felix made of Paul. As far as the book says, the next favor asked is asked of the man who succeeds him: at 25:3 the chief priests ask Festus for a charis against Paul, at 25:9 Festus is described with the same idiom as Felix, and at 25:11 Paul answers with a related verb, charisasthai. Paul’s stay of dietian holēn, “a whole two years,” at 28:30 is a second span of the same length, and the verse does not link the two.
