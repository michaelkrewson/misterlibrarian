The Gospel opens on a genealogy — Abraham to David to the exile to the Christ, forty-two generations in three panels of fourteen, with four irregular women written into the line — and then the birth: Mary found with child of the Holy Spirit, Joseph stopped in a dream, the naming of Jesus ('YHWH saves'), and Isaiah's virgin-sign landed as Emmanuel, 'God with us.'
The genealogy spans roughly two thousand years, from Abraham (c. 2000 BC on the traditional reckoning) to the birth of Jesus — which Matthew places in the last years of Herod the Great (who died 4 BC), so c. 6–4 BC. ⚠ The date sits a few years 'before Christ' because the sixth-century monk who set the AD count miscalculated Herod's reign. Matthew's infancy narrative is told from Joseph's side; Luke's, independently, from Mary's.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–6 · the book of a new genesis, and four women in the line

“The book of the GENESIS.” The very first words of the New Testament, biblos genēseōs, deliberately echo the Greek Old Testament: “the book of the genesis of heaven and earth” (Genesis 2:4) and “the book of the genesis of Adam” (Genesis 5:1). Matthew opens his Gospel with the word that opens the Bible — a new beginning, a new creation reckoned from Abraham. And the same word returns at verse 18, “the genesis of Jesus Christ,” framing the whole chapter: the genealogy and the birth are one genesis.

“Son of David, son of Abraham.” The two names the genealogy is built to reach: David the king (the royal claim, sounded first) and Abraham the father of the nation (the promise that “in you all the families of the earth will be blessed”). “Christ” here is a title, not a surname — Christos, “anointed,” the Greek for the Hebrew Messiah — and verse 16 will underline it: “Jesus, who is called Christ.”

⚠ Four WOMEN break into a list of fathers — and Matthew chose them. Ancient genealogies name men; this one stops to name Tamar (v3), Rahab and Ruth (v5), and “the wife of Uriah” (v6, Bathsheba, pointedly named by her husband instead). ⚠ The four have long been read together, and the pattern argued: three are Gentiles or connected to Gentiles (a Canaanite, a Canaanite of Jericho, a Moabite), and every one is touched by scandal or irregularity — Tamar plays the harlot (Genesis 38), Rahab is one, Ruth comes by night to Boaz’s threshing floor (Ruth), Bathsheba is taken in adultery. The likeliest reading: Matthew is preparing for a fifth irregular woman, Mary, and announcing from the first page a Messiah whose line runs through outsiders and the compromised — grace, not pedigree.

Verses 7–11 · the psalmist, the prophet, and the missing kings

⚠ “Abijah fathered ASAPH… Manasseh fathered AMOS.” The oldest manuscripts read the names of the psalmist Asaph (not the king Asa) and the prophet Amos (not the king Amon) — and this translation prints the critical text. It may be an early scribal slip, or Matthew’s own spelling; either way the difference is real and the versions divide. KJV and most read “Asa” and “Amon,” the kings the history books give; NIV keeps “Asa” but many modern editions of the Greek follow the harder “Asaph”/“Amos” the earliest witnesses carry. The library prints what the manuscripts say and flags the knot.

⚠ Three kings are quietly dropped. Between Joram and Uzziah (v8), Matthew skips three generations — Ahaziah, Joash and Amaziah, all in the record of Kings and Chronicles. ⚠ The omission is almost certainly deliberate: it keeps the middle set to fourteen (see the next note), and it may prune the branch cursed for Athaliah’s Baal-worship. A biblical “X fathered Y” never guaranteed an unbroken father-son chain; it could leap generations, and Matthew uses that freedom to shape a pattern.

“The DEPORTATION to Babylon.” Metoikesia — a change of dwelling, a removal — is the hinge of the whole genealogy, named four times (vv11, 12, 17). The exile is the low point from which the count to the Messiah begins its climb. KJV “they were carried away to Babylon”; NWT “the deportation to Babylon.”

Verses 12–17 · the cursed king, and the fourteens

⚠ The line runs through a man told he would be “childless.” Jechoniah (Coniah) is the king of whom Jeremiah said, “Write this man down as childless… none of his offspring will succeed in sitting on the throne of David” (Jeremiah 22:30). ⚠ Matthew runs the royal line straight through him — a difficulty ancient readers felt keenly. The usual answers: the curse barred his sons from the throne, not from the line; or Jesus’ legal claim comes through Joseph’s Jechoniah-descent while his physical descent (in Luke’s different genealogy) bypasses it. The genealogy carries the tension rather than smoothing it.

⚠ “FOURTEEN generations” — three times, and the sum does not quite add up. Matthew announces the scheme openly (v17): Abraham→David, David→exile, exile→Christ, fourteen each. But the last set lists only thirteen new names — the count works only if David or Jechoniah is reckoned twice (each stands at a seam), or if a name has dropped out. ⚠ Why fourteen? The likeliest reason is gematria: the Hebrew letters of DAVID (D-W-D, 4+6+4) total fourteen, so the king’s name is written into the very architecture of the list — the son of David whose name is counted in every panel. It is a designed genealogy, not a registry.

“Of whom was BORN Jesus.” The drumbeat “fathered… fathered…” (thirty-nine times, egennēsen, active) breaks at the last link: Joseph does not “father” Jesus — Jesus “was born” (egennēthē, passive), “of whom” being feminine, of Mary. The grammar itself steps around Joseph and points to the birth the next paragraph will tell. ⚠ A few witnesses (the Sinaitic Syriac most famously) read “Joseph… fathered Jesus,” but the great weight of manuscripts has the passive, and even that Syriac calls Mary a virgin three verses on. KJV “of whom was born Jesus.”

Verses 18–21 · a righteous man’s mercy, and a name that is a sentence

“Betrothed… before they came together.” Jewish betrothal (eras) was legally binding — a broken engagement needed a certificate of divorce — though the couple did not yet live together. So Mary is “found to be with child” in the gap, and Joseph faces what looks like adultery. That he is called “righteous” (dikaios) and unwilling to disgrace her is the point: his righteousness expresses itself as mercy, resolving to “divorce her quietly” rather than expose her to the law’s full, public penalty.

⚠ “You shall call his name JESUS, for he will SAVE.” The name is a small sermon. Iēsous is the Greek for Yeshua / Yehoshua (Joshua), which means “YHWH saves” — and the angel spells the etymology out: “for he will save (sōsei) his people from their sins.” ⚠ Note what he saves from: not from Rome, not from exile, but “from their sins” — the Gospel’s first redefinition of what a Messiah is for. It is the same name as Israel’s old conqueror Joshua, now given to one who conquers not land but sin.

Verses 22–23 · the virgin, and God with us

⚠ “The VIRGIN shall be with child” — the most argued word in the nativity. Matthew quotes Isaiah 7:14 in its Greek form, where parthenos means “virgin.” ⚠ But Isaiah’s Hebrew reads almah — a young woman of marriageable age, presumed unmarried, without the technical focus on virginity that the Hebrew betulah carries. The Greek Old Testament had already rendered almah as parthenos, and Matthew builds on that reading. The debate is old and real: did Isaiah predict a virgin birth, or a young woman’s child as a sign to King Ahaz, which Matthew reads as fulfilled at a deeper level in Mary? KJV, NIV and NWT all read “virgin” here in Matthew (the Greek is unambiguous); the argument lives back in Isaiah’s Hebrew, which the library will meet when it reaches the prophet.

“They shall call his name EMMANUEL… God with us.” Emmanouēl — Hebrew ‘immanu ’el, “with us (is) God” — and Matthew translates it for his readers, as he rarely does, because it states his whole Gospel in a word. ⚠ It is an inclusio: the book opens on “God with us” and closes on Jesus’ last promise, “I am with you always, to the end of the age” (28:20). Everything between is bracketed by that presence. It is a name he is called (his identity), not the name he is given (Jesus) — the two names say the one thing from two sides: God saves, God is with us.

Verses 24–25 · obedience, and a much-read “until”

Joseph says nothing in the whole Gospel. He only obeys: he “did as the angel of the Lord had commanded him.” The silent, dutiful adoptive father is drawn entirely in acts — taking his wife, naming the child — and it is the naming that matters, for by naming him Joseph legally makes Jesus a son of David, which is the whole point of the genealogy that stands over him.

⚠ “He did not know her UNTIL she had borne a son.” One of the New Testament’s quietly contested verses. Heōs hou, “until,” naturally implies a change after the point named — the plain reading is that normal marriage followed the birth, which fits Matthew’s repeated mention of Jesus’ “brothers” (12:46; 13:55). ⚠ Those who hold Mary’s perpetual virginity answer that “until” need not imply a change (as “till” does not in some idioms), and the verse simply affirms the virginal conception without commenting on afterward. The Greek leans toward the first; the doctrine turns elsewhere. A large part of the later manuscript tradition also expands the ending to “her firstborn son” (borrowed from Luke 2:7); the earliest witnesses read simply “a son.” KJV “her firstborn son”; NIV “a son.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “the book of the genealogy” for biblos genēseōs, with the genesis-echo of the Bible’s opening named and heard again at v18; “fathered” for the thirty-nine-fold egennēsen, and the deliberate switch to the passive “was born” at the last link; “the wife of Uriah” kept as Matthew wrote it, Bathsheba named by her husband; the critical text’s “Asaph” and “Amos” printed with the traditional “Asa”/“Amon” flagged; “deportation” for metoikesia; “Jesus” with its meaning “YHWH saves” unfolded; “virgin” for parthenos, the Hebrew almah debate deferred to Isaiah; and “Emmanuel — God with us,” translated as Matthew translates it.

Echoes paid. The “book of the genesis” of Genesis 2:4 and Genesis 5:1; Tamar of Genesis 38 and Ruth of the book that bears her name (Ruth); the curse on Jechoniah at Jeremiah 22:30; and the “firstborn son” that the later text borrows from Luke 2:7.

Echoes planted. “God with us,” the inclusio that will close the Gospel at 28:20, “I am with you always”; the “brothers” of Jesus who surface at 12:46 and 13:55; the dreams that keep guiding Joseph (2:13, 2:19, 2:22), a second Joseph the dreamer; and the Gentiles in the line that anticipate the Gospel’s last command, to “make disciples of all nations.”

Matthew is new to the library — the Gospel the early church placed first, the bridge from the Old Testament to the New, opening on a genealogy that carries the whole story of Israel down to a birth. Next in this book: chapter 2 — the magi from the east and the star; Herod’s fear and the slaughter of the innocents; the flight into Egypt; and “out of Egypt I called my son.” (The Sermon on the Mount, chapters 5–7, is already in the library.)
