The same letter, still from Ephesus. Paul gives the apostles their office: under-rowers and stewards, judged by faithfulness rather than by the verdict of a court. Timothy is on his way to remind the Corinthians of his ways (4:17), as Acts 19:22 has him sent ahead into Macedonia.
c. AD 54–55, the same letter and date as chapters 1 to 3, written from Ephesus. Acts 19:22 has Paul send Timothy and Erastus into Macedonia; this chapter has him on the road to Corinth, in 4:17 and again in 16:10.

¡Ya están saciados! ¡Ya son ricos! ¡Han empezado a reinar sin nosotros! ¡Y ojalá de verdad hubieran empezado a reinar, para que también nosotros reináramos con ustedes!

No les escribo esto para avergonzarlos, sino para amonestarlos como a mis hijos amados.

Verses 1–4 · under-rowers, stewards, and the one who judges

“Christ’s under-rowers.” Hypērētas is a subordinate, built from hypo, under, and eretēs, a rower: the man who works below deck and is not the captain. Paul gives himself and Apollos that word for their office, the way Luke calls the eyewitnesses who handed on the word hypērētai (Luke 1:2). The KJV has “ministers of Christ,” and the ASV agrees with it. The NWT 1984 has “subordinates of Christ.” This translation keeps the picture of the rower, because the picture carries the point: the rank is low and the work is real.

“Stewards of God’s mysteries.” Oikonómous, stewards, are the managers of a household who keep the stores and the accounts for a master, the figure of Luke 16:1–8. The steward’s office is to hand out what belongs to the master. mystēriōn, mysteries, is the same word as in 2:7, what God has disclosed and not what he hides. The NWT 1984 has “sacred secrets of God,” the KJV “mysteries of God”; the translation keeps “mysteries.”

“What is looked for in stewards.” WH, Treg and NA28 print hōde, “here, in this case,” and the Received Text reads ho de, “but what,” so the apparatus gives the opening word of the verse two readings with the same sense. The test of a steward is not the size of the store but whether he is pistos, faithful, when the master comes back to check. The NWT 1984 has “what is looked for in stewards is for a man to be found faithful,” and the parable of the servant found at work when the master returns uses the same test (Matthew 24:45–46).

“A very small thing to me.” Eis elachiston, a very small matter, is the phrase Paul uses for what counts least with him. The verb anakrinō, to examine, is the verb of a hearing, and hēmera, day, is the word for the day a court was set to sit. That is why the translation says “court day”: the reader sees a tribunal, not an unexplained “human day.” The NIV has “any human court,” the KJV “man’s judgment,” and the Douay “man’s day,” which is the closest to the Greek.

“I am not aware of anything against me.” Oudèn gar emautō súnoida says that Paul has a clear conscience, and the verse refuses to let that settle the case: dedikaiōmai, declared righteous, is a verdict that belongs to the one who judges. The one who examines him is kyrios, the Lord. The NWT 1984 has “he that examines me is Jehovah,” and the ASV “he that judgeth me is the Lord.” The next verse makes the Lord the one who comes to judge (“until the Lord comes”), which is Christ, so this translation keeps “Lord” and says here that the NWT’s rendering is a choice the Greek does not force on this sentence.

Verses 5–7 · judge nothing before the time, and the rule about what is written

“Judge nothing before the time.” Pro kairō, before the proper time, is a phrase about timing, and the chapter puts the judging off to the coming of the Lord. That Lord phōtisei ta krypta tou skotous, will bring to light the hidden things of darkness, the same disclosure the day of judgment makes in Romans 2:16. The Douay and the KJV both read “bring to light the hidden things of darkness,” and the NIV “each will receive their praise from God” for the last clause.

“Not beyond what is written.” The rule is given without a citation: Paul does not name a verse. The editions disagree on two small points. WH, Treg and NA28 read ha where the Received Text reads ho, a difference of gender that does not change the sense, and the Received Text adds phronein, “to think,” so that the rule reads “not beyond what is written [to think].” The NWT 1984 has “Do not go beyond the things that are written,” and the NIV “Do not go beyond what is written.”

“Puffed up for one against another.” Physioō means to inflate, and the verb runs through this chapter in verses 6, 18 and 19 (see physioo). The Corinthians have been inflated in favor of one teacher against another. Love, by contrast, “does not puff itself up” (1 Corinthians 13:4). The TLB reads “you must not have favorites,” and the NIV “puffed up in being a follower of one of us over against the other.”

“Who makes you different?” The question takes apart the whole claim of difference. Every distinction the Corinthians draw between their teachers is a gift, and a gift is received, not earned. The KJV has “maketh thee to differ,” and the Douay “distinguisheth thee.” The verb diakrinō is the one for setting things apart, and the question is about what sets the Corinthians apart from each other.

Verses 8–13 · full, rich, and kings, and the apostles put last

“Already full. Already rich.” The verbs describe what the Corinthians say about themselves: kekoresmenoi, satisfied, and eplouthēsate, you became rich. Paul is describing their claims back to them. Chōris hēmōn ebasileusate, you began to reign without us, and then his wish, that they really had begun to reign so that he and his companions could reign with them, carries the irony to its end. The TLB makes the verse about spiritual food, “all the spiritual food you need,” and the NIV has “Already you have all you want!” The KJV keeps “ye are full” and “ye are rich.”

“Last, as men condemned to die.” Epithanatios is the word for a man given to the beasts in the arena at the end of a show, and eschatous, last, is the place in the procession. The image is a parade of the condemned with the apostles at the back. The NIV has “at the end of the procession, like those condemned to die in the arena,” and the KJV “as it were appointed to death.” The Greek has dōkō, I think, with no hoti; the NIV and the Received Text add hoti, that, and the sense is the same. The audience is theatron: the whole world, the angels, and people (apostle).

“Fools for Christ; wise in Christ.” The sentence sets pairs one against another with no verb between them. Mōroi, fools, is the word of 3:18, where the Corinthians were told to become fools in order to become wise; the apostles are already there. Átimoi, without honor, is the word for a man with no standing, and the Corinthians have the standing. The TLB adds a speaker, “Religion has made us foolish, you say,” which is not in the Greek. The NIV has “You are honored, we are dishonored!”

“To this very hour.” The list is a catalogue of hardship, in the order Paul gives it: hunger and thirst, poor clothing, being struck with fists (kolaphizómetha, the verb of the blows in Matthew 26:67), and no settled home. The KJV has “are naked,” the TLB “without even enough clothes to keep us warm,” and the NIV “we are in rags.” The verb for homelessness, astatoumen, means having no fixed place.

“We work hard with our own hands.” Kopiōmen ergazomenoi, we toil while working, is the labor of a craftsman. Paul worked with his hands to support himself (Acts 20:34). The NIV has “We work hard with our own hands,” and for loidoroumenoi, being reviled, it has “When we are cursed, we bless,” the same blessing as Romans 12:14.

“When slandered, we plead.” The printed text has dysphēmoumenoi, being defamed, with WH and NA28; Treg and the Received Text read blasphēmoumenoi, being blasphemed, and the difference is a single syllable. The KJV has “Being defamed, we intreat,” and the NWT 1984 “we have become as the refuse of the world.” The phrase “the sweepings of the world, the scrapings of all things” is perikátharmata and peripsēma, what is scraped off and cast out. Perikátharma was the word for the outcast that a city was purified by casting away, and Paul uses it of the apostles.

Verses 14–17 · tutors, fathers, and Timothy

“Not to shame you, but to admonish you.” The verbs are entrepōn, shaming, and nouthetōn, admonishing. WH and NA28 print the participle, and Treg and RP the form nouthetō, “I admonish”; the sense is the same either way. The tone is that of a household: tékna agapēta, beloved children. The KJV has “as my beloved sons I warn you.”

“Ten thousand child-minders.” Paidagōgous is not a teacher. The paidagogos was the household slave who walked a boy to school and kept him in order, the guardian of the child and not the one who taught him (Galatians 3:24, not yet on these pages). The contrast is between many guardians and one father: patéres, fathers, are the ones who give a child his life, and Paul says he engennēsa, fathered them, dia tou euangeliou, through the good news. The KJV has “instructors,” the nearest word for a teacher, which misses the slave. The TLB has “ten thousand others to teach you,” which is the teacher’s job. The NWT 2013 has “10,000 guardians,” the closest of the three.

“Become imitators of me.” Parakalō is a strong appeal, and mimētai, imitators, shares its root with the English word “mimic.” Paul asks for the imitation of a life he has already shown: he has said he is a father, and a father’s life is what children copy. The Douay adds “as I also am of Christ,” a phrase the Greek of this verse does not have; the Douay takes it from 1 Corinthians 11:1 (not yet on these pages).

“Timothy, my beloved and faithful child.” The apparatus has the order mou tekna in WH, Treg and NA28 and tekna mou in RP; the phrase is the same. Timothy is the young man whom Paul took on in Acts 16:1. He will remind them of tas hodous mou tas en Christō Iēsou, my ways in Christ Jesus, the word hodos for a road, a manner of life. The NIV has “my way of life in Christ Jesus”; the KJV “in every church” for the last clause, and this translation renders the same word ekklēsia as congregation (ekklesia, and Timothy in the encyclopedia).

Verses 18–21 · puffed up, power, and the rod

“Some have become puffed up.” The verb physiōthēsan is the aorist of the verb of verse 6, and its subject, tines, some, is left unnamed. The clause is a condition with its own reason: they are puffed up as though Paul were not coming. The KJV has “as though I would not come to you,” and the NIV “arrogant, as if I were not coming to you.” The NIV’s “arrogant” is a reasonable gloss, but the Greek is the same verb as in verse 6.

“If the Lord wills.” The condition is ean ho kyrios thélēsē, if the Lord should will. Paul’s travel is subject to the Lord’s will, and the word is kyrios, which the editions give as the Lord with no difference in the text. The shelf reads it two ways: the NWT 1984 has “if Jehovah wills,” and the NIV “if the Lord is willing.” The referent is not fixed by the grammar, so this translation keeps “the Lord” and leaves the question open. The verb gnōsomai, I will know, is what a visit can test: not the talk logon but the power dynamin (dynamis). The TLB has “just big talkers,” a loose reading of the same contrast.

“The kingdom of God is not a matter of talk.” Ou gar en lógō, not in speech, and the kingdom is basileia tou theou, the kingdom of God (basileia). Paul makes the kingdom a matter of power. The TLB has “The Kingdom of God is not just talking; it is living by God’s power,” which adds the living; the Greek has only the two nouns.

“A rod, or love and a spirit of gentleness.” Rhabdos, a rod, is the stick of a teacher or of a man who drives animals, and the question is which of the two the Corinthians prefer. The phrase en pneumati praütētos, in a spirit of gentleness, is the same wording as the request in Galatians 6:1 (not yet on these pages). The NIV has “a rod of discipline”; the KJV “in the spirit of meekness.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “under-rowers” for hypērētas and “stewards” for oikonómos; “puffed up” for physioō at 4:6, 4:18 and 4:19; “boast” for kauchaomai, the verb of 3:21 and 4:7; “fools” for mōroi, the word of 3:18; “court day” for hēmera at 4:3; “child-minders” for paidagōgous at 4:15.

Echoes paid. The stewardship of the mysteries from 2:7; the day that reveals each one’s work and the fire that tests it from 3:13, now the Lord who brings the hidden things to light (4:5); the foolishness of 1:18 and 3:18, turned on the apostles and then on the Corinthians (4:10); power over speech from 2:4, closed in 4:20; the boasting of 1:31, answered in 4:7; and the babies of 3:1, who now have a father (4:15).

Echoes planted. The Lord who brings the hidden things to light (4:5) is the judge the letter returns to at its close; the rod of 4:21 becomes discipline when Paul turns to a case of sexual sin in the congregation (5:1–5, not yet on these pages). Fathers from 4:15 return when Paul writes of the man who has been living with his father’s wife.

Next in this book: 1 Corinthians 5, not yet on these pages. Paul leaves the parties and turns to a man living with his father’s wife, a case he says he has already judged, and to what the congregation must do about it.
