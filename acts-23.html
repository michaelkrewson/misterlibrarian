The day after Paul is set before the Sanhedrin, he says he has lived as a citizen before God in all good conscience; the high priest Ananias has him struck on the mouth; Paul calls him a whitewashed wall and then says he did not know he was high priest. Knowing the council is half Sadducees and half Pharisees, he cries that he is a Pharisee on trial for the hope and resurrection of the dead, and the council splits over resurrection, angel and spirit. The tribune, afraid Paul will be torn apart, has the troop snatch him back to the barracks; that night the Lord stands by him and says he must bear witness in Rome. More than forty men bind themselves under a curse to eat nothing until Paul is dead and ask the chief priests and elders to have him brought down; Paul’s sister’s son hears of it, and the tribune sends Paul by night with two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen and two hundred spearmen to Felix at Caesarea, with a letter. Felix asks his province, learns it is Cilicia, and has him kept in Herod’s praetorium until his accusers come.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are the day after 22:30; ‘the following night’ (v11); ‘when it was day’ (v12), the morning of the conspiracy; ‘tomorrow’ (v20), the day of the planned hearing; ‘from the third hour of the night’ (v23), the departure; ‘through the night’ to Antipatris (v31); and ‘the next day’ (v32), the horsemen alone on to Caesarea. Acts 24:1 follows five days later.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–5 · ἀτενίσας … πεπολίτευμαι … τοῖχε κεκονιαμένε … λοιδορεῖς … Ἄρχοντα τοῦ λαοῦ σου οὐκ ἐρεῖς κακῶς atenisas … pepoliteumai … toiche kekoniamene … loidoreis … Archonta tou laou sou ouk ereis kakōs — Acts 23:1–5: what does it mean to say you have lived as a “citizen” before God, why does Paul call the high priest a “whitewashed wall,” and how does a man not know who the high priest is?

Staring at the council (Acts 23:1). Atenisas de ho Paulos tō synedriō is “and Paul, staring at the Sanhedrin.” The verb atenizō stands in fourteen verses of the New Testament: Luke 4:20 and 22:56; Acts 1:10, 3:4, 3:12, 6:15, 7:55, 10:4, 11:6, 13:9, 14:9 and here; and 2 Corinthians 3:7 and 3:13 (neither yet on these pages). Ten of the fourteen are in Acts. The nearest is 6:15: “all those sitting in the Sanhedrin, staring at him, saw his face like the face of an angel” — the council staring at Stephen. Here the stare runs the other way, and the man doing it is Paul, who stares at a man in the same way at 13:9 and 14:9. Luke does not comment on the reversal. The shelf: KJV “earnestly beholding,” ASV “looking stedfastly on,” Geneva “beheld earnestly,” Douay “looking upon,” NWT 1984 “Looking intently at,” NIV “looked straight at” and TLB “Gazing intently at.” Only the Douay leaves the intensity out.

“Men, brothers” (Acts 23:1). Andres adelphoi is the address that the note at 22:1 counted, and this is the second of the openings that note named ahead (23:1 and 23:6; 28:17 is not yet on these pages). The crowd on the steps was “Men, brothers and fathers” (22:1); the council is “Men, brothers,” and Luke does not say why “fathers” is dropped. Four of the seven keep andres: KJV “Men and brethren,” Geneva “Men and brethren,” Douay “Men, brethren” and NWT 1984 “Men, brothers”; three drop it: ASV “Brethren,” NIV “My brothers” and TLB “Brothers.” This translation keeps it, as it did at 22:1.

“I have lived as a citizen” (Acts 23:1). Pasē syneidēsei agathē pepoliteumai tō theō. Pepoliteumai is the perfect of politeuomai (see the entry), a verb built on politēs, “citizen,” and the lexicons give it the sense of conducting one’s life as a member of a community; that is reported here, not examined. The verb stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Philippians 1:27; the noun politeuma, “commonwealth,” stands in the next chapter of that letter (3:20, not yet on these pages). The word the previous chapter put at the centre is the sister of this one, politeia, “citizenship” (22:28, where the tribune says he bought it for a large sum and Paul says he was born to it; the only other verse with that noun is Ephesians 2:12, “the citizenship of Israel”). One day after the lash was stopped by Paul’s citizenship, he tells the council he has lived as a citizen before God. Luke does not say that the verb was chosen for that contrast. “Up to this day” is achri tautēs tēs hēmeras, a perfect with its endpoint stated, so the claim is about all of his years, the persecutor’s too. None of the seven prints “citizen”: KJV “I have lived in all good conscience,” ASV “I have lived before God,” TLB “I have always lived before God,” Geneva “served God,” NWT 1984 “I have behaved before God,” Douay “I have conversed” (the older English sense of “conducted myself”; the Douay translates the Latin) and NIV “I have fulfilled my duty to God,” which is the nearest to a public duty.

“All good conscience” (Acts 23:1). Syneidēsis (see the entry) with agathē is a phrase of five verses of the New Testament: here; 1 Timothy 1:5 and 1:19; and 1 Peter 3:16 and 3:21 (none yet on these pages). In Acts the noun stands in two verses, this and 24:16, where Paul tells Felix he practices having “a conscience without offense toward God and men.” Both are Paul’s own words, to the council and then to the governor. Pasē, “all,” says “in every respect,” and NWT 1984 “a perfectly clear conscience” is the only shelf version that turns it into a degree of clarity; KJV, ASV, Douay, NIV and TLB “all good conscience” or “a good conscience” keep the adjective agathē, “good.”

“Strike him on the mouth” (Acts 23:2). Epetaxen tois parestōsin autō typtein autou to stoma: the high priest Ananias “ordered those standing beside him to strike his mouth.” The name Hananias stands in ten verses of Acts and belongs to three different men: the husband of Sapphira (5:1, 3, 5; see the entry), the disciple at Damascus (9:10, 12, 13, 17 and 22:12; see the entry), and this high priest, who is named again at 24:1, when he comes down with elders and an advocate named Tertullus. Josephus, in Greek, has a high priest of the name, Ananias son of Nedebaios, whom Herod of Chalcis appointed and whom the rebels killed when the revolt broke out (Antiquities 20.103; War 2.441; see the entry); that he is the man of this verse is the usual identification, not something Josephus says. The scene stands beside John 18:22–23, where an officer standing by gives Jesus a slap before the high priest, and Jesus answers, “If I have spoken wrongly, testify about the wrong. But if rightly, why do you strike me?” Paul answers differently, in the next verse; the page sets the two answers side by side and does not rank them.

“Whitewashed wall” (Acts 23:3). Typtein se mellei ho theos, toiche kekoniamene: “God is going to strike you, whitewashed wall.” Mellei with the infinitive is “is about to” (the verb of 22:16, “why do you delay?”), so the Greek is a future that is close, not a wish; whether it is a curse, a prophecy or a warning, the verse does not say. Only NWT 1984 “is going to strike you” has the nearness; KJV “shall smite,” ASV “shall smite,” Douay “shall strike,” Geneva “will smite,” NIV “will strike” and TLB “shall slap” give a plain future. The participle kekoniamenos (see the entry) stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and Matthew 23:27, where Jesus tells the scribes and Pharisees they are like “whitewashed tombs.” The noun toichos, “wall,” stands in one verse, this one. A plastered wall is also in Ezekiel: at Ezekiel 13:10 the people build a flimsy wall and the prophets “daub it” with tafel, which a storm then brings down (13:11, 14–15); the Hebrew tafel, meaning whitewash or plaster, stands in five verses of Ezekiel (13:10, 11, 14, 15 and 22:28; hits read). Whether Paul’s phrase has that passage in mind, Luke does not say; Ezekiel 13 is not yet on these pages. For the image the shelf agrees on a wall: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “whited wall,” and NWT 1984 and NIV “whitewashed wall.” TLB “whitewashed pigpen” replaces the wall with a pigpen the Greek does not name.

“Acting against the law” (Acts 23:3). Kai sy kathē krinōn me kata ton nomon, kai paranomōn keleueis me typtesthai: “you sit judging me according to the law, and, acting against the law, you order me to be struck.” The verb paranomeō stands in one verse, this one; its noun paranomia stands once, at 2 Peter 2:16 (not yet on these pages). Which law Paul means he does not say; Deuteronomy 25:2 has the judge see the guilty man beaten “in his presence” after the case, which puts the verdict first. The principle that a court hears a man before it deals with him is put to this same body in John 7:51, in Nicodemus’s words: “Does our law judge a man without first hearing from him and learning what he is doing?” KJV “contrary to the law,” ASV “contrary to the law,” Douay “contrary to the law,” Geneva “transgressing the Law,” NWT 1984 “transgressing the Law,” NIV “violate the law” and TLB “break the law yourself” all keep the charge. The Greek puts it as a participle, a thing Ananias is doing while he sits, which this translation keeps.

“Do you revile?” (Acts 23:4). Ton archierea tou theou loidoreis; fronts the object: “the high priest of God, do you revile?” The verb loidoreō stands in four verses of the New Testament: John 9:28, where those questioning the man born blind hurl insults at him; here; 1 Corinthians 4:12, “when reviled, we bless”; and 1 Peter 2:23, where Jesus, “when reviled, did not revile in return” (the last two not yet on these pages). The question is put by “those standing by” (hoi parestōtes), the same men told to do the striking at v2. KJV “Revilest thou,” ASV “Revilest thou,” Geneva “Revilest thou,” Douay “Dost thou revile” and NWT 1984 “Are you reviling” keep the verb; NIV “How dare you insult” and TLB “Is that the way to talk to” turn the question into a rebuke.

“I did not know … that he is high priest” (Acts 23:5). Ouk ēidein, adelphoi, hoti estin archiereus: there is no article, “that he is high priest,” and the verb is the past of oida, “I did not know.” Readers have taken it as sincere (Paul had been away from Jerusalem, and the tribune, not the priest, had called this meeting, 22:30), as poor eyesight, or as irony, a man who does not recognize such conduct as a high priest’s; this page reports the readings and does not choose. What Luke does not give is any word that the high priest had not been the speaker of v2, and none that Paul takes back what he said at v3. The quotation that follows is Archonta tou laou sou ouk ereis kakōs, “you shall not speak evil of a ruler of your people,” the law of Exodus 22:28 in this site’s English numbering (22:27 in the Hebrew), the verse that the note there already said Acts 23:5 quotes. The Hebrew is nasi’ be’ammekha lo ta’or, “a ruler of your people you shall not curse,” a phrase that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The Greek here says “speak evil of” where the Hebrew says “curse”; the wording of the Greek Old Testament, which this library does not hold, is reported to be the same as Paul’s, and that report is not checked here. The shelf prints the quotation nearly alike: KJV “Thou shalt not speak evil of the ruler of thy people,” ASV “Thou shalt not speak evil of a ruler of thy people,” NWT 1984 “You must not speak injuriously of a ruler of your people,” NIV “Do not speak evil about the ruler of your people” and TLB “Never speak evil of any of your rulers” (the only plural and the only “any”).

Verses 6–10 · Φαρισαῖός εἰμι, υἱὸς Φαρισαίων … περὶ ἐλπίδος καὶ ἀναστάσεως νεκρῶν … ἐσχίσθη τὸ πλῆθος … μήτε ἄγγελον μήτε πνεῦμα … ἀμφότερα … διεμάχοντο … μὴ διασπασθῇ Pharisaios eimi, huios Pharisaiōn … peri elpidos kai anastaseōs nekrōn … eschisthē to plēthos … mēte angelon mēte pneuma … amphotera … diemachonto … mē diaspasthē — Acts 23:6–10: why does Paul say he is a Pharisee, what do the Sadducees deny, what does “both” count, and how does a sentence end in the middle?

“A Pharisee, a son of Pharisees” (Acts 23:6). Gnous de ho Paulos hoti to hen meros estin Saddoukaiōn to de heteron Pharisaiōn: “knowing that the one part was of Sadducees and the other of Pharisees.” Then Andres adelphoi, egō Pharisaios eimi, huios Pharisaiōn. The address is the second “Men, brothers” of the chapter. The apparatus divides on the last word: Pharisaiōn, “of Pharisees,” in WH, Treg and NA28, and Pharisaiou, “of a Pharisee,” in the Byzantine text of RP. The English can show the split: KJV “the son of a Pharisee” and Geneva “the son of a Pharisee” print the singular; ASV “a son of Pharisees,” Douay “the son of Pharisees” and NWT 1984 “a son of Pharisees” print the plural; NIV “descended from Pharisees” keeps the plural and says it as descent; TLB “as were all my ancestors” goes beyond both, since the Greek names no ancestors apart from Pharisees. This translation prints the plural. The first person is egō in NA28 and RP and absent in WH and Treg, so that “I am being judged” either has or lacks the stressed “I.” Paul speaks of himself as a Pharisee again in a letter, where the Greek has no verb, ‘as to law, a Pharisee’ (Philippians 3:5, not yet on these pages), and before Agrippa, where it is ‘I lived a Pharisee’ (26:5, not yet on these pages). Whether he says it here as a fact, as a tactic or as both, Luke does not say; the next verses show what it does.

“Hope and resurrection of the dead” (Acts 23:6). Peri elpidos kai anastaseōs nekrōn egō krinomai puts two genitives under one peri, “concerning hope and resurrection of the dead,” and the verb is the present passive krinomai, “I am being judged.” Whether the two nouns are two things or one (“the hope of resurrection”) is not settled by the Greek. Four of the seven keep two nouns with “and”: KJV “of the hope and resurrection of the dead,” ASV “touching the hope and resurrection of the dead,” Douay “concerning the hope and resurrection of the dead” and Geneva “of the hope and resurrection of the dead.” Two make it one hope: NWT 1984 “Over the hope of resurrection of the dead” and NIV “because of the hope of the resurrection of the dead.” TLB “because I believe in the resurrection of the dead” drops “hope.” This translation keeps both nouns. The same charge returns when Paul stands before Felix: “Concerning resurrection of the dead I am being judged before you today” (24:21), with 24:15, “having a hope in God … that there is going to be a resurrection.” And the topic has already divided a crowd: the Sadducees, among the priests who arrest Peter and John, are “deeply irritated because they were teaching the people and proclaiming in Jesus the resurrection from the dead” (4:1–2), and at Athens “hearing of a resurrection of the dead, some began to jeer” (17:32).

The split (Acts 23:7). Egeneto stasis tōn Pharisaiōn kai Saddoukaiōn, kai eschisthē to plēthos: “a dissension arose … and the crowd was split.” The noun stasis (see the entry) stands in nine verses of the New Testament: Mark 15:7 and Luke 23:19 and 23:25, the insurrection for which Barabbas lay in prison; Acts 15:2, the “dissension” between Paul and Barnabas and the men from Judea; 19:40, the town clerk’s “insurrection”; here and v10; 24:5, where Tertullus calls Paul “one stirring up dissensions” among all the Jews; and Hebrews 9:8, where the word means a standing, not a quarrel (not yet on these pages). Luke does not tie 24:5 to this verse. The verb eschisthē, “was split,” stands in six verses: the temple veil at Matthew 27:51, Mark 15:38 and Luke 23:45; the net that was not torn at John 21:11; “the multitude of the city was split” at Acts 14:4; and here. At 14:4 the crowd divided over the apostles; here Paul himself draws the line along which the council divides. KJV “a dissension … the multitude was divided,” ASV “a dissension … the assembly was divided,” NWT 1984 “a dissension arose … the multitude was split” and Douay “a dissension … the multitude was divided” keep the noun; NIV “a dispute broke out” and TLB “This divided the Council right down the middle” do not.

“Neither angel nor spirit … both” (Acts 23:8). Saddoukaioi men gar legousin mē einai anastasin mēte angelon mēte pneuma, Pharisaioi de homologousin ta amphotera. The Sadducees are the party the entry describes; the word stands in fourteen verses of the New Testament, five of them in Acts (4:1, 5:17, and 23:6, 7, 8). The sentence about them is an aside by Luke, in the present tense, and it repeats what the Gospels already say: that they are “those who say there is no resurrection” (Luke 20:27; also Matthew 22:23 and Mark 12:18). The new part is the other two denials, and then the count. The Sadducees deny three things (resurrection, angel, spirit), but the Pharisees acknowledge ta amphotera, “the both.” Amphoteros is the word for “both” of two. Two readings are open, and the Greek does not choose: “both” may be the two sides of the denial (resurrection, on one side, and angel-or-spirit taken together on the other), or the two items that follow “neither … nor” (angel and spirit). Both readings are held, and this page reports them without voting. The shelf: KJV “confess both,” ASV “confess both,” Douay “confess both” and Geneva “confess both” keep “both” and so keep the open question; NWT 1984 “publicly declare them all,” NIV “believe all these things” and TLB “believe in all of these” count three and so print the first reading as a count the Greek does not make. TLB “eternal spirit within us” adds “eternal” and “within us,” neither of which is in the Greek, and so turns pneuma into something inside a person, which the Greek does not say.

“We find nothing evil in this man” (Acts 23:9). Ginetai de kraugē megalē, “a great outcry arose,” and some of the scribes of the Pharisees’ part diemachonto, “argued fiercely.” The verb diamachomai stands in one verse, this one: a machē, a fight, carried through (dia-) to the end. The scribes are said to be “of the part (meros) of the Pharisees,” the same noun as the “one part” of v6. Their words, Ouden kakon heuriskomen en tō anthrōpō toutō, are in the shape of what Pilate says of Jesus: Ouden heuriskō aition en tō anthrōpō toutō, “I find no guilt in this man” (Luke 23:4), with the same last four words, which also stand at 23:14. The phrase “in this man” with a finding of this kind stands in three verses: Luke 23:4, 23:14 and here. In Luke it is a Roman judge speaking of Jesus; here it is Pharisees speaking of Paul. Luke does not say whether the likeness is meant.

The sentence that stops (Acts 23:9). The Greek of the verse ends ei de pneuma elalēsen autō ē angelos, “but if a spirit spoke to him, or an angel,” and there is no verb to finish it. The break is in WH, Treg and NA28. The Byzantine text of RP adds mē theomachōmen, “let us not fight against God.” The stem theomach- stands in one verse of the New Testament in the text this page prints, Acts 5:39, where Gamaliel warns the council “lest you even be found fighters against God” (see the entry); the longer reading would add a second, and it would give the Pharisee scribes a word of the stem Gamaliel used. Which reading is the older this page does not decide. The shelf divides on the ending: KJV “let us not fight against God” and Geneva “let us not fight against God” carry the longer reading; ASV “what if a spirit hath spoken to him, or an angel?”, Douay “What if a spirit hath spoken to him, or an angel?” and NIV “What if a spirit or an angel has spoken to him?” finish the thought as a question the Greek does not mark; NWT 1984 “if a spirit or an angel spoke to him,—.” leaves it broken, as the Greek does; and TLB “Perhaps a spirit or angel spoke to him there on the Damascus road” adds a place the verse does not name. This translation breaks the sentence with a dash.

“Torn apart,” “snatch” (Acts 23:10). Phobētheis ho chiliarchos mē diaspasthē ho Paulos hyp’ autōn: the tribune, “afraid that Paul would be torn apart by them.” The verb diaspaō stands in two verses, here and Mark 5:4, where the chains were “torn apart” by the man among the tombs. “Afraid” is phobētheis in WH, Treg and NA28; RP has eulabētheis, a word of the root of eulabēs, “devout,” which is what Ananias is called at 22:12. The order he gives is harpasai auton ek mesou autōn, “to snatch him from their midst” (see the entry), the verb that carries Philip away at 8:39; here it is the troop that does the snatching, and the place they take him to is the barracks of 21:34 and 22:24. “The troop” is to strateuma, which the tribune himself uses at v27 of the letter. Only the Douay “the tribune” keeps chiliarchos as a tribune; KJV, ASV and Geneva “chief captain,” NIV and TLB “commander” and NWT 1984 “military commander” do not.

Verse 11 · Τῇ δὲ ἐπιούσῃ νυκτὶ … ἐπιστὰς … ὁ κύριος … Θάρσει … διεμαρτύρω … οὕτω σε δεῖ καὶ εἰς Ῥώμην μαρτυρῆσαι Tē de epiousē nykti … epistas … ho kyrios … Tharsei … diemartyrō … houtō se dei kai eis Rhōmēn martyrēsai — Acts 23:11: who is “the Lord” who stands by Paul in the night, what is the word “take courage,” and what is the “must” that sends him to Rome?

“The following night” and “stood by him” (Acts 23:11). Tē de epiousē nykti epistas autō ho kyrios. The participle epiousa, “coming on, following,” marks the next day in Acts, at 7:26, 16:11, 20:15, 21:18 and here, and this is the only one of the five where the following period is a night. Epistas, “having stood by,” is the verb of a sudden appearance: an angel of Jehovah “stood over” the shepherds (Luke 2:9) and “stood by” Peter in the cell (Acts 12:7), and Ananias of Damascus “standing by” Paul says “Saul, brother, recover your sight” (22:13, the note there promised this verse). Here it is the Lord. Nothing in the Greek says it is a vision or a dream; at 18:9 Luke says “through a vision,” and here he does not. “The Lord” is Jesus: what he says is “the things about me,” and the word is the one Paul has used of Jesus since the road (22:8, 10 and 19). So it stays “Lord.”

“Take courage” (Acts 23:11). Tharsei (see the entry) is a word of seven verses of the New Testament in its two forms, the singular tharsei and the plural tharseite: Matthew 9:2 and 9:22, 14:27, Mark 6:50, 10:49, John 16:33 and here. Each is spoken by Jesus, once (Mark 10:49) by those he sent to call the blind man, who relay it. This is the only one in Acts. The English shelf: KJV “Be of good cheer, Paul,” Geneva “Be of good courage, Paul,” ASV “Be of good cheer,” NWT 1984 “Be of good courage!”, NIV “Take courage!”, Douay “Be constant” and TLB “Don’t worry, Paul”; the “Paul” that KJV, Geneva and TLB print is in the longer Byzantine text of RP and not in the Greek this page prints. The Douay translates the Latin, and its “Be constant” is reported to follow the Latin there; that is not checked here.

“You have solemnly testified” and “you must” (Acts 23:11). Hōs gar diemartyrō ta peri emou eis Ierousalēm houtō se dei kai eis Rhōmēn martyrēsai. The verb diamartyromai (see the entry) stands in fifteen verses of the New Testament, and of them nine are in Acts (2:40, 8:25, 10:42, 18:5, 20:21, 20:23, 20:24, here and 28:23): the word is a witness given with weight, dia- carrying it through. Paul used it of his own work at Miletus, “solemnly testifying” to Jews and Greeks (20:21) and “to solemnly testify to the gospel of the grace of God” (20:24); here the Lord uses it of what Paul has done in Jerusalem, and then the plain martyreō, “bear witness,” of Rome. The Lord’s dei, “it is necessary,” answers Paul’s own at 19:21, “I must also see Rome,” and the “witnesses” of 1:8 who go “to the end of the earth”; it is the Lord who says the necessity now, and the Greek puts “Rome” as the object of a witness, not of a visit. Luke does not say how Paul will come to Rome, or in what condition. KJV “thou hast testified,” ASV “thou hast testified,” Geneva “thou hast testified,” Douay “thou hast testified,” NIV “you have testified” and NWT 1984 “you have been giving a thorough witness” are the verb; the one that stresses the dia- is the NWT 1984, with “thorough”; TLB “you have told the people about me” has lost the testimony.

Verses 12–15 · ποιήσαντες συστροφὴν … ἀνεθεμάτισαν ἑαυτοὺς … μήτε φαγεῖν μήτε πιεῖν … συνωμοσίαν … Ἀναθέματι ἀνεθεματίσαμεν … ἐμφανίσατε poiēsantes systrophēn … anethematisan heautous … mēte phagein mēte piein … synōmosian … Anathemati anethematisamen … emphanisate — Acts 23:12–15: what do the forty bind themselves to, what is the “curse” they put on themselves, and what do they ask the priests to do?

“Formed a conspiracy” (Acts 23:12). Genomenēs de hēmeras poiēsantes systrophēn hoi Ioudaioi: “when it was day, the Jews, having made a systrophē.” The noun systrophē stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Acts 19:40, where the town clerk at Ephesus speaks of “this turbulent crowd”; it is a turning-together, a gathering, and what the gathering is for has to come from the sentence, which here says conspiracy. The next verse gives a second noun for the same thing, synōmosia, “sworn conspiracy,” a “swearing-together” that stands in one verse, v13, and no other. The apparatus divides on the subject: hoi Ioudaioi, “the Jews,” in WH, Treg and NA28, and tines tōn Ioudaiōn, “some of the Jews,” in the Byzantine text of RP. The shelf shows it: KJV “certain of the Jews,” Geneva “certain of the Jews,” Douay “some of the Jews,” NIV “some Jews” and TLB “some forty or more of the Jews” print the smaller group; ASV “the Jews” and NWT 1984 “the Jews” print the Greek of the text on this page. This translation keeps “the Jews,” and the verse that follows says who they are: “more than forty” men. A line like this one has been quoted against a whole people. The Greek names forty-odd men in Jerusalem; it does not name a nation.

“Bound themselves under a curse” (Acts 23:12, 14, 21). Anethematisan heautous is the verb anathematizō (see the entry), which stands in four verses of the New Testament: here, v14 and v21 of this chapter, and Mark 14:71, where Peter begins “to call down curses.” The noun anathema stands in six verses (v14 here, with Romans 9:3, 1 Corinthians 12:3 and 16:22, and Galatians 1:8 and 1:9). At v14 the two are set together: Anathemati anethematisamen heautous, with a dative of the noun before the verb, “with a curse we have cursed ourselves.” That construction is the one a Hebrew writer gets by doubling a verb, and it is reported here, not examined. This translation keeps the doubling (“with a curse we have bound ourselves under a curse”) and does not trim it. What is cursed is the speakers, in the event they fail: the oath is that they will taste nothing until Paul is dead, and Luke does not say what becomes of men whose condition is not met. The shelf shows how the repetition fares: KJV “We have bound ourselves under a great curse,” ASV “We have bound ourselves under a great curse,” Douay “We have bound ourselves under a great curse,” Geneva “We have bound ourselves with a solemn curse” and NWT 1984 “We have solemnly bound ourselves with a curse” spread the doubling into an adjective (“great,” “solemn,” “solemnly”), and NIV “We have taken a solemn oath” turns a curse into an oath; TLB reports only that they “told them what they had done.”

“Neither eat nor drink” (Acts 23:12, 21). Mēte phagein mēte piein heōs hou apokteinōsin ton Paulon: the vow is a fast until Paul is killed. The pair mēte phagein mēte piein stands in two verses, v12 and v21; in v14 the Greek says mēdenos geusasthai, “to taste nothing.” The same two verbs stand in a very different place at 9:9: after the road to Damascus Saul “neither ate nor drank” for three days (ouk ephagen oude epien, which is not the same pair of words). Luke does not set the two beside each other. KJV “neither eat nor drink till they had killed Paul,” ASV “neither eat nor drink till they had killed Paul” and Douay “neither eat, nor drink, till they killed Paul” are literal; NWT 1984 “neither eat nor drink until they had killed Paul” and NIV “not to eat or drink until they had killed Paul” are too.

“The chief priests and the elders” (Acts 23:14). The forty go not to the whole Sanhedrin but tois archiereusin kai tois presbyterois, “to the chief priests and the elders,” the first of the two groups the tribune summoned at 22:30 (“the chief priests and the whole Sanhedrin”), with the elders in place of the rest. What they ask is not that the priests kill anyone. They ask them to take part in a deception: nyn oun hymeis emphanisate tō chiliarchō syn tō synedriō hopōs katagagē auton eis hymas hōs mellontas diaginōskein akribesteron ta peri autou, “now therefore you, with the Sanhedrin, make it clear to the tribune that he should bring him down to you, as though you were going to determine more exactly the things about him.” The verb emphanizō is a verb of laying information before an official, and in Acts it stands in five verses, all in the last six chapters: here, 23:22, 24:1, 25:2 and 25:15; at v22 the tribune orders the young man not to say that he has “made known” this to him. “As though” (hōs) is the pretext, and the word “more exactly” (akribesteron, the comparative of the word the notes on 22:3 counted) is the pretext’s own claim: the council would pretend to want greater accuracy. The stem that Paul used of his own strict schooling at 22:3 stands here in the supposed purpose of the hearing. The ambush itself is named in v15 as a plain intent, hetoimoi esmen tou anelein auton, “we are ready to kill him,” “before he comes near,” so on the road. The Byzantine text of RP adds aurion, “tomorrow,” to v15, and KJV “to morrow” and Geneva “tomorrow” print it; ASV, Douay, NWT 1984, NIV and TLB do not; “tomorrow” is in every text at v20, where the nephew reports the plan. The same plan comes back after the two years of 24:27, at 25:3, with the same verb for killing (anelein, v15) and the same noun for the ambush (enedra, v16): “requesting a favor against him, that he would summon him to Jerusalem, making an ambush to kill him on the way.”

Verses 16–22 · ὁ υἱὸς τῆς ἀδελφῆς Παύλου … τὴν ἐνέδραν … Ὁ δέσμιος Παῦλος … νεανίαν … συνέθεντο … τὴν ἀπὸ σοῦ ἐπαγγελίαν … μηδενὶ ἐκλαλῆσαι ho huios tēs adelphēs Paulou … tēn enedran … Ho desmios Paulos … neanian … synetheto … tēn apo sou epangelian … mēdeni eklalēsai — Acts 23:16–22: who is the son of Paul’s sister, why is Paul called “the prisoner,” and what is the “promise” the forty are waiting for?

The son of Paul’s sister (Acts 23:16). Akousas de ho huios tēs adelphēs Paulou tēn enedran: “and the son of Paul’s sister, hearing of the ambush.” Luke gives neither his name nor his mother’s, nor where either lives, nor how he came to hear of a plot that only the forty and the priests were party to. The genitive “sister’s” (adelphēs) stands in three verses of the New Testament: John 11:1, where it is Martha who is called Mary’s sister; here; and 2 John 13. This is the only verse in the New Testament that mentions a sister of Paul’s or her son (searched, hits read); in his letters Paul names relatives (“kinsmen” at Romans 16:7, 11 and 21, not yet on these pages), but none of them is called a sister’s son. The shelf agrees: KJV “Paul’s sister’s son,” ASV “Paul’s sister’s son,” Geneva “Paul’s sister’s son,” Douay “Paul’s sister’s son,” NWT 1984 “the son of Paul’s sister” and NIV “the son of Paul’s sister”; only TLB “Paul’s nephew” uses the single English word. The noun enedra, “ambush,” stands in two verses, here and 25:3; the verb enedreuō stands in two, Luke 11:54, where the scribes and Pharisees lie in wait “to catch something out of his mouth,” and v21 here. The apparatus gives tēn enedran to WH, Treg and NA28 and to enedron, the neuter form of the same noun, to RP.

“The prisoner Paul” (Acts 23:18). Ho desmios Paulos prosokalesamenos me ērōtēsen: “the prisoner Paul, having called me to him, asked.” The speaker is the centurion, and he does not say “Paul the prisoner” but puts the article and the noun first, so that Paul is “the prisoner” before he is named. The noun desmios stands in six verses of Acts: 16:25, 16:27, here, 25:14, 25:27 and 28:17 (the last not yet on these pages). Paul uses the same two words of himself in the opposite order: “Paul, a prisoner of Christ Jesus” (Philemon 1 and 9), and “I, Paul, the prisoner of Christ Jesus” at Ephesians 3:1 (not yet on these pages). A centurion of the garrison says the two words; Luke does not remark on it. In these verses the man in chains does the commanding: he calls (proskalesamenos) the centurion at v17, as the centurion reports at v18, and the centurion obeys. The “young man” is neanias at v17 in every text; at v18 it is neanias in WH and RP and neaniskos in NA28 and Treg; and at v22 it is neaniskos in WH, Treg and NA28 and neanias in RP. The two nouns do not differ in sense here (see the entry), but the witnesses do not agree on which stands where, and the English has only “young man.”

“Agreed to ask you” (Acts 23:20). Hoi Ioudaioi synetheto tou erōtēsai se: “the Jews have agreed to ask you.” The form synethento, from syntithemai, stands in two verses, both of a plan made together: here, and Luke 22:5, where the chief priests and scribes “agreed to give him money” (Judas). In v20 the participle after “as though” reads mellon in NA28, mellōn in WH and Treg and mellonta in RP; the English cannot show which of the three forms the Greek has, and this translation prints no subject for it: “as though intending to inquire something more exactly about him.” The nephew’s report repeats the plan of v15 with the same verb, katagō, “bring down,” and the same “as though.”

“The promise from you” (Acts 23:21). Eisin hetoimoi prosdechomenoi tēn apo sou epangelian: they are “ready, waiting for the promise from you.” The noun epangelia stands in eight verses of Acts: 1:4, 2:33, 2:39, 7:17, 13:23, 13:32, here, and 26:6 (not yet on these pages). In seven of the eight it is God’s promise, to Abraham, to the fathers, of the Spirit; in this one it is the promise of a Roman tribune, to give his consent. Luke does not comment on the word. The shelf: KJV “a promise from thee,” ASV “the promise from thee,” Geneva “thy promise” and NWT 1984 “the promise from you” keep the noun; Douay “a promise from thee” keeps it too; NIV “your consent to their request” and TLB “expecting you to agree to their request” lose the word and with it the echo.

“Tell no one” (Acts 23:22). Parangeilas mēdeni eklalēsai hoti tauta enephanisas pros eme: the tribune lets the young man go with an order to tell no one that he “made these things known” (the verb of v15, emphanizō). The infinitive eklaleō, “to speak out,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one (the other hit for the letters, 1 Peter 1:8, is the different word “inexpressible”). The tribune, who in v10 had acted to save Paul from a crowd, now acts in silence and by night; the Greek does not say why he wants the report kept quiet.

Verses 23–30 · δεξιολάβους … ἀπὸ τρίτης ὥρας τῆς νυκτός … κτήνη … ἐπιβιβάσαντες … Κλαύδιος Λυσίας τῷ κρατίστῳ … ἐξειλάμην … ζητημάτων τοῦ νόμου … ἄξιον θανάτου ἢ δεσμῶν … ἐπιβουλῆς dexiolabous … apo tritēs hōras tēs nyktos … kentē … epibibasantes … Klaudios Lysias tō kratistō … exeilamēn … zētēmatōn tou nomou … axion thanatou ē desmōn … epiboulēs — Acts 23:23–30: who rides out with Paul, and what does the tribune’s letter say, and leave out?

The escort (Acts 23:23). Hetoimasate stratiōtas diakosious hopōs poreuthōsin heōs Kaisareias, kai hippeis hebdomēkonta kai dexiolabous diakosious, apo tritēs hōras tēs nyktos: two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen and two hundred dexiolaboi, which is four hundred and seventy men by addition and which Luke does not total. The noun hippeus, “horseman,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and v32; the dexiolabos stands in one, this one, and its meaning is uncertain. It is built of dexios, “right,” and lambanō, “take,” and lexicons are reported to offer “spearmen” and, among other glosses, “those who take with the right hand”; that is reported here, not examined. Six of the seven print “spearmen”: KJV, ASV, Douay, NWT 1984, NIV and TLB “spearmen”; Geneva “with darts” is the exception. This translation prints “spearmen” with the doubt left to this note. “From the third hour of the night” is a count of the night’s hours, and if they are reckoned from sunset it is about nine in the evening; that is reported, since the hours were a twelfth part of the night and moved with the seasons, and the Greek gives no clock. NIV “at nine tonight” and TLB “at nine o’clock tonight” give the clock time; KJV “at the third hour of the night,” Geneva “at the third hour of the night” and NWT 1984 “at the third hour of the night” keep the Greek’s own count.

Animals to mount him on (Acts 23:24). Ktēnē te parastēsai hina epibibasantes ton Paulon diasōsōsi pros Phēlika ton hēgemona: “and to provide animals, so that, mounting Paul, they might bring him safely through to Felix the governor.” Ktēnos is an animal, and the noun stands in four verses of the New Testament: here, in the plural; Luke 10:34, in the singular, where the Samaritan sets the wounded man “on his own animal”; 1 Corinthians 15:39 (“of beasts”); and Revelation 18:13 (not yet on these pages). The verb epibibazō, “to set on,” stands in three verses: Luke 10:34, where the Samaritan does it, Luke 19:35, where they set Jesus on the colt, and here. Luke does not set them side by side. The Greek says “animals,” and only Paul is said to be mounted; what the soldiers rode or walked on is not stated. The shelf: KJV “beasts,” ASV “beasts,” Douay “beasts” and NWT 1984 “beasts of burden” keep the Greek’s general word; NIV “horses,” Geneva “an horse” and TLB “a horse” narrow it, and Geneva and TLB to a single animal.

Claudius Lysias, to the most excellent Felix (Acts 23:26). Klaudios Lysias tō kratistō hēgemoni Phēliki chairein. The tribune is named here for the first time: a Roman first name and a Greek second one, and his name stands again at 24:22 (“Lysias the tribune”). “Claudius” is also the name of the emperor of 11:28 and 18:2 (see the entry); whether this man took it from him when he bought his citizenship, as the note on the tribune’s purchase leaves open, Luke does not say. Felix’s own name stands in eight verses of Acts, all from here on (23:24, 23:26, 24:3, 24:22, 24:24, 24:25, 24:27 and 25:14; see the entry). Kratistos, “most excellent,” the title of rank, stands in four verses: the Theophilus of Luke 1:3, here, the advocate Tertullus’s “most excellent Felix” (24:3) and Paul’s “most excellent Festus” (26:25), the last not yet on these pages. The greeting chairein opens a letter in three places: the Jerusalem council’s letter at 15:23, this one, and James 1:1. KJV “the most excellent governor Felix,” ASV “the most excellent governor Felix” and Douay “the most excellent governor, Felix” keep the title; Geneva “the most noble governor Felix,” NWT 1984 “his excellency, Governor Felix,” NIV “His Excellency, Governor Felix” and TLB “His Excellency, Governor Felix” vary it.

“I rescued him, having learned that he is a Roman” (Acts 23:27). Ton andra touton syllēmphthenta hypo tōn Ioudaiōn kai mellonta anaireisthai hyp’ autōn epistas syn tō strateumati exeilamēn mathōn hoti Rhōmaios estin. The letter is what the tribune chooses to tell, and it can be read beside what Luke has already narrated. In the narrative the tribune hears that Jerusalem is in confusion, takes soldiers and runs down, has Paul bound with two chains and asks who he is and what he has done (21:31–33), orders him brought into the barracks to be examined by flogging, and learns that Paul is a Roman only when Paul says so, with the straps already on him; then “the tribune too was afraid, having learned that he was a Roman and that he had bound him” (22:24–29). The letter has the rescue come “having learned that he is a Roman,” and says nothing of the chains, the order to flog, or the order in which he learned; the participle mathōn says “having learned” and does not say “because.” Luke does not call the letter false and does not say the tribune is lying; he gives the letter as it was written and the reader the earlier chapters. The shelf makes the order a cause in some places: NWT 1984 “because I learned he was a Roman,” NIV “for I had learned that he is a Roman citizen” and TLB “for I learned that he was a Roman citizen” give a reason, while KJV “having understood that he was a Roman,” ASV “having learned that he was a Roman,” Geneva “perceiving that he was a Roman” and Douay “understanding that he is a Roman” keep the participle. The verb exaireō, “to take out, rescue,” is the one of Joseph’s rescue from his troubles (7:10), of the rescue of Israel from Egypt (7:34), of Peter’s from Herod (12:11) and of the Lord’s word to Paul that he is “rescuing” him from the people and the nations (26:17, not yet on these pages): it stands in five verses of Acts, and four of them are the act of God or of the Lord. Here it is the tribune’s. The participle that says he “came upon them” is epistas, the one that said the Lord “stood by” Paul in v11.

“Questions of their law” (Acts 23:29). Hon heuron enkaloumenon peri zētēmatōn tou nomou autōn, mēden de axion thanatou ē desmōn echonta enklēma. The noun zētēma, “question,” stands in five verses, all in Acts: 15:2, 18:15, here, 25:19 and 26:3 (the last not yet on these pages). The nearest is Gallio’s at 18:15: “if they are questions about a word and names and your own law, see to it yourselves; I do not wish to be a judge of these things.” Two Roman officials, in two cities, read the charge against Paul the same way. The pair “death or bonds” (thanatou ē desmōn) stands in two verses, here and 26:31 (not yet on these pages), where Agrippa and Festus say the same to one another; the plainer “nothing worthy of death” is said of Jesus at Luke 23:15. Enklēma, “accusation,” stands in two verses, here and 25:16. KJV “worthy of death or of bonds,” ASV “worthy of death or of bonds,” Douay “worthy of death or of bands,” Geneva “worthy of death, or of bonds” and NWT 1984 “deserving of death or bonds” keep “bonds,” five of the seven; NIV “deserved death or imprisonment” and TLB “worthy of imprisonment or death” say “imprisonment.”

“A plot” and the missing farewell (Acts 23:30). Mēnytheisēs de moi epiboulēs eis ton andra esesthai: “a plot against the man having been disclosed to me.” The noun epiboulē stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts and all plots against Paul: 9:24, at Damascus; 20:3, as he was about to set sail for Syria; 20:19, “the plots of the Jews”; and here. The Byzantine text of RP ends the letter with Errōso, “Farewell,” the closing of the Jerusalem letter at 15:29 (errōsthe), which is in every text there. KJV “Farewell,” Geneva “Farewell” and Douay “Farewell” end the letter with it; ASV, NWT 1984, NIV and TLB do not, and this translation, which follows the Greek that has no such word, does not either. The letter closes by turning the accusers over to Felix, to speak against him “before you.”

Verses 31–35 · ἤγαγον διὰ νυκτὸς εἰς τὴν Ἀντιπατρίδα … ἐκ ποίας ἐπαρχείας … ἀπὸ Κιλικίας … Διακούσομαί σου … ἐν τῷ πραιτωρίῳ τοῦ Ἡρῴδου ēgagon dia nyktos eis tēn Antipatrida … ek poias eparcheias … apo Kilikias … Diakousomai sou … en tō praitōriō tou Hērōdou — Acts 23:31–35: where is Antipatris, what is a “province” here, and what is Herod’s praetorium?

Through the night to Antipatris (Acts 23:31). Hoi men oun stratiōtai kata to diatetagmenon autois analabontes ton Paulon ēgagon dia nyktos eis tēn Antipatrida: the soldiers, “as had been ordered them, took Paul up and brought him through the night to Antipatris.” The name Antipatris stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one (see the entry); it is a town on the way from Jerusalem toward Caesarea, which Josephus says Herod the Great built in the plain of Kapharsaba and named for his father Antipater (Antiquities 16.142–143). How far the night’s march was Luke does not say, and the number given by reference works (a march of some thirty-five miles or more) is reported, not measured here.

Letting the horsemen go on (Acts 23:32). Tē de epaurion easantes tous hippeis aperchesthai syn autō hypestrepsan eis tēn parembolēn: “and on the next day, letting the horsemen go on with him, they returned to the barracks.” The rest of the escort turns back at Antipatris and the horsemen go on; Luke does not say why, and the Greek does not call the ones who turn back infantry. The barracks is the parembolē of 21:34, 22:24 and v10, so that the troop returns to the place Paul left. KJV “they left the horsemen to go with him,” ASV “they left the horsemen to go with him,” Douay “leaving the horsemen to go with him” and Geneva “they left the horsemen to go with him” are literal; NWT 1984 “they permitted the horsemen to go on with him,” NIV “they let the cavalry go on with him” and TLB “leaving him with the cavalry” are close.

“From what province” (Acts 23:34). Epērōtēsas ek poias eparcheias estin kai pythomenos hoti apo Kilikias: Felix, having read the letter, “asked what province he was from, and learning that he was from Cilicia.” The noun eparcheia stands in two verses, here and 25:1, where Festus “having entered the province” goes up to Jerusalem; the spelling is eparcheias in WH and NA28 and eparchias in Treg and RP. The question is the governor’s, and Luke does not say why he asks or what he would have done had the answer been different. The answer is the Cilicia of Paul’s own account of himself, “born in Tarsus of Cilicia” (22:3; 21:39; Tarsus), a province named in Acts at 6:9, 15:23, 15:41, 21:39, 22:3, here and 27:5 (not yet on these pages). KJV, ASV, Douay, Geneva, NWT 1984 and NIV print “province”; TLB “where he was from” does not.

“I will hear you out” (Acts 23:35). Diakousomai sou, ephē, hotan kai hoi katēgoroi sou paragenōntai. The verb diakouō, “to hear through, hear fully,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, a hearing carried through to the end (dia-). The condition is stated: when the accusers have arrived. The shelf: KJV “I will hear thee,” Geneva “I will hear thee” and Douay “I will hear thee” drop the prefix; ASV “I will hear thee fully,” NWT 1984 “I shall give you a thorough hearing” and TLB “I will hear your case fully” keep it as “fully” or “thorough”; NIV “I will hear your case” does not.

Herod’s praetorium (Acts 23:35). Ekeleusen en tō praitōriō tou Hērōdou phylassesthai auton: he “commanded that he be kept under guard in Herod’s praetorium.” The noun praitōrion (see the entry) stands in seven verses of the New Testament: five are about the trial of Jesus, Matthew 27:27, Mark 15:16 and John 18:28, 18:33 and 19:9; then here; and Philippians 1:13, where Paul’s chains have become known “in the whole praetorium.” The word names the governor’s headquarters, and here it is in the genitive, “of Herod,” a name Luke has already used for the king who killed James (12:1) and does not distinguish here; Josephus says Herod the Great built very costly palaces in Caesarea (Antiquities 15.331; War 1.408) but never uses the word praitōrion of them (searched in Antiquities and War) and says nothing there of governors living in them; that the governors later used a Herodian palace is read from this verse and from reference works, not checked here. KJV “Herod’s judgment hall,” Geneva “Herod’s judgment hall” and Douay “Herod’s judgment hall” print an English law-court; ASV “Herod’s palace,” NIV “Herod’s palace” and TLB “King Herod’s palace” print a building; NWT 1984 “palace of Herod,” in its “praetorian palace,” keeps the Latin word with the building. This translation keeps “praetorium.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “staring” for atenisas at v1, the verb of 6:15 turned around; “Men, brothers” at vv1 and 6, with andres kept as at 22:1; “lived as a citizen” for pepoliteumai at v1, the verb of the noun “citizenship” at 22:28; “whitewashed wall” for toiche kekoniamene at v3; “acting against the law” for paranomōn at v3; “revile” for loidoreō at v4; “a son of Pharisees” at v6, the plural of the printed Greek; “hope and resurrection of the dead” at v6, both nouns kept; “dissension” for stasis at vv7 and 10 and “split” for eschisthē at v7, as at 14:4; “acknowledge both” for homologousin ta amphotera at v8, the count left as the Greek leaves it; “argued fiercely” for diemachonto at v9, with the sentence broken at “or an angel—”; “torn apart” and “snatch” at v10; “Take courage” for tharsei and “solemnly testified” for diemartyrō at v11, with “Lord” kept because the Lord speaks of “the things about me”; “formed a conspiracy” and “bound themselves under a curse” at v12, and “with a curse … under a curse” at v14, the doubling kept; “make it clear” for emphanizō at v15 and “made known” at v22, the one verb; “ambush” for enedra at v16 and for enedreuō at v21; “prisoner” for desmios at v18; “promise” for epangelia at v21, the word of Acts for what God promises; “spearmen” for dexiolaboi at v23, the meaning left uncertain in the note; “animals” for ktēnē at v24; “most excellent” for kratistos at v26; “rescued” for exeilamēn at v27; “questions” for zētēmata at v29, as at 18:15; “plot” for epiboulē at v30, as at 9:24 and 20:3; “province” for eparcheia at v34; “hear you out” for diakousomai at v35; and “praetorium” for praitōrion at v35, as at Philippians 1:13.

Echoes paid. “Men, brothers” as an opening — 22:1, at vv1 and 6. The stare at the council — 6:15, 13:9 and 14:9, at v1. The verb built on “citizen,” and the citizenship of the day before — 22:28 and Philippians 1:27, at v1. The whitewash — Matthew 23:27, at v3. The blow before the high priest — John 18:22–23, and the hearing before judging, John 7:51, at v3. The law against cursing a ruler — Exodus 22:28, at v5, which the note there promised. The Sadducees and the resurrection — 4:1–2, 5:17, Luke 20:27, Matthew 22:23 and Mark 12:18, at vv6–8. The split crowd — 14:4, at v7. “We find nothing evil in this man” — Luke 23:4 and 23:14, at v9. Gamaliel’s “fighters against God” — 5:39, at v9. “Torn apart,” and the verb of snatching — Mark 5:4 and 8:39, at v10. The Lord who stands by — 22:13, 12:7 and Luke 2:9, at v11. “Take courage” — Matthew 9:2, 9:22, 14:27, Mark 6:50, 10:49 and John 16:33, at v11. “I must also see Rome,” and the witnesses to the end of the earth — 19:21 and 1:8, at v11. The ones who neither eat nor drink — 9:9, at vv12 and 21. The turbulent crowd — 19:40, at v12. The curse of Peter and of the Galatians — Mark 14:71 and Galatians 1:8, at v14. The plot against Saul — 9:24 and 20:3, at v30. “Prisoner” — Philemon 1 and 9, at v18. The promise, the word of Acts — 1:4, 2:33, 2:39, 7:17, 13:23 and 13:32, at v21. The tribune’s purchase of citizenship, and his name — 22:28, at v26. The letter with its greeting — 15:23 and 15:29, at v26. The rescue verb — 7:10, 7:34 and 12:11, at v27. The tribune’s own account, set beside the narrative — 21:31–33 and 22:24–29, at vv27–28. “Questions of the law” — 15:2 and 18:15, at v29. Nothing worthy of death — Luke 23:15, at v29. The one who sets another on an animal — Luke 10:34 and 19:35, at v24. Cilicia and Tarsus — 22:3, 21:39, 6:9, 15:23 and 15:41, at v34. The praetorium — Matthew 27:27, John 18:28, 18:33 and Philippians 1:13, at v35. The barracks — 21:34 and 22:24, at vv10, 16 and 32.

Echoes planted. The charge of the resurrection, at 24:15 and 24:21, and at 26:6 (not yet on these pages). The Pharisee, at 26:5 and Philippians 3:5 (not yet on these pages). The conscience, at 24:16. Ananias the high priest, coming down with the elders, at 24:1. “Dissensions” charged against Paul, at 24:5. The ambush on the road, again, at 25:3. “Nothing worthy of death or bonds”, at 25:25 and at 26:31 (not yet on these pages). “Lysias the tribune”, at 24:22. Felix, at 24:3, 24:22–27, and at 25:14. Festus and the province, at 25:1. “Most excellent”, at 24:3, and at 26:25 (not yet on these pages). “Questions” again, at 25:19 and at 26:3 (not yet on these pages). The prisoner, at 25:14 and 25:27, and at 28:17 (not yet on these pages). “Men, brothers”, at 28:17 (not yet on these pages). “You must bear witness in Rome,” at 27:24 and 28:14–31 (not yet on these pages). The rescue verb in the Lord’s own mouth, at 26:17 (not yet on these pages).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether “lived as a citizen” at v1 was chosen for the contrast with the citizenship of the day before. Whether Paul’s “I did not know” at v5 is sincere, sight, or irony, and which law he says Ananias broke at v3. Whether Paul’s “I am a Pharisee” at v6 is fact, tactic or both. Whether “hope and resurrection” at v6 is two things or one. What “both” counts at v8: resurrection against angel-and-spirit, or angel against spirit.
