'Come now, you rich': rotted wealth and rust as a witness; the wages of the mowers held back, crying to Jehovah of armies; 'be patient until the presence of the Lord,' the farmer and the early and late rain; the endurance of Job; 'let your yes be yes'; the elders, the oil and the prayer of faith; Elijah's prayer; and turning a sinner back. The letter ends with no farewell.
Undated in the letter, and one of the most disputed dates in the New Testament (see chapter 1).

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–3 · “Come now, you rich” — rotted wealth, moth-eaten clothes, and rust as a witness

“Come now, you rich, weep, howling over your miseries that are coming upon you. Your wealth has rotted, and your clothes have become moth-eaten.” “Come now” is the opening of James 4:13, now turned on the rich, as Jesus turns “woe to you who are rich” on them at Luke 6:24. Ololyzō, “howl,” stands in one verse. Talaipōria, “misery,” stands in two verses, here and “ruin and misery are in their ways” at Romans 3:16; it is the noun of “be wretched” at James 4:9 (dictionary). Sēpō, “rot,” stands in one verse, and so does sētobrōtos, “moth-eaten,” the moth of “where moth and devouring make them disappear” at Matthew 6:19 (dictionary). The KJV reads “Go to now, ye rich men, weep and howl”; the NWT 1984 reads “weep, howling over YOUR miseries”; the NIV reads “Your wealth has rotted, and moths have eaten your clothes.”

“Your gold and silver have rusted, and their rust will be a witness against you and will eat your flesh. Like fire you have stored up treasure in the last days.” Katioō, “rust through,” stands in one verse. “Rust” is ios, the same word as the “venom” of the tongue at James 3:8 and the “venom of asps” at Romans 3:13 (dictionary). Where “like fire” belongs is a question of punctuation: with “will eat your flesh like fire,” as most versions have it, or with “like fire you have stored up,” as the edition followed here punctuates; readings differ. The verb thēsaurizō, “store up treasure,” stands in seven verses, among them “Do not store up for yourselves treasures on the earth” at Matthew 6:19 and “you are storing up for yourself wrath in the day of wrath” at Romans 2:5 (dictionary). The KJV reads “and shall eat your flesh as it were fire. Ye have heaped treasure together for the last days”; the NWT 1984 reads “Something like fire is what YOU have stored up in the last days”; the Douay reads “You have stored up to yourselves wrath against the last days.”

Verses 4–6 · Wages held back that “cry out” to Jehovah of armies, a day of slaughter, and “the righteous one”

“Look — the wages of the workers who mowed your fields, which you have held back, cry out, and the cries of those who harvested have entered the ears of Jehovah of armies.” The law says to pay a hired man on the day, “so that he does not cry out against you to Jehovah, and it be sin in you” (Deuteronomy 24:15). Aphystereō, “hold back,” stands in one verse (dictionary); NA28 and Robinson–Pierpont read apesterēmenos, “defrauded” (the apparatus). Amaō, “mow,” stands in one verse, and so does boē, “cry”. “The ears of Jehovah of armies” is, word for word, the Greek of Isaiah 5:9, where the Hebrew reads “in my ears, Jehovah of armies” (both checked). The Greek Kyriou Sabaōth keeps the Hebrew tseva’ot, “armies,” untranslated, and Sabaōth stands in two verses; at Romans 9:29 this translation prints “Jehovah of armies,” and does so here (dictionary). The KJV reads “which is of you kept back by fraud”; the ASV reads “the Lord of Sabaoth”; the NWT 1984 reads “Jehovah of armies”; the NIV reads “the Lord Almighty.”

⚠ “You have lived in luxury on the earth and in self-indulgence; you have fattened your hearts in a day of slaughter. You have condemned, you have murdered the righteous one. Does he not oppose you?” Tryphaō, “live in luxury,” stands in one verse; spatalaō, “live in self-indulgence,” stands in two verses, the other the widow who “lives in self-indulgence” at 1 Timothy 5:6 (dictionary). “A day of slaughter” is the phrase of the Greek of Jeremiah 12:3 (yet to come in this translation, checked, with the Hebrew); sphagē, “slaughter,” stands in three verses, the others are “like a sheep he was led to slaughter” at Acts 8:32 and “sheep for slaughter” at Romans 8:36 (dictionary). Katadikazō, “condemn,” stands in four verses, among them “you would not have condemned the guiltless” at Matthew 12:7. “The righteous one” is read as any righteous poor man, as the righteous in general, or as Jesus, called “the Righteous One” at Acts 3:14 and Acts 7:52; readings differ. The last sentence is printed as a question, as the edition followed here punctuates it, and then it echoes “God opposes the proud” at James 4:6, the same verb; other editions make it a statement, “he does not resist you.” The KJV reads “Ye have condemned and killed the just; and he doth not resist you”; the NWT 1984 reads “Is he not opposing YOU?”; the NIV reads “who was not opposing you.”

Verses 7–9 · “Be patient until the presence of the Lord” — the farmer, the early and the late rain, and the judge before the doors

“Be patient, then, brothers, until the presence of the Lord. Look — the farmer waits for the precious fruit of the earth, being patient over it until it receives the early and the late rain. You too be patient; strengthen your hearts, because the presence of the Lord has drawn near.” The verb makrothymeō, “be patient,” literally “be long-tempered,” stands in nine verses; it is “Love waits long” at 1 Corinthians 13:4 and “having waited patiently” at Hebrews 6:15 (dictionary). “Presence” is parousia, printed so in Matthew 24:3 and through Paul’s letters (dictionary); “strengthen your hearts” is the phrase of “to strengthen your hearts … at the presence of our Lord Jesus” at 1 Thessalonians 3:13 (dictionary). The Greek says only “the early and the late”; “rain” is supplied, as Robinson–Pierpont writes it, and the pair is the “early rain and the late rain” of Deuteronomy 11:14 (dictionary). Proïmos, “early,” stands in one verse, and so does opsimos, “late.” The KJV reads “Be patient therefore, brethren, unto the coming of the Lord”; the NWT 1984 reads “until the presence of the Lord”; the NIV reads “patiently waiting for the autumn and spring rains.”

“Do not groan against one another, brothers, so that you may not be judged. Look — the judge is standing before the doors.” The verb stenazō, “groan,” stands in six verses; elsewhere it is “we ourselves groan within ourselves” at Romans 8:23 and Jesus who “sighed” at Mark 7:34 (dictionary). “So that you may not be judged” recalls “one lawgiver and judge” at James 4:12. “Before the doors” is Jesus’ “he is near — at the doors” at Matthew 24:33 and “at the very door” at Mark 13:29. The KJV reads “Grudge not one against another”; the ASV reads “Murmur not, brethren, one against another”; the NWT 1984 reads “Do not heave sighs against one another”; the NIV reads “The Judge is standing at the door!”

Verses 10–11 · The prophets, “the endurance of Job,” and “the end Jehovah brought about”

“Take as an example of suffering and of patience, brothers, the prophets who spoke in the name of Jehovah.” “Example” is hypodeigma (dictionary). Kakopatheia, “suffering,” with its verb stands in four verses (dictionary), and the verb returns at verse 13, “Is anyone among you suffering?” “In the name of Jehovah”: the Greek reads kyriou, and the prophets spoke in the name of the God of Israel, so this translation prints “Jehovah,” as the NWT also does; Robinson–Pierpont lacks “in” (the apparatus). The ASV reads “for an example of suffering and of patience”; the NWT 1984 reads “who spoke in the name of Jehovah”; the NIV reads “as an example of patience in the face of suffering.”

⚠ “Look — we call happy those who endured. You have heard of the endurance of Job, and you have seen the end Jehovah brought about, that Jehovah is full of compassion and merciful.” Makarizō, “call happy,” stands in two verses, the other Mary’s “all generations will call me happy” at Luke 1:48 (dictionary). “Endured” and “endurance” are the words of “the testing of your faith produces endurance” at James 1:3 and “the man who endures trial” at James 1:12 (dictionary). This is the one place the New Testament names Job (searched); his words are “Jehovah gave and Jehovah has taken” at Job 1:21, and the book ends with Jehovah blessing “the latter end of Job more than his beginning” at Job 42:12 (yet to come in this translation, checked) (encyclopedia). “The end Jehovah brought about” renders to telos kyriou, “the end of the Lord”: most read it of the outcome God gave Job, some of God’s purpose, and some early readers of the end of Jesus’ life (reported, not checked here); readings differ. Polysplanchnos, “full of compassion,” built on splanchna, stands in one verse (dictionary); its root is splanchna; “merciful” is the word of “just as your Father is merciful” at Luke 6:36, and the pair recalls the Greek of Psalm 103:8 (102:8 in the Greek numbering, checked), where the Lord is oiktirmōn kai eleēmōn, and “a God merciful and gracious” at Exodus 34:6. The KJV reads “and have seen the end of the Lord; that the Lord is very pitiful, and of tender mercy”; the NWT 1984 reads “and have seen the outcome Jehovah gave”; the NIV reads “have seen what the Lord finally brought about.”

Verse 12 · “Do not swear” — “let your yes be yes, and your no be no”

“But above all, my brothers, do not swear, either by heaven or by the earth or by any other oath; but let your yes be yes, and your no be no, so that you may not fall under judgment.” The verb omnyō, “swear,” stands in twenty verses (dictionary). The saying is Jesus’ at Matthew 5:34, “do not swear at all — not by the heaven,” and Matthew 5:37, “let your word be ‘Yes, yes — no, no’”; James gives it in a simpler form. Readers differ on whether it forbids every oath, including those in court, or the casual oaths of daily speech; the shelf does not settle it. Robinson–Pierpont reads “so that you may not fall into hypocrisy” (the apparatus). The KJV reads “lest ye fall into condemnation”; the ASV reads “that ye fall not under judgment”; the NWT 1984 reads “let YOUR Yes mean Yes, and YOUR No, No”; the NIV reads “All you need to say is a simple” yes or no.

Verses 13–16 · Prayer and praise, “the elders of the congregation,” anointing with oil, and “confess your sins to one another”

“Is anyone among you suffering? Let him pray. Is anyone cheerful? Let him sing praise. Is anyone among you sick? Let him call for the elders of the congregation, and let them pray over him, anointing him with oil in the name of the Lord.” The verb euthymeō, “be cheerful,” stands in three verses, the others Paul’s “take courage” at Acts 27:22 and 27:25 (dictionary). Psallō, “sing praise,” stands in four verses, among them “I will sing with the spirit” at 1 Corinthians 14:15 (dictionary). “Elders” is presbyteroi (dictionary), and “congregation” is ekklēsia, printed so throughout this translation (dictionary). Aleiphō, “anoint,” stands in eight verses; the Twelve “anointed with oil many who were sick, and healed them” at Mark 6:13, and oil is poured on wounds at Luke 10:34 (dictionary). “In the name of the Lord” can name God or Jesus; this translation keeps “Lord,” and the NWT prints “Jehovah.” Catholic and Orthodox churches read these verses as the ground of a rite of anointing the sick, and others read them as prayer joined to the care of the body (reported, not checked here); readings differ. The KJV reads “let him call for the elders of the church”; the Douay reads “Let him bring in the priests of the church”; the NWT 1984 reads “the older men of the congregation”; the NIV reads “Is anyone among you sick?”

“And the prayer of faith will save the one who is sick, and the Lord will raise him up; and if he has committed sins, it will be forgiven him. So confess your sins to one another, and pray for one another, so that you may be healed. The supplication of a righteous person is very strong in its working.” Kamnō, “be sick, grow weary,” stands in two verses, the other “so that you do not grow weary” at Hebrews 12:3 (dictionary). “Save” is sōzō, used of healing and of rescue, as “able to save your souls” at James 1:21; “raise up” is egeirō, the verb also used of rising from the dead. Exomologeō, “confess,” stands in nine verses, among them the crowds “confessing their sins” at Mark 1:5 (dictionary). Robinson–Pierpont reads “your faults” for “your sins” (the apparatus). “In its working” renders a participle that can mean “when it is at work” or “when it is made to work”; readings differ. The KJV reads “Confess your faults one to another” and “The effectual fervent prayer of a righteous man availeth much”; the ASV reads “availeth much in its working”; the NWT 1984 reads “A righteous man’s supplication, when it is at work, has much force.”

Verses 17–18 · Elijah, “a human who shared our nature” — three years and six months without rain

“Elijah was a human who shared our nature, and he prayed a prayer that it would not rain, and it did not rain on the land for three years and six months. And he prayed again, and the heaven gave rain, and the land sprouted its fruit.” Homoiopathēs, “sharing the same nature,” stands in two verses, the other Paul and Barnabas at Lystra, “We also are humans who share your nature,” at Acts 14:15 (dictionary). “He prayed a prayer” keeps the Greek repetition, proseuchē prosēuxato, a Hebrew way of stressing a verb. The account in 1 Kings 17–18 does not say that Elijah prayed for the drought; it has his oath before Ahab at 17:1 and his crouching on Carmel, face between his knees, at 18:42 (checked). “Three years and six months” is also Jesus’ figure at Luke 4:25, while 1 Kings 18:1 has the word of Jehovah come “in the third year” (checked); readings differ on how the two are counted (encyclopedia). The verb brechō, “rain,” stands in six verses, among them the Father who “sends rain on just and unjust” at Matthew 5:45. The KJV reads “a man subject to like passions as we are”; the NWT 1984 reads “a man with feelings like ours, and yet in prayer he prayed”; the Douay reads “and with prayer he prayed that it might not rain”; the NIV reads “for three and a half years.”

Verses 19–20 · Turning a sinner back — “will cover a multitude of sins,” and a letter that ends without a farewell

“My brothers, if anyone among you is led astray from the truth and someone turns him back, let him know that the one who turns a sinner back from the straying of his way will save his soul from death and will cover a multitude of sins.” “Led astray” is the verb of “Do not be led astray, my beloved brothers” at James 1:16 and of the sheep that “goes astray” at Matthew 18:12; “straying” is its noun. “Turns back” is epistrephō, as John “will turn many of the sons of Israel back to Jehovah their God” at Luke 1:16 (dictionary); compare “mend such a one in a spirit of gentleness” at Galatians 6:1. ⚠ Whose soul is saved, the sinner’s or the one who turns him back, the Greek leaves open, and some editions have no “his” at all (the apparatus); readings differ. Plēthos hamartiōn, “a multitude of sins,” stands in two verses; the other is “love covers a multitude of sins” at 1 Peter 4:8 (yet to come in this translation, checked), and both recall the Hebrew of Proverbs 10:12, “love covers all transgressions,” where the Greek of Proverbs reads differently (checked). Whose sins are covered is open in the same way. The KJV reads “shall save a soul from death, and shall hide a multitude of sins”; the ASV reads “and shall cover a multitude of sins”; the NWT 1984 reads “will save his soul from death”; the NIV reads “will save them from death.”

The end of the letter. James stops here, with no greetings, no names and no farewell; the last word of the Greek is “sins.” The letter opened with “Count it all joy … whenever you fall into trials” at James 1:2 and closes with a brother brought back from straying.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “Come now,” as at 4:13; “miseries,” the noun of 4:9; “rust,” the same word as the “venom” of 3:8; “store up treasure,” as at Matthew 6:19; “Jehovah of armies,” as at Romans 9:29; “self-indulgence,” as at 1 Timothy 5:6; “murdered,” as at 4:2; “presence,” as at Matthew 24:3; “strengthen your hearts,” as at 1 Thessalonians 3:13; “the early and the late rain,” as at Deuteronomy 11:14; “endurance,” as at 1:3; “call happy,” as at Luke 1:48; “Jehovah” at verses 4, 10 and 11, and “the Lord” at verses 7, 8, 14 and 15; “congregation”; “who shared our nature,” as at Acts 14:15; “prayed a prayer”; and “led astray,” as at 1:16.

Echoes paid. “Come now” from James 4:13; the wretchedness of James 4:9; the rust, the venom of James 3:8; oppose from James 4:6; the judge of James 4:12; endurance from James 1:3 and James 1:12; prayer after “you ask wrongly” at James 4:3; save from James 1:21; led astray from James 1:16; the rich of James 1:10 and James 2:6; and the congregation, after the “synagogue” of James 2:2.

The letter as a whole. James is written to “the twelve tribes in the Dispersion” (James 1:1) and names no city and no companion of the writer. It moves by short sayings rather than a single argument, and its recurring words (“endurance,” “perfect,” “double-souled,” “doers,” “the rich,” “the tongue,” “wisdom from above”) are printed the same way in every chapter of this translation.

Next in this book: none; James ends here.
