🕰
Moses argues from eyewitness: the fathers, not their children, are the ones who watched the discipline of Jehovah — the sea flooded over Egypt's army, and the ground gaping under Dathan and Abiram. He then contrasts the two countries as agriculture: Egypt is sown and watered by foot at ground level, while the land ahead is hills and valleys that drinks rain from the sky, watched by God from the year's beginning to its end. Rain becomes the whole mechanism of blessing and curse — the early and late rains for grain, new wine and oil if Israel listens; a shut sky and a barren ground if the heart is seduced into serving other gods. The second paragraph of the Shema follows, re-issuing chapter 6's instruction to bind, teach and write the words, and the chapter closes by setting a blessing and a curse on two named mountains, Gerizim and Ebal, before either has been explained.
Still the plains of Moab in the fortieth year (1:3), continuing the second address without a break from chapter 10; no new narrated event. What the chapter REPORTS ranges much wider: the Sea of Reeds and the plagues (Exodus 7-14, already on these pages) roughly forty years earlier, and the rebellion of Dathan and Abiram (Numbers 16, already on these pages) somewhere in the wilderness years between — retold here without Korah, whom Numbers names first. The chapter is prospective at its end rather than retrospective: vv29-30 look forward to a ceremony at Gerizim and Ebal that Deuteronomy 27 will prescribe and Joshua 8 will perform, neither yet on these pages, and vv24-25 state the borders that Joshua 1:3-5 (already on these pages) will quote back in the perfect tense once Moses is dead.

Deuteronomy 11 — The Mister Translation

1
וְאָהַבְתָּ, אֵת יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ; וְשָׁמַרְתָּ מִשְׁמַרְתּוֹ, וְחֻקֹּתָיו וּמִשְׁפָּטָיו וּמִצְוֺתָיו--כָּל-הַיָּמִים.
And you shall love Jehovah your God, and keep his charge — his statutes and his judgments and his commandments — all the days.note
Y amarás a Jehová tu Dios, y guardarás su encargo —sus estatutos y sus juicios y sus mandamientos— todos los días.
2
וִידַעְתֶּם, הַיּוֹם, כִּי לֹא אֶת-בְּנֵיכֶם אֲשֶׁר לֹא-יָדְעוּ וַאֲשֶׁר לֹא-רָאוּ, אֶת-מוּסַר יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם:  אֶת-גָּדְלוֹ--אֶת-יָדוֹ הַחֲזָקָה, וּזְרֹעוֹ הַנְּטוּיָה.
And know today — for it is not with your children, who have not known and have not seen — the discipline of Jehovah your God: his greatness, his strong hand and his outstretched arm,note
Y sepan hoy —porque no es con sus hijos, que no han conocido ni han visto— la disciplina de Jehová su Dios: su grandeza, su mano fuerte y su brazo extendido,
3
וְאֶת-אֹתֹתָיו, וְאֶת-מַעֲשָׂיו, אֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה, בְּתוֹךְ מִצְרָיִם--לְפַרְעֹה מֶלֶךְ-מִצְרַיִם, וּלְכָל-אַרְצוֹ.
and his signs and his deeds that he did in the middle of Egypt, to Pharaoh king of Egypt and to all his land;note
y sus señales y sus obras que hizo en medio de Egipto, al faraón rey de Egipto y a toda su tierra;
4
וַאֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה לְחֵיל מִצְרַיִם לְסוּסָיו וּלְרִכְבּוֹ, אֲשֶׁר הֵצִיף אֶת-מֵי יַם-סוּף עַל-פְּנֵיהֶם, בְּרָדְפָם, אַחֲרֵיכֶם; וַיְאַבְּדֵם יְהוָה, עַד הַיּוֹם הַזֶּה.
and what he did to the army of Egypt, to its horses and to its chariotry, when he flooded the waters of the Sea of Reeds over their faces as they chased after you — and Jehovah destroyed them, to this day;note
y lo que hizo al ejército de Egipto, a sus caballos y a sus carros, cuando desbordó sobre sus rostros las aguas del mar de los Juncos mientras los perseguían a ustedes —y Jehová los destruyó, hasta el día de hoy—;
5
וַאֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה לָכֶם, בַּמִּדְבָּר, עַד-בֹּאֲכֶם, עַד-הַמָּקוֹם הַזֶּה.
and what he did to you in the wilderness, until you came to this place;note
y lo que hizo con ustedes en el desierto, hasta que llegaron a este lugar;
6
וַאֲשֶׁר עָשָׂה לְדָתָן וְלַאֲבִירָם, בְּנֵי אֱלִיאָב בֶּן-רְאוּבֵן, אֲשֶׁר פָּצְתָה הָאָרֶץ אֶת-פִּיהָ, וַתִּבְלָעֵם וְאֶת-בָּתֵּיהֶם וְאֶת-אָהֳלֵיהֶם--וְאֵת כָּל-הַיְקוּם אֲשֶׁר בְּרַגְלֵיהֶם, בְּקֶרֶב כָּל-יִשְׂרָאֵל.
and what he did to Dathan and Abiram, the sons of Eliab son of Reuben — how the earth gaped its mouth and swallowed them, and their households and their tents, and every living thing that was at their feet, in the middle of all Israel.note
y lo que hizo a Datán y Abiram, hijos de Eliab hijo de Rubén —cómo la tierra abrió de par en par su boca y se los tragó, con sus casas y sus tiendas, y todo ser viviente que estaba a sus pies, en medio de todo Israel.
7
כִּי עֵינֵיכֶם הָרֹאֹת, אֶת-כָּל-מַעֲשֵׂה יְהוָה הַגָּדֹל, אֲשֶׁר, עָשָׂה.
For it is your own eyes that have seen every great deed of Jehovah, which he did.note
Porque son los ojos de ustedes los que han visto toda la gran obra de Jehová, la que hizo.
8
וּשְׁמַרְתֶּם, אֶת-כָּל-הַמִּצְוָה, אֲשֶׁר אָנֹכִי מְצַוְּךָ, הַיּוֹם--לְמַעַן תֶּחֶזְקוּ, וּבָאתֶם וִירִשְׁתֶּם אֶת-הָאָרֶץ, אֲשֶׁר אַתֶּם עֹבְרִים שָׁמָּה, לְרִשְׁתָּהּ.
So keep every commandment that I am commanding you today, so that you may be strong, and go in and take possession of the land that you are crossing over to take;note
Guarden, pues, todo el mandamiento que hoy te mando, para que sean fuertes, y entren y tomen posesión de la tierra a la que están pasando para tomarla;
9
וּלְמַעַן תַּאֲרִיכוּ יָמִים עַל-הָאֲדָמָה, אֲשֶׁר נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה לַאֲבֹתֵיכֶם לָתֵת לָהֶם וּלְזַרְעָם--אֶרֶץ זָבַת חָלָב, וּדְבָשׁ.  {ס}
and so that you may lengthen your days on the ground that Jehovah swore to your fathers to give to them and to their seed — a land flowing with milk and honey. {S}note
y para que alarguen los días sobre el suelo que Jehová juró a sus padres darles a ellos y a su descendencia —una tierra que mana leche y miel—. {S}
10
כִּי הָאָרֶץ, אֲשֶׁר אַתָּה בָא-שָׁמָּה לְרִשְׁתָּהּ--לֹא כְאֶרֶץ מִצְרַיִם הִוא, אֲשֶׁר יְצָאתֶם מִשָּׁם:  אֲשֶׁר תִּזְרַע אֶת-זַרְעֲךָ, וְהִשְׁקִיתָ בְרַגְלְךָ כְּגַן הַיָּרָק.
For the land that you are going in to take is not like the land of Egypt, which you came out of, where you would sow your seed and water it with your foot, like a garden of greens.note
Porque la tierra a la que entras para tomarla no es como la tierra de Egipto, de donde salieron, donde sembrabas tu semilla y la regabas con tu pie, como un huerto de hortalizas.
11
וְהָאָרֶץ, אֲשֶׁר אַתֶּם עֹבְרִים שָׁמָּה לְרִשְׁתָּהּ--אֶרֶץ הָרִים, וּבְקָעֹת; לִמְטַר הַשָּׁמַיִם, תִּשְׁתֶּה-מָּיִם.
But the land that you are crossing over to take is a land of hills and valleys; it drinks water from the rain of the heavens —note
Pero la tierra a la que están pasando para tomarla es tierra de montes y valles; bebe agua de la lluvia de los cielos —
12
אֶרֶץ, אֲשֶׁר-יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ דֹּרֵשׁ אֹתָהּ:  תָּמִיד, עֵינֵי יְהוָה אֱלֹהֶיךָ בָּהּ--מֵרֵשִׁית הַשָּׁנָה, וְעַד אַחֲרִית שָׁנָה.  {ס}
a land that Jehovah your God seeks after. The eyes of Jehovah your God are on it continually, from the beginning of the year to the end of the year. {S}note
una tierra que Jehová tu Dios busca. Los ojos de Jehová tu Dios están sobre ella de continuo, desde el principio del año hasta el fin del año. {S}
13
וְהָיָה, אִם-שָׁמֹעַ תִּשְׁמְעוּ אֶל-מִצְוֺתַי, אֲשֶׁר אָנֹכִי מְצַוֶּה אֶתְכֶם, הַיּוֹם--לְאַהֲבָה אֶת-יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם, וּלְעָבְדוֹ, בְּכָל-לְבַבְכֶם, וּבְכָל-נַפְשְׁכֶם.
And it shall be, if you truly listen to my commandments that I am commanding you today, to love Jehovah your God and to serve him with all your heart and with all your soul,note
Y sucederá que, si de veras escuchan mis mandamientos que hoy les mando, amando a Jehová su Dios y sirviéndole con todo su corazón y con toda su alma,
14
וְנָתַתִּי מְטַר-אַרְצְכֶם בְּעִתּוֹ, יוֹרֶה וּמַלְקוֹשׁ; וְאָסַפְתָּ דְגָנֶךָ, וְתִירֹשְׁךָ וְיִצְהָרֶךָ.
then I will give the rain of your land in its season, the early rain and the late rain, and you will gather in your grain and your new wine and your fresh oil.note
yo daré la lluvia de la tierra de ustedes a su tiempo, la lluvia temprana y la tardía, y recogerás tu grano y tu vino nuevo y tu aceite fresco.
15
וְנָתַתִּי עֵשֶׂב בְּשָׂדְךָ, לִבְהֶמְתֶּךָ; וְאָכַלְתָּ, וְשָׂבָעְתָּ.
And I will give grass in your field for your cattle, and you will eat and be satisfied.note
Y daré hierba en tu campo para tu ganado, y comerás y quedarás saciado.
16
הִשָּׁמְרוּ לָכֶם, פֶּן יִפְתֶּה לְבַבְכֶם; וְסַרְתֶּם, וַעֲבַדְתֶּם אֱלֹהִים אֲחֵרִים, וְהִשְׁתַּחֲוִיתֶם, לָהֶם.
Watch yourselves, or your heart will be seduced, and you will turn aside and serve other gods and bow down to them;note
Cuídense, no sea que su corazón se deje seducir, y se aparten y sirvan a otros dioses y se inclinen ante ellos;
17
וְחָרָה אַף-יְהוָה בָּכֶם, וְעָצַר אֶת-הַשָּׁמַיִם וְלֹא-יִהְיֶה מָטָר, וְהָאֲדָמָה, לֹא תִתֵּן אֶת-יְבוּלָהּ; וַאֲבַדְתֶּם מְהֵרָה, מֵעַל הָאָרֶץ הַטֹּבָה, אֲשֶׁר יְהוָה, נֹתֵן לָכֶם.
and the anger of Jehovah will burn against you, and he will shut up the heavens and there will be no rain, and the ground will not give its yield, and you will perish quickly from off the good land that Jehovah is giving you.note
y arda la ira de Jehová contra ustedes, y cierre los cielos y no haya lluvia, y el suelo no dé su producto, y perezcan pronto de sobre la buena tierra que Jehová les da.
18
וְשַׂמְתֶּם אֶת-דְּבָרַי אֵלֶּה, עַל-לְבַבְכֶם וְעַל-נַפְשְׁכֶם; וּקְשַׁרְתֶּם אֹתָם לְאוֹת עַל-יֶדְכֶם, וְהָיוּ לְטוֹטָפֹת בֵּין עֵינֵיכֶם.
So set these words of mine on your heart and on your soul, and bind them for a sign on your hand, and let them be frontlets between your eyes.note
Pongan, pues, estas palabras mías sobre su corazón y sobre su alma, y átenlas por señal en su mano, y sean por frontales entre sus ojos.
19
וְלִמַּדְתֶּם אֹתָם אֶת-בְּנֵיכֶם, לְדַבֵּר בָּם, בְּשִׁבְתְּךָ בְּבֵיתֶךָ וּבְלֶכְתְּךָ בַדֶּרֶךְ, וּבְשָׁכְבְּךָ וּבְקוּמֶךָ.
And teach them to your children, speaking of them when you sit in your house and when you walk on the road, and when you lie down and when you get up.note
Y enséñenlas a sus hijos, hablando de ellas cuando te sientes en tu casa y cuando andes por el camino, y cuando te acuestes y cuando te levantes.
20
וּכְתַבְתָּם עַל-מְזוּזוֹת בֵּיתֶךָ, וּבִשְׁעָרֶיךָ.
And write them on the doorposts of your house and on your gates,note
Y escríbelas en las jambas de tu casa y en tus puertas,
21
לְמַעַן יִרְבּוּ יְמֵיכֶם, וִימֵי בְנֵיכֶם, עַל הָאֲדָמָה, אֲשֶׁר נִשְׁבַּע יְהוָה לַאֲבֹתֵיכֶם לָתֵת לָהֶם--כִּימֵי הַשָּׁמַיִם, עַל-הָאָרֶץ.  {ס}
so that your days and your children’s days may be many on the ground that Jehovah swore to your fathers to give them — as the days of the heavens over the earth. {S}note
para que se multipliquen los días de ustedes y los días de sus hijos sobre el suelo que Jehová juró a sus padres darles —como los días de los cielos sobre la tierra—. {S}
22
כִּי אִם-שָׁמֹר תִּשְׁמְרוּן אֶת-כָּל-הַמִּצְוָה הַזֹּאת, אֲשֶׁר אָנֹכִי מְצַוֶּה אֶתְכֶם--לַעֲשֹׂתָהּ:  לְאַהֲבָה אֶת-יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם, לָלֶכֶת בְּכָל-דְּרָכָיו--וּלְדָבְקָה-בוֹ.
For if you carefully keep all this commandment that I am commanding you, to do it — to love Jehovah your God, to walk in all his ways, and to cling to him —note
Porque si guardan cuidadosamente todo este mandamiento que les mando, para hacerlo —amando a Jehová su Dios, andando en todos sus caminos y aferándose a él—,
23
וְהוֹרִישׁ יְהוָה אֶת-כָּל-הַגּוֹיִם הָאֵלֶּה, מִלִּפְנֵיכֶם; וִירִשְׁתֶּם גּוֹיִם, גְּדֹלִים וַעֲצֻמִים מִכֶּם.
then Jehovah will dispossess all these nations from before you, and you will dispossess nations greater and mightier than you.note
Jehová desposeerá a todas estas naciones delante de ustedes, y desposeerán naciones más grandes y más fuertes que ustedes.
24
כָּל-הַמָּקוֹם, אֲשֶׁר תִּדְרֹךְ כַּף-רַגְלְכֶם בּוֹ--לָכֶם יִהְיֶה:  מִן-הַמִּדְבָּר וְהַלְּבָנוֹן מִן-הַנָּהָר נְהַר-פְּרָת, וְעַד הַיָּם הָאַחֲרוֹן--יִהְיֶה, גְּבֻלְכֶם.
Every place that the sole of your foot treads on shall be yours: from the wilderness and the Lebanon, from the River, the river Euphrates, as far as the sea at your back, shall be your border.note
Todo lugar que pise la planta de su pie será de ustedes: desde el desierto y el Líbano, desde el Río, el río Éufrates, hasta el mar que queda a sus espaldas, será su territorio.
25
לֹא-יִתְיַצֵּב אִישׁ, בִּפְנֵיכֶם:  פַּחְדְּכֶם וּמוֹרַאֲכֶם יִתֵּן יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם, עַל-פְּנֵי כָל-הָאָרֶץ אֲשֶׁר תִּדְרְכוּ-בָהּ, כַּאֲשֶׁר, דִּבֶּר לָכֶם.  {ס}
No one will stand up in front of you. Jehovah your God will put the dread of you and the fear of you on the face of all the land that you tread on, just as he spoke to you. {S}note
Nadie se plantará delante de ustedes. Jehová su Dios pondrá el terror de ustedes y el miedo de ustedes sobre la faz de toda la tierra que pisen, tal como les habló. {S}
26
רְאֵה, אָנֹכִי נֹתֵן לִפְנֵיכֶם--הַיּוֹם:  בְּרָכָה, וּקְלָלָה.
Look — I am setting before you today a blessing and a curse:note
Mira —hoy pongo delante de ustedes bendición y maldición—:
27
אֶת-הַבְּרָכָה--אֲשֶׁר תִּשְׁמְעוּ, אֶל-מִצְוֺת יְהוָה אֱלֹהֵיכֶם, אֲשֶׁר אָנֹכִי מְצַוֶּה אֶתְכֶם, הַיּוֹם.
the blessing, if you listen to the commandments of Jehovah your God that I am commanding you today;note
la bendición, si escuchan los mandamientos de Jehová su Dios que hoy les mando;

Verses 1–3 · Musar — discipline, chastisement or instruction? The word for what one generation watched and the next did not

The chapter opens on ve-ahavta, the same verb in the same form that opens the Shema at Deuteronomy 6:5 — second person SINGULAR — and then turns plural in the very next word of v2, vi-yda’tem. ★ Counted form by form, these thirty-two verses fall into twelve alternating blocks of singular and plural address — eleven switches in all. Two of the eleven fall MID-VERSE (v14 and v19), and three further verses carry a single form out of step inside an otherwise uniform block (v8, v10 and v26) — a distinction worth keeping, because the two kinds are not the same phenomenon. ⚠ That is dense, but it is not a record, and the near-rival is the whole point. Run one crude uniform count across every Deuteronomy chapter on these pages and Deuteronomy 6 comes out marginally AHEAD of this one, with chapters 7, 4, 5 and 9 just behind. So Deuteronomy 11 is not the chapter that switches most — it is the chapter where the switching becomes EXPLICABLE, because here, uniquely so far, the source being quoted is itself on these pages to be set beside it. The phenomenon is treated on its own at vv18–21, where the reason for it finally shows.

★ Musar is the wisdom-literature word for correction, and it is the object of the sentence, not its subject. The noun is built on yasar, to chastise or correct, and the shelf divides on how hard to hit it: the KJV, the ASV and the Geneva 1599 all print ‘chastisement’, while the NWT 1984 softens to ‘discipline’ and the NWT 2013 keeps that. This translation keeps ‘discipline’ because English ‘chastisement’ has drifted toward the merely verbal, and what the verse then lists — a strong hand, an outstretched arm, an army drowned, a ground that opens — is not a scolding. ★ The dictionary entry notes that the VERB of this root is said of God directly at Deuteronomy 8:5, already on these pages; here the NOUN arrives, and its content is the whole of the last forty years.

⚠ The sentence has no main clause, and all eight versions fetched for it repair that silently. All eight versions fetched for this verse patch it, each in its own way. The Hebrew of v2 runs: and know today — for not your children who have not known and have not seen — the discipline of Jehovah your God, and then piles up five more objects across vv3–6, each introduced by and what he did, before the thought finally lands at v7. There is no verb governing the parenthesis about the children. The KJV supplies one — ‘for I speak not with your children’ — and the ASV and the Geneva 1599 do the same. On the Spanish shelf the RV 1909 supplies the identical verb. The NWT 1984 and the TNM 1987 bracket their supplied words visibly, which is the honest way to do it, and the NWT 2013 and TNM 2019 smooth the brackets away into a finished sentence. This translation leaves the gap open with dashes rather than filling it, because the piled-up objects are the effect: the list of things seen keeps going for six verses, which is the argument.

⚠ And the argument sits oddly next to two claims already on these pages. Deuteronomy 5:3 insists the covenant was made not with the fathers but with us, we, these here today, all of us alive — addressed to a generation that had not stood at Horeb. Deuteronomy 1:39 promised the land to the children who today have no knowledge of good and evil. Now the same speech tells the same audience that its children are the ones who did NOT see. Deuteronomy does not reconcile these; it keeps sliding the boundary of ‘you’ forward and backward across the generations, and the sliding is closer to the point than any one of the three statements.

Verses 4–5 · Hetsif — the verb for what the sea did to Egypt occurs exactly once in the Bible, and it is not ‘covered’

★ The word for what the water did is a one-off. Hetsif — the causative of tsuf, to float or flow over — appears in exactly one verse in the whole Hebrew Bible, this one, checked consonantally against all 929 archived Old Testament chapters. The plain stem of the same root is what makes the axe-head float in 2 Kings 6:6 (not yet on these pages). So the sentence is not ‘he covered them’ and not ‘he drowned them’: it is he made the water flood over — a causative built on a verb of floating, used once, for this.

⚠ ‘Over their faces’ is what the Hebrew says, and it is kept. Al-peneihem is the ordinary idiom for ‘over the surface of’ — but the noun is face, and it is the busiest noun in the chapter after land: it stands seven times in six verses, and this is the only one of the seven where it is a face. The other six are all positional — from before you at v23, in front of you and on the face of all the land at v25, and before you at v26 and v32. Hebrew builds its prepositions of place out of the word for a face, which is why a drowning and a boundary line can be described with the same noun. The KJV and the ASV keep it visible here; the modern versions generally do not. ★ The Spanish shelf splits on it along the usual seam: the TNM 1987 keeps the faces and the TNM 2019 deletes them, having the waters simply swallow the army instead.

The Sea of Reeds keeps this project’s established rendering rather than ‘Red Sea’; the encyclopedia entry lays out the three candidate crossing zones and declines to choose among them.

Verses 6–7 · Dathan and Abiram without Korah — the rebellion retold with its ringleader deleted, in the Flood’s own vocabulary

⚠⚠ Korah is not here. Numbers 16:1 names him FIRST — Korah son of Izhar son of Kohath son of Levi, and Dathan and Abiram sons of Eliab, and On son of Peleth, sons of Reuben — and Numbers 26:10 says explicitly that the earth swallowed them together with Korah. Deuteronomy names the two Reubenites and stops. Nothing is denied; a name is simply not said. This project does not choose an explanation, but it is worth noticing what the omission does to the sermon: with Korah gone, the story is no longer about a Levite contesting the priesthood, and becomes purely about laymen contesting Moses — which is what an audience about to be handed a land needs to hear. ★ Numbers 26:11, already on these pages, adds the other half nobody expects: the sons of Korah did not die.

★ The verb for the opening ground is the PREDICTED one, not the narrated one, and it stands in only three verses in the Bible. Patsah with ‘its mouth’ — to gape, to open wide — is at Genesis 4:11, where the ground opens its mouth to take Abel’s blood; at Numbers 16:30, where Moses PREDICTS what will happen; and here. But the narration of the event itself, at Numbers 16:32 and again at Numbers 26:10, uses the ordinary verb patach, to open. Deuteronomy reaches back past the report to the prophecy and reuses Moses’ own word — which is defensible, since Moses is the one speaking. ⚠ No English reader can see any of this from the shelf, because seeing it would take a distinct word for the distinct verb: at this verse the KJV, the ASV, the Geneva 1599 and the Douay-Rheims all print simply that the earth opened its mouth — the same word they use for the ordinary verb in Numbers. This translation uses gaped here and reserves ‘opened’ for patach, so that the two Hebrew verbs stay two English verbs.

★ And what went down with them is a Flood word. Ha-yequm — the standing-things, everything that exists — is in exactly three verses: Genesis 7:4 and Genesis 7:23, both times for what the Flood wiped off the face of the ground, and here. Deuteronomy is describing a hole in the desert with the only noun the Bible otherwise reserves for the drowning of the world. The shelf mostly renders it as a quantity rather than a category — the KJV has ‘all the substance that was in their possession’, the Geneva and the Douay-Rheims both ‘substance’, and the NIV ‘every living thing that belonged to them’. The ASV alone keeps the word’s category-sense with ‘every living thing that followed them’. ⚠ The Hebrew adds at their feet, which is the first of this chapter’s three feet — see v10 and v24.

Verses 8–9 · A plural verse with one singular word inside it — the first seam, and the clue to all of them

⚠ Verse 8 is plural from end to end — keep, you may be strong, go in, take possession, you are crossing, all of them plural — with a single exception buried in the middle: asher anokhi metzavvekha ha-yom, ‘that I am commanding YOU (singular) today.’ The same three-word formula recurs at v13, v22, v27 and v28 — and in every one of those it is PLURAL (metzavveh etkhem). So the singular here is not the chapter’s habit; it is one word out of step with the four other times the same phrase is said.

★ And it is exactly the wording of Deuteronomy 6:6, singular, in the chapter this one is about to quote outright. The suggestion is held until vv18–21, where it can be tested rather than asserted.

★ Only one version on the shelf catches it, and it is not the King James. Modern English has no singular/plural ‘you’, so every recent version is structurally blind here — but the KJV and the ASV still had thou and ye, and only the ASV uses them: it prints ‘which I command thee this day’ inside a verse that is otherwise all ‘ye’, and then correctly switches to ‘which I command you’ at v13 and v22 where the Hebrew is plural. The KJV had the same equipment and flattened all three to ‘you’. ⚠ The same verse shows the ASV winning a second time: the Hebrew has kol ha-mitzvah, all the commandment, SINGULAR — the collective noun this project has flagged since Deuteronomy 6:1 — and the ASV keeps it singular where the KJV prints ‘all the commandments’. Two literal wins in one verse for the version with the smaller reputation, which is the standing reason this project checks the ASV and the NWT before it trusts the familiar rendering.

Verses 10–12 · Watered with the foot — what Egypt’s irrigation actually was, and the land that drinks rain instead

★ ‘You would water it with your foot’ is the chapter’s most argued-over phrase, and the shelf splits three ways. The Hebrew is plain and the puzzle is agricultural, not lexical: what is a foot doing in an irrigation ditch? Three readings have long standing. (1) Foot-powered lifting gear — a treadle-driven water-wheel or shaduf worked by the feet. (2) Feet on the dykes — a farmer walking the channels of a Nile-flood basin, breaking and closing the earth walls with his heel to send water down one row and not another. (3) A euphemism, since ‘foot’ is used that way elsewhere in Hebrew. This translation keeps the plain words and does not choose, but reading (2) is the one that needs no machinery Egypt may or may not have had, and it fits the contrast being drawn: Egypt’s water is at ground level and has to be pushed, and the new land’s water falls out of the sky and cannot be.

⚠ The Douay-Rheims deletes the foot entirely — it has water simply brought in to the land ‘after the manner of gardens’ — and this is a Latin divergence rather than a Hebrew one, the pattern this project keeps finding in that version. The KJV, ASV and Geneva all keep the foot; so do the NWT 1984 (‘irrigating with your foot’) and both the RV 1909 and the RV60 («regabas con tu pie»). ⚠ The NVI reads «regarlas a pie», which in Spanish says the farmer went there ON foot — a different claim from watering WITH the foot, and the one place on either shelf where the image is not just softened but replaced.

★ The two verses reverse a word-pair from three chapters back, and move the water from underground to overhead. Deuteronomy 8:7, already on these pages, calls the same country a land of watercourses, springs and deeps, coming out in valley and in hill — ba-biq’ah u-va-har. Here at v11 it is eretz harim u-veqa’ot, a land of hills and valleys: the identical pair of nouns, in the opposite order, with the water no longer rising from below but falling from above. Same speaker, same land, three chapters apart. Deuteronomy 8’s land supplies itself; this one has to be given rain, and v17 is about to say what happens when it is not.

⚠ ‘Seeks after’ refuses eleven of the twelve versions fetched for this verse. All eleven render doresh otah as CARING for the land — the KJV and the ASV with ‘careth for’. The Geneva 1599 prints the same verb a beat plainer, and the NIV modernises it. The NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 both make it continuous. On the Spanish side the RV 1909, the RV60 and the NVI all use one verb of caring, and both editions of the TNM its progressive. ★ The twelfth is the interesting one. The Douay-Rheims, following the Vulgate, has God VISIT the land — and of all twelve it is the one that lands nearest the Hebrew, since visiting is something done TO a place by someone with a reason to check on it. But darash is the verb for seeking, inquiring after, requiring an account — the word behind midrash. It is the verb for consulting an oracle and for a judge demanding an answer. Applied to a piece of ground it is strange in Hebrew too, and ‘cares for’ makes it unstrange. The reading kept here is the one the next clause supports: a land God is watching, all year, the way one watches something one intends to ask about.

⚠ A scribal oddity worth flagging, since the eye slides over it. Verse 12 spells reshit, beginning, WITHOUT its aleph — me-reshit for the expected me-re’shit — and a consonantal search finds that this is the only verse in the Hebrew Bible that spells it that way; the other six write it full. Nothing turns on it, and no translation could show it. It is here because a defective spelling in a Torah verse is the sort of thing a Masoretic apparatus notices and a reader never does.

Verses 13–15 · Yoreh and malqosh — the early rain and the late rain, named here for the first time in the Bible

★ This is the first appearance of malqosh, the late rain, in the Bible — it stands in eight verses altogether, and the other seven are all later: Jeremiah 3:3 and 5:24, Hosea 6:3, Joel 2:23, Zechariah 10:1, Job 29:23 and Proverbs 16:15, none of them yet on these pages. Three of those seven pair it with yoreh exactly as this verse does, so the phrase Deuteronomy coins here becomes the standard way Hebrew names the agricultural year’s two ends: the autumn rain that softens the ground for ploughing, and the spring rain that fills the grain before harvest. Miss either and there is no crop, which is why they are named separately.

⚠ The shelf divides on whether to translate the seasons or the sequence. The KJV prints ‘the first rain and the latter rain’ and the ASV ‘the former rain and the latter rain’; the Geneva 1599 shortens to ‘the first rain and the latter’. The modern versions name the seasons instead — the NIV ‘autumn and spring rains’, the NWT 1984 the same pair. Both are defensible and each loses something: the seasonal rendering tells a reader what the words mean and hides that Hebrew names them by ORDER, not by month. This translation keeps early and late.

★ The triad dagan, tirosh, yitzhar is not a list of foods but the standard Hebrew shorthand for a whole harvest — grain, new wine, fresh oil. Each of the three is the RAW product rather than the finished one: tirosh is must, wine still in the vat, not yayin; yitzhar is oil fresh from the press, not shemen. The KJV’s ‘thy corn, and thy wine, and thine oil’ is the familiar version of the phrase and flattens all three — and its ‘corn’ is the older English word for grain generally, not maize, which is a real trap for a modern reader. The ASV corrects to ‘grain’ and ‘new wine’.

⚠ The number changes in the middle of v14, and the seam runs between the rain and the barn. The rain is given to a plural — the rain of YOUR (pl.) land — and then, without a break, and YOU (sg.) will gather in YOUR (sg.) grain and YOUR (sg.) new wine and YOUR (sg.) fresh oil, singular through the end of v15, including your field and your cattle. Weather falls on a nation; a harvest is carried in by one household. It is the cleanest case in the chapter of what vv18–21 works out.

Verses 16–17 · A heart that can be seduced, and a sky that can be shut — the promise of v14 run backwards

Pen yifteh levavkhem — the verb patah is not ‘be deceived’ in the sense of being handed false information; it is to be enticed, talked round, opened up. It is used of persuading someone into bed and of talking someone out of a decision. ‘Seduced’ is chosen here for that reason, and because the danger the sermon keeps naming is not an argument but an attraction. The KJV and Geneva print ‘deceived’; the ASV ‘deceived’ likewise. None of them is wrong; all of them make the heart a victim of a lie rather than of a preference.

★ Verse 17 is v14 played in reverse, item for item. Verse 14 gives rain in its season, so grain and wine and oil can be gathered; verse 17 shuts the heavens so there is no rain, and the ground gives nothing. The same two nouns govern both — shamayim and adamah, the sky above and the soil below — and the mechanism of the curse is not an invading army but the withdrawal of the one thing v11 said this land depends on and Egypt did not. The threat is agricultural because the argument has been agricultural since v10.

Verses 18–21 · The second Shema paragraph — and why the ‘you’ keeps changing number: the seam is a quotation mark

★★ This is the answer to the question Deuteronomy 9 opened and Deuteronomy 10 could not settle, and it is mechanical rather than thematic. Those two chapters found the grammatical number switching mid-verse and asked what governed it; chapter 10 tested the obvious explanation — that the singular carries one kind of content and the plural another — and reported it failed. This passage shows why it failed. The number changes where the text stops rewriting and starts copying.

Set the two side by side. Deuteronomy 6:6–9, already on these pages, is singular throughout. Here:

— v18 REPHRASES 6:6 and 6:8 (set these words on your heart AND ON YOUR SOUL, a clause 6:6 does not have) — and every word of it is plural.
— v19 opens with a rephrase, and you shall TEACH them to your children where 6:7 had and you shall repeat them to your sons, a different verb — plural. Then, mid-verse, it lifts 6:7’s four time-phrases word for word: when you sit in your house and when you walk on the road, and when you lie down and when you get up. Those four are singular, exactly as they stand in chapter 6.
— v20 is singular, and it is Deuteronomy 6:9 reproduced letter for letter, the two verses differing in nothing but the spelling of mezuzot (chapter 6 writes it short, this chapter writes it full).

So the rule holds across the whole passage: what was rewritten was pluralised, and what was quoted verbatim kept the singular it was already in. That also retro-explains the stray singular at v8 — a three-word formula that is plural in this chapter’s own four other uses of it, and singular in 6:6. ⚠ Honest limits. This accounts for the seams inside vv14–15, 19–20 and v8, where a source is identifiable. It does NOT account for v1, v10, v12, v26 or v29, where the singular arrives with nothing obvious being quoted — though v10 and v15 share a feature worth watching, in that the singular turns up precisely where the text narrows to one man’s own seed, field, cattle and doorway. One chapter cannot establish that; it can only put it on the record to be checked.

★ Frontlets: a word nobody can translate, and a location the modern shelf quietly moves. Totafot stands in exactly three verses — Exodus 13:16, Deuteronomy 6:8 and here — and its origin is genuinely unknown; ‘frontlets’ is a placeholder the English tradition has kept since Tyndale rather than a translation. Read literally these verses are the basis of tefillin, the boxes of Torah text bound to arm and head in Jewish practice to this day. ⚠ The thing to watch on the shelf is not the untranslatable noun but the PLACE: the Hebrew says between your eyes, and the NIV moves it to the forehead, as do the NWT 2013 and the NVI — which is where the practice actually puts the box, and therefore a rendering of the custom rather than of the clause. The KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims, the NWT 1984, the RV 1909 and the RV60 all keep the eyes.

⚠ And the Living Bible does not render the word at all here, printing simply ‘commandments’ bound to hand and forehead — both the strange noun and the strange location gone in one stroke. That is the version behaving as it always does; it is noted rather than scolded.

Verses 22–23 · Cling — the marriage verb aimed at God for the second time on these pages

★ Davaq is the verb of Genesis 2:24, where a man leaves his father and mother and CLINGS to his wife, and of Ruth 1:14, where Ruth clings to Naomi as Orpah lets go. Deuteronomy 10:20, last chapter, was the first place this project had reached where it is aimed at God. This is the second, one chapter later — and it is not a repetition but an escalation: at 10:20 clinging stood in a list of four things owed (fear, serve, cling, swear), while here it is the LAST of three infinitives that spell out what keeping the commandment means, and the whole conditional promise of v23 hangs off it. Whatever the word does elsewhere, in Deuteronomy it has become the summary term for the relationship.

⚠ The RV60 loses it in the same way it lost it last chapter, printing a verb of following where the RV 1909 had a verb of joining — a second instance of the pattern flagged at 10:20, in the very next chapter.

Verses 24–25 · The sea at your back — a Hebrew compass with no north in it, and the borders Joshua will quote in a different tense

★ ‘The sea at your back’ is this chapter’s flagship departure, and it is a compass, not a description. The Hebrew is ha-yam ha-acharon, literally the hindmost sea — from the root that also gives ‘afterward’ and ‘the end’. Biblical Hebrew orients a speaker FACING EAST, toward the sunrise: so qedem, in front, means east and also ‘long ago’, and achor, behind, means west and also ‘later’. The Mediterranean is therefore the sea behind you. All thirteen versions on the shelf give a direction or a name, and none keeps the body it is measured from: the KJV has ‘the uttermost sea’, the NWT 1984, the NWT 2013, the RV60 and both editions of the TNM the western sea, the Douay-Rheims the same. ★ Two get closest without arriving: the ASV’s ‘the hinder sea’ and the RV 1909’s «la mar postrera» both keep the root’s backward sense, but neither says whose back — and the NIV, the TLB and the NVI all name it the Mediterranean outright, which is certainly the body of water and certainly not what the sentence says. This rendering keeps the body facing east, because the compass it implies is a real feature of the language and a reader who meets it once will find it everywhere.

★ The same root does that work three times in this chapter, and a reader can watch it turn: acharit ha-shanah, the after-part of the year, at v12; the hindmost sea here; and acharei derekh mevo ha-shemesh, behind the road of the sun’s going-in, at v30. ⚠ And it is the same three letters that give elohim acherim, OTHER gods, at v16 and v28 — the ‘other’ that is simply the one behind. The two families are related in Hebrew and cannot be joined in English; the note is the only place the connection can be shown.

★ Joshua inherits this promise and changes its tense. Joshua 1:3–5, already on these pages, repeats v24 almost word for word — every place the sole of your foot treads on — but where Deuteronomy says shall BE yours, a future, Joshua says I HAVE GIVEN it to you, a perfect, and adds as I spoke to Moses. Three other things move with it: Joshua calls the Euphrates the GREAT river and inserts all the land of the Hittites, renames this verse’s hindmost sea the GREAT sea toward the going-down of the sun, and — the change that matters most — turns v25’s plural no one will stand up in front of YOU (pl.) into a singular addressed to one man, no one will stand before YOU (sg.) all the days of your life. A promise made to a nation on the far bank is handed, on the day Moses dies, to its commander.

⚠ This chapter’s third and last foot: v6’s living things at the feet of the swallowed, v10’s foot in the Egyptian irrigation ditch, and here the sole of a foot measuring out a country. The same noun each time.

Verses 26–28 · Re’eh — a blessing and a curse set out before the reader knows what either one contains

Re’eh — ‘Look’ — opens the weekly Torah portion that takes its name from this word, and it is worth noticing what it does NOT do: it does not explain. The blessing and the curse are set out with a bare condition attached to each and no content given to either. Deuteronomy 28 will fill them in at devastating length; here they are two named alternatives and a today.

⚠ The verse changes number inside four words. Re’eh is a SINGULAR imperative, and its object is immediately plural: I am setting before YOU (pl.) today. One person is told to look at something laid in front of everybody. The KJV and ASV can show this and do — ‘Behold, I set before you’ — but only in the Hebrew is the singular audible, and no modern English version can carry it at all.

★ The curse is defined by a preposition, not by a deity. What earns it, at v28, is turning aside from the way to go after other gods THAT YOU HAVE NOT KNOWN — and the relative clause is doing real work. It is not that the gods are false; it is that they are strangers. That is the same argument the chapter opened with at v2, where knowing and seeing were what separated this generation from its children, and it closes the frame: Israel is being asked to prefer the God whose record it has watched over gods with no record at all.

Verses 29–30 · Gerizim and Ebal — two mountains named without explanation, and a Douay-Rheims verse that describes somewhere else entirely

★ Two mountains, no reason given. Gerizim and Ebal face each other across the valley where Shechem sits, and the blessing goes on the southern one and the curse on the northern. Deuteronomy does not say why that way round, does not say what the ceremony consists of, and does not say when. Deuteronomy 27 will give the liturgy and Joshua 8 will perform it, neither yet on these pages. What this chapter supplies is only the pairing — and the pairing outlived the book: Gerizim is the Samaritan holy mountain to this day, and the Samaritan Pentateuch reads Gerizim where the Masoretic Text of Deuteronomy 27:4 reads Ebal. This project follows the Masoretic Text and notes the variant rather than adopting it.

★ The great trees of Moreh, and a pun the chapter has already used. Moreh is where Abram stopped first in the land, at the great tree of Moreh by Shechem — already on these pages, and there it is singular where here it is plural. ⚠ The name is built on the root yarah, to throw or shoot and hence to point out, to teach: a moreh is a teacher or an oracle-giver, so this is plausibly the divination tree. And the same root gives yoreh, the early rain of v14 — the rain that is ‘thrown down’. The chapter names its first rain and its last landmark with the same three letters, sixteen verses apart. Joel 2:23 (not yet on these pages) plays on exactly this ambiguity, which is why that verse has been read both as an early rain and as a teacher of righteousness.

⚠⚠ The Douay-Rheims does not have these trees. Where the Hebrew ends beside the great trees of Moreh, it, following the Vulgate, ends with a valley that stretches and reaches far. The name is gone and a landform stands in its place. This is the clearest example in the chapter of the pattern this project keeps flagging in that version: its divergences usually trace to the Latin rather than to the Hebrew, and a reader comparing it against the others here would conclude the Hebrew was uncertain when it is not. The KJV has ‘the plains of Moreh’, the Geneva 1599 ‘the grove of Moreh’, the ASV ‘the oaks of Moreh’, the NIV ‘great trees of Moreh’ and the RV60 «encinar de More» — five different trees and a plain, from one Hebrew word (elon) whose species has never been settled.

⚠ Gilgal is a problem of its own: v30 locates the mountains ‘opposite Gilgal’, but the Gilgal of Joshua 4 is down by Jericho, some twenty-five miles south of Shechem, from which no mountain near Shechem is visible. Either a second Gilgal is meant or the verse is orienting a reader from far off. The encyclopedia entry sets out both and marks its coordinates approximate.

Verses 31–32 · Crossing, in the present tense — the chapter’s last word is the one it started with

The chapter ends where the whole book stands: you are crossing, a participle, an action in progress on the wrong side of a river. Verse 32 closes on shemartem la’asot, keep-in-order-to-do, the pairing Deuteronomy uses to insist that keeping is not remembering.

★ A small structural point worth recording: the formula which I am commanding you today / which I am setting before you today stands six times with the today attached (v8, 13, 26, 27, 28, 32) and a seventh time without it at v22. Ha-yom itself is in the chapter eight times, though one of the eight is a different sense — v4’s to this day, looking back at the drowned army rather than forward at the audience. It is the most insistent single word in the sermon, and it is doing the same work as the argument from eyewitness at v2: nothing here is being reported about the past, it is being handed over in the present.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew, English and Spanish all number this chapter identically at 32 verses — checked against every fetched witness on both shelves, with no splits and no renumbering. Three renderings depart from the whole shelf and are argued in their notes rather than asserted here: ‘the sea at your back’ for ha-yam ha-acharon at v24, where every other version translates the direction (western, uttermost, hinder, Mediterranean) and drops the posture the Hebrew compass is built on (n11-24); ‘seeks after’ for doresh at v12, where the entire shelf reads ‘cares for’ (n11-10); and ‘gaped’ for patsah at v6, kept distinct from the ordinary ‘open’ so that two Hebrew verbs stay two English verbs (n11-6). Frontlets is kept as the traditional placeholder for a word of genuinely unknown meaning, matching Exodus 13:16 and Deuteronomy 6:8 already on these pages — consistency across the three occurrences matters more than finding a better guess for one of them. ⚠ The parenthesis at v2 is left grammatically open with dashes rather than repaired with a supplied verb, which is what every shelf version does silently.

Library. Five new dictionary entries in English, seven in Spanish, and four new encyclopedia entries in both languages, every place with atlas coordinates. New in both: yequm, the Flood’s own word for everything that stands, used once outside Genesis 7 and used here for what goes down a hole in the desert; malqosh, the late rain, making its first biblical appearance in this chapter; patsah, the gaping mouth of the ground, three verses in the whole Bible; tirosh, wine still in the vat, and the raw-product triad it belongs to; and aravah, given a dictionary entry rather than an atlas point because a rift valley has no surveyed edges. Spanish additionally gains darash and berakhah, which had existed in English only — two entries of legacy backlog closed rather than added to. Four English entries extended (musar, totafot, davaq, darash) and three Spanish (musar, totafot, davaq); totafot’s standing forward-reference to this chapter is now a live link in both languages rather than a promise. New places: Gerizim and Ebal with exact coordinates, Moreh and Gilgal marked approximate — Moreh because no tree can be located and Gilgal because the verse’s own geography does not work (see its entry). ⚠ VIDEO_QUEUE was checked at the moment these four entries were written, per the standing rule; the queued Shechem film stays reserved for Joshua 24, and nothing in the queue belongs on a Deuteronomy 11 entry.

Echoes paid. Deuteronomy 6:5’s ve-ahavta, the Shema’s opening verb in its own form, reused as this chapter’s first word (n11-1). Deuteronomy 6:6–9, quoted outright at vv18–20 — and 6:9 reproduced letter for letter at v20 (n11-18). Deuteronomy 8:5’s musar VERB, arriving here as the noun (n11-1). Deuteronomy 8:7’s valley-and-hill pair, reversed at v11 with the water moved from underground to overhead (n11-10). Deuteronomy 10:20’s davaq aimed at God, aimed at God again one chapter later at v22 (n11-22). Numbers 16’s rebellion, retold at v6 without Korah in it, and with the verb Moses PREDICTED rather than the one the narrator used (n11-6). Numbers 26:11’s surviving sons of Korah, standing behind that omission (n11-6). Genesis 7’s yequm, the Flood’s vocabulary borrowed for a sinkhole (n11-6). Genesis 4:11’s gaping ground, the only other place it gapes (n11-6). Exodus 13:16’s totafot, completing the word’s three occurrences (n11-18). Genesis 12:6’s tree of Moreh, singular there and plural here, used to fix the two mountains by the spot where the promise was first made (n11-29). Deuteronomy 5:3 and 1:39’s sliding boundary of ‘you’, slid again at v2 (n11-1). And Joshua 1:3–5, which pays this chapter forward by quoting vv24–25 back in the perfect tense on the day Moses dies (n11-24).

Patterns worth carrying forward. ★★ The question Deuteronomy 9 opened and Deuteronomy 10 could not settle is answered, and the answer is a mechanism rather than a theme. Chapter 9 found the grammatical number switching mid-verse and asked what governed it; chapter 10 tested the obvious explanation — that singular and plural carry different KINDS of content — and reported honestly that it failed. This chapter shows why it failed, because it is the densest case yet: counted form by form, its thirty-two verses fall into twelve alternating blocks — eleven switches, two of them mid-verse, with three more verses carrying a single form out of step. ⚠ Not the densest chapter on these pages, incidentally: Deuteronomy 6 edges it on the same crude count, which is fitting, since Deuteronomy 6 is the chapter this one turns out to be quoting. And at vv18–20 the seam becomes legible, because there the source is visible. What Deuteronomy 11 REWROTE from Deuteronomy 6 it pluralised; what it COPIED it left in the singular it found. Verse 20 is 6:9 letter for letter and is singular; v19’s four time-phrases are 6:7 word for word and are singular, inside a plural frame that rewrote the verb around them; v18, which rephrases and adds a clause, is plural throughout. That retro-explains the stray singular at v8 too — a formula this chapter renders plural in its own four other uses, and singular in 6:6. ⚠ Stated with its limits, because they matter: this accounts for the seams where a source is identifiable and NOT for v1, v10, v12, v26 or v29. ⚠ The one correlation Deuteronomy 10 said it would still permit — plural for the concrete demands, singular for the character of God — is refuted here outright, and by the clearest possible case: the most physical command in the chapter, writing on your doorposts and your gates at v20, is SINGULAR, while binding them on your hand at v18 is plural. That hypothesis can now be closed. ★ A different one takes its place, offered as a hypothesis and not a finding: the unexplained singulars at v10, v15 and v20 all arrive where the text narrows to one household’s own property — his seed, his foot, his field, his cattle, his house, his gates. It does not cover v1 or v29. Worth watching in Deuteronomy 12 and after (not yet on these pages). ★ The 1984/1987-versus-2013/2019 split produced five more instances, and one of them is doubled across both languages. At v4 the TNM 1987’s faces of the Egyptian chariots are deleted outright by the TNM 2019; at v18 the frontlets move from between the eyes to the forehead in the NWT 2013 and the TNM 2019 where the NWT 1984 and TNM 1987 both keep the eyes; at v24 the sole of the foot becomes simply setting a foot down in both revisions; at v6 the TNM 1987’s existing-thing-that-stepped-after-them becomes a living-thing-that-followed; and at v2 the 1984’s visibly bracketed supplement becomes a smooth finished sentence in the 2013. Running total across the six chapters that have counted this deliberately: twenty-nine places — Deuteronomy 6’s four, 7’s seven, 8’s four, 9’s five, 10’s four, and this chapter’s five. ★ The Reina-Valera pattern behaves the opposite way from last chapter, which is worth as much as a confirmation. Deuteronomy 10 found the 1909→1960 gap moving four times; here it moves once — davaq at v22, «á él os allegareis» weakening to «siguiéndole», the identical loss that chapter flagged at 10:20, now recurring in the very next chapter. Everywhere else the two Reina-Valeras are word for word identical, including at v16, where both choose «se infatúe» for the seduced heart, and at v10, where both keep the foot in the irrigation ditch. ⚠ So the honest reading after two chapters is that the RV gap is not a steady drift but a thing that happens to particular WORDS — and davaq is now twice one of them. ★ The retelling method holds, and this time what Deuteronomy changes is a name it does not say. Verse 6 retells Numbers 16 and leaves Korah out of it entirely, which turns a Levite’s challenge to the priesthood into two laymen’s challenge to Moses — and reaches past the narrator’s ordinary verb to reuse Moses’ own predicted one. Nothing is corrected and nothing is denied; the omission does the argument’s work.

Next installment in this book: Deuteronomy 12 — the one place. After eleven chapters of argument, the law itself begins, and it begins by demolishing everything: tear down their altars, smash their pillars, burn their poles, erase their names off the ground. Then the counter-command that will reorganize Israelite worship for the rest of the Bible — not wherever you like, but the place Jehovah your God chooses to put his name. And with it the concession that follows from it, the one that lets an ordinary family eat meat at home without an altar, and the blood that still may not be eaten because the blood is the life.
