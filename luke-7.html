At Capernaum a centurion who never meets Jesus sends word that he is not worthy to have him under his roof, and his slave is healed at a distance. At the gate of Nain Jesus stops a funeral and gives a widow's only son back to her. From prison John the Baptist sends to ask, 'Are you the one who is to come?', and Jesus calls him more than a prophet. At a Pharisee's table a woman known as a sinner wets his feet with her tears, and hears 'your faith has saved you; go in peace.'
c. AD 28-29, in the Galilean ministry, directly after chapter 6 on Luke's own sequence — corresponding to Matthew 8:5-13's account of the centurion and Matthew 11:2-19's of John's question; the raising at Nain is found only in Luke, and the anointing at Simon the Pharisee's house is distinct in setting from the Bethany anointing of Mark 14 and Matthew 26.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–10 · Ἄξιός ἐστιν… οὐ γὰρ ἱκανός εἰμι… οὐδὲ ἐν τῷ Ἰσραὴλ τοσαύτην πίστιν εὗρον Axios estin… ou gar hikanos eimi… oude en tō Israēl tosautēn pistin heuron — the centurion’s faith: a man Jesus never meets, two different words for “worthy,” and the only time in Luke that Jesus marvels

⭐⭐ The centurion never appears. In Matthew’s telling the centurion comes to Jesus himself and speaks for himself (Matthew 8:5–13). In Luke he is never on stage at all: he sends elders of the Jews (v3), then friends (v6), and every word he says reaches Jesus through someone else’s mouth — including the famous sentence about his roof. Harmonizers have long answered that a man can be said to do what he does through his agents; the two Gospels, as they stand, simply tell it two ways. The same town and the same healing at a distance appear a third time in John 4:46–54, with a royal official and his son instead of a centurion and his slave; whether that is the same event is disputed, and the texts do not settle it.

⚠⚠ “He deserves” — “I am not worthy” — two different Greek words, and the shelf splits on keeping them apart. The elders make a case from merit: he is axios, deserving, because he loves the nation and built the synagogue (vv4–5). The man himself answers with a different word: ou… hikanos, not worthy, not enough — the word John the Baptist used of himself at 3:16 and Matthew’s centurion at 8:8, rendered “not worthy” in all three places here — and only then takes up the elders’ word to refuse it: he did not count himself axios, deserving, to come (v7). This translation keeps “deserve” for the one root and “worthy” for the other. The KJV prints one English word for all three, ‘he was worthy’… ‘I am not worthy’… ‘neither thought I myself worthy’. The NWT 1984 keeps the two roots apart, ‘I am not fit’ and then ‘I did not consider myself worthy’; its own 2013 revision merges them again, NWT 2013 ‘I am not worthy’. The NIV keeps a distinction but draws the line in a different place, ‘This man deserves’ and ‘I do not deserve’ for the two different words.

⚠ “Let my boy be healed” — a request, not a prediction. The text followed here, with Codex Vaticanus, reads iathētō, an imperative: “let him be healed.” Many later manuscripts read iathēsetai, “will be healed” — which is Matthew’s verb in the same sentence (8:8). The KJV, ASV and Douay all read ‘shall be healed’, and the NIV ‘will be healed’; the NWT is the witness that prints the imperative, ‘let my servant be healed’. The man also changes nouns: the narrator calls the sick man a doulos, a slave (vv2, 3, 10), and the centurion calls him pais, “my boy,” Matthew’s word all the way through — which is why Matthew 8’s note treats Luke’s doulos as the evidence that settles what Matthew’s ambiguous word meant.

⭐ “He marveled at him.” Jesus is the subject of the verb “marvel” in three places in the Gospels: here, in Matthew’s parallel (8:10), and at Mark 6:6, where he is amazed at his own hometown’s unbelief. Luke tells the Nazareth rejection without that sentence (4:16–30), so in this Gospel the one thing Jesus is ever said to marvel at is a Gentile soldier’s faith. ⚠ Luke’s wording, “not even in Israel,” is the gentler form that Matthew 8’s note found in the later manuscripts of Matthew; the KJV prints the identical English in both Gospels, ‘I have not found so great faith, no, not in Israel’.

⭐⭐ A foreign officer — the first half of a promise made at Nazareth. In his first sermon Jesus named two outsiders whom Israel’s prophets helped when Israel did not listen: a widow of Zarephath in Elijah’s time and “Naaman the Syrian,” the commander of a foreign army, in Elisha’s (4:25–27). This chapter opens with a foreign officer and, one scene later, a widow at a town gate. Galilee was ruled by Herod Antipas (3:1), not directly by Rome, so a centurion in Capernaum was more likely an officer in Antipas’s forces than in a legion — Luke does not say, and the TLB’s ‘Roman army captain’ says more than the Greek does; the NWT’s ‘army officer’ says less. And the elders’ reason is the chapter’s first use of the verb agapaō: “he loves our nation.” It will be the last word of the chapter’s last argument (v47).

Verses 11–17 · τῇ πύλῃ τῆς πόλεως… ὁ κύριος ἐσπλαγχνίσθη… καὶ ἔδωκεν αὐτὸν τῇ μητρὶ αὐτοῦ tē pylē tēs poleōs… ho kyrios esplanchnisthē… kai edōken auton tē mētri autou — the widow of Nain, only in Luke: Elijah’s own words, the first time the narrator calls Jesus “the Lord,” and the first feeling Luke gives him

⭐⭐⭐ Elijah’s story, told with Elijah’s words. The raising at Nain is in no other Gospel, and it is built out of 1 Kings 17 (not yet on these pages) — the widow of Zarephath whom Jesus named at 4:26. Elijah “came to the gate of the city, and look, a widow was there” (1 Kings 17:10, petach ha-ir); Jesus “drew near to the gate of the town, look” (v12). When Elijah has raised her son, “he gave him to his mother” (17:23, vayyittenehu le-immo); here, word for word, kai edōken auton tē mētri autou, “and he gave him to his mother” (v15). The crowd draws the conclusion the widow of Zarephath drew (“now I know that you are a man of God,” 17:24): “a great prophet has risen among us.” The NIV names the subject and adds a word, ‘Jesus gave him back to his mother’; the ASV keeps the bare Elijah sentence, ‘he gave him to his mother’. ⚠ Elisha’s raising of the Shunammite woman’s son (2 Kings 4:8–37, not yet on these pages) belongs to the same landscape: Shunem, usually placed at the village of Sulam, lies about three kilometres from Nain as the two sites are located today (see Nain).

⭐⭐ “The Lord” — the narrator’s first use of the title for Jesus. Luke’s narrator has said kyrios many times before this verse, and every time he meant God: “an angel of Jehovah” (1:11), “Jehovah’s power was present for him to heal” (5:17) — which is why this translation prints “Jehovah” in those places, where the Greek reads kyrios. Characters have called Jesus “Lord” to his face (5:8, 5:12), and an angel called him “Christ the Lord” (2:11); but at v13, for the first time, the storyteller himself writes ho kyrios and means Jesus. The referent here is plainly Jesus, so this translation keeps “the Lord” — the same rule, sorted by referent, that the kyrios entry lays out. Six verses later he does it again (v19), and there the later manuscripts replace the title with the name (see the next note).

⭐⭐ “Moved with compassion” — after two chapters of Luke removing Jesus’s feelings, a story with no Mark behind it gives him one. At the leper (5:12–16) and the withered hand (6:10) Luke kept Mark’s actions and dropped Mark’s emotions. Nain is Luke’s alone, and here the narrator says Jesus was splanchnizomai — moved in the gut — for a woman who has asked for nothing and whose faith is never mentioned. Luke uses the verb three times, and this is the only one of the three where Jesus is its subject; the other two belong to figures in parables, the Samaritan on the road and the father who sees his son far off (10:33; 15:20, neither yet on these pages). The KJV reads ‘had compassion on her’, the NWT ‘moved with pity’, the NIV ‘his heart went out to her’, and the TLB ‘his heart overflowed with sympathy’.

⚠ “The only son” — and the NWT’s two editions again part ways. Monogenēs, the word of John 3:16, is here simply a family fact: she had one son, and she is a widow. The NWT 1984 and Geneva print ‘only-begotten son’ / ‘only begotten son’; the NWT 2013 revises to ‘the only son’, as the KJV and the ASV already read, ‘the only son of his mother’. Luke gives the word to three parents — this widow, Jairus, whose only daughter is dying, and a father whose boy is his only child (8:42; 9:38, neither yet on these pages) — and in both of the later scenes Mark’s parallel does not have it. Jesus touches the bier, soros, a word found nowhere else in the New Testament; the Geneva and the TLB make it a ‘coffin’, the KJV a ‘bier’, and the TLB’s Jesus then says ‘Laddie’. The dead man “sat up,” anekathisen, a verb the New Testament uses in only one other verse: Tabitha sitting up at Peter’s word (Acts 9:40, not yet on these pages).

“Rise” — “has risen” — “are raised”: one verb, kept one word. Jesus says egerthēti to the dead man (v14); two verses later the crowd uses the same verb, ēgerthē, of Jesus — “a great prophet has risen among us” (v16) — and at v22 Jesus sends John word that “the dead are raised,” egeirontai. And “God has visited his people” is the verb of Zechariah’s song, “he has visited and worked redemption for his people” (1:68; again at 1:78) — the promise sung in chapter 1 now said about a funeral stopped at a gate. The KJV keeps the verb, ‘God hath visited his people’; the NWT reads ‘God has turned his attention to his people’, the NIV ‘God has come to help his people’, and the TLB ‘We have seen the hand of God at work today’. ⚠ The report goes out through “Judea” (v17), though Nain is in Galilee — either the news travels south, or Luke uses “Judea” for the whole Jewish land, the question already raised at 4:44.

Verses 18–23 · Σὺ εἶ ὁ ἐρχόμενος… ἐχαρίσατο βλέπειν… μακάριός ἐστιν ὃς ἐὰν μὴ σκανδαλισθῇ Sy ei ho erchomenos… echarisato blepein… makarios estin hos ean mē skandalisthē — John’s question, asked twice word for word, and an answer made of what the messengers had just watched

⭐ The question, twice. Matthew asks John’s question once (11:3). Luke has John dictate it (v19) and then has the two messengers repeat it verbatim when they arrive (v20) — the kind of repetition this translation keeps rather than trims. The phrase “the one who is to come” is discussed in Matthew 11’s note; in Luke it also answers John’s own words, “one stronger than I is coming” (3:16). Luke has already told the reader that Herod “shut John up in prison” (3:20), so he does not say again where the question comes from.

⚠ “The Lord” or “Jesus” at v19. The text followed here has the narrator’s title a second time, pros ton kyrion; later manuscripts read pros ton Iēsoun. The KJV, the Douay and the Geneva all read ‘sent them to Jesus’; the ASV, the NIV and the NWT read ‘sent them to the Lord’. The substitution is the easier reading — a copyist can turn a title into a name without any sense of loss, while the reverse needs a reason.

⭐⭐ “In that very hour” — Luke makes the messengers eyewitnesses. Verse 21 is Luke’s alone: before Jesus answers, he heals, and “to many who were blind he freely gave sight” — so “report what you have seen and heard” means what the two men have just watched. The verb is charizomai, to give as a favor, from charis, grace; in the four Gospels it appears in this chapter and nowhere else, three times, and the other two are the forgiving of the two debtors (vv42–43). The Geneva catches the gift in its ‘he gave sight freely’; the NWT 1984 reads ‘granted many blind persons the favor of seeing’.

⭐ A list in Isaiah’s words, nearly all of it already on Luke’s own pages. The answer (v22) is Isaiah’s vocabulary without a citation formula — Matthew 11’s note sets out the Hebrew of Isaiah 35:5–6 and 61:1 — and 61:1 is the passage Jesus read aloud at Nazareth (4:18). In Luke the list is also a table of contents: lepers cleansed (5:13), a paralyzed man walking home (5:25), good news for the poor (6:20), the dead raised (v15) and the blind given sight (v21), the last two in this chapter. Only the deaf hearing has not yet happened on these pages.

“Happy” and “offended,” as in Matthew. The sentence is identical in Greek to Matthew 11:6 and is printed identically in English: makarios is “happy,” this translation’s fixed rendering, and skandalizō, to trip someone up, is “offended.” The KJV reads ‘shall not be offended in me’, the NIV ‘does not stumble on account of me’, and the TLB, turning the trap into a loss, ‘does not lose his faith in me’.

Verses 24–30 · ἐν τοῖς βασιλείοις… ἀποστέλλω τὸν ἄγγελόν μου… ἐδικαίωσαν τὸν θεόν en tois basileiois… apostellō ton angelon mou… edikaiōsan ton theon — more than a prophet: soft clothes in a king’s palace, a messenger sent “before your face,” and a parenthesis nobody can assign to a speaker

Royal palaces — and the man in them. The three questions (a reed? a man in soft clothes? a prophet?) are the same as Matthew 11:7–9, and that note follows their logic. Luke’s version sharpens the second: those who live “in splendid clothing and luxury” are in ta basileia, royal palaces. And the man in this Gospel who holds John is a ruler in just such a house: Herod, who shut him in prison (3:19–20). Luke never describes John’s own clothing; Jesus’ contrast does it by negation.

⭐⭐ “My messenger before your face” — Malachi’s “before me,” turned toward Jesus. The quotation is the composite of Malachi 3:1 and Exodus 23:20 that Mark 1:2 opens with and Matthew 11’s note takes apart. In Malachi the speaker is Jehovah of hosts, and the messenger clears the way “before me” (lefanai); the Gospel form reads “before you” — the direction of Exodus 23:20, “I am sending an angel ahead of you.” So the way Malachi’s God said would be prepared for himself is prepared here for the one being addressed. ⚠ Luke’s Greek, in the text followed here, has no emphatic egō, “I,” where Matthew’s has one; later manuscripts of Luke supply it. And angelos is both “messenger” and “angel”: the Douay, following the Latin, prints ‘my angel’ — three verses after it has called John’s two disciples, with the same Greek word, ‘the messengers of John’.

⚠ “None greater than John” — not “a greater prophet than John the Baptist.” The critical text followed here says only that among those born of women none is greater than John. Later manuscripts add “prophet” and “the Baptist,” and the older shelf prints both: the KJV and the Douay read ‘not a greater prophet than John the Baptist’, the Geneva ‘no greater Prophet than John’. The ASV and the NWT read ‘none greater than John’. The paradox of the second half — the least in the kingdom greater still — is read in Matthew 11’s note as a statement about eras rather than persons.

⚠⚠ Verses 29–30: Jesus speaking, or Luke commenting? Greek manuscripts have no quotation marks, and nothing in the wording decides whether these two verses continue Jesus’ speech or are the narrator’s aside; the critical text prints them in parentheses, and so does this translation, without red letter. “All the people… and the tax collectors” — Levi’s trade (5:27) — “vindicated God,” edikaiōsan ton theon: they acknowledged God was in the right by accepting John’s baptism. The KJV keeps the startling verb, ‘justified God’; the NWT reads ‘declared God to be righteous’ and the NIV ‘acknowledged that God’s way was right’. The same verb closes the next paragraph (v35). The Pharisees and “the lawyers,” nomikoi, experts in the Law of Moses — the word’s first appearance in Luke, which uses it in six verses where Matthew has it once and Mark never — rejected God’s purpose eis heautous: the ASV reads ‘for themselves’, the KJV ‘against themselves’.

Verses 31–35 · ἐθρηνήσαμεν καὶ οὐκ ἐκλαύσατε… φάγος καὶ οἰνοπότης… ἀπὸ πάντων τῶν τέκνων αὐτῆς ethrēnēsamen kai ouk eklausate… phagos kai oinopotēs… apo pantōn tōn teknōn autēs — children in the marketplace: a dirge no one weeps to, a charge out of Deuteronomy, and wisdom vindicated by all her children

⭐ “You did not weep” — the chapter’s weeping verb. Luke’s children complain “we mourned, and you did not weep,” ouk eklausate, from klaiō, where Matthew’s parallel (11:17) has a different verb, koptō, to beat the breast in mourning. Klaiō is the word Jesus said to the widow at Nain, “stop weeping” (v13), and the word for the woman at his feet, “weeping” (v38). This generation will not weep to the dirge; a woman known as a sinner will. The game itself, a wedding tune and a funeral tune with no takers for either, is read in Matthew 11’s note. The KJV, Douay and Geneva open v31 with ‘And the Lord said’, a line found in later manuscripts and not in the critical text.

Bread and wine. Matthew says only that John came “neither eating nor drinking” (11:18). Luke names what John went without — bread and wine — and the wine was promised at his birth: “he must never drink wine or strong drink” (1:15).

⭐⭐ “A glutton and a drunkard” — the charge Deuteronomy lays against a son to be stoned. In Deuteronomy 21:20 the parents of a stubborn and rebellious son bring him to the elders of the city and say, “he does not listen to our voice — a glutton and a drunkard” (zolel ve-sove), and the next verse is his stoning. The insult quoted here pairs the same two vices. Whether the crowd meant the echo is not recoverable; the pairing is on the page. “A friend of tax collectors and sinners” repeats the complaint at Levi’s table (5:30) — and the next scene brings a sinner to another table.

⚠ “By all her children” — where Matthew has “by her deeds.” Matthew 11’s note sets out the difference: wisdom is vindicated “by her deeds” in Matthew’s best-attested text and “by her children” here, and Luke adds “all.” The verb is the one the people used of God at v29, dikaioō, and this translation prints “vindicated” both times so the link stays visible. The KJV reads ‘wisdom is justified of all her children’, the NWT ‘wisdom is proved righteous by all its children’, and the TLB turns the proverb into a jab at the audience: ‘you can always justify your inconsistencies’.

Verses 36–39 · γυνὴ ἥτις ἦν ἐν τῇ πόλει ἁμαρτωλός… τοῖς δάκρυσιν ἤρξατο βρέχειν… κατεφίλει gynē hētis ēn en tē polei hamartōlos… tois dakrysin ērxato brechein… katephilei — the woman who was a sinner: no name, no named sin, tears the Greek describes as rain, and a Pharisee’s test of a prophet

A Pharisee’s table. This is the first of three meals in this Gospel that Jesus eats in a Pharisee’s house (11:37; 14:1, neither yet on these pages). Diners at a formal meal reclined on couches, propped on one elbow with their feet away from the table — which is how a woman could stand “behind him at his feet” without ever being at the table.

⚠⚠ “A sinner” — no name, and no named sin. Luke says only that she was hamartōlos, a sinner, “in the town” — known for it there. The NIV reads ‘who lived a sinful life’; the TLB decides what the sin was, ‘a woman of the streets—a prostitute’, which the Greek does not say. Nor does Luke name her. The long Western habit of identifying her with Mary Magdalene goes back to a sermon of Gregory the Great and has no basis in the text (see Mary Magdalene); Luke introduces Mary Magdalene in the next chapter as someone not yet met (8:2, not yet on these pages). ⚠ A different anointing — at Bethany, in the house of a host also named Simon, “the leper,” with the oil poured on Jesus’ head and the objection about the cost rather than the woman — stands in Mark 14:3–9 and Matthew 26:6–13, and John tells a Bethany anointing of the feet by Mary, Martha’s sister (John 12:1–8, not yet on these pages). Whether these are one event remembered differently or more than one is disputed; the entry on Simon the leper sets out what is known. Luke’s scene has a different town, a different moment in the ministry, a Pharisee host, and a different argument.

⭐⭐ “Wet his feet with her tears” — the Greek verb for rain. Brechō, used here and again at v44, is everywhere else in the New Testament a weather word: the Father sends rain on the just and the unjust (Matthew 5:45), it rained fire and sulfur on the day Lot left Sodom (Luke 17:29), Elijah prayed that it might not rain (James 5:17). Her tears come down on his feet the way the sky does. The KJV, Douay and Geneva all make her ‘wash his feet’, which is more than the verb says; the ASV, the NIV and the NWT read ‘wet his feet’.

⭐ “Kept kissing his feet warmly” — Judas’s verb, and Luke gives it to someone else. Kataphileō is the intensive of the ordinary verb for a kiss, and it is the word Mark and Matthew use for Judas’s kiss in the garden, printed on these pages as “kissed him warmly” (Mark 14:45; Matthew 26:49). In Luke’s own garden scene Judas approaches Jesus with the plain verb (22:47, not yet on these pages), and Luke keeps the warm one for this woman (vv38, 45) and for the father who runs to his returning son (15:20). The imperfect tense here is continuous: she kept on. The NWT reads ‘tenderly kissed his feet’.

⭐ The Pharisee’s test of a prophet. A town has just called Jesus “a great prophet” (v16), and Jesus has called John “more than a prophet” (v26). Simon’s reasoning runs the other way: a real prophet would know what kind of woman is touching him. He says it “to himself,” and the next verse begins “Jesus answered him” — a reply to a sentence nobody spoke aloud. Simon’s test was whether Jesus could read the woman; Jesus passes it by reading Simon.

Verses 40–47 · δηνάρια πεντακόσια… ἀμφοτέροις ἐχαρίσατο… ἀφέωνται αἱ ἁμαρτίαι αὐτῆς αἱ πολλαί, ὅτι ἠγάπησεν πολύ dēnaria pentakosia… amphoterois echarisato… apheōntai hai hamartiai autēs hai pollai, hoti ēgapēsen poly — “she loved much”: two debtors, three missing courtesies, and a “for” the shelf cannot agree how to read

Five hundred denarii and fifty. A denarius was a day’s wage for a laborer (Matthew 20:2), so the larger debt is more than a year and a half of working days and the smaller about two months’ — a tenfold difference, impossible to mistake. The shelf has turned the same coin into three currencies: the KJV reads ‘five hundred pence’, the ASV ‘five hundred shillings’, and the TLB ‘$5,000 to one and $500 to the other’ — one Roman coin that has been a penny, a shilling and ten dollars, depending on who was counting. The lender, danistēs, appears only here in the New Testament, and the word for his debtors only here and in the parable of the dishonest steward (16:5, not yet on these pages).

⭐⭐ “He freely forgave them both” — the gift verb, not the release verb. The lender does not aphiēmi, release, the ordinary verb for forgiving that Jesus will use of the woman at v47; he charizomai — gives it as a favor, the verb of v21’s sight “freely given” to the blind. This translation marks both with “freely.” The NWT does the same, ‘freely forgave them both’; the KJV’s ‘frankly forgave them both’ meant the same thing in 1611, before “frankly” came to mean candidly.

⭐ Water, a kiss, oil — and three times “but she.” Jesus sets the host’s omissions against the woman’s actions in three matched pairs, each turning on hautē de, “but she”: no water for his feet, but tears; no kiss, but she has not stopped kissing his feet; no olive oil (elaion) for his head, but perfumed oil (myron) for his feet. Water for a guest’s feet (Genesis 18:4), a kiss of welcome (Genesis 29:13) and oil on the head at a host’s table (Psalm 23:5) are all courtesies the Hebrew Bible knows. Whether Simon’s omissions were an insult or merely the absence of special honor is a point on which readings differ; the text records only the contrast.

⭐⭐⭐ “For she loved much” — the reason she was forgiven, or the evidence that she had been? Three things in the Greek bear on it. First, the parable Jesus has just told runs in one direction only: two debts are cancelled, and the question is which debtor will love more (v42) — forgiveness first, love as the result; the second half of v47 says it again, “the one who is forgiven little loves little.” Second, the verb apheōntai is a perfect: her sins “have been forgiven,” a forgiveness already standing when Jesus speaks. Third, the connecting word hoti can give a cause (“because she loved”) or the grounds for a statement (“I can say this, since she loved”), and grammar alone does not choose. The Greek therefore does not say that her love earned her forgiveness, and it does not grammatically rule that reading out; what the surrounding parable says is that great love is what great forgiveness produces. This translation prints “for,” which in English can carry either sense, as the Greek does. The shelf divides the same way. The KJV, the ASV and the Geneva read ‘for she loved much’; the Douay ‘because she hath loved much’ and the NWT ‘because she loved much’ lean causal; the NIV writes the evidential reading into the text, ‘as her great love has shown’; and the TLB adds an object the Greek does not have, ‘for she loved me much’. ⚠ The verb is agapaō, the ordinary New Testament verb for love, the same one used of the centurion’s love for the nation at v5 — it should not be made to carry more than the word does (see agapē).

“Loves little” — said to Simon, about no one in particular. The last clause is general, “the one who is forgiven little,” and in the present tense. Jesus never says that Simon was forgiven little, or that he loves little; he describes a kind of person and leaves the host to decide whether it describes him.

Verses 48–50 · Ἀφέωνταί σου αἱ ἁμαρτίαι… Τίς οὗτός ἐστιν… Ἡ πίστις σου σέσωκέν σε Apheōntai sou hai hamartiai… Tis houtos estin… Hē pistis sou sesōken se — your faith has saved you: the forgiveness said to her face, the question from chapter 5 asked again, and a sentence Mark uses for a healing

Said to her this time. Verse 47 was spoken to Simon, about her; v48 turns and says it to her, in the same perfect tense — the form Jesus used to the paralytic let down through the roof (5:20). There the scribes and Pharisees asked aloud, “Who is this who speaks blasphemies? Who can forgive sins but God alone?” (5:21). Here the guests ask it “among themselves,” “Who is this, who even forgives sins?” — and the question is left hanging. The KJV reads ‘Who is this that forgiveth sins also?’.

⭐⭐ “Your faith has saved you” — Mark’s sentence for a healing, with no illness in sight. The Greek, hē pistis sou sesōken se, is word for word what Jesus says to the woman healed of her bleeding in Mark 5:34, where this translation reads “your faith has made you well” because a body has just been healed. Here nothing physical has happened, so “saved” is the only sense the sentence can carry. Luke will use the same four words three more times, for a healing each time (8:48; 17:19; 18:42, none yet on these pages). The Douay, following the Latin, reads ‘Thy faith hath made thee safe’. And “go in peace” is literally “go into peace,” eis eirēnēn — the preposition of Eli’s blessing on Hannah, lekhi le-shalom (1 Samuel 1:17).

⭐ Faith at both ends of the chapter. The word pistis stands twice in Luke 7: at v9, where a Gentile officer’s faith is greater than any in Israel, and at v50, where a woman known in her town as a sinner is told her faith has saved her. The woman never says a word in the whole scene, and the centurion speaks to Jesus only through messengers; in both cases the faith is named by Jesus himself.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: the Lord for the narrator’s ho kyrios when the referent is Jesus (vv13, 19), kept apart from “Jehovah” where Luke’s narrator means God (1:11; 5:17); not worthy for hikanos (as at 3:16 and Matthew 8:8) and deserve / deserving for the axios root (vv4, 7); slave for doulos and boy for pais; freely for every form of charizomai (vv21, 42, 43); rise / risen / raised for egeirō (vv14, 16, 22); vindicated for dikaioō (vv29, 35); wet for the rain-verb brechō; kissed warmly for kataphileō, as in Mark 14:45 and Matthew 26:49; have been forgiven for the perfect apheōntai; and for she loved much, keeping hoti as open in English as it is in Greek.

Echoes paid. The two outsiders named at Nazareth (4:25–27) both return: a foreign officer, and a widow met at a town gate whose son is “given to his mother” in the words of 1 Kings 17. Zechariah’s “he has visited his people” (1:68) is said by a crowd at Nain. John’s “not worthy” (3:16) is said by a centurion. The question of 5:21, “who can forgive sins?”, is asked again and left unanswered. And the thread of Luke removing Mark’s emotions from Jesus (5:12–16; 6:10) meets its counterweight: in two scenes where Mark is not his source, Luke has Jesus marvel (v9) and be moved with compassion (v13).

Echoes planted. Agapaō opens the chapter (v5) and closes its argument (v47). Monogenēs will return for Jairus’s only daughter and a father’s only child (8:42; 9:38). “Your faith has saved you; go in peace” will be said again in the next chapter, with “Daughter” in front of it, to another woman (8:48). The warm kiss comes back at the prodigal’s homecoming (15:20), and compassion at the Samaritan’s roadside (10:33). The Pharisee’s table will be set twice more (11:37; 14:1). And this Gospel’s only other centurion stands at the cross (23:47) — the first centurion never sees Jesus, the second watches him die.

Next in sequence: Luke 8 — women who provide for Jesus and the Twelve out of their own means, among them Mary Magdalene, introduced as if for the first time; the sower; a storm that ends with the disciples asking, as the guests at Simon’s table did, “Who then is this?”; and an only daughter and a woman who touches the fringe of his cloak.
