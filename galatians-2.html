Paul goes up to Jerusalem a second time, fourteen years on, with Barnabas and Titus, lays his gospel before James, Cephas and John, and leaves with their right hands of partnership and one request, to remember the poor. Later, at Antioch, he opposes Cephas to his face for withdrawing from the table of the nations.
Undated by the text: the chapter gives only its own interval, ‘after fourteen years,’ counted either from Paul’s call or from his first visit to Jerusalem. Which visit in Acts it is (the famine relief of Acts 11:30 or the council of Acts 15) is argued, and the Antioch incident has no date of its own.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · fourteen years later, by revelation, and a gospel laid out in private

⚠ “After fourteen years” — which visit to Jerusalem is this? Dia dekatessarōn etōn: fourteen years counted from the call of 1:15–16, or from the first visit of 1:18, “after three years”; the Greek does not say which. Acts has Paul and Barnabas go up to Jerusalem together twice: with famine relief, “by the hand of Barnabas and Saul” (Acts 11:30, Acts 12:25), and to the apostles and elders “about this question” of circumcision (Acts 15:2). Readers identify Galatians 2 with the first (the poor of v10 fit it) or with the second (the subject fits it), and this page does not choose. No Titus is named in the Greek of Acts printed here; the man at Acts 18:7 is Titius Justus. “Taking along” is symparalambanō, which stands in four verses of the New Testament; the other three are about Barnabas taking along John Mark (Acts 12:25, Acts 15:37 and 15:38). The KJV reads “Then fourteen years after I went up again to Jerusalem with Barnabas, and took Titus with me also”; the NIV adds “this time with Barnabas,” a “this time” the Greek does not have; the NWT 1984 reads “after fourteen years I again went up to Jerusalem.”

“Because of a revelation.” Kata apokalypsin. The gospel came to Paul “through a revelation of Jesus Christ” (1:12), and now the journey does too; Acts 15:2 says the congregation at Antioch arranged it, which is either a second description of the same decision or the account of a different journey. “I laid before them” is anatithēmi, which stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Acts 25:14, where Festus “laid before” King Agrippa the matters about Paul. The KJV reads “And I went up by revelation”; the NIV reads “I went in response to a revelation”; the TLB reads “with definite orders from God.”

⚠ “Those who seemed” — one verb four times. Tois dokousin, “to those who seemed,” and the verb dokeō comes back three more times: “those who seemed to be something” and “those who seemed important” in v6, and “who seemed to be pillars” in v9. This translation prints “seemed” all four times. Whether the word is a plain title, “the men of repute,” or carries an edge, “those who seemed to be,” is argued; v6 adds “whatever they once were makes no difference to me,” which readers weigh on both sides. The KJV reads “privately to them which were of reputation”; the ASV reads “privately before them who were of repute”; the Douay reads “apart to them who seemed to be some thing”; the NIV reads “those esteemed as leaders”; the NWT 1984 reads “those who were outstanding men.”

“Running, or had run, in vain.” Mē pōs eis kenon trechō ē edramon: the runner of 1 Corinthians 9:24–26, “So I run like this, not aimlessly,” and the “in vain” of 2 Corinthians 6:1, “not to accept the grace of God in vain.” The fear is not explained. It can be that the gospel itself might be wrong, which the rest of the chapter makes unlikely, or that a break with Jerusalem would undo his work among the nations. The KJV reads “lest by any means I should run, or had run, in vain”; the TLB reads “and, I hoped, agree that it was right.”

Verses 3–5 · Titus not compelled, false brothers smuggled in, and not yielding for an hour

“Not even Titus … was compelled to be circumcised.” Titus is Hellēn, a Greek, and he came to Jerusalem and went home uncircumcised. Acts tells the opposite decision about another companion: Paul circumcised Timothy “because of the Jews who were in those places, for they all knew that his father was a Greek” (Acts 16:3); Timothy’s mother was Jewish, which Titus had no claim to, and readers explain the two cases by that difference or by the difference between a demand and a courtesy. “Compelled” is anankazō, and it returns at v14: Titus was not compelled at Jerusalem, and at Antioch Cephas is said to “compel the nations” by his example. The KJV reads “But neither Titus, who was with me, being a Greek, was compelled to be circumcised”; the Douay reads “being a Gentile,” from the Vulgate’s gentilis.

⚠ “False brothers smuggled in” — and a sentence that never finishes. Pareisaktous pseudadelphous: pseudadelphos, “false brother,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 2 Corinthians 11:26, “dangers among false brothers.” Pareisaktos, “smuggled in,” is used only here, and its verb follows at once, pareisēlthon, “slipped in”: pareiserchomai stands in two verses, this one and Romans 5:20, where law “came in alongside.” “To spy out” is kataskopeō; its noun is Rahab’s “spies” at Hebrews 11:31. “Enslave” is the katadouloō of 2 Corinthians 11:20, “if someone enslaves you.” And the sentence breaks off: v4 has no main verb, and v5 begins again with “to them.” The Greek printed here marks the break with a dash, and so does this translation. “Freedom,” eleutheria, appears here for the first time in the letter; it returns at 5:1 and 5:13. The KJV reads “false brethren unawares brought in, who came in privily to spy out our liberty”; the NWT 1984 reads “the false brothers brought in quietly, who sneaked in to spy upon our freedom”; the NIV reads “some false believers had infiltrated our ranks.”

“Not even for an hour.” Hois oude pros hōran eixamen tē hypotagē. Some early Latin witnesses and writers are reported to read the verse without “to them … not,” so that Paul did yield for an hour; the critical editions and the Byzantine text all print the negative, and that report is not checked here against the manuscripts. “The truth of the gospel” (the phrase) stands in two verses of the New Testament, and both are in this chapter: here, and at v14, where Cephas and the others are not walking straight toward it. The KJV reads “To whom we gave place by subjection, no, not for an hour”; the NWT 1984 reads “to these we did not yield by way of submission, no, not for an hour”; the NIV reads “We did not give in to them for a moment.”

Verses 6–10 · those who seemed to be something, Peter and Cephas, pillars, and right hands of partnership

⚠ “God shows no partiality” — and another broken sentence. Verse 6 begins “from those who seemed to be something” and never finishes; it starts again with “for to me those who seemed important added nothing.” In between: hopoioi pote ēsan, “whatever they once were,” where pote can be “once,” pointing to something in their past, or simply “ever,” “whatever they were”; then prosōpon ho theos anthrōpou ou lambanei, literally “God does not receive the face of a man,” the idiom this translation printed “show no partiality” at Luke 20:21. It is the Hebrew idiom of Deuteronomy 10:17, where God “does not lift the face,” and its noun is the “no partiality” of Romans 2:11. The face returns at v11: Paul opposed Cephas “to his face.” “Added nothing” is prosanatithēmi, which stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and 1:16, “I did not consult with flesh and blood.” He did not consult them then, and they added nothing now. The KJV reads “God accepteth no man’s person”; the NWT 1984 reads “God does not go by a man’s outward appearance”; the NIV reads “God does not show favoritism.”

⚠ “Peter” at verses 7 and 8, and “Cephas” everywhere else. In Paul’s letters the name Petros stands only here, at vv7 and 8. Everywhere else Paul writes Kēphas, the Aramaic form: 1 Corinthians 1:12, 3:22, 9:5 and 15:5, and Galatians 1:18, and 2:9, 11 and 14 in this chapter. The Byzantine text reads Petros at v11 and v14 as well, which is why the KJV reads “But when Peter was come to Antioch,” while the ASV and the NWT 1984 keep “Cephas” there. The division of the work is stated twice: “the gospel of the uncircumcision” and “of the circumcision” in v7, and in v8 the same God “at work” in each, energeō, the verb of 2 Corinthians 4:12, “death is at work in us.” The KJV reads “For he that wrought effectually in Peter”; the NWT 1984 reads “He who gave Peter powers necessary for an apostleship.”

“Who seemed to be pillars.” Stylos, “pillar,” stands in four verses of the New Testament: here; 1 Timothy 3:15; Revelation 3:12, “I will make him a pillar in the temple of my God”; and Revelation 10:1, an angel whose feet were “like pillars of fire.” The order is James, Cephas and John, with James first, where 1:18–19 had Cephas and then James. That this James is “the Lord’s brother” of 1:19 is the common reading; the verse does not repeat the title. The Douay reads “who seemed to be pillars”; the ASV reads “they who were reputed to be pillars”; the TLB moves Peter to the front of the list and adds “the pillars of the church.”

⚠ “Right hands of partnership” — koinōnia on a handshake. Dexias edōkan … koinōnias: right hands, plural, given in koinōnia, the word this translation printed “partnership” at 1 Corinthians 1:9 and Philippians 1:5 (dictionary). What the handshake sealed was a division of the work, “we to the nations and they to the circumcision,” which is what a partnership is. The KJV and the ASV both read “the right hands of fellowship”; the NIV reads “the right hand of fellowship”; the NWT 1984 reads “the right hand of sharing together”; the TLB reads “they shook hands with Barnabas and me.”

“Only, we were to remember the poor.” The one condition, and Paul says he was eager to meet it. His letters show him doing it: the contribution “for the poor among the holy ones in Jerusalem” (Romans 15:26), the collection of 1 Corinthians 16:1, which he says he “directed the congregations of Galatia” to make as well, and the two chapters on it in 2 Corinthians 8–9. Whether “the poor” here means the believers in Jerusalem in particular is argued. Espoudasa is an aorist, “I was eager”; the NIV reads “the very thing I had been eager to do all along”; the KJV reads “the same which I also was forward to do.”

Verses 11–14 · Antioch: Cephas opposed to his face, the men from James, and “how can you compel the nations to Judaize?”

“I opposed him to his face, because he stood condemned.” Kata prosōpon, the face of v6 again. Kategnōsmenos ēn is a perfect participle, “he stood condemned,” and it does not say who condemned him: his own conduct, most readers say, though the Greek does not. The ASV reads “because he stood condemned”; the KJV reads “because he was to be blamed,” and the Douay too, “because he was to be blamed,” the Vulgate’s reprehensibilis; the NWT’s 2013 revision reads “because he was clearly in the wrong.”

⚠ “He used to eat with the nations” — and then shrank back. The imperfect synēsthien is a habit, not a single meal. The verb is the one in the complaint against Jesus at Luke 15:2, “This man welcomes sinners and eats with them,” and in the complaint against Peter himself at Acts 11:3, “You went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them,” made by “those of the circumcision” (Acts 11:2), the same phrase as here. “Some came from James”: sent by James, or only from his circle, the Greek does not say; Tregelles prints ēlthen, “when he came,” for ēlthon, “when they came.” Then two more imperfects, a change that happened by degrees: hypestellen, “he began to shrink back,” the verb of Paul at Acts 20:20, “I shrank back from nothing that was profitable”; and aphōrizen heauton, “set himself apart,” the verb of 1:15, where God “set me apart from my mother’s womb” for the nations. Paul was set apart for them; Cephas set himself apart from them. The NIV reads “he began to draw back and separate himself from the Gentiles”; the NWT 1984 reads “he went withdrawing and separating himself.”

“Playing the hypocrite” — even Barnabas. Synypekrithēsan, “they played the hypocrite with him,” and then the noun, hypokrisis; the word belongs to the stage, where a hypokritēs was an actor, and in the Gospels it is the charge Jesus brings against the Pharisees, “the yeast of the Pharisees, which is hypocrisy” (Luke 12:1). Barnabas, Paul’s partner in vv1 and 9, was “carried away”: synapagō stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Romans 12:16, where it is the opposite pull, “go along with the lowly.” The KJV reads “the other Jews dissembled likewise with him”; the NWT 1984 reads “joined him in putting on this pretense”; the NIV reads “The other Jews joined him in his hypocrisy.”

⚠ “Not walking straight” and “to Judaize” — words the New Testament uses once. Orthopodeō, “to walk with straight feet,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. So do the two adverbs of Paul’s question, ethnikōs, “like the nations,” and Ioudaikōs, “like the Jews,” and its last verb, ioudaizō, “to Judaize,” which stands in one verse of the New Testament as well. Three words from one root, Ioudaios … Ioudaikōs … ioudaizein, close the sentence. “To Judaize” here is what the nations are pressed to do, to live by Jewish custom; this translation keeps the English word, which says exactly that. “Compel” is the verb of v3, where Titus was not compelled. The critical text asks “how,” pōs; the Byzantine “why,” ti, which is the KJV’s “why compellest thou the Gentiles to live as do the Jews?”; the ASV reads “how compellest thou the Gentiles to live as do the Jews?”; the NIV reads “How is it, then, that you force Gentiles to follow Jewish customs?”

⚠ Where do Paul’s words to Cephas end? The Greek has no quotation marks, and the sentence of v14 turns into the “we” of vv15–21 without a break. The NIV runs the quotation to the end of the chapter, “Christ died for nothing!”; the NWT 1984 closes it at the end of v14, after “to live according to Jewish practice?”; the King James Version and the ASV print no quotation marks at all. This translation closes it at v14, where “you” gives way to “we,” and the reader should know that vv15–21 may still be addressed to Cephas, in front of everyone at Antioch.

Verses 15–16 · Jews by birth, “sinners from the nations,” and justified through faith in Jesus Christ

“Jews by birth, and not sinners from the nations.” Physei is “by nature,” and here that means by birth, as in “the uncircumcised by nature” of Romans 2:27. “Sinners from the nations” is how one side of the line spoke of the other: those outside the covenant were sinners by definition. Paul uses the phrase in order to break it, and two verses later Jews seeking to be justified in Christ are “found to be sinners” themselves. The KJV and the NWT 1984 read “Jews by nature”; the NIV reads “Jews by birth and not sinful Gentiles.”

⚠ “Faith in Jesus Christ” or “the faith of Jesus Christ” — Galatians 2:16. Dia pisteōs Iēsou Christou and then ek pisteōs Christou: the genitive can be “faith in” Christ, with Christ as the one trusted, or “the faith, the faithfulness, of” Christ, his own, shown in his death. This is the question set out at Romans 3:22, and this translation prints “faith in,” as there, with the same range open. Between the two genitives stands a clause that is not ambiguous, kai hēmeis eis Christon Iēsoun episteusamen, “we too believed in Christ Jesus,” and how that clause bears on the two around it is part of the argument. The genitive comes back at v20, “the faith in the Son of God.” The KJV, the Geneva and the Douay keep the “of,” “by the faith of Jesus Christ”; the ASV and the NIV read “faith in Jesus Christ”; the NWT 1984 reads “through faith toward Christ Jesus,” with the order “Christ Jesus” that Westcott–Hort and Tregelles print.

⚠ “Except through faith” — ean mē. The Greek joins the two halves with ean mē, “if not, except,” and this translation prints “except.” Most readers take it as “but only”: not by works of law, but by faith. A few have read it as “not by works of law unless through faith,” works joined to faith; the same sentence then says “and not by works of law,” which most readers take to settle it. The NWT 1984 supplies the “only”: “not due to works of law, but only through faith.” The KJV and the ASV read “but.”

“By works of law no flesh will be justified” — a psalm, adjusted. The last clause is Romans 3:20 nearly word for word, and behind both stands Psalm 143:2: in the Hebrew, “all living,” kol-chai, “will not be justified before you.” Paul writes “all flesh” for “all living” and adds “by works of law,” which the psalm does not have. “Works of law” has no article in the Greek, three times in this verse, and this translation leaves it out, as at Romans 3:20 and 3:28; the NWT 1984 does the same, “due to works of law,” while the KJV and the ASV read “by the works of the law.” “Justified,” dikaioō, comes three times in v16 and again in v17, as in Romans; the NWT 1984 reads “declared righteous” each time.

Verses 17–21 · a servant of sin?, crucified with Christ, and Christ died for nothing

“Is Christ then a servant of sin? May it never be!” If seeking to be justified in Christ puts Jews in the place of the “sinners from the nations,” has Christ made them sinners? Mē genoito, “May it never be!”, as at Romans 3:4. Diakonos is “servant,” as when Christ “has become a servant of the circumcision” at Romans 15:8. The KJV and the ASV read “God forbid,” though the Greek has no “God”; the NWT 1984 reads “May that never happen!”; the NIV reads “Absolutely not!”

“If I build again the very things I destroyed.” Katalyō and oikodomeō, the pair in the charge at Jesus’ trial, “I am able to destroy the sanctuary of God and to build it in three days” (Matthew 26:61). Read with Antioch in view, what was destroyed is the separation from the nations that Cephas had stopped keeping, and what was rebuilt is the same separation, when he withdrew. “Transgressor,” parabatēs, is the word of Romans 2:25, “a transgressor of the law.” “I demonstrate” is synistanō, the verb of Romans 5:8, “God demonstrates his own love.” The KJV reads “I make myself a transgressor”; the NWT 1984 reads “I demonstrate myself to be a transgressor”; the Douay reads “I make myself a prevaricator.”

⚠ Galatians 2:20 or 2:19? “I have been crucified with Christ.” The Greek printed here ends v19 with Christō synestaurōmai, and the Vulgate and the Douay end v19 there too: “with Christ I am nailed to the cross.” The King James Version begins v20 with the same words, and so do the ASV, the NIV and the NWT 1984, which is why “I have been crucified with Christ” is widely quoted as Galatians 2:20. The KJV reads “I am crucified with Christ: nevertheless I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me”; the NIV reads “I have been crucified with Christ and I no longer live.” Nothing in the text changes. Systauroō, “to crucify with,” stands in five verses of the New Testament. Three are the men crucified beside Jesus (Matthew 27:44, Mark 15:32, John 19:32); one is Romans 6:6, “our old man was crucified with him”; and this one is the only one in the first person singular. The tense is perfect, a death that happened and still stands. The NWT 1984 reads “I am impaled along with Christ,” its rendering of the stauroō family.

“It is no longer I who live, but Christ lives in me.” Zō de ouketi egō. The KJV’s “nevertheless I live; yet not I” follows the comma that the Textus Receptus prints after zō de; without it the clause reads as here. “In the flesh,” en sarki, is ordinary bodily life, which the NIV prints “in the body.” “By faith, the faith in the Son of God” repeats the article, en pistei … tē tou huiou tou theou, and carries the genitive question of v16. Tregelles prints “of God and Christ” in place of “of the Son of God.” The ASV reads “the faith which is in the Son of God”; the KJV reads “by the faith of the Son of God.”

“Who loved me and handed himself over for me.” Paradontos heauton: paradidōmi is “hand over,” this translation’s word for it wherever it stands, the betrayal of Luke 22:48 (“do you hand over the Son of Man with a kiss?”) and the Father who “handed him over for us all” at Romans 8:32 (dictionary). Here the one handed over is the one who does it. At 1:4 the verb was the plain didōmi, “who gave himself.” The NWT 1984 keeps the verb, “handed himself over for me”; the KJV and the NIV read “gave himself for me.”

“Then Christ died for nothing” — dōrean. Dōrean is an adverb made from “gift”: “as a gift, freely,” and so “for nothing.” At Romans 3:24 it is the “freely” of “being justified freely by his grace”; at John 15:25 it is “They hated me for nothing.” Here, if righteousness came through law, the death that was the gift becomes the death for nothing. “Set aside” is atheteō, the verb of 1 Corinthians 1:19, “the intelligence of the intelligent I will set aside.” The KJV reads “I do not frustrate the grace of God: for if righteousness come by the law, then Christ is dead in vain”; the NWT 1984 reads “I do not shove aside the undeserved kindness of God”; the NIV reads “Christ died for nothing!”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “seemed” for dokeō all four times (2:2, 2:6 twice, 2:9); “compelled” and “compel” for anankazō at 2:3 and 2:14; “right hands of partnership” for koinōnia at 2:9, as at 1 Corinthians 1:9 and Philippians 1:5; “shrink back” at 2:12, as at Acts 20:20, and “set himself apart” at 2:12, as at 1:15; “Cephas” and “Peter” exactly where the Greek printed here has each; “to Judaize” for ioudaizō at 2:14; “justified,” “works of law” without the article and “faith in Jesus Christ” at 2:16, as at Romans 3:20–28; “May it never be!” at 2:17, as at Romans 3:4; “crucified with” at 2:19, as at Romans 6:6; “handed himself over” for paradidōmi at 2:20; and “for nothing” for dōrean at 2:21.

Echoes paid. The fourteen years of 2 Corinthians 12:2; the revelation of 1:12; the consulting that Paul did not do at 1:16, now the adding that Jerusalem did not do (2:6); set apart from 1:15, turned around at Antioch (2:12); Cephas and James from 1:18–19; the false brothers of 2 Corinthians 11:26 and the enslaving of 2 Corinthians 11:20; the poor of Romans 15:26; eating with the uncircumcised at Acts 11:3; no flesh justified by works of law at Romans 3:20; faith in Jesus Christ at Romans 3:22; and crucified with him at Romans 6:6.

Echoes planted. Freedom and slavery at 5:1 and 5:13; justified by faith, Abraham and the curse of the law in chapter 3; the genitive of faith again at 3:22 and Philippians 3:9; Christ in me answered at 4:19, “until Christ is formed in you”; the flesh of 2:20 against the Spirit at 5:16–25; and the pillar of 1 Timothy 3:15.

Next in this book: Galatians 3, already on these pages. “O senseless Galatians, who has put the evil eye on you?”, Abraham who believed God, and the curse of the law.
