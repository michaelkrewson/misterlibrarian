🕰
Invited to eat at the house of a ruler of the Pharisees on the Sabbath, Jesus heals a man with dropsy and silences the watching lawyers with the same ox-in-a-well argument this Gospel already tried once and lost, two chapters back. Watching the guests scramble for the best couches, he tells a parable about taking the lowest place instead, then tells his host to invite the poor, the crippled, the lame and the blind rather than friends who can repay the favor. A guest's blessing on 'whoever eats bread in the kingdom of God' draws out the parable of a great dinner whose invited guests all beg off, so the master sends his slave first into the city and then out to the country roads to fill the house instead. To the large crowds now traveling with him he says that whoever does not hate father and mother, and carry his own cross, cannot be his disciple, illustrated by a builder who counts the cost of a tower and a king who counts his army before a war. It closes on salt that has lost its taste, useless for either the soil or the manure pile.
c. AD 29, continuing the same journey-to-Jerusalem sequence opened at 9:51 and carried through chapters 10-13; Matthew and Mark both have Sabbath-controversy and cost-of-discipleship material, but scattered across other settings and chapters (the hate-your-family and carry-your-cross sayings recur in Matthew 10; the salt saying in Matthew 5 and Mark 9), while the dropsy healing, the seating parable, and the tower-builder and warring-king parables are found only here.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–6 · τῶν ἀρχόντων τῶν Φαρισαίων … ἄνθρωπός τις ἦν ὑδρωπικὸς … υἱὸς ἢ βοῦς εἰς φρέαρ tōn archontōn tōn Pharisaiōn … anthrōpos tis ēn hydrōpikos … huios ē bous eis phrear — a dropsy that appears exactly once in the New Testament, and an argument this Gospel has already tried once and lost

⭐⭐ “One of the rulers of the Pharisees” is a title this exact phrase names nowhere else in the New Testament. The exact phrase stands in exactly one verse of the whole New Testament: here. The nearest relative is John’s Nicodemus, ‘a man from among the Pharisees… a ruler of the Jews’ (John 3:1, already on these pages) — a wider title, and a different scene entirely: Nicodemus comes to Jesus carefully, by night, on his own initiative; here it is Jesus who walks into the ruler’s own house, in daylight, on the Sabbath, and it is Jesus who is watched. Both Gospels give a cautious Pharisee host a title built on archōn; only Luke narrows it to the Pharisees specifically, and only Luke puts Jesus, not his host, under surveillance.

⭐⭐⭐ “Dropsy” is a true New Testament hapax — hydrōpikos stands in exactly one verse of the whole New Testament, this one. The condition (an abnormal swelling from fluid retention, what a modern reader would recognize as edema) never recurs as a diagnosis anywhere else in the Gospels or Acts; Luke, the one Gospel writer ancient tradition calls a physician (Colossians 4:14, not yet on these pages), is also the only one who names it.

⭐⭐⭐ The Sabbath argument here is not the first time this Gospel has made it — and it is sharper the second time. One chapter earlier, a synagogue ruler and the opponents siding with him had no answer to a milder case: an ox or a donkey led to water on the Sabbath, ordinary daily business (13:15, already on these pages). Here the case is not routine care but emergency rescue — a fall into a well, danger rather than thirst — and the endangered party is named first: ‘your SON or your ox’, a human being set beside the animal rather than left out of the sentence, as if 13:15–16’s ‘daughter of Abraham’ argument (that a woman deserves at least what an ox and a donkey already get) has been heard and is now being answered on its own ground. Both scenes end identically: the opponents ‘were not able to answer back’ (v6), matching 13:17’s ‘all his opponents were put to shame’.

⭐ An old textual variant some English Bibles still carry is printed by none of the editions compared here. The KJV, Geneva, Douay and ASV all read ‘an ass or an ox’ at v5, matching the wording of 13:15; the NWT reads ‘son or bull’, and the NIV ‘child or an ox’, which this translation follows in substance. The apparatus behind this chapter (Westcott-Hort, Tregelles, NA28 and the Byzantine text Robinson-Pierpont all agree) shows no variant at this word at all — every edition compared here reads ‘son’, so the ‘ass’ some older English Bibles print traces to manuscripts outside these editions — Codex Sinaiticus among them reads onos, ‘donkey’, very likely brought into line with 13:15’s own ox and donkey.

Verses 7–11 · πῶς τὰς πρωτοκλισίας ἐξελέγοντο … ὁ ταπεινῶν ἑαυτὸν ὑψωθήσεται pōs tas prōtoklisias exelegonto … ho tapeinōn heauton hypsōthēsetai — the one place in the New Testament where loving the best seat is a lesson taught, not a charge laid

⭐⭐⭐ “The places of honor” is a phrase the New Testament uses in three other passages, and every time it is a condemnation. The noun stands in five verses of the whole New Testament, two of them here (vv7–8): Matthew’s scribes and Pharisees ‘love the place of honor at dinners’ (Matthew 23:6, already on these pages), Mark’s scribes do the same (Mark 12:39, already on these pages), and Luke elsewhere levels the identical charge at the same targets (20:46, already on these pages) — three verses, all condemnations of religious leaders caught loving status. Only here, at a Pharisee’s own dinner table, does the word turn into an actual lesson taught to the guests doing it, in the second person, with a way out offered rather than a verdict pronounced. The behavior Luke elsewhere denounces from a distance, Jesus here corrects from across the same table where it is happening.

The advice is shrewd as well as humble: taking the lowest place either earns a public promotion (v10) or costs nothing if none comes, where taking the highest place either holds (unremarkably) or ends in a public demotion (v9) — the two outcomes are not symmetrical, and the parable’s own arithmetic argues for humility before its closing maxim ever states the principle outright.

“Everyone who exalts himself will be humbled, and the one who humbles himself will be exalted” (v11) is a saying Luke plants once more, verbatim, closing a very different scene: the Pharisee and the tax collector at prayer (18:14, already on these pages). Matthew sets the same maxim inside Jesus’s denunciation of the scribes and Pharisees (Matthew 23:12, already on these pages) — a denunciation whose ‘places of honor’ charge Luke gives at 20:46–47 without the maxim. One maxim, doing duty for table manners, private prayer, and public religious status in turn.

Verses 12–14 · ὅταν δοχὴν ποιῇς, κάλει πτωχούς … ἐν τῇ ἀναστάσει τῶν δικαίων hotan dochēn poiēs, kalei ptōchous … en tē anastasei tōn dikaiōn — the word planted nine chapters back for exactly this verse, paid in full

⭐⭐⭐ “When you give a banquet” uses the identical rare word this library already promised would return here. The dochē dictionary entry, anchored at Levi’s own ‘great reception’ for Jesus (5:29, already on these pages), already named this exact verse as the word’s only other New Testament occurrence — and here it is, in Jesus’s own teaching about whom to invite to one. Levi’s reception, in fact, already kept this very instruction before Jesus ever stated it: his guest list was tax collectors and sinners, not the friends, brothers, relatives and rich neighbors this chapter says NOT to invite.

⭐⭐ The guest list at v13 is not a new one — it is the same four words the master of the house will send his slave to gather two verses into the very next parable (v21: ‘the poor and the crippled and the blind and the lame’, the identical four terms in a different order). What Jesus tells his host to do by choice here, the parable’s householder does after his first choice of guests refuses him — the same guest list, offered first as generosity and then as a fallback.

“The resurrection of the righteous” (v14) is a phrase found nowhere else in the New Testament in this exact form — it stands in exactly one verse of the whole New Testament. It is not a denial that the unrighteous are also raised: Paul, before Felix, affirms both together, ‘there is going to be a resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous’ (Acts 24:15, not yet on these pages, using the same root noun with both classes named). This verse simply names the resurrection that matters for the point being made — a reward that cannot be collected at the dinner table, because the guests who cannot repay a favor are the only ones a favor done for them still counts, later, as a kindness rather than a transaction.

Verses 15–24 · εἰς τὰς πλατείας καὶ ῥύμας τῆς πόλεως … εἰς τὰς ὁδοὺς καὶ φραγμοὺς καὶ ἀνάγκασον εἰσελθεῖν eis tas plateias kai rhymas tēs poleōs … eis tas hodous kai phragmous kai anankason eiselthein — two sendings, one inside the city and one outside it, and a single verb history has put to uses this parable never intended

⭐⭐ The householder sends his slave out TWICE, to two different kinds of place, with two different verbs. The first sending stays inside the city, to its ‘streets and lanes’, and simply BRINGS IN (eisagage, ‘bring in here’) the poor, the crippled, the blind and the lame — the identical guest list Jesus has just told his host to prefer (v13). The house is still not full, so a second sending goes OUTSIDE the city altogether, to its ‘roads and hedgerows’ — open country, not a second neighborhood — and this time the verb changes to COMPEL (anankasē… anankason). Whatever the compulsion means, it answers a real gap the first sending left: not everyone worth inviting to a banquet lives inside a city’s own streets.

⚠⚠ “Compel them to come in” has a documented history of being read as a mandate for religious coercion, and that reading is a misuse of the verse rather than its meaning. Augustine, writing against the Donatists in North Africa, cited this exact clause (compelle intrare in the Latin he read) to argue that the state could legitimately use force to bring schismatics back into the church — an argument later generations invoked, in turn, to justify measures against Jews and heretics well into the medieval period. Nothing in the parable’s own scene supports that reading: the people being gathered from the roads and hedgerows are society’s poor and disabled, being urged in by a servant, not converts being coerced by a sword: anankazō elsewhere in the New Testament covers Paul ‘compelling’ Gentiles to live like Jews by social pressure, not force (Galatians 2:14, not yet on these pages), and here most plausibly names the pressing INSISTENCE a homeless stranger would need before believing an invitation to a rich man’s table was genuinely meant for him — urgency aimed at overcoming disbelief, not resistance overcome by threat.

⭐⭐⭐ “For many are called, but few are chosen” is not part of this chapter’s own text — and this is the THIRD place copyists have reached for that exact line. The Byzantine manuscript tradition (Robinson-Pierpont) appends this sentence to the end of v24, after ‘my dinner’; the earliest editions compared here (Westcott-Hort, Tregelles, NA28) do not have it, and this translation follows them in leaving it out. The sentence is a real one — it closes a different parable at Matthew 22:14 (already on these pages), where it fits the scene that precedes it (a guest thrown out for lacking a wedding garment) — and the identical line was ALSO imported by the same manuscript tradition into Matthew 20:16 (already on these pages, its own note tracing the same move), closing the parable of the workers in the vineyard. Twice within one Gospel and now once in another, copyists have reached for Matthew 22:14’s memorable closing line to round off a different story that ends on a related but distinct point — here, that the FIRST guests specifically will miss the meal, not that a wide net catches both worthy and unworthy alike.

Verses 25–27 · οὐ μισεῖ τὸν πατέρα … οὐ βαστάζει τὸν σταυρὸν ἑαυτοῦ ou misei ton patera … ou bastazei ton stauron heautou — the one verse in the whole New Testament that tells a disciple to hate his own family, and a verb for carrying a cross Luke has already used a different way

⭐⭐⭐ “Hate” is the New Testament’s own word here — this form of the verb stands in eight verses of the whole New Testament, and every other one names something genuinely hostile: the world hating Jesus and his disciples (John 15:18–19, 23, already on these pages; 1 John 3:13, not yet on these pages), a man who does evil hating the light (John 3:20, already on these pages), Jesus’s own charge against the deeds of the Nicolaitans (Revelation 2:6, already on these pages). This is the one place the object of that same hostile verb is a disciple’s own father, mother, wife, children, brothers and sisters — and, in the same breath, his own life. Matthew’s version of this same saying softens the verb to a comparison: ‘whoever LOVES father or mother MORE THAN me is not worthy of me’ (Matthew 10:37, already on these pages) — the identical demand for absolute priority, stated as an excess of love rather than an act of hatred. This translation keeps Luke’s own harder word rather than borrowing Matthew’s softer one, and lets the note carry what the two Gospels share: neither is describing an emotion to feel, but a rank to give — Jesus first, even before the closest ties a hearer has.

⚠ The NVI on the Spanish shelf, like the TLB on the English (‘must love me far more than he does his own father’), avoids the word entirely: ‘no sacrifica el amor a su padre’, ‘does not sacrifice his love for his father’ — a paraphrase that trades the Greek’s own shock for something more comfortable to hear. Both the RV 1909 and the RV60 keep the harder «aborrece», hates, matching this translation’s own choice.

⭐⭐ Luke uses two different verbs for carrying a cross in his own Gospel, and drops a word the first time carried. Five chapters earlier, Jesus told a crowd, ‘let him deny himself and TAKE UP (aratō, from airō) his cross DAILY, and follow me’ (9:23, already on these pages). Here the verb is different, bastazei, CARRY or BEAR — and ‘daily’ is gone. Matthew’s own version of this saying uses a third verb again, lambanei, TAKE (Matthew 10:38, already on these pages) and closes not on ‘cannot be my disciple’ but ‘is not worthy of me’, the same substitution already noted above. And where Matthew’s disciple follows Jesus (akolouthei), both of Luke’s own sayings have the disciple COME (erchetai) after him — Luke’s consistent verb for the second half of the saying, kept the same across five chapters even as the verb for carrying the cross itself changes. Three Greek verbs, two Gospels, one gesture: this translation keeps each verse’s own word rather than smoothing the three into one English verb.

Verses 28–33 · τίς…θέλων πύργον οἰκοδομῆσαι … τίς βασιλεὺς πορευόμενος … συμβαλεῖν εἰς πόλεμον tis…thelōn pyrgon oikodomēsai … tis basileus poreuomenos … symbalein eis polemon — a builder and a king found nowhere else in the New Testament, arguing the identical point from opposite directions

⭐⭐⭐ Neither parable in this pair has a parallel anywhere else in the Gospels — both are found only in Luke. The builder who sits down first to count the cost, and the king who sits down first to count his army, argue the same point from opposite directions: one is a private, small-scale failure (a half-built tower, a public joke), the other a public, large-scale one (an army destroyed, or a king suing for peace before the fighting even starts) — poverty against death, the cost of a wall against the cost of a war, bracketing the range of what ‘counting the cost’ can mean before either story is applied.

The king’s arithmetic is not a rhetorical flourish: ten thousand against twenty thousand is a two-to-one disadvantage that ancient military writers (and modern ones) treat as effectively hopeless without some other decisive advantage, which is exactly why the sensible king in the story does not fight at all — he sends envoys ‘while the other is still far away’ and asks terms of peace, the one move in either parable that heads off the failure altogether rather than merely explaining it after the fact.

⭐⭐ “Say goodbye to” (apotassetai) is the New Testament’s own verb for taking leave of PEOPLE everywhere else it appears — and here, for the first and only time, its object is possessions rather than a person. The verb, in all its forms, stands in six verses of the whole New Testament: Jesus ‘taking leave’ of a crowd before going up a mountain to pray (Mark 6:46, already on these pages), Paul ‘taking leave’ of the brothers at Ephesus and Corinth (Acts 18:18, 21; both not yet on these pages) and of the believers at Troas (2 Corinthians 2:13, not yet on these pages) — every other instance a farewell to companions. The sixth is a would-be follower who asks Jesus, ‘first let me say goodbye to those in my house’ (Luke 9:61, already on these pages) — and is refused, since even that farewell counts as looking back at the plow. Here the same verb, the same demand for finality, is turned on a disciple’s belongings instead of his family: where 9:61 will not let a man say goodbye to people before following, 14:33 requires him to say goodbye to property instead.

Verses 34–35 · τὸ ἅλας μωρανθῇ, ἐν τίνι ἀρτυθήσεται … οὔτε εἰς γῆν οὔτε εἰς κοπρίαν to halas mōranthē, en tini artythēsetai … oute eis gēn oute eis koprian — the same Greek verb used of salt losing its point and of clever people losing theirs

⭐⭐⭐ The verb for salt ‘losing its taste’ is built on the same root as the Greek word for a FOOL — and Paul uses the identical verb of people, not salt. The verb mōrainō stands in exactly four verses of the whole New Testament: here, its one other Gospel use at Matthew 5:13 (already on these pages, ‘if the salt loses its taste’, the identical verb), and Paul’s own description of the philosophers of this age — ‘claiming to be wise, they became fools’ (Romans 1:22, already on these pages), the exact same verb, now describing not a seasoning but a mind — and once more of God himself: ‘Did not God make the wisdom of the world foolish?’ (1 Corinthians 1:20, already on these pages). Salt that stops tasting like salt and a clever argument that curdles into nonsense are, in the Greek, the same verb doing the same kind of work.

⭐⭐ Three Gospel sayings about salt losing its usefulness, three different pictures of what happens next. Matthew’s version ends with the salt ‘thrown out and trampled underfoot BY MEN’ (Matthew 5:13, already on these pages) — a public humiliation. Mark’s drops the disposal image entirely and turns inward: ‘have salt in yourselves, and be at peace with one another’ (Mark 9:50, already on these pages) — not a warning about being thrown away but an instruction about keeping peace in a community. Luke keeps the verb for ‘seasoned’ (artythēsetai) that Mark uses (artysete) rather than Matthew’s different verb (halisthēsetai, from the same root as ‘salt’ itself), but supplies a detail neither of the others has: salt too spent to season food is ‘fit neither for the soil nor for the manure pile’ — a small quantity of salt was a known additive to compost in the ancient Near East, so ruined salt fails a SECOND use as well as its first, useless twice over rather than simply thrown away once.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: son or ox (v5) kept as the reading every compared edition of the Greek actually carries, rather than the ‘ass or ox’ some English Bibles import from elsewhere; hate (v26) kept as Luke’s own harder word rather than softened toward Matthew’s ‘loves more than’; three separate Greek verbs for carrying a cross (airō, bastazō, lambanō) each kept in their own verse rather than flattened into one English verb; and the line ‘for many are called, but few are chosen’ left OUT of v24, on the same textual grounds it was already left out of Matthew 20:16.

Echoes paid. Luke 13:15’s own ox-or-donkey Sabbath argument, promised to return ‘at a Pharisee’s own table’, is answered and sharpened at vv1–6 — the same crowd, the same silence, now with a human being named first in the sentence. The dochē dictionary entry’s own forward promise, planted at Levi’s reception (5:29), is paid at v13. And Luke 9:61’s refused request to ‘say goodbye’ to family before following returns at v33, the identical verb now demanding a disciple say goodbye to possessions instead.

Echoes planted. The guest list of vv13 and 21 — the poor, the crippled, the lame, the blind — is not the last time this Gospel names the socially unwanted as the ones truly welcomed; the theme recurs through the parables still ahead. And the salt saying’s closing line, ‘let the one who has ears to hear, hear’, is a refrain this Gospel has used before and will use again whenever a hard teaching needs a reader to slow down rather than move on.

Next in sequence: Luke 15 — a shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep to find one, a woman who sweeps her whole house for one lost coin, and a father who runs to meet a son who is still a long way off.
