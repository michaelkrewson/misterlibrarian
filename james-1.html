James, the brother of Jesus and head of the Jerusalem church, writes to Jewish believers scattered abroad — a letter of practical wisdom: welcome trials, ask God for wisdom, tame the tongue, and prove faith by works. 'Pure religion is to look after orphans and widows in their affliction.'
Genuinely uncertain — one of the most disputed dates in the New Testament, ranging from the 40s to the 90s. ⚠ On the EARLY view (c. AD 45–49), before the Jerusalem council, it is among the very earliest Christian writings, rivalling Galatians and 1 Thessalonians for the title; the absence of any mention of the Gentile controversy or the council fits an early date. On the LATE view it is pseudonymous, written after James's death (c. AD 62) in his name. The traditional early date, with James the Just as author, is taken here; the range is reported.

Translator’s Notes

Verse 1 · the brother of the Lord who calls himself a slave

⚠ James is almost certainly JAMES the brother of Jesus — not one of the Twelve, but the head of the Jerusalem church, called “the Just,” the man Paul names as a “pillar” (Galatians 2:9) and whose verdict settled the Jerusalem council of Acts 15. ⚠ And the striking thing is what he does not say: he had every right to write “the brother of the Lord,” and instead writes “a slave of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ.” The one man who could claim the family tie renounces it for the servant’s title, and puts God and the risen brother on the same footing in the same breath.

“To the twelve tribes in the DISPERSION.” Diaspora — the scattering of the Jews among the nations. The address reads as Jewish-Christian believers outside the land, though it also carries a second sense: the whole people of God, scattered and awaiting gathering. It is one of the marks that make James the most Jewish book in the New Testament.

“GREETINGS.” Chairein — the ordinary, secular Greek way to open a letter (“greetings,” literally “rejoice”), not Paul’s theological charis, “grace.” ⚠ Only two other places in the New Testament use it to open: the field commander’s letter in Acts 23:26 — and the letter of the Jerusalem council in Acts 15:23, which James himself drafted. The small verbal fingerprint quietly supports the traditional author. KJV “greeting”; NIV “greetings.”

Verses 2–4 · joy in trials, and the word that will pivot

⚠ “Count it all joy… when you fall into TRIALS.” The book’s key word enters here: peirasmos, which means both a trial that comes from outside (hardship, persecution) and a temptation that works from inside — and James will pivot on that double sense at verse 13. Here it is the outer kind, and the counsel is nearly the opening of the Sermon on the Mount in another key (“rejoice and be glad,” Matthew 5:12): the testing is not to be merely survived but welcomed, because of what it makes. KJV “when ye fall into divers temptations”; NIV “trials of many kinds.”

“The testing of your faith produces ENDURANCE… that you may be PERFECT.” Hypomonē, the remaining-under met already in the Thessalonian letters; and teleios, “perfect,” the keyword of James — not flawless but complete, finished, having reached its end, the way fruit is ripe or a task is done. The chain is deliberate: trial makes endurance, endurance does its full work, and the work is a whole person “lacking in nothing.”

Verses 5–8 · ask for wisdom — but not with a divided soul

“If any of you lacks WISDOM, let him ask.” James stands in the stream of Jewish wisdom literature — Proverbs, Job, Sirach — and sophia here is not cleverness but the practical skill of living rightly under God. God gives it “generously” (haplōs, single-mindedly, with no strings) “and without reproach” — he does not make you feel small for asking. The promise all but quotes Jesus: “ask, and it will be given you” (Matthew 7:7).

⚠ “But let him ask in faith, DOUBTING nothing.” Diakrinomenos is the one divided against himself, of two minds; and the image James gives him is unforgettable — “a wave of the sea, driven by the wind and tossed about,” heaved this way and that with no will of its own. The prayer of a divided heart has no direction, and so gets nowhere.

⚠ “A DOUBLE-SOULED man.” Dipsychos — literally “two-souled” — is a word that appears nowhere in Greek before James and may well be his own coinage; the King James made it the familiar “double minded.” It names the person with two centres, half-wanting God and half-wanting the world, and it is James’s diagnosis of the whole spiritual disease he is writing against. KJV “a double minded man”; NWT “indecisive.”

Verses 9–11 · the low lifted, the rich a fading flower

⚠ The great reversal, stated flatly. The lowly brother is to boast “in his HEIGHT” (his exaltation in Christ), and the rich “in his LOWNESS” — the same paradox the Magnificat sings, the humble lifted and the mighty pulled down. James is the New Testament’s bluntest book on wealth (it only sharpens: 2:6, 5:1-6), and here the rich man’s glory is a wildflower under a hot wind.

“Like a flower of the grass he will pass away.” The picture is Isaiah 40:6-7 — “all flesh is grass… the grass withers, the flower fades” — and the detail is exact: the kausōn is the scorching east wind off the desert that can kill a field of flowers between morning and noon. “In the midst of his goings” — his comings and goings, his busy pursuits — “the rich man will wither”: not after his enterprises, but in the very middle of them.

Verses 12–15 · the pivot: God tests no one, desire does

“Blessed is the man who endures TRIAL… the crown of life.” A beatitude (makarios, the “blessed” of the Sermon on the Mount), and the “crown of life” that returns as the promise to the faithful at Revelation 2:10, “be faithful unto death.” The reward is for the one who stays under the weight (hypomenei, the verb of endurance) until he is dokimos — approved, like metal that has passed assay.

⚠ And here the one word turns. The same root that meant outer trial in verse 2 now means inner temptation — “let no one being tempted say, ‘I am tempted from God.’” James blocks the oldest excuse (“God set me up to fail”) with a hard theological claim: God is apeirastos kakōn, “untemptable by evils” — evil gets no purchase on him — “and he himself tempts no one.” The trial may come from God; the temptation never does.

⚠ The anatomy of a sin, told as a pregnancy. The mechanism is entirely internal: “each is tempted by his own desire (epithymia), dragged off and baited” (deleazō, the word for luring a fish or animal to a hook). Then a grim birth-sequence — desire conceives and bears sin; sin grows up and brings forth death. ⚠ The verb for “brings forth,” apokyēo, is a mother’s word, and James will use it again, deliberately reversed, three verses on: desire gives birth to death; God gives birth to us (verse 18).

Verses 16–21 · the Father who does not change, and the word to be received

⚠ “Every good giving… from the Father of the LIGHTS, with whom there is no… shadow of turning.” The counter-claim to verse 13: God is not the source of temptation but of every good gift, and the proof is his constancy. The imagery is astronomical — the “Father of the lights” made the sun, moon and stars, and those lights are exactly the things that do change: they rise and set, wax and wane, are eclipsed and turn at the solstice. God, unlike the things he made to mark time, has “no parallagē (variation) or tropēs aposkiasma (shadow cast by turning).” ⚠ The phrase is one of the hardest in the letter and the manuscripts vary; the sense is sure even where the syntax is knotted — the God of the changing lights is himself unchanging. KJV “no variableness, neither shadow of turning”; NIV “who does not change like shifting shadows.”

“He gave us BIRTH by the word of truth.” The reversed birth-word (apokyēo again, from verse 15): where desire mothers death, God mothers life — “of his own will,” by the gospel, “that we might be a kind of firstfruits (aparchē) of his creatures,” the first sheaf of a new creation given back to God.

“Quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger.” A wisdom proverb in three beats, and the hinge to what follows: since the “word of truth” birthed you, receive it — “with meekness the implanted word” (emphytos, engrafted, planted-in), the seed set in you that “is able to save your souls.” The anger of a man, James adds, “does not work the righteousness of God” — you do not argue people into holiness.

Verses 22–27 · the mirror, and the only religion God calls pure

⚠ “Be DOERS of the word, and not hearers only.” The sentence James is most remembered for, and the seed of the “faith without works” argument of chapter 2. The hearer-who-does-nothing is caught in a small, exact parable: he is like a man who “observes the face of his birth” — his natural, God-given face — “in a mirror,” then walks off and at once forgets what he looks like. ⚠ The ancient mirror was polished bronze, giving a real but dim image (Paul’s “through a glass darkly”); the point is not the mirror’s dimness but the man’s forgetfulness — the word shows you yourself, and doing nothing about it is walking away from your own reflection. This is nearly the close of the Sermon on the Mount: the one who hears these words and does them (Matthew 7:24).

“The perfect law, the law of LIBERTY.” Where Paul often sets law against freedom, James joins them: the nomos teleios is the law brought to its completion in the command to love (2:8), and to keep it is not bondage but liberty. The one who “bends down to look into” it — the verb is a stooping to peer closely, the opposite of the glance-and-forget — and stays, is “blessed in his doing.”

⚠ “Pure RELIGION… orphans and widows.” Thrēskeia is religion as observance — the outward practice, ritual, the cult — and James does something startling with it: he does not abolish it, he redefines it. The truly religious act, the pure and undefiled worship, is “to look in on orphans and widows in their affliction” (the two groups with no protector in the ancient world) “and to keep oneself unspotted from the world.” ⚠ It is the exact verdict of Isaiah 1:17, where God rejects the sacrifices and says “defend the orphan, plead for the widow” — care and cleanness put in the place of ceremony, and called the real thing. KJV “pure religion and undefiled”; NWT “the form of worship that is clean.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “greetings” for chairein, the plain letter-opening, not Paul’s “grace”; “trials” and then “tempted” for the single word peirasmos / peirazō, keeping the pivot at verses 2 and 13; “perfect” for teleios, complete and finished rather than flawless; “double-souled” for dipsychos, the likely coinage the KJV made “double minded”; “gives birth” for apokyēo kept in both directions (desire to death, God to us); “the Father of the lights… no shadow of turning” for the astronomical figure; and “religion” for thrēskeia, observance redefined as mercy and cleanness.

Echoes paid. The Sermon on the Mount throughout — joy in trial (Matthew 5:12), “ask and it will be given” (Matthew 7:7), the good Father’s gifts (Matthew 7:11), and the hearer who does (Matthew 7:24); the “crown of life” promised at Revelation 2:10; the fading flower of grass of Isaiah 40:6-7; and “pure religion” as the orphan-and-widow verdict of Isaiah 1:17.

Echoes planted. Faith and works, opened at “be doers, not hearers” and argued out at 2:14-26, “faith without works is dead” — the passage that made Luther call James “an epistle of straw”; the tongue, bridled here (1:26) and unleashed as “a fire… a restless evil” in chapter 3; the rich, a fading flower here and under judgment at 5:1-6; and wisdom, asked for here and defined at 3:17 as “first pure, then peaceable.”

James is new to the library — the most Jewish and most practical book in the New Testament, wisdom literature in a Christian key, very possibly written by the Lord’s own brother. Next in this book: chapter 2 — “my brothers, show no favouritism”, the poor man and the gold-ringed man in the assembly, the “royal law” to love your neighbour, and the letter’s most famous and most argued paragraph: “faith, if it has no works, is dead.”
