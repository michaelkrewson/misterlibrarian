Saul, still breathing threat and murder, goes to the high priest for letters to the synagogues of Damascus; a light from heaven and a voice stop him on the road, he is led blind into the city, and Ananias, sent to him in a vision, lays hands on him; he recovers sight, is baptized and proclaims Jesus in the synagogues. After considerable days the Jews plot to kill him and he is let down the wall in a basket; at Jerusalem Barnabas brings him to the apostles, the Hellenists try to kill him, and the brothers send him to Tarsus. The congregation has peace; Peter heals Aeneas at Lydda and raises Tabitha at Joppa, and stays there with Simon, a tanner.
Tied to chapter 8 by ‘still’ (v1) and otherwise undated: the chapter names no year and gives only ‘three days’ of blindness (v9), ‘some days’ with the disciples at Damascus (v19) and ‘considerable days’ before the plot (v23). Paul’s own account puts ‘three years’ between his return to Damascus from Arabia and his first visit to Jerusalem (Galatians 1:18); whether Luke’s ‘considerable days’ contain them is read both ways, and this library does not decide. The Peter scenes of vv32–43 are set ‘in those days’ only at v37 and are otherwise undated. This library does not supply a year.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · ἐμπνέων ἀπειλῆς καὶ φόνου … τῆς ὁδοῦ ὄντας empneōn apeilēs kai phonou … tēs hodou ontas — Saul on the road to Damascus in Acts 9:1–2: “breathing threat and murder,” letters to the synagogues, and “the Way”

“Breathing threat and murder.” Empneōn is the participle of empneō, “to breathe in,” and the verb stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. It takes a genitive, so that threat and murder are the air the man is breathing: Luke does not say that Saul utters threats but that he lives on them. Eti, “still,” ties the verse to 8:3, where he was “ravaging the congregation, going into house after house”; the scattering of 8:1 did not end the matter, it moved it. The stem of apeilē, “threat,” stands in four verses of Acts, and three of them are in chapter 4: the council’s threats at 4:17 and 4:21, and the apostles’ prayer about them at 4:29, “Jehovah, look upon their threats.” This verse is the fourth. KJV “breathing out threatenings and slaughter,” ASV “yet breathing threatening and slaughter,” Douay “as yet breathing out threatenings and slaughter,” NWT 1984 “still breathing threat and murder” (the 2013 revision the same), NIV “still breathing out murderous threats,” TLB “threatening with every breath and eager to destroy every Christian.” The TLB also opens the verse “But Paul,” and has “Paul! Paul!” from the sky at v4; the Greek has Saulos in the narration and Saoul in the voice, and Luke first writes “Saul, who is also Paul” at Acts 13:9.

The letters, and what the Greek leaves out. Saul “asked from him letters to Damascus, to the synagogues”: the letters go to the synagogues, not to a magistrate. The high priest is not named here (compare 4:6, where Annas and Caiaphas are). In his own tellings Saul says that “the high priest bears me witness, and the whole body of elders” (Acts 22:5) and that he went “with authority and commission from the chief priests” (26:12), neither yet on these pages. What force a Jerusalem letter had in Damascus, the Greek does not say, and this translation does not supply one. The errand is in the verse: ean tinas heurē … dedemenous agagē eis Ierousalēm, “if he found any … he might bring them to Jerusalem bound.” The same root returns in the mouths of the people who describe the errand — “to bind all who call on your name” (v14) and “bring them bound to the chief priests” (v21) — three times in one chapter, each time as what Saul came to do.

“The Way.” Tēs hodou ontas, “being of the Way,” is the first use in Acts of hodos, “road,” as the name of the movement (dictionary); it stands at 9:2, 19:9, 19:23, 22:4, 24:14 and 24:22, and Saul himself will say at 22:4 that he “persecuted this Way to the death.” The Greek does not say who named it, or why a road. Whether to capitalize it is already a reading: KJV “any of this way,” Douay “of this way,” Geneva “that were of that way,” ASV “of the Way,” NWT 1984 “belonged to The Way,” NIV “belonged to the Way.” “Both men and women,” andras te kai gynaikas, are the words of 8:3 (there without te) and of Saul’s own account at 22:4.

“Asked” — and the name. The verb is ēitēsato, “he asked for,” the verb Luke uses at Acts 13:21 of Israel’s request: “they asked for a king, and God gave them Saul, the son of Kish.” The Hebrew root behind it, sha’al, “to ask,” is the root of the verb for the people’s request in 1 Samuel 12:13 (not yet on these pages), “the king whom you chose, whom you asked for” (sh’eltem), and of Hannah’s “I have asked him of Jehovah” at 1 Samuel 1:20, where the name Samuel is explained. Reference works usually explain the name Sha’ul itself as “asked for”; that explanation is reported here and not verified. A man whose name means “asked for” asking for letters is a small coincidence that the Greek never remarks on; it is recorded and not leaned on.

Verses 3–6 · περιήστραψεν φῶς … Σαοὺλ Σαούλ, τί με διώκεις; … Τίς εἶ, κύριε; periēstrapsen phōs … Saoul Saoul, ti me diōkeis? … Tis ei, kyrie? — “Why are you persecuting me?” on the Damascus road in Acts 9:3–6: the light, the doubled name, “Who are you, Lord?” and the words the KJV adds

The light. Periēstrapsen, “flashed around,” is peri, “around,” with the verb of lightning, and it stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Acts 22:6 (not yet on these pages). The light does not strike Saul; it surrounds him, and it comes ek tou ouranou, “out of heaven.” Luke tells the event three times, once as narrator and twice in Paul’s mouth, and the tellings differ: at 22:6 it is “about noon” and the light is hikanon, “considerable”; at 26:13–14 it is “midday,” “beyond the brightness of the sun,” it shines around Saul “and those traveling with me,” and “all of us” fall to the ground — where at v7 here the men are left standing. This translation prints each account as it stands and does not merge them. KJV “there shined round about him a light from heaven,” ASV “there shone round about him a light out of heaven,” NWT 1984 and NIV “a light from heaven flashed around him,” TLB “a brilliant light from heaven spotted down upon him.”

“Saul, Saul.” The voice says Saoul Saoul, the Hebraic form of the name, where Luke’s narration has the Greek Saulos. The Hebraic form stands in six verses of the New Testament: here; v17, where Ananias uses it; Paul’s tellings at 22:7, at 22:13 (Ananias again) and at 26:14, where Luke adds that the voice spoke tē Hebraidi dialektō, “in the Hebrew dialect”; and, of the first king of Israel, at 13:21. The doubled name is an old pattern: Abraham, Abraham, Jacob, Jacob and Moses, Moses in the Hebrew; Martha, Martha and Simon, Simon in Luke’s own Gospel. In the three Hebrew cases the man answers “Here I am” (hinneni); Saul answers with a question. Ananias, at v10, gives the old answer: “Look — here I am, Lord.”

“Why are you persecuting me?” The pronoun is me. The people Saul is hunting are “the disciples of the Lord” (v1), “the Way” (v2) and “your holy ones” (v13), and the voice does not say “my disciples” but “me.” The identification is made from the other side in Matthew 25:40 and 45 — “you did it to me,” “neither did you do it to me.” How it is to be understood is read differently: the harm to his people counted as harm to him, or the congregation as his body. The verse states the pronoun and nothing more.

“Who are you, Lord?” Tis ei, kyrie: from a man who does not yet know who is speaking, kyrie is “sir” as easily as “Lord,” and the TLB prints it so: “Who is speaking, sir?” This translation keeps “Lord” so that the word reads the same here and at v10, where it is plainly Jesus speaking to Ananias (dictionary); the answer, Egō eimi Iēsous, “I am Jesus,” is what turns the vocative into a title. In the second telling the answer adds ho Nazōraios, “the Nazarene” (22:8, not yet on these pages); here the name stands bare.

⚠ What the KJV adds at vv5–6. After “I am Jesus whom thou persecutest,” the KJV goes on, “it is hard for thee to kick against the pricks,” and at v6, “And he trembling and astonished said, Lord, what wilt thou have me to do? And the Lord said unto him.” The Douay has both additions (“kick against the goad”) and so has the Geneva (“kick against pricks”); the ASV, NWT, NIV and TLB do not. The Greek printed here does not have them, and this translation follows it. They are not invented: the proverb is in the Greek of Acts 26:14, sklēron soi pros kentra laktizein, “hard for you to kick against the goads,” and the question is in the Greek of 22:10, Ti poiēsō, kyrie?, “What shall I do, Lord?” (neither yet on these pages). Reference works report that later manuscripts carried both into chapter 9 from the other two tellings; that is reported here and not verified. What the shorter text does to the scene is plain: after “I am Jesus,” Saul says nothing, and the next words spoken to him are an order. Lalēthēsetai soi ho ti se dei poiein, “it will be told you what you must do”: dei, “it is necessary,” is the verb of divine necessity in Luke and Acts, and it comes back at v16 for what Saul “must suffer.”

Verses 7–9 · ἀκούοντες μὲν τῆς φωνῆς μηδένα δὲ θεωροῦντες … οὐδὲν ἔβλεπεν … μὴ βλέπων akouontes men tēs phōnēs mēdena de theōrountes … ouden eblepen … mē blepōn — Did Saul’s companions hear the voice? Acts 9:7 against 22:9, the speechless men, and three days without sight

Hearing the voice, seeing no one. Two words in v7 stand in this verse alone in the New Testament: eneoi, “speechless,” and synodeuontes, “traveling with.” The men are akouontes men tēs phōnēs, “hearing indeed the voice,” with phōnēs in the genitive. At 22:9 (not yet on these pages) Paul says of the same men that they beheld the light tēn de phōnēn ouk ēkousan tou lalountos moi, “but the voice of the one speaking to me they did not hear,” with phōnēn in the accusative. The verb and the noun are the same in both verses; the case differs. A common way of reconciling them takes akouō with the genitive as hearing a sound and with the accusative as hearing what is said; Greek grammars report that distinction, and it is not a rule without exceptions, so this translation prints each verse as it stands and does not decide. KJV “hearing a voice,” Douay “hearing indeed a voice,” Geneva “hearing his voice,” ASV “hearing the voice,” NWT 1984 “hearing, indeed, the sound of a voice,” NIV “they heard the sound,” TLB “they heard the sound of someone’s voice.”

“He saw nothing.” Aneōgmenōn de tōn ophthalmōn autou ouden eblepen: his eyes are standing open (a perfect participle), and he sees ouden, “nothing.” Reference works report that the Textus Receptus line reads oudena, “no one,” here, which is the form the KJV prints, “he saw no man,” and the Geneva too, “saw no man”; the ASV and Douay have “he saw nothing,” NWT 1984 “he was seeing nothing,” NIV “he could see nothing.” The men lead him by the hand into the city he had come to enter with authority. His blindness is not closed eyes but open ones with nothing in them.

Three days. Ēn hēmeras treis mē blepōn, kai ouk ephagen oude epien: “he was three days not seeing, and he did not eat or drink.” The Greek does not call it a fast (it has no verb of fasting), and it says nothing of what he did; v11 gives the one fact, that he is praying. Esther 4:16 (not yet on these pages) has the same pair for a three-day fast, “neither eat nor drink three days, night or day,” so the verse has a Hebrew precedent in its wording. Luke elsewhere counts the Lord’s death and rising by “the third day” (Luke 24:46), which is not the same phrase as “three days,” and a reader may hear an echo; the Greek states the number and nothing about what it means. The word for blindness is only a participle of “seeing,” blepōn, and its negative mē; the verb of recovery at v12, v17 and v18 will be anablepō (dictionary).

Verses 10–12 · Ἁνανίας … Ἰδοὺ ἐγώ, κύριε … τὴν ῥύμην τὴν καλουμένην Εὐθεῖαν … ἐν ὁράματι Hananias … Idou egō, kyrie … tēn rhymēn tēn kaloumenēn Eutheian … en horamati — Ananias of Damascus and the street called Straight in Acts 9:10–12: “Here I am, Lord,” the house of Judas, and a vision inside a vision

Ananias. The name is the Greek form of Hebrew Ḥananyah, “Jehovah has been gracious,” and it is the name of the man who lied at Acts 5:1 (Ananias, husband of Sapphira); this is another man, introduced by Luke as tis mathētēs, “a certain disciple,” with nothing else said about him here. In Paul’s telling at 22:12 (not yet on these pages) he is “devout according to the law, well spoken of by all the Jews who live there”; here he is only a disciple who has heard of Saul. His answer to the call, Idou egō, kyrie, “Look — here I am, Lord,” is the answer of Abraham, Jacob and Moses (see the note at vv3–6), and the same idou that this translation prints “Look” (compare hineh).

“The lane called Straight.” Rhymē is a lane or a street, not the broad avenue: the word stands in four verses of the New Testament, Matthew 6:2, Luke 14:21, here and Acts 12:10 (Luke 14:21 sets plateias kai rhymas, “streets and lanes,” side by side). Eutheia, “straight,” is the feminine of euthys (dictionary), the adjective of “make his paths straight” in Matthew 3:3, Mark 1:3 and Luke 3:4, where the Gospels quote Isaiah 40:3. Reference works report that a long straight street still runs east–west through old Damascus and is called by this name; that is reported here, not verified. KJV “the street which is called Straight,” ASV “the street which is called Straight,” NWT 1984 “the street called Straight,” NIV “Straight Street,” Douay “the street that is called Stait,” TLB “Straight Street.” The house is en oikia Iouda, “in the house of Judas” — one more Judas, after Iscariot and Judas Barsabbas — and Saul is Tarsea, “a Tarsian” (the city is at Tarsus).

“For look, he is praying” — and a vision in a vision. Idou gar proseuchetai is a present tense: the man who was breathing murder at v1 is, in the house of Judas, praying. The Lord tells Ananias what Saul has seen: kai eiden andra en horamati Hananian onomati eiselthonta kai epithenta autō cheiras hopōs anablepsē, “and he has seen in a vision a man named Ananias come in and lay hands on him, so that he might recover sight.” Luke never narrates Saul’s own vision; it is known only because Ananias is told of it, and the Greek order differs by manuscript (the printed text has andra en horamati, the Textus Receptus line en horamati andra, reported). En horamati stands in Acts at 9:10, 9:12 and 10:3, the vision of Cornelius. NWT 1984 “come in and lay his hands upon him that he might recover sight,” NIV “a man named Ananias come and place his hands on him to restore his sight,” KJV “putting his hand on him, that he might receive his sight” (singular “hand”; the printed Greek has the plural, cheiras, and the Textus Receptus line is reported to have the singular), Douay and Geneva set the whole verse in parentheses.

Verses 13–16 · τοῖς ἁγίοις σου … ἐπικαλουμένους τὸ ὄνομά σου … σκεῦος ἐκλογῆς … ὑποδείξω … δεῖ … παθεῖν tois hagiois sou … epikaloumenous to onoma sou … skeuos eklogēs … hypodeixō … dei … pathein — The “chosen vessel” in Acts 9:13–16: skeuos eklogēs, “holy ones,” calling on the name, and what Saul “must suffer”

“Holy ones,” and “those who call on your name.” Ananias objects in the words of what he has heard: tois hagiois sou, “your holy ones” (dictionary). It is the first time in Acts that hagioi is used as a name for the believers, and it stands as such in four verses of the book, three of them in this chapter (9:13, 9:32, 9:41) and the fourth in Saul’s own words, 26:10. KJV “thy saints,” ASV “thy saints,” Douay “thy saints,” Geneva “thy saints,” NWT 1984 “your holy ones,” NIV “your holy people,” TLB “the believers.” At v14 the people are tous epikaloumenous to onoma sou, “those who call on your name” — the phrase of Acts 2:21, “everyone who calls on the name of Jehovah will be saved,” here turned from Jehovah’s name to Jesus’. The stem stands in Acts in this sense at 2:21, 9:14, 9:21 and 22:16; at 7:59 Stephen is the one “calling on” (epikaloumenon), and Saul was standing by.

“A chosen vessel.” Skeuos eklogēs is a vessel “of choice,” a Hebraic genitive that is usually read “a chosen vessel,” and this translation prints that and says what the Greek has. Ekloge, “choosing, election,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament (here, Romans 9:11, 11:5, 11:7 and 11:28, 1 Thessalonians 1:4, 2 Peter 1:10), and Acts has it here alone. Skeuos, “vessel,” is any article or container, and after this verse it stands in Acts at 10:11, 10:16 and 11:5, where it is the “vessel like a great sheet” that Peter sees let down at Joppa. KJV, ASV and Geneva “a chosen vessel,” NWT 1984 “a chosen vessel,” Douay “a vessel of election,” NIV “my chosen instrument,” TLB “my chosen instrument.” The task is bastasai to onoma mou, “to bear my name”: bastazō is to carry a load, and the name is the load, carried “before nations and kings and sons of Israel” in an order that puts the nations first.

“I will show him — how much he must suffer.” Egō gar hypodeixō autō hosa dei auton hyper tou onomatos mou pathein: hypodeiknymi, “to show, to make plain,” stands in six verses of the New Testament (Matthew 3:7, Luke 3:7, 6:47, 12:5, here and Acts 20:35), and here the thing shown is a cost. Dei … pathein is the phrase of Luke 24:26, “Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things?” and of 24:46, laid now on the man who was breathing murder; and hyper tou onomatos mou, “for the sake of my name,” answers to onoma mou of the verse before. The apostles at Acts 5:41 had already been “counted worthy to be dishonored for the Name.” KJV “how great things he must suffer for my name’s sake,” ASV “how many things he must suffer for my name’s sake,” NWT 1984 “how many things he must suffer for my name,” NIV “how much he must suffer for my name.” The conversation also gives the one reply that Ananias gets: poreuou, “Go,” and a reason, hoti, “because” — an order with a reason attached, not an argument.

Verses 17–19 · Σαοὺλ ἀδελφέ … πλησθῇς πνεύματος ἁγίου … ὡς λεπίδες … ἀναστὰς ἐβαπτίσθη Saoul adelphe … plēsthēs pneumatos hagiou … hōs lepides … anastas ebaptisthē — Saul’s baptism in Acts 9:17–19: “Brother Saul,” filled with Holy Spirit, the “scales,” and “rising, he was baptized”

“Saul, brother.” The first word Ananias says to the man he was afraid of is the Hebraic name and a family word, Saoul adelphe. He then says who sent him: ho kyrios apestalken me, Iēsous ho ophtheis soi en tē hodō hē ērchou, “the Lord has sent me — Jesus, who appeared to you on the road by which you were coming.” “The Lord” is Jesus here, named in apposition, and Ananias knows of the road because he was told at vv10–12; Saul has said nothing since “Who are you, Lord?” Ophtheis is the passive of horaō, “was seen by,” the verb of the risen Lord’s appearances (Acts 1:3 has the related optanomenos; 13:31 has ōphthē). KJV “Brother Saul, the Lord, even Jesus, that appeared unto thee in the way as thou camest,” NWT 1984 “Saul, brother, the Lord, the Jesus that appeared to you on the road over which you were coming,” NIV “Brother Saul, the Lord—Jesus, who appeared to you on the road as you were coming here—has sent me,” Douay “the Lord Jesus hath sent me, he that appeared to thee in the way as thou camest,” TLB “Brother Paul, the Lord Jesus, who appeared to you on the road, has sent me.”

Hands, sight, Spirit, water. Ananias lays on his hands (epitheis ep’ auton tas cheiras, dictionary) with a purpose clause in two parts: hopōs anablepsēs kai plēsthēs pneumatos hagiou, “so that you may recover sight and be filled with Holy Spirit.” The next verse reports the sight and the baptism and does not report the Spirit’s arrival; the Greek states the purpose and not the moment. Acts does not keep one order for hands, Spirit and water: at 8:14–17 the Samaritans are baptized, and later receive Holy Spirit when hands are laid; here hands come first, sight returns, and water follows; at 10:44–48 the Spirit falls before anyone is baptized. This translation prints what each passage says and does not make one order the rule.

“As if scales.” Hōs lepides stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the word lepis is not found anywhere else in it. The hōs, “as if,” is part of the Greek: Luke says something fell away that looked like scales, not that scales fell. The scales belong to this telling alone. In Ananias’s words at 22:13 (not yet on these pages) the command is anablepson, “recover your sight,” and Paul’s answer is kagō autē tē hōra aneblepsa eis auton, “and in that very hour I looked up at him”: the verb twice, command and fulfillment, as in the three-fold anablepō of Luke 18:41–43. KJV and Geneva “as it had been scales,” ASV and Douay “as it were scales,” NWT 1984 “what looked like scales,” NIV “something like scales,” TLB “it was as though scales fell from his eyes.”

“Rising, he was baptized.” Anastas ebaptisthē: the participle is of anistēmi, “to stand up, to rise,” and the verb is the spine of this chapter. It is said to Saul at v6 (anastēthi), to Ananias at v11 (anastas), of Saul at v18, to Aeneas at v34 (anastēthi, “rise”), of Peter at v39 (anastas), to Tabitha at v40 (Tabitha, anastēthi), and of Peter’s hand at v41 (anestēsen autēn, “he raised her up”). The same participle will stand in Ananias’s own words at 22:16, anastas baptisai, “rising, be baptized,” with “calling on his name” beside it (not yet on these pages). The verb is passive, ebaptisthē, with no agent: the Greek does not say who baptized him. At v19 he “took food” and “was strengthened,” and was “with the disciples in Damascus for some days,” hēmeras tinas, a phrase that will be answered by the “considerable days” of vv23 and 43.

Verses 20–22 · ἐκήρυσσεν τὸν Ἰησοῦν ὅτι οὗτός ἐστιν ὁ υἱὸς τοῦ θεοῦ … ὁ πορθήσας … συνέχυννεν … συμβιβάζων ekēryssen ton Iēsoun hoti houtos estin ho huios tou theou … ho porthēsas … synechynnen … symbibazōn — Saul proclaims “the Son of God” in Acts 9:20–22: the man who “laid waste” (porthēsas) and the Jews of Damascus “bewildered” by proof

“The Son of God.” The phrase ho huios tou theou stands in Acts in this verse alone; what Saul is proving at v22 is a different title, ho christos, “the Christ.” Ekēryssen is “was proclaiming,” the verb of Philip at 8:5 (dictionary), and the place is en tais synagōgais, “in the synagogues”: the very synagogues to which the letters of v2 were addressed. The printed Greek has ton Iēsoun, “Jesus”; the Textus Receptus line is reported to read ton Christon, “Christ,” which is what the KJV prints, “he preached Christ in the synagogues,” and the Geneva too, “preached Christ.” ASV “proclaimed Jesus,” Douay “preached Jesus in the synagogues,” NWT 1984 “preach Jesus,” NIV “preach in the synagogues that Jesus is the Son of God,” TLB “tell everyone there the Good News about Jesus.”

“Laid waste.” The crowd’s question reads ouch houtos estin ho porthēsas en Ierousalēm tous epikaloumenous to onoma touto, “Is not this the man who laid waste in Jerusalem those who call on this name?” Portheō is the verb of sacking a city. It stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, and in Saul’s own words at Galatians 1:13, “I was persecuting the congregation of God and trying to destroy it,” and 1:23, “the one who once persecuted us is now announcing the faith he once tried to destroy.” It is a different verb from the elymaineto, “ravaging,” of 8:3, and this translation keeps the two apart. KJV “destroyed,” ASV and Geneva “made havoc of,” Douay “persecuted,” NWT 1984 “ravaged,” NIV “raised havoc,” TLB “persecuted Jesus’ followers so bitterly.” The crowd goes on to state the errand almost word for word as v2 stated it — hina dedemenous autous agagē epi tous archiereis, “that he might bring them bound to the chief priests” — and the verb that opens their reaction, existanto, “were amazed,” is the verb of Simon’s amazement and of the amazement of the Samaritans at 8:9–13 (dictionary).

“Bewildering” by proof. Enedynamouto is passive, “was being empowered”: the power is received, not claimed. Synechynnen, “was bewildering,” is the verb of the Pentecost crowd that came together and was “bewildered” (Acts 2:6) and of the whole city “in confusion” at 21:31 (not yet on these pages); this translation prints it the same way at 2:6 and here. Symbibazōn is “bringing together”: the verb is that of the body “fitted together” in Ephesians 4:16 and Colossians 2:19 (neither yet on these pages), and in Acts 16:10 it is the verb of “concluding.” The Greek does not say from what he put the proof together. KJV “confounded the Jews which dwelt at Damascus, proving that this is very Christ,” ASV “confounded the Jews that dwelt at Damascus, proving that this is the Christ,” NWT 1984 “proved logically that this is the Christ,” NIV “baffled the Jews living in Damascus by proving that Jesus is the Messiah,” Douay “affirming that this is the Christ,” Geneva “confirming that this was that Christ,” TLB “couldn’t withstand his proofs that Jesus was indeed the Christ.”

Verses 23–25 · ἡμέραι ἱκαναί … ἀνελεῖν … ἐν σπυρίδι hēmerai hikanai … anelein … en spyridi — The escape from Damascus in a basket, Acts 9:23–25: “considerable days,” the plot against Saul, and the spyris — against 2 Corinthians 11:32–33 and Galatians 1:17–18

“Considerable days.” Hēmerai hikanai, “considerable days,” is a phrase that will close the chapter at v43, hēmeras hikanas, of Peter’s stay at Joppa; the chapter’s two halves are bracketed by it. Hikanos is elastic in time: at 8:11 it is the “considerable time” of Simon’s magic, at 14:3 a “considerable time” of speaking boldly. In Galatians Paul says that after the revelation he “went away into Arabia, and again returned to Damascus,” and that “after three years” he went up to Jerusalem (Galatians 1:17–18). Acts has no Arabia. Whether Luke’s “considerable days” contain Paul’s three years, and where Arabia falls between v22 and v23, is read both ways, and neither text says; this translation prints the Greek of each and does not join them.

The plot, and who was watching the gates. Synebouleusanto hoi Ioudaioi anelein auton: “the Jews took counsel to kill him.” The stem of anaireō, “to kill,” is the stem of 8:1, where Saul was “approving of his killing” (anairesei), and it stands three times in this chapter with Saul as its object, at vv23, 24 and 29. In Acts the persecutor has become the one hunted with his own chapter’s verb. At v24 “they were watching the gates, day and night” — the Jews, by the Greek. In 2 Corinthians 11:32 (not yet on these pages), Paul says that “in Damascus the ethnarch of King Aretas was guarding the city of the Damascenes to seize me.” Joining the two so that the ethnarch acts at the Jews’ urging is a common reading; it is in neither text.

The basket. Kathēkan auton chalasantes en spyridi: “they let him down, lowering him in a basket.” Spyris stands in five verses of the New Testament: here, and the four that tell of the seven loaves and the four thousand (Matthew 15:37, 16:10, Mark 8:8 and 8:20). The twelve baskets of the five thousand are a different word, kophinos (Mark 6:43), and Jesus himself keeps the two apart at Mark 8:19–20: “how many kophinous … how many spyridōn.” Paul’s own word, at 2 Corinthians 11:33, is a third, sarganē, which stands in that verse alone, and he adds a detail Luke does not: “through a window, … through the wall.” Both accounts have the same verb of lowering, chalaō. This translation prints “basket” for spyris, as at Matthew 15:37 (Mark 8:8 has “hampers”). KJV “let him down by the wall in a basket,” ASV “let him down through the wall, lowering him in a basket,” Douay “letting him down in a basket,” NWT 1984 “through an opening in the wall, lowering him in a basket,” NIV “lowered him in a basket through an opening in the wall,” TLB “let him down in a basket through an opening in the city wall,” Geneva “let him down by a rope in a basket” (the rope is not in the Greek).

“His disciples.” The printed Greek has hoi mathētai autou, “his disciples” — the people who have come to believe through Saul’s own preaching, for he has disciples already. The Textus Receptus line is reported to have auton, “him,” in that place, and that is the form behind the KJV, “the disciples took him by night,” and the Geneva has the same words, “the disciples took him by night”; the Douay “the disciples taking him in the night.” ASV and NWT 1984 “his disciples took him,” NIV “his followers took him,” TLB “some of his converts.”

Verses 26–30 · ἐπείραζεν κολλᾶσθαι … Βαρναβᾶς ἐπιλαβόμενος … ἐπαρρησιάσατο … Ἑλληνιστάς … Ταρσόν epeirazen kollasthai … Barnabas epilabomenos … eparrēsiasato … Hellēnistas … Tarson — Saul in Jerusalem in Acts 9:26–30: Barnabas, “spoke boldly,” the Hellenists, and Tarsus — against Galatians 1:18–24

“He tried to join himself.” Epeirazen kollasthai tois mathētais: the verb kollaomai, “to be glued to, to join oneself,” stands in Acts at 5:13, where of the rest “not one dared to join them”; at 8:29, where the Spirit tells Philip “join yourself to this chariot”; here; and at 10:28 and 17:34 (the second not yet on these pages). The imperfect epeirazen is “was trying,” and the verse says what stood in the way: pantes ephobounto auton, mē pisteuontes hoti estin mathētēs, “all were afraid of him, not believing that he was a disciple.” They are not said to doubt that he had changed; they are said to doubt that he was one of them. KJV “assayed to join himself to the disciples,” ASV “assayed to join himself to the disciples,” Douay “essayed to join himself to the disciples,” Geneva “assayed to join himself with the disciples,” NWT 1984 “made efforts to join himself to the disciples,” NIV “tried to join the disciples,” TLB “tried to meet with the believers.”

Barnabas, and what Galatians says of the same visit. Barnabas de epilabomenos auton ēgagen pros tous apostolous: “Barnabas, taking hold of him, brought him to the apostles.” This is the Barnabas of 4:36, “son of encouragement,” and the encyclopedia entry is Barnabas. What he “described in full” (diēgēsato) is three things: that Saul “saw the Lord on the road,” that the Lord “spoke to him,” and how in Damascus he “spoke boldly in the name of Jesus.” Paul’s own account of the first Jerusalem visit is in Galatians 1:18–19: “I went up to Jerusalem to get to know Cephas, and I stayed with him fifteen days; but I saw none of the other apostles except James the Lord’s brother.” He adds an oath, “before God I am not lying” (1:20), and says he was “unknown by face to the congregations of Judea” (1:22). Acts has “the apostles” and “going in and out in Jerusalem.” Reading Luke’s “apostles” as Peter and James is a common way of joining the two; it is a reading, not a sentence in either text, and Paul’s “Judea” is a region and Luke’s “Jerusalem” a city.

“Spoke boldly,” and “going in and out.” Eparrēsiasato and parrēsiazomenos (vv27, 28) are the verb of parrēsia, “boldness” (dictionary), the apostles’ prayer at 4:29 and their answer at 4:31. The noun has been in Acts since 2:29; the verb stands in seven verses of Acts (9:27, 9:28, 13:46, 14:3, 18:26, 19:8, 26:26), and these are the first two. Eisporeuomenos kai ekporeuomenos, “going in and going out,” is the Hebrew idiom of a life lived among people, “your going out and your coming in” (Psalm 121:8); Acts 1:21 has the same pair with different verbs, “the Lord Jesus went in and went out among us.” At the end of v28 the printed Greek has tou kyriou, “the Lord,” and the Textus Receptus line is reported to read “the Lord Jesus,” which is the KJV and Geneva (“in the name of the Lord Jesus”); ASV, Douay, NWT 1984, NIV and TLB have “the Lord.” For v27 the shelf: KJV “preached boldly at Damascus,” ASV “preached boldly in the name of Jesus,” NWT 1984 “spoken boldly in the name of Jesus,” NIV “preached fearlessly in the name of Jesus,” Douay “dealt confidently in the name of Jesus.”

The Hellenists, and Tarsus. Elalei te kai synezētei pros tous Hellēnistas: “he was speaking and disputing with the Hellenists” (dictionary). Synezētei is the verb of Acts 6:9, where men from the synagogues of the Freedmen, of Cyrenians and Alexandrians, “and of those from Cilicia and Asia” rose up “disputing with Stephen.” Saul is from Tarsus in Cilicia (22:3), and reference works propose that he was among those at 6:9; Luke does not say so, and this translation does not add it. Epecheiroun anelein auton, “they were attempting to kill him,” is the plot of v23 again, with the Hellenists, the circle in which Stephen was killed, as the ones attempting it. KJV and Geneva “Grecians,” ASV “Grecian Jews,” NWT 1984 and TLB “Greek-speaking Jews,” NIV “Hellenistic Jews,” Douay “disputed with the Greeks.” At v30 “the brothers” — the word used of the whole body — “brought him down to Caesarea” (Caesarea) and “sent him out to Tarsus”: Tarsus, in Acts at 9:11, 9:30, 11:25, 21:39 and 22:3. Paul’s own account ends at the same country: “Then I went into the regions of Syria and Cilicia” (Galatians 1:21). Saul is not named again in Acts until Barnabas goes to Tarsus to look for him, at 11:25.

Verse 31 · Ἡ μὲν οὖν ἐκκλησία … εἶχεν εἰρήνην οἰκοδομουμένη … τῷ φόβῳ τοῦ κυρίου καὶ τῇ παρακλήσει τοῦ ἁγίου πνεύματος Hē men oun ekklēsia … eichen eirēnēn oikodomoumenē … tō phobō tou kyriou kai tē paraklēsei tou hagiou pneumatos — “The congregation … had peace” in Acts 9:31: one congregation across Judea, Galilee and Samaria, “the fear of Jehovah” and “the comfort of the Holy Spirit”

One congregation, not several. The Greek is hē ekklēsia, singular, and every verb that follows is singular: it “had” (eichen) peace, “being built up” (oikodomoumenē), “walking” (poreuomenē), “was being multiplied” (eplēthyneto) — one congregation spread over three regions. The Textus Receptus line is reported to read the plural, and the KJV prints it, “Then had the churches rest throughout all Judaea and Galilee and Samaria, and were edified,” as the Geneva does, “Then had the Churches rest.” ASV “the church throughout all Judaea and Galilee and Samaria had peace,” Douay “the church had peace throughout all Judea,” NWT 1984 “entered into a period of peace,” NIV “the church throughout Judea, Galilee and Samaria enjoyed a time of peace,” TLB “the church had peace throughout Judea, Galilee and Samaria.” This translation reads “congregation” for ekklēsia (dictionary), as at 8:1 and 8:3.

Judea, Galilee, Samaria. Acts 1:8 named “Jerusalem and all Judea and Samaria”; this is the first verse of Acts to add Galilee as a region, and the name stands as a region in three verses of the book (9:31, 10:37, 13:31). How a congregation came to be in Galilee is not narrated: in Acts the Galileans are the men who stand looking up at 1:11, and the crowd at 2:7. The verse is a summary of the kind Luke sets between narratives, like 6:7, “the word of God kept growing … the number of the disciples in Jerusalem was being multiplied exceedingly”; the same verb, plēthynō, ends this one. Luke does not say that the peace came because Saul had gone to Tarsus. The order of the verses lets a reader draw that line, and the Greek does not draw it.

“The fear of Jehovah” and “the comfort of the Holy Spirit.” Tō phobō tou kyriou reads “Jehovah” here because it is the formula of the Hebrew Bible, yir’at Yhwh, as in Proverbs 1:7, “The fear of Jehovah is the beginning of knowledge”; the NWT 1984 prints “the fear of Jehovah,” and KJV, ASV, Douay, NIV and TLB “the fear of the Lord.” Paraklēsis is the noun in huios paraklēseōs, “son of encouragement,” the name the apostles gave Barnabas at 4:36 (dictionary); Barnabas has just been in the chapter, at v27. The shelf reads it “comfort” in KJV, ASV and NWT 1984, “consolation” in Douay, “encouraged” in NIV.

Verses 32–35 · Λύδδα … Αἰνέαν … κραβάττου … Ἰᾶταί σε Ἰησοῦς Χριστός· ἀνάστηθι καὶ στρῶσον σεαυτῷ … τὸν Σαρῶνα Lydda … Aineian … krabattou … Iatai se Iēsous Christos; anastēthi kai strōson seautō … ton Sarōna — Peter heals Aeneas at Lydda in Acts 9:32–35: “Jesus Christ heals you,” the mat, “make your own bed,” and the Sharon

Peter, and the holy ones at Lydda. Dierchomenon dia pantōn, “passing through all,” has no noun after “all”; the shelf supplies one or none. KJV “throughout all quarters,” Geneva “throughout all quarters,” ASV “throughout all parts,” NWT 1984 “going through all [parts]” (the brackets mark a supplied word), Douay “as he passed through, visiting all,” NIV “traveled about the country,” TLB “traveled from place to place to visit them.” Peter was last seen returning toward Jerusalem from Samaria (8:25). The people at Lydda are tous hagious, “the holy ones” — the second use of the word in Acts (the note at vv13–16) — so there is already a congregation there; Luke does not say how it began. Lydda is on the coastal plain, near Joppa by the Greek of v38, and the encyclopedia entry is Lydda.

Aeneas, and the mat. Luke calls him anthrōpon tina, “a certain man,” and not “a certain disciple” as he calls Tabitha at v36; the Greek does not say whether he was one of the holy ones, a Jew or neither. Ex etōn oktō is “from eight years,” and he is paralelymenos, “paralyzed,” the word of those Philip healed at 8:7. The krabattos, “mat,” is the word of Mark 2:11 (“take up your mat”) and of John 5:8, and it stands in Acts at 5:15, where the sick were laid “on cots and mats, so that as Peter came by at least his shadow might overshadow some of them”; here Peter meets one man on one. The word stands in eleven verses of the New Testament, and Peter’s two are among them.

“Jesus Christ heals you.” Iatai se Iēsous Christos is a present tense: Jesus Christ heals you, now, and Peter is the one who says it. There is no formula of the name, as there was at 3:6, “in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene.” KJV and Geneva “Jesus Christ maketh thee whole,” ASV “Jesus Christ healeth thee,” Douay “the Lord Jesus Christ healeth thee,” NWT 1984 and NIV “Jesus Christ heals you,” TLB “Jesus Christ has healed you!” (a past tense the Greek does not have). Anastēthi kai strōson seautō: “rise and spread for yourself.” The verb strōnnymi has no object here. Most of the shelf supplies “make your bed”: KJV, ASV and Douay “make thy bed,” NWT 1984 “make up your bed,” TLB “make your bed”; NIV has “roll up your mat,” and Geneva “truss thy couch together.” The same verb is the verb of the “furnished” upper room at Mark 14:15 and Luke 22:12, so a reader can also supply a meal; the sentence itself says only “spread for yourself.” This translation prints “make your own bed” and says what the Greek has. Kai euthys anestē: “and immediately he rose.”

The Sharon, and “turned to the Lord.” Ton Sarōna has the article: “the Sharon,” the coastal plain (Sharon), where the Hebrew of Joshua 12:18 has “the king of the Sharon.” KJV and Douay spell it “Saron,” ASV, Geneva, NIV and TLB “Sharon,” NWT 1984 “the [plain of] Sharon.” Everyone who lived at Lydda and in the Sharon “saw him, and they turned to the Lord” — epestrepsan epi ton kyrion. The same verb turns up five verses later, in a different sense: Peter, epistrepsas pros to sōma, “turning to the body,” at v40. “Lord” is kept here, because the verb is also an Old Testament idiom and the verse before names Jesus Christ as the healer, so the referent is open (dictionary). KJV, ASV, NWT 1984, NIV, Geneva and TLB “turned to the Lord,” Douay “were converted to the Lord.”

Verses 36–38 · μαθήτρια … Ταβιθά … Δορκάς … ἔργων ἀγαθῶν καὶ ἐλεημοσυνῶν … ὑπερῴῳ … μὴ ὀκνήσῃς mathētria … Tabitha … Dorkas … ergōn agathōn kai eleēmosynōn … hyperōō … mē oknēsēs — Tabitha, also called Dorcas, in Acts 9:36–38: mathētria, the one woman “disciple” by that word in the New Testament, “good works and alms,” and the upper room

A woman disciple. Mathētria is the feminine of mathētēs, “disciple,” and it stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. This translation prints “a woman disciple.” The shelf mostly loses the feminine: KJV, ASV and Douay “a certain disciple named Tabitha,” NWT 1984 “a certain disciple,” NIV “a disciple named Tabitha,” and only the Geneva marks it, “a certain woman, a disciple named Tabitha.” The TLB drops the Aramaic name and prints “a woman named Dorcas” with “Gazelle” as her gloss, and “a believer” for the disciple.

Tabitha, which is Dorcas. Hē diermēneuomenē legetai Dorkas: “which, translated, is called Dorcas.” Dorkas is the Greek word for a gazelle; the Hebrew word for the animal is tsevi, in the list of clean animals at Deuteronomy 14:5, “the deer and the gazelle,” and its feminine form is a woman’s name in the Hebrew Bible, Tsivyah (Zibiah of Beersheba, the mother of Joash, 2 Chronicles 24:1, not yet on these pages). Reference works give the Aramaic Tabitha as “gazelle” too; that is reported here and not verified from the archive. The NIV’s parenthesis, “in Greek her name is Dorcas,” says the same thing from the other end. Luke gives both names and uses the Aramaic one at v40, in her raising; the Greek name is the translation.

“Good works and alms.” Plērēs ergōn agathōn kai eleēmosynōn hōn epoiei: “full of good works and alms that she used to do” (the imperfect, epoiei, is a habit). Eleēmosynē stands in Acts in eight verses: at 3:2, 3:3 and 3:10 it is what the lame man asks for at the gate, here it is what a woman gives, and at 10:2, 10:4 and 10:31 it is what Cornelius gives. KJV, ASV and Douay “full of good works and almsdeeds which she did,” Geneva “full of good works and alms which she did,” NWT 1984 “abounded in good deeds and gifts of mercy that she was rendering,” NIV “always doing good and helping the poor,” TLB “always doing kind things for others, especially for the poor.”

The upper room, and “do not hesitate.” Lousantes ethēkan autēn en hyperōō: “having washed her, they laid her in an upper room.” Hyperōon stands in Acts at 1:13, where the eleven went up to it, here twice (vv37, 39) and at 20:8. The two men sent to Peter say Mē oknēsēs dielthein heōs hēmōn, “Do not hesitate to come over to us”: the verb okneō stands in this verse alone in the New Testament (the adjective, “slothful,” is at Matthew 25:26, Romans 12:11 and Philippians 3:1). They do not ask him to raise her; Luke does not say what they hoped. KJV “desiring him that he would not delay to come to them,” ASV “Delay not to come on unto us,” Douay “not be slack to come unto them,” NWT 1984 “Please do not hesitate to come on as far as us,” NIV “Please come at once!,” TLB “beg him to return with them to Joppa.”

Verses 39–42 · χιτῶνας καὶ ἱμάτια … ἐκβαλὼν ἔξω πάντας … θεὶς τὰ γόνατα … Ταβιθά, ἀνάστηθι … ἀνεκάθισεν … παρέστησεν αὐτὴν ζῶσαν chitōnas kai himatia … ekbalōn exō pantas … theis ta gonata … Tabitha, anastēthi … anekathisen … parestēsen autēn zōsan — Peter raises Tabitha in Acts 9:39–42: the widows and the garments, “Tabitha, rise,” and the echoes of Jesus raising Jairus’s daughter and the widow’s son

The widows and the garments. Paresthēsan autō pasai hai chērai klaiousai kai epideiknymenai chitōnas kai himatia hosa epoiei met’ autōn ousa hē Dorkas: “all the widows stood by him weeping and showing the tunics and garments that Dorcas used to make while she was with them.” Himatia is the word of Acts 7:58, where the witnesses laid down their garments at the feet of a young man called Saul; there garments are put down for a killing, here they are held up as the evidence of a life. KJV, ASV and Geneva “the coats and garments which Dorcas made,” Douay “the coats and garments which Dorcas made them,” NWT 1984 “many inner garments and outer garments that Dorcas used to make,” NIV “the robes and other clothing that Dorcas had made while she was still with them,” TLB “the coats and other garments Dorcas had made for them.”

Put outside, kneeling, rise. Ekbalōn de exō pantas ho Petros kai theis ta gonata prosēuxato, kai epistrepsas pros to sōma eipen, Tabitha, anastēthi. Three things in the verse are the Gospels’ own. Jesus at Jairus’s house “put them all outside” (ekbalōn pantas, Mark 5:40); kneeling, theis ta gonata, stands in Luke 22:41 (Jesus), Acts 7:60 (Stephen), here, and 20:36 and 21:5; and the command is the verb of this chapter, anastēthi, “rise,” where Mark 5:41 has the Aramaic Talitha koum and Luke 8:54 the Greek Hē pais, egeire, “Child, arise” (Luke 8:54). Reference works have long noticed that the Aramaic of Mark’s command (Talitha) and Peter’s Tabitha differ by one letter; the Greek of Acts 9:40 does not contain the Aramaic koum, and the likeness is in the name and the order, not in a quotation. There is no formula of the name here either; Peter prays first, then speaks to the body. KJV “Peter put them all forth, and kneeled down, and prayed,” ASV “Peter put them all forth, and kneeled down and prayed,” NWT 1984 “Peter put everybody outside and, bending his knees, he prayed,” NIV “Peter sent them all out of the room; then he got down on his knees and prayed.” At the command: KJV, Douay and Geneva “Tabitha, arise,” NWT 1984 “rise!,” NIV “Tabitha, get up,” TLB “Get up, Dorcas” — the one version that drops her Aramaic name at the moment Luke uses it.

“Sat up,” and “presented alive.” Anekathisen, “she sat up,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and Luke 7:15, where the widow’s son at Nain “sat up and began to speak.” Dous de autē cheira anestēsen autēn, “giving her a hand, he raised her up,” is not the “taking hold of her hand” of Mark 5:41 and Luke 8:54 (kratēsas); the Greek verb is “give.” The last clause, parestēsen autēn zōsan, “he presented her alive,” uses the verb and the adjective of Acts 1:3, where the risen Jesus “presented himself alive after his suffering.” The holy ones are called with the widows (tous hagious kai tas chēras, the third use of the noun in the chapter). KJV “presented her alive,” ASV “he presented her alive,” NWT 1984 “presented her alive,” NIV “presented her to them alive,” Geneva “restored her alive.”

“It became known.” Gnōston de egeneto kath’ holēs tēs Ioppēs: “it became known throughout all Joppa.” The phrase gnōston egeneto is the phrase of Acts 1:19, where what became known to all who lived in Jerusalem was Judas’s field; here it is a raising. Episteusan polloi epi ton kyrion, “many believed on the Lord,” closes the Lydda and Joppa pair as v35 opened it. KJV, Douay and Geneva “many believed in the Lord,” ASV “many believed on the Lord,” NWT 1984 “many became believers on the Lord,” NIV “many people believed in the Lord.”

Verse 43 · ἡμέρας ἱκανὰς … Σίμωνι βυρσεῖ hēmeras hikanas … Simōni byrsei — Simon the tanner of Joppa in Acts 9:43: byrseus, “considerable days,” and the house by the sea

A tanner named Simon. Byrseus, “tanner,” stands in three verses, all of them about this one man: here, and 10:6 and 10:32, where his house is para thalassan, “by the sea.” The Greek says nothing about what the trade meant to the people who heard of it. A tanner works the hides of dead animals, and touching a carcass makes a person unclean in Leviticus 11:39–40; later Jewish sources are reported to class the trade among the unpleasant ones, and that is reported here and not checked. What the text gives is the fact and the address: Peter stays para tini Simōni byrsei, with a certain Simon, a tanner. Luke will call Peter himself “Simon, who is called Peter” (10:5, 10:18, 10:32), so that two Simons share the roof. KJV “he tarried many days in Joppa with one Simon a tanner,” ASV “he abode many days in Joppa with one Simon a tanner,” Douay “he abode many days in Joppe, with one Simon a tanner,” NWT 1984 “For quite a few days he remained in Joppa with a certain Simon, a tanner,” NIV “Peter stayed in Joppa for some time with a tanner named Simon,” TLB “Peter stayed a long time in Joppa, living with Simon, the tanner.”

“Considerable days.” Hēmeras hikanas is the phrase of v23, hēmerai hikanai, which opened the plot against Saul at Damascus; the chapter is bracketed by it, the first half closing on Saul’s going and the second on Peter’s staying. Where Peter stays decides what happens next: the next chapter opens on a man at Caesarea who sends to this address.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “the Way” for hodos at v2; “bound” for deō at vv2, 14 and 21; “persecute” for diōkō at vv4 and 5; “rise” for anistēmi at vv6, 11, 18, 34, 39, 40 and 41; “recover sight” for anablepō at vv12, 17 and 18; “holy ones” for hagioi at vv13, 32 and 41; “vessel” for skeuos at v15; “proclaiming” for kēryssō at v20, as at 8:5; “laid waste” for portheō at v21, kept apart from “ravaging” for lymainomai at 8:3; “amazed” for existēmi at v21, as at 8:9–13; “bewildering” for synchynnō at v22, as at 2:6; “kill” for anaireō at vv23, 24 and 29, as at 7:28; “basket” for spyris at v25, as at Matthew 15:37; “join himself” for kollaomai at v26, as at 5:13 and 8:29; “boldly” for parrhēsiazomai at vv27 and 28, as “boldness” for parrhēsia at 4:13, 29 and 31; “congregation” for ekklēsia at v31, as at 8:1 and 3; “comfort” for paraklēsis at v31; “mat” for krabattos at v33, as at 5:15; “alms” for eleēmosynē at v36, as at 3:2; “upper room” for hyperōon at vv37 and 39, as at 1:13; “garments” for himatia at v39, as at 7:58; “presented” for paristēmi at v41, as at 1:3; “considerable days” for hēmerai hikanai at vv23 and 43; and “Jehovah” for kyrios at v31, where the phrase is the Hebrew Bible’s “fear of Jehovah,” against “Lord” at vv1, 5, 10, 11, 13, 15, 17, 27, 28, 35 and 42, where Jesus is the referent or the referent is open.

Echoes paid. Saul, approving and ravaging — Acts 7:58 and 8:3, at v1. “Look upon their threats” — Acts 4:29, at v1. “Men and women” — Acts 8:3, at v2. The doubled name — Genesis 22:11, 46:2 and Exodus 3:4, at vv4 and 10. “You did it to me” — Matthew 25:40, at v4. The second Ananias — Acts 5:1, at v10. “Make his paths straight” — Matthew 3:3, at v11. “Those who call on your name” — Acts 2:21 and 7:59, at vv14 and 21. “Must suffer” — Luke 24:26, at v16. The same verb of sight — Luke 18:41–43, at v18. Bewildered crowds — Acts 2:6, at v22. The seven baskets — Matthew 15:37, at v25. Not daring to join — Acts 5:13, at v26. The son of encouragement — Acts 4:36, at vv27 and 31. Boldness — Acts 4:29–31, at vv27–28. Going in and out — Acts 1:21 and Psalm 121:8, at v28. The Hellenists, and the disputing — Acts 6:1 and 6:9, at v29. “Judea and Samaria” — Acts 1:8, at v31. “Being multiplied” — Acts 6:7, at v31. Peter’s shadow, and the mats — Acts 5:15, at v33. The beggar’s alms — Acts 3:2, at v36. The upper room — Acts 1:13, at vv37 and 39. Garments — Acts 7:58, at v39. Kneeling — Acts 7:60, at v40. The Gospels’ raisings — Mark 5:40–42, Luke 7:15 and 8:54, at vv40–41. “Presented alive” — Acts 1:3, at v41. “It became known” — Acts 1:19, at v42.

Echoes planted. Saul’s own tellings of the road, the light and Ananias at 22:3–16 and 26:9–20, with the details that differ. “Saul, who is also Paul,” at 13:9. “This Way” at 19:9, 19:23, 22:4, 24:14 and 24:22. Barnabas going to Tarsus for Saul at 11:25. The Hellenists at 11:20. The vessel at 10:11, 10:16 and 11:5. Alms at 10:2, 10:4 and 10:31, in the hand of a Gentile centurion. Peter at Joppa, on the roof, in 10:9–16. Caesarea at 10:1. The order of hands, Spirit and water at 10:44–48. “Simon, who is called Peter,” at 10:5, 10:18 and 10:32. The ethnarch of King Aretas, in Paul’s own words, at 2 Corinthians 11:32–33.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. What force the high priest’s letters had in Damascus (v2). What the men with Saul heard, here against 22:9, and whether they stood or fell (v7). The longer text of vv5–6, which the KJV, Douay and Geneva print and the printed Greek does not. Where Arabia and Paul’s three years fall against “considerable days” (v23). Who was watching the gates, the Jews of v24 or the ethnarch of 2 Corinthians 11:32. Which apostles Saul met at Jerusalem (v27), against Galatians 1:18–19. Whether Saul had been among those who disputed with Stephen (v29). What “spread for yourself” means (v34). What the Joppa disciples hoped when they sent for Peter (v38). The manuscript questions: “nothing” or “no one” at v8, “Jesus” or “Christ” at v20, “his disciples” or “the disciples” at v25, “the Lord” or “the Lord Jesus” at v28, and “congregation” or “congregations” at v31, each followed by the printed text and reported.
