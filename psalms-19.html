“The heavens recount the glory of God”: a psalm of David in two halves. The first lets a sky pour out speech that has no words and sends its line to the ends of the world, and sets a tent in it for the sun, which comes out like a bridegroom and runs like a warrior; the second turns to the law of Jehovah, six names for it in three verses, more to be desired than gold and sweeter than honey, and ends with a servant asking to be cleared of hidden errors and kept from the arrogant, and for the words of his mouth and the meditation of his heart to be accepted.
The heading names no event and the psalm names none; it is not dated beyond “a psalm of David”, and it is filed here under the monarchy as an undated psalm, as Psalm 100 is. Romans 10:18 quotes verse 5 of it.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verse 1 · Psalm 19:1 “For the director. A psalm of David” — and why the verse numbers on this page are one more than in most English Bibles

⭐ The numbers. In the Hebrew the heading, “For the director. A psalm of David”, is verse 1, so “The heavens recount” is verse 2 and the last line is verse 15. The KJV, the ASV, the NIV and the NWT 1984 leave the heading unnumbered and begin at the heavens, so every verse here is one more than the number printed in those Bibles, and a reader who looks up “Psalm 19:7” in an English Bible is looking for verse 8 on this page. The Latin and Greek Psalter number the whole psalm 18 and count the heading as verse 1, as the Hebrew does; the Douay follows them.

⭐ The heading. It is two standing words and a name: lamnatzeach, for the director (dictionary), mizmor, a psalm (dictionary), and le-David, which can mean of, by, for or belonging to David. They are the same words that open Psalm 22 (now on these pages).

Verse 2 · Psalm 19:2 “The heavens declare the glory of God” (Psalm 19:1 in most English Bibles) — a sky that recounts, and a vault that tells

⭐ “Recount”. Mesapperim is the verb sapar, to count and so to tell a thing through in order (dictionary), and the sky is doing both: the heavens keep the tally and tell it. The Hebrew sets two actions in two lines, the heavens recounting and the vault telling, and two nouns, the glory (kavod) and the work of his hands. The KJV and the ASV have “declare”, the NWT 1984 “are declaring”, the TLB “are telling”, and the Douay “shew forth”. The word for the sky in the second line is raqia, the one God makes on the second day (Genesis 1:6, now on these pages; dictionary), and the translation keeps “vault”. The KJV and the ASV read “the firmament”, the NIV “the skies”, and the NWT 1984 “the expanse”. The TLB has “a marvelous display of his craftsmanship” for “the work of his hands”.

⚠ Two halves, two names. God is El in verse 2 and never again in the psalm, and from verse 8 on he is Jehovah (note on verses 8–10). Readers differ on whether the two halves were composed together or joined; this page does not vote. What the Hebrew shows is a poem that goes from the sky to the written word and then to one man speaking, and it never mentions the join.

Verses 3–4 · Psalm 19:3–4 day pours out speech, and there is no speech — “their voice is not heard”

⭐ “Pours out speech”. Yabbia is the verb of a spring bubbling up, and the Bible uses it of mouths: the mouth of fools “pours out folly” (Proverbs 15:2), the mouth of the wicked “pours out evil things” (Proverbs 15:28), and a whole people “pour out the memory of your great goodness” (Psalm 145:7). Here it is the days that do it, and the speech is omer, a spoken word. The NWT 1984 keeps the spring: “causes speech to bubble forth”. The KJV has “uttereth speech”, the NIV “pour forth speech”, and the Douay “uttereth speech”. The second line has the same shape with a different verb, yechavveh, “declares”, and the object is knowledge (da’at): the days give speech and the nights give knowledge, one handing it to the next like a relay.

⚠ “There is no speech and there are no words; their voice is not heard”. The line follows two verses of talking, and it says the sky is silent. Three of the words are plain: omer “speech”, devarim “words”, qol “voice”. The fourth, bli, is a negative that means “without”, and it can be read two ways: “their voice is not heard” as a plain denial, or “without their voice being heard”, a sound that does not need to be heard to be there. The ASV reads “Their voice is not heard”, the NIV “no sound is heard from them”, and the NWT 1984 “No voice on their part is being heard”. The KJV turns it into a relative clause that says the opposite, “where their voice is not heard”, and the Douay does the same, “where their voices are not heard”, so that the speech of the heavens is understood in every place, even where there is no listener. The Hebrew has no word for “where”, and the translation keeps the plain denial and the paradox with it: the heavens recount, and nothing is said.

Verses 5–7 · Psalm 19:5–7 “their line has gone out” — a tent for the sun, the bridegroom and the warrior, a circuit, and nothing hidden from his heat

⭐ “Their line”. Qavvam is “their line”, the measuring cord of a surveyor, laid out “into all the earth”. The ASV has “Their line”, the NWT 1984 “their measuring line”, and the NIV “their voice”; the Douay has “Their sound”, while the RV 1909 reads «su hilo» and the RV60 «su voz». Paul quotes the verse at Romans 10:18 with “sound”, ho phthongos autōn: “Into all the earth their sound went out, and to the ends of the inhabited world their words” (Romans 10:18, already on these pages), and he puts it after the question “they did not fail to hear, did they?” He takes the silent witness of the heavens and says it describes the word that went out. The Greek word he has is a sound, and the Hebrew word is a line, which is one reason the shelf is split. “The world” is tevel, the inhabited land (dictionary), a different word from the tevel of Leviticus 18:23, “a perversion”, though the two are spelled with the same consonants.

⭐ A tent for the sun. The sun is not named until this clause, and it is named as a lodger: God “has set a tent” for it, ohel (dictionary), “in them”, in the heavens. The KJV and the ASV read “In them hath he set a tabernacle for the sun”, the NWT 1984 “In them he has set a tent for the sun”, the NIV “In the heavens God has pitched a tent for the sun”. The Douay, following the Latin, makes it “He hath set his tabernacle in the sun”, which puts God’s tent inside the sun instead of the sun inside God’s tent. At Genesis 1:16 the sun is “the greater light”, “to govern the day” (Genesis 1:16, now on these pages) and has no name at all; here it has a lodging and a path. Moses warns that to look up at “the sun and the moon and the stars, all the host of heaven” and bow to them is the way of the peoples to whom Jehovah “allotted” them (Deuteronomy 4:19, now on these pages). In this psalm the sun does not rule anything; it runs.

⭐ The bridegroom and the warrior. The sun comes out “like a bridegroom from his canopy”. Chuppah (dictionary) is the covering or chamber a bridegroom comes out of. It is the bride’s as well: “let the bridegroom come out from his room, and the bride from her canopy” (Joel 2:16), and at Isaiah 4:5 it is the cover of glory over Zion. The KJV and the ASV have “his chamber”, the NIV “his chamber” and the NWT 1984 “his nuptial chamber”. Then the image changes without a pause: he “rejoices like a warrior to run his course”. Gibbor is a warrior, a mighty man (dictionary). The KJV has “a strong man”, the NIV “a champion”, the NWT 1984 “a mighty man”, and the Douay “a giant”, from the Latin, and the RV 1909 and the RV60 both have «gigante». The joy belongs to both: the groom is glad to come out, and the runner is glad to run.

⭐ The circuit, and nothing hidden. “His going out” is the sunrise, motsa’o, from one end of the heavens, and “his circuit” is tequfah (dictionary). The word stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and three times as the turn of a period of time, the feast of ingathering “at the turn of the year” (Exodus 34:22, now on these pages), Hannah conceiving “at the turn of the days” (1 Samuel 1:20, now on these pages, where this translation has “in due time” and the plural is spelled without its vav) and the year in which the army of Aram came up (2 Chronicles 24:23). The sun’s circuit and the year’s turn are one word. The KJV has “his circuit unto the ends of it”, and the NWT 1984 “its [finished] circuit is to their [other] extremities”. “Nothing is hidden from his heat”: nistar, “hidden”, is the root that returns in verse 13 (note on verses 13–14), and chammah, “heat”, is also an old name for the sun itself (dictionary). The KJV has “there is nothing hid from the heat thereof”, the NIV “nothing is deprived of its warmth”, and the Douay “there is no one that can hide himself from his heat”.

⚠ The sun that stood. At Gibeon the sun “stood in the middle of the heavens, and did not hurry to set, for about a whole day” (Joshua 10:13, already on these pages). That is the same sun, with the same word for its day: tamim, “whole” (dictionary), the word that opens verse 8 of this psalm for the law.

Verses 8–10 · Psalm 19:8–10 “The law of Jehovah is whole” — six names for the law, six words for what it is, and the first of seven Jehovahs

⭐ From the sky to the page. There is no joining word. The poem that has just followed the sun to the ends of the earth now says, with a new name for God, “The law of Jehovah is whole”. The name El of verse 2 is gone, and Jehovah is written seven times between here and the end: twice in each of verses 8, 9 and 10, and once in verse 15, and in no other verse of the psalm. Each of the three verses is a pair of lines of the same shape, a name of the law with Jehovah, a quality, and what it does.

⭐ Six names. The law (torah, dictionary), the testimony (edut, dictionary), the precepts (piqqudim, dictionary), the commandment (mitzvah, dictionary), the fear (yirah, dictionary) and the ordinances (mishpatim, dictionary). They are six different words, and this translation keeps them apart. Psalm 119 opens with four of them in this order, “the law of Jehovah”, “his testimonies”, “your precepts”, “your righteous ordinances” (Psalm 119:1, 2, 4, 7, now on these pages), and the commandments follow at 86 and 127. The KJV has “the law”, “the testimony”, “the statutes”, “the commandment”, “the fear”, “the judgments”; the NIV has “the law”, “the statutes”, “the precepts”, “the commands”, “the fear”, “the decrees”; the NWT 1984 has “the law”, “the reminder”, “the orders”, “the commandment”, “the fear”, “the judicial decisions”; and the Douay has “the justices” for the precepts and “the judgments” for the ordinances.

⚠ “Whole”, and “bringing back the life”. Temimah is the word for the unflawed animal and the blameless man (tamim), and it is not “perfect” in the English sense of having nothing to add. The sun’s day at Gibeon is “a whole day” (Joshua 10:13, already on these pages), and the Rock’s “work is perfect” (Deuteronomy 32:4, already on these pages, where this translation prints “perfect”) is the same word. The KJV and the ASV read “perfect”, the NIV “perfect”, the NWT 1984 “perfect”, and the Douay “unspotted”. Meshivat nefesh is “bringing back the nefesh”, the same verb and noun as “he brings my life back” (Psalm 23:3, now on these pages; dictionary). The KJV has “converting the soul”, the ASV “restoring the soul”, the NIV “refreshing the soul”, the NWT 1984 “bringing back the soul”, and the RV60 reads «que convierte el alma». “The simple one” is peti, the open mind that has not been shaped (dictionary); the law makes it wise, and the Douay says “giving wisdom to little ones”.

⭐ “Upright”, “clear”, “clean”. The adjectives are yesharim (dictionary), barah (dictionary) and tehorah (dictionary), and each has a result: the precepts make the heart glad, the commandment lights up the eyes, and the fear “stands for ever”. The KJV has “right”, “pure” and “clean”; the NIV has “right”, “radiant” and “pure”; the NWT 1984 has “upright”, “clean” and “pure”; the Douay has “right”, “lightsome” and “holy”. Barah is also what the Song of Songs says of the sun: the bride is “clear as the sun” (Song of Solomon 6:10), so the commandment that lights up the eyes is named with the sun’s own adjective, and the sun that was just crossing the sky is still in the sentence. The last pair closes on “righteous all together”, tsadequ yachdav: the KJV has “righteous altogether”, the NIV “all of them are righteous”, and the NWT 1984 “they have proved altogether righteous”.

Verses 11–12 · Psalm 19:11–12 more desirable than gold, sweeter than honey — and “your servant too is warned by them”, the reward on the heel of keeping

⭐ “Gold, and fine gold”. “The desirable ones” are not named; the participle points back to all six at once. Zahav is gold, and paz is fine gold (dictionary). The pair “from gold and from fine gold” with that second word stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, Psalm 119:127 (“I love your commandments more than gold, more than fine gold”) and Proverbs 8:19 (wisdom’s fruit is “better than gold, and than fine gold”). The honey that follows is devash (dictionary), and the second honey word, nofet tsufim, is the drip from the combs, a phrase that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one (dictionary). Psalm 119 takes the honey too: “How sweet your saying is to my palate, sweeter than honey to my mouth” (Psalm 119:103). The KJV has “honey and the honeycomb”, the ASV “the droppings of the honeycomb”, the NIV “honey from the honeycomb”, and the NWT 1984 “the flowing honey of the combs”. The Douay has “gold and many precious stones”, the Latin’s addition to the Hebrew’s two golds.

⭐ “Your servant too”. The first person comes in at verse 12 without warning: gam avdekha, “your servant too”, the same “too” that opens verse 14. The servant is nizhar, “warned”, by the laws that were just praised, and he does not say the warning has worked. The KJV has “is thy servant warned”, the NIV “is warned”, the NWT 1984 “has been warned by them”, and the TLB “they warn us away from harm”. The reward is eqev, a word that means a heel and, as a preposition, “on the heel of” (dictionary): the reward is what follows on the heel of the keeping. The same word opens Deuteronomy 7:12, “on the heel of your listening to these ordinances and keeping them and doing them” (now on these pages), with this psalm’s own two words, the ordinances and the keeping, and it stands in Proverbs 22:4 (now on these pages) after the same fear that is “clean” and “standing for ever” in verse 10: “on the heels of humility, the fear of Jehovah, come riches and honor and life”. The KJV has “great reward”, the NIV “great reward”, and the NWT 1984 “a large reward”.

Verses 13–14 · Psalm 19:13–14 “Errors — who can discern them?” — hidden faults, the arrogant, “then I shall be whole”, and a clearing that Exodus 34 refuses

⭐ “Errors”, and “the hidden ones”. Shegi’ot is a plural that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one (dictionary), built on the root of shegagah, a sin “through error”: the sin of a person who did not know, with its own sacrifice (Leviticus 4:2, Numbers 15:27, 29, all now on these pages). The question “who can discern” is rhetorical, so the second line asks for what cannot be asked for by name: mi-nistarot, “from the hidden ones”. The same root, nistar, was in verse 7, where nothing is hidden from the sun’s heat, and it is the word of Deuteronomy 29:28: “The hidden things belong to Jehovah our God” (now on these pages; dictionary). The psalm does not add a noun, and the translation does not: “the hidden ones” may be hidden sins, as the shelf reads them, or hidden things. The KJV has “cleanse thou me from secret faults”, the ASV “Clear thou me from hidden faults”, the NIV “Forgive my hidden faults”, the NWT 1984 “From concealed sins pronounce me innocent”, and the TLB “Cleanse me from these hidden faults”. The verb is naqqeni, “clear me”, the legal word (naqah), and it is the verb that Jehovah uses of himself at Sinai when he says that he forgives “iniquity and transgression and sin” and “will by no means clear” (Exodus 34:7, now on these pages). The one who asks to be cleared is asking for the thing the name says is not given to the guilty, and the last words of verse 14 take up its list: pesha rav, “great transgression” (dictionary), is the middle word of Exodus 34:7’s three, iniquity, transgression and sin.

⚠ “The arrogant”. Zedim is a plural that the shelf takes as “sins”: the KJV has “presumptuous sins”, the Geneva “presumptuous sins”, the NIV “willful sins”, the NWT 1984 “presumptuous acts”, and the RV60 has «soberbias», “prides”. The Hebrew has no noun for “sins” in the word. It stands in eight verses of the Psalms, and in the seven others it is people: “the arrogant have risen against me” (Psalm 86:14), and six verses of Psalm 119, where they mock the speaker, smear him with falsehood, dig pits for him and oppress him, and where this translation renders the word “the arrogant” (119:21, 51, 69, 78, 85, 122). So the translation reads people: “hold your servant back from the arrogant; let them not rule over me”. The sense of arrogant acts is the tradition’s and can stand beside it (zadon); it is the Torah’s own second category, the sin done “with a high hand”, be-yad ramah, which has no sacrifice and cuts the person off (Numbers 15:30), where the first category, the errors of verse 13, is the one the sacrifice covers. The Douay follows the Latin with “from those of others”, which is a different word again: zarim, “strangers”, differs from zedim by one letter.

⭐ “Then I shall be whole”. Etam is the first-person verb of tamam, the root of tamim and temimah (dictionary): the law of verse 8 is temimah, and the servant who is kept from the arrogant will be etam, in the law’s own condition. The KJV has “then shall I be upright”, the ASV “then shall I be upright”, the NIV “Then I will be blameless”, the NWT 1984 “In that case I shall be complete”, the Geneva “so shall I be upright”, and the Douay “then shall I be without spot”. The last clause repeats the verb of verse 13 in its passive form, ve-nikkeiti, “and I shall be cleared”.

Verse 15 · Psalm 19:15 “Let the sayings of my mouth and the meditation of my heart be for acceptance” — the meditation (hegyon), the rock, and the redeemer

⭐ “For acceptance”. Yihyu le-ratson, “let them be for acceptance”, is the language of the altar: the burnt offering of Leviticus 1:3 is brought li-rtsono lifnei Jehovah, for acceptance before Jehovah (now on these pages; dictionary), and what the man brings here are his words and his thoughts. The KJV and the ASV have “acceptable in thy sight”, the NIV “pleasing in your sight”, the NWT 1984 “pleasurable before you”, and the TLB “pleasing even to you”. The Douay has the sentence run differently, “the words of my mouth shall be such as may please: and the meditation of my heart always in thy sight”. The sayings are imrei-fi (dictionary), the spoken, and the hegyon is the unspoken: the noun stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, here, Psalm 9:17, where it stands before “Selah”, and Psalm 92:4, “with the lyre”, and it is built on hagah, to murmur (dictionary), the verb of “you shall murmur over it day and night” (Joshua 1:8) and of the man who “meditates” on the law “day and night” (Psalm 1:2, both now on these pages). So the last prayer asks that the murmuring of the heart, the very practice the law asks for, be accepted along with the speech.

⭐ “My rock and my redeemer”. The psalm ends on two titles, and it names Jehovah as the first of them: tsuri, “my rock” (dictionary), the title of the Song of Moses, “The Rock — his work is perfect” (Deuteronomy 32:4, already on these pages), whose “perfect” is the same word that opened verse 8 for the law; and go’ali, “my redeemer”, the kinsman who buys back (dictionary; Ruth 4:14, now on these pages). The ASV has “my rock, and my redeemer”, the NIV “my Rock and my Redeemer”, the TLB “my Rock and my Redeemer”, and the NWT 1984 “my Rock and my Redeemer”. The KJV and the Geneva have “my strength” where the Hebrew has “my rock”, and the Douay has “my helper”. The psalm began with the heavens counting out a glory that has no voice, and it ends with a single voice that asks to be heard.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “vault” for raqia, as at Genesis 1:6 and Psalm 150:1; “law” for torah, “testimony” for edut, “precepts” for piqqudim, “commandment” for mitzvah, “fear” for yirah and “ordinances” for mishpatim, the six names kept apart, as in Psalm 119; “whole” for tamim and its family, as at Joshua 10:13 (Deuteronomy 32:4 prints the same word “perfect”); “bringing back the life” for meshivat nefesh, as at Psalm 23:3; “the simple one” for peti; “gold” and “fine gold” for zahav and paz, as at Psalm 119:127; “clear me” for naqqeni, the verb of Exodus 34:7; “the arrogant” for zedim, as in Psalm 119; “acceptance” for ratson; “rock” for tsur and “redeemer” for go’el. The Hebrew has no written and read pairs in this psalm.

Echoes paid. The vault of Genesis 1:6 and the greater light of Genesis 1:16; Moses’s warning about the host of heaven (Deuteronomy 4:19); the hidden things that belong to Jehovah (Deuteronomy 29:28); the Rock whose work is perfect (Deuteronomy 32:4); the sun that stood a whole day at Gibeon (Joshua 10:13); the verb of the one who murmurs and meditates on the law day and night (Joshua 1:8, Psalm 1:2); the pair gold and fine gold, and the honey, of Psalm 119; the life brought back of Psalm 23:3; the errors that need a sacrifice and the high hand that has none (Leviticus 4:2, Numbers 15:27–30); Jehovah’s “will by no means clear the guilty” (Exodus 34:7); the heel that is reward and the fear of Jehovah (Proverbs 22:4); the kinsman who redeems (Ruth 4); the bride who is clear as the sun in the Song of Songs (6:10); and Paul’s “their sound has gone out” (Romans 10:18).

Echoes planted. The arrogant of Psalm 86:14; the meditation before “Selah” and with the lyre (Psalm 9:17, 92:4); the bridegroom and bride coming out of their rooms (Joel 2:16) and the canopy of glory over Zion (Isaiah 4:5); the fine gold of Proverbs 8:19; the year’s turn at 2 Chronicles 24:23. None of these is translated here yet. The heavens that Psalm 8 sees as “the work of your fingers” are now on these pages.
