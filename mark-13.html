Leaving the temple, a disciple admires its stones; Jesus predicts their total destruction. On the Mount of Olives, Peter, James, John and Andrew ask privately when this will be, and Jesus answers with wars, earthquakes and famines ('the beginning of birth pangs'), persecution before councils and kings, 'the abomination that desolates' standing where it ought not, false christs and false prophets, and cosmic signs before the Son of Man comes in the clouds. A parable of the fig tree teaches watchfulness rather than a date; 'concerning that day or hour no one knows, not even the Son, but only the Father.' The discourse closes with a parable of a doorkeeper commanded to keep watch.
c. AD 30, the Tuesday evening of Holy Week, delivered privately on the Mount of Olives overlooking the temple, immediately after the confrontations of chapter 12 — corresponding to Matthew 24's own Olivet Discourse, delivered on the same evening in that Gospel's own harmony.

Verses 1–4 · Πέτρος καὶ Ἰάκωβος καὶ Ἰωάννης καὶ Ἀνδρέας Petros kai Iakōbos kai Iōannēs kai Andreas — an inner circle within the inner circle

⭐ Mark names four men where Matthew's parallel names none. “The disciples came to him privately” is Matthew 24:3's whole roster; Mark lists Peter, James and John — the three who alone saw the transfiguration (9:2, already on these pages) and were taken alone into Jairus's room (5:37) — and adds a fourth, Andrew, who belongs to none of those earlier scenes. He is Peter's own brother and one of the first two men called (1:16), which may be reason enough; the text does not explain the inclusion, only reports it. ⚠ Whatever the fixed three-man circle otherwise means in this Gospel, it is not a closed set here.

A private discourse about a public building. The temple's own destruction was predicted in the temple's own hearing one chapter ago in a parable no one there could miss (12:1–9); here the same subject moves outside the walls, up the slope facing them, and into a small, named audience. Kat' idian, “privately,” is doing real work: what follows is not proclaimed to a crowd the way the earlier parables were, and the discourse is unique in this Gospel for being addressed to no one else.

“Stones” and “buildings,” twice each. Potapoi lithoi kai potapai oikodomai repeats the same interrogative adjective for both nouns rather than using it once — a doubling this Gospel favors elsewhere (“group by group” and “rows like garden plots” at 6:39–40, already on these pages). Herod's Temple Mount platform, then still under construction, used stones the ancient historian Josephus records at twenty-five cubits long in one account and forty-five in another; the disciple's astonishment is not naive.

Verses 5–8 · Ἐγώ εἰμι egō eimi — the same two bare words, claimed by impostors

⚠ “I am he” is the identical Greek this Gospel already used, without a predicate, for something entirely different. Egō eimi is what Jesus says walking on the water at 6:50 (already on these pages), and John's Gospel builds a whole set of absolute “I am” sayings on the same bare construction. Here the same two words are put in the mouth of impostors — the phrase itself proves nothing about who is speaking; only the one saying it, and what follows from it, does. The false claimants borrow the sentence, not the substance.

Birth pangs, not death throes. Ōdinōn is a labor pain specifically, not pain in general — see the ōdin dictionary entry, first written at Matthew 24:8, already on these pages. The image says the opposite of what a reader collecting disasters wants to hear: these are the first contractions, evidence the process has started, not that it is over. Mark's own sentence performs the point structurally — two short, parallel clauses (“there will be earthquakes… there will be famines…”) each opening with its own esontai, “there will be,” where Matthew's parallel (24:7) compresses the same list into one verb shared by both nouns. Mark counts the pains one at a time; Matthew lists them together.

Shelf comparison on v7's throeisthe: NWT reads “terrified” (its Spanish twin TNM likewise, «aterroricen»), ASV and KJV read “troubled,” and this translation follows Matthew 24:6's own already-published “alarmed” for the identical Greek word, keeping one English rendering for one Greek verb across both Gospels. ⚠ At v8, KJV and Geneva alone among the English shelf add “and troubles” after “famines” — a Byzantine-tradition addition the earlier and better manuscripts do not carry, and which ASV, NIV and NWT all correctly omit.

Verses 9–13 · οὐ γάρ ἐστε ὑμεῖς οἱ λαλοῦντες ou gar este hymeis hoi lalountes — a promise Matthew keeps for a different discourse entirely

⭐ The Spirit-in-the-hour promise (v11) has no equivalent anywhere in Matthew's Olivet Discourse — Matthew keeps it for a different scene entirely. Matthew 10:19–20 (already on these pages) makes the identical promise, in close to identical words, but in the mission charge of chapter 10, not the Mount of Olives discourse of chapter 24. Mark folds material Matthew keeps for a different setting into this one; a single saying of Jesus, remembered and placed by two different evangelists into two different scenes. ⚠ The two versions even name the speaker differently: Matthew's is “the Spirit OF YOUR FATHER speaking in you”, where Mark's is simply “the Holy Spirit” — the same promise, given a family title in one Gospel and its own proper name in the other.

⚠ Where Mark is shorter than Matthew, and where it is more specific. Matthew 24:10–12 (already on these pages) adds a full three verses absent here entirely: believers stumbling and betraying and hating one another, false prophets multiplying, love growing cold as lawlessness spreads. Mark has none of it — its persecution section moves directly from the councils and synagogues (v9) to the Spirit's help (v11) to the family's own betrayal (v12). And where Matthew's “betray one another” (allēlous paradōsousin) stays general, Mark names the betrayers: brother against brother, father against child, children against parents — the family itself named as the site of the danger, not a generalized breakdown of “many.”

The gospel to all nations, moved earlier. Mark places “the good news must first be proclaimed to all the nations” (v10) in the middle of the persecution material, immediately after the courts and before the family betrayals. Matthew's version of the identical clause (24:14, already on these pages, with “of the kingdom” added to “good news”) closes out the WHOLE persecution section instead, placed as its final word just before the abomination that desolates. Same sentence, two different structural jobs.

⚠ A scribal doubling-down at v11: the Byzantine tradition behind KJV adds “neither do ye premeditate” (mēde meletate) right after “do not worry beforehand what you are to say” — absent from the earlier manuscripts and from ASV, NIV and NWT alike. It says nothing the verse does not already say; a later hand underlining the point rather than adding a new one.

Verses 14–20 · ἑστηκότα hestēkota — a masculine word standing where a neuter one was expected

⭐⭐ Two chapters of this project's own Daniel promised this phrase would return here, and it is now a live link on both ends. The Daniel 11 note on v29–35 named the exact moment: “Jesus will lift the phrase again toward a horizon of his own — in the same English, on purpose.” Bdelygma tēs erēmōseōs, “the abomination that desolates,” is Daniel's own compound (Daniel 9:27, not yet on these pages; 11:31 and 12:11, already on these pages), naming the defiling object as the CAUSE of the desolation rather than merely describing it — and it had already been fulfilled once, in 167 BC, when Antiochus IV set up an altar to Zeus on the temple's own altar. Mark's Jesus reaches for a phrase his audience already knew had happened before, and says it will happen again.

⚠ A grammatical anomaly the Greek itself flags, and Matthew quietly repairs it. Bdelygma, “abomination,” is a NEUTER noun, and a participle agreeing with it should be neuter too — hestos. Mark instead writes hestēkota, the MASCULINE form: grammatically, a “he” standing where a “he ought not,” not an “it.” Matthew's version of the same sentence corrects the solecism outright, printing the properly neuter hestos. Whether Mark's grammar is a deliberate signal of a PERSON behind the desolating object, or simply looser Greek from a bilingual writer, the library does not decide — but the fact itself, that Matthew smooths what Mark leaves rough, is a real and checkable difference between the two texts, not an interpretive gloss laid on top of them.

⭐ And the Byzantine tradition tried to smooth Mark's own text the same way — by borrowing from Matthew — and still could not land on Matthew's own clean fix. The manuscripts behind KJV's “spoken of by Daniel the prophet” insert almost exactly Matthew's six words (to rhēthen HYPO Daniēl tou prophētou, against Matthew's own dia, “spoken BY” rather than “THROUGH” Daniel — close, not a copy) into Mark at this exact point — absent from Sinaiticus, Vaticanus and the rest of the earliest witnesses, and from ASV, NIV and NWT alike, none of which have it. ⚠ But the same manuscripts that add Daniel's name also swap hestēkota for a DIFFERENT irregular form, hestōs — nominative, not accusative, so still not the case a participle modifying bdelygma as its object requires, let alone the neuter gender. Even scribes copying in Matthew's own clarifying phrase did not copy Matthew's own clean hestos; whatever roughness sits in Mark's Greek here resisted smoothing all the way down, in every manuscript tradition that has tried.

Also unique to Mark's telling: the flight instructions carry no mention of the Sabbath. Matthew 24:20 has the fleeing believers pray that their flight fall “neither in winter nor on a Sabbath” — a concern that assumes readers who keep it. Mark's parallel prays only against winter (v18); the Sabbath clause is Matthew's own addition, consistent with a more Jewish-facing audience than this Gospel's own Roman-facing conventions elsewhere (the glossed Aramaic, the Latin-derived coin names already noted at 12:42).

The divine name at v20. The Greek is kyrios, “Lord,” as in every manuscript, and this sentence quotes no Hebrew text — it only echoes the tribulation language of passages like Daniel 12:1. But its kyrios is plainly God, not Jesus: the one who “cut short the days” for “the chosen, whom he chose.” This translation prints “Jehovah” wherever the New Testament’s “the Lord” is the God of Israel rather than Jesus — in quotations, in the Old Testament’s own formulas, and in sentences like this one — and keeps “Lord” where the referent is Jesus or genuinely open. The NWT (and its Spanish twin, the TNM) reads the same way; KJV, ASV and NIV print “the Lord,” which is what the Greek literally says.

Verses 21–23 · ψευδόχριστοι pseudochristoi — a word that exists, in the whole of the Greek Bible, only here and in one other verse

⭐ Confirmed directly against the archived Greek New Testament: pseudochristos, “false christ,” occurs in exactly two verses in the entire New Testament, and they are this one and its own Matthean parallel — nowhere else, in any other book. The word appears to be coined for this saying rather than borrowed from earlier use; its partner pseudoprophētēs, “false prophet,” is the older and commoner term, appearing across the Gospels, Acts and Revelation, which makes pseudochristos look like a deliberate coinage built to match it — a false ANOINTED ONE invented on the model of a false MOUTHPIECE. See the new pseudochristos dictionary entry.

Order matters here: Mark's Jesus warns against false CHRISTS at v6 by implication (“many will come in my name, saying, I am he”) and only names the pairing outright here, sixteen verses later, once the abomination-that-desolates crisis has already been described. The warning brackets the whole central section of the discourse — open at v6, closed at v22 — rather than sitting in one place.

Verses 24–27 · τὸν υἱὸν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἐρχόμενον ἐν νεφέλαις ton huion tou anthrōpou erchomenon en nephelais — a phrase this Gospel has already used once, of a very different coming

⚠ The Son of Man “coming” is not new vocabulary in Mark; what is new is the direction of travel. At 8:38 and 14:62, the Son of Man's coming is spoken of from earth looking up. Daniel's own vision behind the image — “one like a son of man, coming WITH the clouds” presented before the Ancient of Days (Daniel 7:13, not yet on these pages) — is itself a scene of ARRIVAL at a heavenly throne, not a descent to earth; which reading Mark intends here, arrival or descent, the discourse does not settle, and readers of Daniel 7 divide on the same question when this project reaches that chapter.

The cosmic list — sun darkened, moon dimmed, stars falling, the powers of heaven shaken — borrows its whole vocabulary from the Hebrew prophets' own stock imagery for a coming day of judgment (Isaiah 13:10, Joel 2:10, both not yet on these pages), applied here to a single, personal event rather than a generalized national catastrophe.

⚠ Mark's compass runs from earth to sky; Matthew's stays inside the sky. Mark's angels gather the chosen “from the end of the EARTH to the end of HEAVEN” (ap' akrou gēs heōs akrou ouranou) — an axis running from the ground up. Matthew 24:31's parallel keeps the whole sweep aloft: “from the ends of the HEAVENS to their ends” (ap' akrōn ouranōn heōs tōn akrōn autōn), a full circuit of the sky's own horizon rather than a line from the world's floor to its ceiling. Both mean everywhere; they say it by two different routes.

Verses 28–32 · ἡ γενεὰ αὕτη… οὐδὲ ὁ υἱός hē genea hautē… oude ho huios — the sentence C. S. Lewis called embarrassing, and the one three verses later that answers it

⭐⭐ Matthew 24's own note, written before this chapter existed, already named this exact verse. Discussing “this generation will not pass away” at 24:34, that note quotes C. S. Lewis calling the saying “the most embarrassing verse in the Bible” — and says so “of Mark's wording (13:30) rather than Matthew's, though the sentence is the same.” That forward mention is now a live citation rather than a promise: this is the verse Lewis meant. His own argument, reported there and worth repeating here since it is built on the pairing of these two verses specifically: the admission of ignorance at v32 stands three verses after the hard prediction at v30, and copyists who could plainly see the apparent difficulty preserved both anyway — evidence, on his reading, that the record was not smoothed to remove the tension.

The readings on offer, briefly, since Matthew's own note sets them out at length: (1) genea means Jesus's own contemporaries, and “all these things” is the temple's fall within the following forty years; (2) it means “race” or “kind,” a strained sense; (3) it points forward to whichever generation is alive when the final signs begin; (4) it is a prediction whose terms were not met as stated. The library reports all four and votes for none.

⭐⭐ “Nor the Son” is uniformly attested in Mark, and Matthew's own note on its parallel already said so before this chapter could confirm it. Oude ho huios carries no variant in any of the editions this translation compares, Byzantine text included. Matthew's own note on 24:36 (already on these pages) observed that “the parallel at Mark 13:32 has it in every witness, which also tells against the idea that it was imported” into Matthew by a later hand; that claim can now be checked directly against this verse, and it holds. In Matthew's own transmission the clause is present in the earliest and best witnesses (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, Bezae) and absent from the Byzantine tradition — exactly the asymmetry a scribe uncomfortable with the Son's professed ignorance would produce by DELETING a hard clause, never by inventing one. Mark’s own steady attestation is strong corroboration that the saying is original, not a later insertion into either Gospel.

The fig tree parable is a lesson in reading a season, not a date: the branch tells you summer is near, and the discourse claims no more precision than that. What follows immediately — the flat admission that not even the Son knows the day or the hour — is this Gospel's own check on any reader tempted to turn “near” into a calendar.

Verses 33–37 · τῷ θυρωρῷ ἐνετείλατο ἵνα γρηγορῇ tō thyrōrō eneteilato hina grēgorē — a word Mark's own chapter ends on three times

⭐ Grēgoreō, “keep watch,” is this chapter's own closing refrain — the last word of the doorkeeper's charge (v34), the command repeated to open the paragraph proper (v35), and the single word the whole discourse ends on (v37). The English name Gregory descends from the same root. A related but distinct verb, agrypneite, “stay sleepless, be alert” (v33, from a- + hypnos, sleep), opens the section — two different watch-words for the same command, the second answered three times over by the first before the chapter is done.

⭐ A third Byzantine-tradition addition in this same chapter, and the only one of the three not taken from Matthew’s parallel to this discourse. The manuscripts behind KJV’s “watch and pray” add kai proseuchesthe, “and pray,” after v33’s agrypneite — absent from the earlier witnesses and from the NIV and NWT, though the ASV, the Geneva and the Douay-Rheims print it too. Matthew’s own parallel at this exact point (24:42, already on these pages) reads only “stay awake,” with no prayer clause there to borrow. The pairing itself was everywhere a scribe might look: Jesus tells the sleeping disciples at Gethsemane grēgoreite kai proseuchesthe, “keep watch and pray,” in Mark (14:38) and in Matthew (26:41, already on these pages) alike, and Luke’s parallel to this very saying pairs v33’s own verb with prayer (21:36, not yet on these pages). Which of them the scribe had in his ear, the manuscripts do not say.

The doorkeeper (thyrōros) appears in only two other places in the New Testament, and both are worth having beside this one: the gatekeeper who recognizes the shepherd's voice in John 10 (not yet on these pages), and the servant girl doorkeeper who lets Peter in — and asks him the question he denies — at John 18 (not yet on these pages). Three doorkeepers, three different kinds of watching: one commanded to it here, one who watches rightly, one whose watching becomes the setting for a betrayal.

Roman hours for a Jewish parable. The four watches named at v35 — evening, midnight, cockcrow, early morning — are the Roman military division of the night into four three-hour shifts, not the Jewish three-watch system. It is the same Roman-facing habit already noted in this Gospel's glossed Aramaic and its currency conversions (12:41–42, already on these pages): a detail pitched at a reader who thinks in Roman time.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: the abomination that desolates at v14, matching Daniel 11:31/12:11 and Matthew 24:15’s already-published wording exactly, so the same English phrase names the same Greek and Hebrew phrase across both books; the chosen at vv20, 22, 27, matching Matthew 24's own rendering of eklektos rather than “the elect” or “chosen ones”; cut short for koloboō at v20, again matching Matthew 24:22; alarmed for throeisthe at v7, matching Matthew 24:6's identical Greek verb; and the Lord, not “Jehovah,” kept at v20 where NWT and TNM restore the divine name over a narrative sentence rather than a direct quotation — flagged in the note as doctrinal, not adopted.

Echoes paid. The Daniel 11 note's own forward mention of this chapter — “Jesus will lift the phrase again toward a horizon of his own” — is now a live link rather than a promise, as is Daniel 12's own parallel mention of “the abomination-phrase still waiting on the Mount of Olives.” Matthew 24:34's note, which quoted C. S. Lewis reading “this embarrassing verse” as Mark's OWN wording rather than Matthew's, is answered directly at v30. Matthew 24:36's note, which cited “the parallel at Mark 13:32” as evidence “nor the Son” was not imported into either Gospel, is confirmed at v32: the clause carries no variant in any of the editions compared here. And Mark 12's closing look ahead, to the building whose destruction this Gospel predicts one chapter on, is paid at 13:1–2.

Echoes planted. The Son of Man “coming in clouds” (v26) draws on Daniel 7:13's vision of one like a son of man PRESENTED before the Ancient of Days — a scene of arrival at a throne, not a descent to earth — a question this project will be able to answer more fully once it reaches Daniel's own chapter. The doorkeeper of v34 is one of only three in the New Testament; the other two, in John 10 and John 18, are both still ahead. The Byzantine “and pray” a scribe once added to v33's “Keep alert!” is watching for a real payoff one chapter ahead: Jesus himself pairs a stay-awake verb with prayer, grēgoreite kai proseuchesthe, at Gethsemane (14:38) — whether that scene reads, in hindsight, like the source the addition was reaching for. And this Gospel's own habit of physical specificity, already flagged in Mark 12's own closing note (see 12:4), is worth testing again here: whether the coming trial and crucifixion chapters keep naming individual wounds, individual betrayers, individual family members, the way this chapter names Peter, James, John and Andrew by name where Matthew's parallel names no one.
