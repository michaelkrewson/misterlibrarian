Leaving the temple for the last time, Jesus predicts its total destruction; on the Mount of Olives opposite, Peter, James, John and Andrew ask him privately when this will happen and what the sign will be. He answers with a long discourse: false christs, wars, earthquakes and famines as the beginning of birth pains; persecution and a mission to all nations; the abomination that desolates, standing where it ought not, and instructions to flee to the mountains; a tribulation unlike any other, cut short for the elect's sake; the sun darkened, the stars falling, and the Son of Man coming in clouds with great power and glory; the fig tree's lesson that summer is near; 'this generation will not pass away'; the day and hour known to the Father alone; and the parable of the doorkeeper commanded to stay awake, closing on a single word repeated to all: watch.
c. AD 30, the Tuesday before Passover, spoken privately after leaving the same temple courts chapter 12 closes inside — corresponding to Matthew 24-25's own telling of the same Olivet discourse, where the persecution material (vv9-13) instead appears earlier, inside Matthew 10's mission charge.

Verses 1–2 · οὐ μὴ ἀφεθῇ ὧδε λίθος ἐπὶ λίθον ou mē aphethē hōde lithos epi lithon — the prediction the previous chapter's own closing note promised was coming

⭐ This is the payoff a chapter already promised. Mark 12 closed inside this same temple, at the very treasury where a poor widow gave two coins, its own closing note naming exactly what was still ahead: “the building whose destruction this Gospel predicts one chapter ahead (13:1–2, not yet on these pages).” It is no longer a forward mention — the disciple's admiring remark and Jesus's answer are the text itself now, spoken on the way out of the courts where four hostile questions had just failed to trap him.

⚠ The same adjective, doubled across two different genders, is the whole of the disciple's amazement. Potapoi lithoi kai potapai oikodomai repeats potapos, “what sort of, what a” — masculine for the stones, feminine for the buildings — rather than reaching for two different words of praise. The temple Herod the Great rebuilt was genuinely enormous: some of its foundation stones, still visible today in the Western Wall, run to many tons apiece, cut and set without mortar. A disciple from Galilee's fishing villages had every reason to be amazed by the masonry alone.

Shelf comparison on the disciple's line: NIV keeps the double exclamation, “What massive stones! What magnificent buildings!”; TLB paraphrases past the stones themselves to “the decorated stonework on the walls”; KJV and the Douay-Rheims both keep the older “what manner of stones and what buildings”, and Geneva comes close but drops one small word, “what manner stones, and what manner buildings.” Jesus's own answer is flatly unqualified — not one stone will be left ON ANOTHER, a phrase later historians of the war of AD 70 connect to the actual physical dismantling of the temple's ruins, stone pulled from stone in the search for melted gold after the fire.

Verses 3–4 · Πέτρος καὶ Ἰάκωβος καὶ Ἰωάννης καὶ Ἀνδρέας Petros kai Iakōbos kai Iōannēs kai Andreas — the fixed inner three, plus the one name that never usually joins them

⭐ This is the only time in this Gospel that Jesus's private inner circle grows to four. “Peter and James and John” is Mark's own fixed formula for who gets taken aside alone — at Jairus's daughter (5:37, already on these pages) and at the Transfiguration (9:2, already on these pages), always those three names, always in that order, and it will recur once more at Gethsemane (14:33, not yet on these pages). Here alone Andrew is added — Peter's own brother, one of the first four called at the Sea of Galilee, but never otherwise part of the private three. No note in this Gospel explains why; the text simply widens the circle for this one discourse and narrows back to three afterward.

The disciples ask two things, and it is worth noticing what Mark's own Greek does NOT do to settle whether they meant one event or two. “When will these things be” and “what will be the sign when all these things are about to be accomplished” repeat the bare demonstrative tauta, “these things,” with nothing marking whether the second use still names the temple's fall just predicted or something wider. This translation leaves that ambiguity exactly where the Greek leaves it. Matthew's own parallel raises the same kind of one-event-or-two question by a more visible route — a single shared article spanning parousia and synteleia tou aiōnos in one Greek phrase (see the dictionary entry), a different pair of terms than Mark's, reaching a comparable uncertainty by grammar rather than by bare repetition.

⚠ Mark's Jesus never once says the word Matthew's parallel discourse uses four times for exactly this event. Parousia, “presence, arrival” — the state-visit word Matthew reaches for at 24:3, 27, 37 and 39, and nowhere else in any Gospel — never appears in Mark's own telling of this same discourse. Mark's vocabulary throughout stays with the ordinary verb erchomai, “to come” (vv26, 35), the ordinary word this translation likewise keeps ordinary.

Verses 5–8 · ἀρχὴ ὠδίνων ταῦτα archē ōdinōn tauta — a phrase Matthew's own Gospel keeps word for word

The discourse's opening warning is against being misled, not against fear — mē tis hymas planēsē, “let no one lead you astray,” frames everything that follows as a question of who to trust rather than a countdown to watch. The false claimants who come “in my name” say only Egō eimi, “I am he” — the ordinary way of confirming an identity in Greek, no more loaded here than answering a knock at the door; nothing in this verse's own grammar reaches for the weightier “I AM” some other New Testament passages carry.

⚠ Wars, earthquakes and famine are named and then immediately reclassified. “These are the beginning of birth pains” (v8) does the opposite of what a reader collecting disasters wants to hear: labour pain is pain that PRODUCES something, and it means a process is under way rather than that the end has arrived — the same force the identical Greek phrase carries at its other occurrence on these pages, see the dictionary entry for the full sense. The list itself — nation against nation, kingdom against kingdom, earthquakes, famine — is generic ancient-world catastrophe language, not a checklist naming any one century's headlines.

Verses 9–13 · εἰς μαρτύριον αὐτοῖς eis martyrion autois — the identical Greek Matthew's Gospel places inside a wholly different speech

⭐ This whole block is, almost word for word, a speech Matthew's Gospel already prints — inside a different discourse entirely. Councils, synagogue floggings, governors and kings, brother betraying brother to death, being hated by all for the name, and “the one who endures to the end will be saved” are the identical Greek (Matthew 10:17–22, already on these pages), there spoken as part of the charge sending the Twelve out on their own mission, here spoken privately on the Mount of Olives about the world's own final stretch. Whether Mark places shared material inside a later setting or Matthew moves the same words into an earlier one is a Synoptic-relationship question this library does not try to settle from inside either chapter; what is checkable is that the two settings are genuinely different, and the sentences genuinely are not.

⚠ “The gospel must first be proclaimed” and Matthew's own version of the identical idea are not quite the identical sentence. Mark's v10 says only to euangelion, “the good news,” must be proclaimed eis panta ta ethnē, “to all the nations,” with prōton, “first” — folded into the middle of the persecution warnings themselves. Matthew's parallel (24:14, already on these pages) stands as its own separate verse, names it “this good news OF THE KINGDOM,” sends it into hē oikoumenē, “the inhabited world” rather than plainly “the nations,” and sequences it with tote, “AND THEN the end will come,” rather than Mark's “first.” Both link universal proclamation to the end; neither uses quite the other's words to say so.

Verses 14–20 · τὸ βδέλυγμα τῆς ἐρημώσεως ἑστηκότα to bdelygma tēs erēmōseōs hestēkota — a neuter noun, and a participle that refuses to agree with it

Full treatment of the phrase itself — bdelygma's disgust-root, erēmōsis's devastation, and the genitive that makes the abomination the CAUSE of the desolation rather than merely its description — is at the dictionary entry, first built for Matthew 24:15's own citation of Daniel. Two things are new here, both Mark's own.

⚠ Mark's Jesus never names Daniel; the KJV's Jesus does. The critical Greek text this translation follows has no “spoken of by Daniel the prophet” at this verse — that clause belongs to Matthew's version of the identical saying. Yet KJV, Geneva and both RV 1909 and RV60 print it here too, matching Matthew's wording exactly; ASV, NWT, the Douay-Rheims, NIV and NVI all lack it, matching this translation's own source. The Byzantine tradition behind the KJV has harmonized Mark's shorter sentence toward its better-known parallel; the earliest witnesses have not.

⭐ And the Greek itself will not agree with its own subject. Bdelygma is grammatically NEUTER, and ordinary Greek grammar wants a neuter participle to match it — hestēkos. Mark writes hestēkota instead, MASCULINE, “standing” as if the abomination were a HE rather than an IT. This translation keeps the oddity visible — “standing where he ought not” — rather than smoothing it into the expected “it,” the same choice ASV makes (“standing where HE ought not”) against NWT's “standing where IT ought not” and NIV's neutral “standing where it does not belong.” Whether the mismatch personifies the abomination on purpose or is simply the loose grammar a translated Semitic idiom picks up in Greek, this library reports rather than resolves; the aside “let the reader understand” (also present, identically, in Matthew's version) is the one place in either Gospel the writer speaks directly rather than quoting Jesus.

⚠ “The Lord had not cut short the days” leaves an ambiguity this translation keeps open, and one shelf version does not. Kyrios (v20) carries no article and no direct Old Testament citation to anchor it — unlike the Shema quotation two chapters earlier, this is the narrator's own sentence, not a quoted verse, so the “Jehovah our God, Jehovah is one” convention established there is not simply carried forward here. NWT restores “Jehovah” at this verse anyway, a doctrinal reading flagged here rather than silently adopted; the rest of the shelf — KJV, ASV, NIV, Geneva, the Douay-Rheims, and the Spanish RV/RV60/NVI — all keep the plain “the Lord,” the reading this translation follows.

The flight instructions themselves — a rooftop dweller not going back inside, a field worker not returning for a cloak, prayer that the flight not fall in winter — are short-range and local: real hills a person could reach on foot from real Judean villages within the warning's own short notice, one reason this library reads the whole passage as tied to a specific, nearby horizon rather than a distant, global one. See the new Judea encyclopedia entry for the region itself.

Verses 21–23 · ψευδόχριστοι καὶ ψευδοπροφῆται pseudochristoi kai pseudoprophētai — two compounds coined for exactly this warning

The chapter's opening warning (v5–6) and this one are the same danger stated twice, tightened the second time: false claimants who merely SAY “I am he” have become false christs and false prophets who back the claim with sēmeia kai terata, “signs and wonders” — the identical two Greek nouns Peter uses of Jesus's own works, in the opposite order and with a third term added, “wonders and signs” (Acts 2:22, already on these pages), here turned to a real, backed deception rather than a bare assertion. “If possible” (ei dynaton) quietly concedes that the deception is genuinely persuasive, not merely a test only the careless would fail.

Verses 24–27 · μετὰ τὴν θλῖψιν ἐκείνην meta tēn thlipsin ekeinēn — after that tribulation, with the one adverb Matthew's own parallel adds and Mark's own sentence does not

⚠ Matthew's parallel tightens the timing with one word Mark's own sentence never supplies. Mark writes plainly “in those days, after that tribulation” (v24); Matthew's version of the identical moment reads eutheōs de meta tēn thlipsin, “IMMEDIATELY after the tribulation” (24:29, already on these pages) — eutheōs is absent from Mark's own critical text here, a real difference in how closely each Gospel binds the cosmic signs to what has just been described, reported rather than harmonized.

⭐ “The Son of Man coming in clouds” borrows its imagery from a vision Daniel already gave a throne room to. Daniel's “one like a son of man, coming WITH the clouds of heaven,” brought before the Ancient of Days and given an everlasting kingdom (Daniel 7:13, not yet on these pages), is the scene this verse's own language draws on — the same allusion already flagged at Matthew 8:20's note on the “Son of Man” title itself, now paid off at the title's own climactic use. The angels sent “from the four winds, from the end of the earth to the end of heaven” (v27) is stock Old Testament language for the full compass of the world, not a technical description of direction.

Verses 28–32 · οὐ μὴ παρέλθῃ ἡ γενεὰ αὕτη ou mē parelthē hē genea hautē — the hardest sentence in the discourse, argued at length one chapter over in the other Gospel

“This generation will not pass away until all these things happen” (v30) is word for word the sentence Matthew 24:34's own note already sets out four separate readings for, with what each one costs — including the reading that concedes the terms were not met on the timeline a plain sense would suggest. This library does not re-argue that case twice; what is new here is only that Mark's own version of the saying carries neither Matthew's explicit naming of Daniel two verses earlier nor Matthew's “immediately,” which if anything leaves Mark's own text the LESS specific of the two about exactly how soon “these things” follow the tribulation just described.

⭐ “Heaven and earth will pass away, but my words will not” makes an extraordinary claim sit inside one plain sentence. The most permanent things a first-century listener could name — the sky and the ground beneath it — are set as LESS durable than Jesus's own spoken words, with no argument offered for the comparison at all.

⚠ “Nor the Son” is not a disputed reading here the way its Matthean cousin is. Mark's critical text carries oude ho huios, “nor the Son,” with no variant marked at this word in the archived source this translation follows — a straightforward admission, in Jesus's own reported voice, of a limit on what the Son knows. TLB paraphrases the same clause as “nor I myself,” collapsing the third-person “the Son” into first person; every other shelf version keeps the title.

Verses 33–37 · γρηγορεῖτε grēgoreite — the word the whole discourse ends on, after a different verb opens the same command

⭐ The closing charge uses two different Greek verbs for watchfulness, not one repeated four times. V33 reaches for agrypneite, “be sleepless, stay alert” (a-, “without,” + hypnos, “sleep”) — a word this discourse uses exactly once. The doorkeeper parable that follows switches to grēgoreō, “stay awake,” three times in five verses (34, 35, 37), closing the whole chapter on it: “what I say to you, I say to all: Stay awake.” Full treatment of the second verb's own root, and the “and pray” some manuscripts add at v33 to match Gethsemane's later wording, is at the new dictionary entry.

The doorkeeper parable itself has no equivalent scene in Matthew's own version of this discourse at this point in the story — Matthew moves instead to the days of Noah, the two in the field, the householder watching for a thief, and the faithful-and-wicked-slave parable, all still inside chapter 24, before turning to the ten virgins in its own next chapter. Mark's householder-with-servants image is compact where Matthew's sequence is expansive, and ends the whole discourse on a single word rather than a parable of its own.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “standing where he ought not” at v14, keeping the Greek's own masculine participle against a neuter noun rather than smoothing it to “it”; “the Lord” kept plain at v20, distinct from the “Jehovah” convention this book's own Shema quotation established two chapters earlier, because this sentence is narration rather than a quoted verse; stay awake (grēgoreite) kept distinct from be alert (agrypneite) across the closing five verses, two related but different commands rather than one repeated word; and the good news, plain, at v10, against Matthew's fuller “the good news OF THE KINGDOM” at the same idea's own separate verse.

Echoes paid. Mark 12's own closing note named “13:1–2, not yet on these pages” as the destruction this chapter would predict; it is now the chapter's own opening scene, linked back from the previous chapter. The birth pains entry, first built for Matthew 24:8, now carries this chapter's own identical phrase as a second, word-for-word occurrence. The abomination that desolates entry gains what Mark's own version of the saying does differently — no named source, and a grammatical mismatch Matthew's version does not share. And Matthew 8:20's own note on the “Son of Man” title's Daniel 7:13 background is now anchored to the title's own climactic use in this Gospel, at v26.

Echoes planted. The persecution block (vv9–13) is now linked, for the first time on these pages, to its own word-for-word twin inside Matthew 10's mission charge — a live cross-reference neither chapter carried before this one existed. The doorkeeper left watching at the door, commanded to grēgorē, is a scene this Gospel does not explain further here; watch for whether the same command returns, and on whom, when Jesus himself asks three sleeping disciples to do the identical thing two chapters ahead, at Gethsemane.

Next in this book: chapter 14 — a woman breaks a jar of very expensive perfume over Jesus's head at Bethany, Judas goes to the chief priests, and the Twelve keep the Passover one last time before the arrest in the garden where three of them are told, once more, to stay awake.
