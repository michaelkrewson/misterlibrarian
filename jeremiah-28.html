Hananiah breaks Jeremiah's wooden yoke and promises Babylon's own fall within two years; Jeremiah answers with a yoke of iron and predicts Hananiah's death within the year -- which happens.
'That same year' as ch. 27, dated independently and precisely: 'the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah... in the fourth year, in the fifth month' (v1) -- roughly 594/593 BC, confirming which king's reign ch. 27 actually belongs to. Hananiah dies 'in the seventh month' of the same year (v17), two months later.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–4 · שָׁבַרְתִּי אֶת-עֹל מֶלֶךְ בָּבֶל shavarti et-ol melekh Bavel — I have broken the yoke, spoken by a prophet wearing nothing on his own neck

⚠ This date settles a question the previous chapter had to leave open. Jeremiah 27 dated itself to “the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim” even though its own content clearly addresses Zedekiah — a well-known textual crux this translation flagged rather than corrected. This chapter opens “in that same year” and dates ITSELF, cleanly, to “the beginning of the reign of Zedekiah king of Judah, in the fourth year” (roughly 594/593 BC). The two chapters are telling one continuous story, and this verse is independent confirmation of which king's reign the previous chapter's yoke-sign actually belongs to.

Hananiah is introduced with full credentials — a named father, a hometown (Gibeon, a Levitical town a few miles from Jerusalem), and the same title, “the prophet,” that the text gives Jeremiah throughout this chapter. Nothing in his introduction marks him as false. The whole chapter's tension depends on that: two men who look, sound, and are titled identically, saying opposite things in Jehovah's name in the same room.

Hananiah's message directly answers the sign Jeremiah is still wearing. “I have broken the yoke of the king of Babylon” (v2) is spoken to a man with an actual wooden yoke on his neck (27:2) — and Hananiah's promise reverses every specific claim of the previous chapter's oracle: the vessels come back (not go further into exile, as 27:19-22 said), within two years, and Jeconiah and all the captives come home too. It is a coherent, detailed, comforting counter-prophecy, not a vague hope.

Verses 5–9 · יִוָּדַע הַנָּבִיא yivvada ha-navi — then the prophet will be known, and not before

⚠ Jeremiah's first word is “Amen.” This is genuinely surprising and this translation does not soften it into irony: “Amen! May Jehovah do so! May Jehovah fulfill your words” (v6) is not sarcasm, it is Jeremiah saying, in front of the same priests and people who tried to kill him a chapter earlier, that he would be glad to be wrong. NWT renders it almost identically, “Amen! May Jehovah do this!”; KJV and ASV both keep the bare “Amen: the LORD do so.” He wants the restoration. What he does not do is pretend Hananiah has actually said it on Jehovah's authority.

The test Jeremiah states is asymmetric, and he says so plainly. Prophets of “war, and disaster, and pestilence” (v8) need no verification — that is the default expectation of a covenant already broken, the assumption every prophet since Moses' own curses in Deuteronomy 28 has been building on. But a prophet of peace (v9) is making the harder claim, the one that requires proof, because comfort is what everyone already wants to hear and is therefore the easiest lie to sell. “Then it will be known” — not now, not from the words alone, only from the outcome. KJV and ASV both keep the plain passive, “shall be known”; NIV shifts it to “will be recognized,” which reads slightly more like a human judgment than an outcome simply becoming visible. This translation follows the plainer, more passive shelf. Jeremiah does not claim special insight into Hananiah's heart; he states a public, falsifiable rule and lets time judge it.

Verses 10–11 · וַיִּשְׁבְּרֵהוּ va-yishbrehu — and he broke it, the one purely physical act in the whole confrontation

Hananiah does not argue back. He acts. There is no rebuttal to Jeremiah's test in the text — Hananiah simply takes the wooden bar off Jeremiah's neck and breaks it in front of everyone, then restates his prophecy as if the physical act had itself proven something. It is street theater answering street theater: Jeremiah wore a sign, so Hananiah destroys it as a counter-sign, using the acted-out-prophecy genre against the man who introduced it into this dispute.

“The prophet Jeremiah went his way” closes the scene with no recorded response at all — not agreement, not argument. The silence is doing narrative work: the real answer to a broken yoke does not arrive from Jeremiah's mouth in this scene, but from Jehovah's, in the next one.

Verses 12–14 · עֹל בַּרְזֶל ol barzel — a yoke of iron, and the exact words of the previous chapter turned harder

⚠ The reply escalates by repeating, almost word for word, the previous chapter's own claim. “And even the beasts of the field I have given him” (v14) is nearly identical to 27:6's “even the beasts of the field I have given him, to serve him” — this is not a new oracle contradicting the last one; it is the same oracle, restated in a harder material, after a false prophet publicly broke the wooden version of it.

Wood breaks; iron does not. The upgrade is the whole argument, delivered as an image rather than an insult: Hananiah's one real action in this story — destroying a piece of wood — becomes the occasion for Jehovah to specify that the thing it symbolized was never made of wood in the first place. Nothing Hananiah did changed the outcome; it only changed what the sign was made of.

Verses 15–17 · הַשָּׁנָה אַתָּה מֵת ha-shanah attah met — this year you will die, and the test from nine verses earlier runs its course

Jeremiah's verdict uses the exact charge already leveled against the peace-prophets of the previous chapter. “Jehovah has not sent you… you have made this people trust in a lie” repeats, almost verbatim, 27:15's “I have not sent them… they are prophesying falsely.” Hananiah is not a new problem; he is the previous chapter's warning, now with a face and a name attached.

⚠ And then the test stated at v9 is actually run, inside the same chapter, in real time. Jeremiah does not simply predict Hananiah's death as one more unverifiable claim — he sets a specific, near-term, checkable deadline: this year, and the closing verse confirms it happened, in the seventh month. This is the rarest thing in the prophetic books: a prophecy about a rival prophet's death, made publicly, in front of the same crowd, and reported as fulfilled within the same chapter, without a chapter break's worth of narrative distance to obscure whether it happened on schedule.

⚠ “Spoken rebellion” (sarah, v16) is a rarer, sharper word than the ordinary verb for revolt — closer to defection or apostasy, a turning-away built into the word itself. KJV ASV NIV NWT all converge on “rebellion,” a rare point of full agreement across the shelf. The charge is not merely that Hananiah was wrong, but that inventing words and putting them in Jehovah's mouth is itself the rebellion, regardless of how comforting the invented words were. The identical phrase, sarah dibber al-Jehovah, is used of a second false prophet, Shemaiah the Nehelamite, already on these pages at 29:32 — the same rare charge, the same rare word, brought against two different men who claimed Jehovah's voice without his sending.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “vessels” (v3) kept identical to 27:19-22's own word, so the reversal is visible; “then it will be known” (v9) kept as a plain future, not softened into “will be recognized” or “proven,” because the whole point is that nothing is known yet; and “spoken rebellion” (sarah, v16) kept as its own word rather than merged into the more common term for revolt.

Echoes paid. The yoke Jeremiah wore in the previous chapter (27:2), physically broken here and then replaced in iron; the vessels and Jeconiah (vv3-4), the exact items 27:19-22 and 24:1 already named; “the beasts of the field I have given him” (v14), restated almost word for word from 27:6; and “Jehovah has not sent you” (v15), the identical verdict already passed on peace-prophets in general at 27:15, now delivered to one of them by name.

⚠ And the place the library reports rather than decides. The text never says whether Hananiah believed his own prophecy or knowingly invented it. Jeremiah's charge is only that Jehovah did not send him — a claim about the message's source, not a claim about the messenger's sincerity. Both readings remain open: a true believer whose comfort was still a lie, or a man who knew better and said it anyway.

This library already carries Jeremiah 29 and 31, both translated out of order; the sequential thread is still open here. Next in sequence: Jeremiah 30 — the start of the “Book of Consolation,” chapters 30–31 together, where the tone turns from judgment to a promise of return and a new covenant.
