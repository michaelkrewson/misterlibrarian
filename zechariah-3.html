The fourth night vision: Joshua the high priest stands before the angel of Jehovah in filthy garments, Satan accusing him, until Jehovah rebukes the accuser and has the filthy garments removed and a clean turban set on his head. The chapter closes on the promise of 'my servant the Branch' and a single stone engraved with seven eyes.
The fourth of the eight night visions, same setting as chapters 1-2 and 4 — 520 BC. Joshua the high priest, the vision's central figure, is Haggai's own contemporary and partner in the temple rebuilding (Haggai 1:1, Ezra 5:2).

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · the high priest, and “the Accuser” — a promise from Job paid off

⚠ The FOURTH night vision, and the first with a human figure standing trial. Joshua the high priest stands “before the angel of Jehovah,” and at his right hand — the accuser’s position in a courtroom — stands ha-satan, “the Accuser,” WITH the definite article. Hebrew does not put “the” in front of a proper name: this is a title, an office, exactly as it was already established at Job 1 (which named this very scene in advance — “in Zechariah 3:1-2 the same figure... stands at the right hand of the high priest ‘to accuse him,’ in a courtroom scene, and is rebuked”). Two chapters, two courtroom scenes, the same officer of the divine court doing the same job in both.

⚠ And here he is not merely dismissed — he is REBUKED, twice in one breath (v2), by Jehovah himself, on the specific ground that Jehovah “has chosen Jerusalem.” The Accuser’s case against Joshua may be entirely accurate (v3 confirms it: the man really is filthy); the verdict does not turn on the facts but on whose city this is. “Is this not a burning stick snatched from the fire?” — Joshua, and by extension the whole priesthood and people he represents, is a survivor of the exile, already pulled out of the fire once. KJV and ASV both keep “a brand plucked out of the fire” — the same vivid image, in the older word for a burning stick.

Verses 3–5 · “filthy garments” — a word stronger than it sounds, and a rare word for what replaces it

⚠ Tzoim, “filthy,” is one of the crudest words in Biblical Hebrew — built on a root elsewhere used of human excrement, not ordinary dirt. Every major English version softens it to “filthy” and this translation follows them, but the note should say plainly what the image actually is: the high priest, whose garments Exodus required to be spotless before he could so much as enter the sanctuary, is shown standing in court dressed in something closer to waste than dust. That is the accusation Joshua cannot answer, and it is removed without a word of self-defense from him — he never speaks in this entire vision.

The replacement is just as deliberate a word-choice in the other direction: machalatzot, “fine robes,” is a rare term (its only other Old Testament appearance is Isaiah 3:22, in a list of women’s finery) built on a root meaning “to strip off, to change” — garments FOR a change, not garments in general. From the crudest possible word for what comes off to one of the rarest words in the language for what goes on: the vision spends its vocabulary on the contrast on purpose. The turban (v5) is tsanif, the same word used of the high priest’s headpiece in Exodus’s priestly instructions (not yet on these pages) — explicitly the HIGH priestly turban, not an ordinary head-covering.

Verses 6–7 · the charge, and what “free access” is worth

The cleansing comes with conditions attached — “IF you walk in my ways” — but the reward for meeting them is striking: not merely permission to serve, but mahlekhim, “free access,” a right of way “among these who stand here,” that is, among the angelic attendants of the heavenly court itself. Joshua, who a moment ago stood accused in filthy garments, is offered a standing invitation into the divine council — the same council whose members “present themselves” before Jehovah in Job 1:6, already on this shelf. The forgiven accused is promised a security clearance his accuser never had reason to doubt he lacked.

Verses 8–9 · “my servant, Sprout” — a name, not a title, and a stone with seven eyes

⚠ Tsemach, “Sprout,” makes its FIRST appearance on these pages here — already a fixed rendering, carried over from its fuller unveiling at Jeremiah 23:5 (“a righteous Sprout”), which was itself written with this very verse in view. Here the word is bare and stranger still: the Hebrew carries no definite article — not “the Sprout,” the way ha-satan two verses earlier IS “the Accuser,” fitted with the article Hebrew uses to mark an already-known office. Tsemach instead sits in plain apposition to “my servant,” the same construction as “my servant David” or “my servant Job” — a name being conferred, not a title being explained. (At 6:12 the book will settle the question outright: “Tsemach is his name.”) So “my servant, Sprout” — not yet a king, just a shoot and a servant folded into one figure before either role is explained. The word will surface once more, crowning an actual head, at 6:12. And centuries later a priest who happens to share this prophet’s own name will bless his infant son with the promise that “the dawn from on high will visit us” — anatole, the Greek Old Testament’s own word for this same figure (Luke 1:78, already on this shelf). Two men named Zechariah, seven centuries apart, both reaching for the same word to name the same hope.

⚠ “Seven eyes” (v9) is usually softened in English to “seven facets,” reading the stone as a gem cut with seven faces — but the Hebrew plural (eynayim) is the ordinary plural for the body part, not a dual, and the more defensible reading is the plainer one: seven actual eyes, engraved or set into the stone. This translation keeps “eyes.” ⚠ The very next vision in this book will use the identical image for a lampstand’s seven lamps — “these seven are the eyes of Jehovah, ranging through the whole earth” (4:10) — and centuries later John sees a slain Lamb with “seven horns and seven eyes, which are the seven spirits of God, sent out into all the earth” (Revelation 5:6, already on this shelf) — the identical count, doing the identical work of describing total, sent-out oversight. NIV and NWT both print “seven eyes” as well; only the oldest English shelf reaches for “facets.”

Verse 10 · under the vine and the fig tree

The vision closes on the most domestic image in the whole book: neighbors inviting each other to sit under their own vine and fig tree — a fixed Hebrew idiom for settled peace and prosperity, each family safely rooted on its own land with enough to share. The same phrase describes Solomon’s reign at its height and reappears in Micah’s vision of the last days (neither yet on these pages). After a vision that opened in a courtroom with an accusation, ended in a coronation-in-miniature, and closed with a promise to erase iniquity “in a single day,” the picture Zechariah leaves the reader with is not a throne room but a garden — ordinary neighbors, sitting outside, with nothing left to fear.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “the Accuser” for ha-satan (v1), matching the rendering already fixed at Job 1 rather than importing a proper name the Hebrew grammar does not support; “filthy” for tzoim (v3), a word this note flags as cruder than it sounds rather than pretending it is ordinary dirt; “fine robes” for machalatzot (v4), one of the rarest words in the language, kept distinct from ordinary garment-words; and “Sprout,” with no article, for tsemach (v8) — matching the rendering already fixed at Jeremiah 23:5, and following the anarthrous Hebrew rather than importing an English “the” the text does not supply.

Echoes paid. “The Accuser” (ha-satan), named in advance at Job 1’s own note as exactly this scene. “Sprout” (tsemach), the rendering already fixed at Jeremiah 23:5 and now given its first bare appearance as a name in the book that made it one. And “the dawn from on high” of Luke 1:78, whose note already named this exact verse as the source of its underlying Greek word, seven centuries before a namesake priest ever spoke it.

Echoes planted. The seven eyes on one stone (v9), which return one chapter from now as the seven eyes of Jehovah on the lampstand (4:10) and, centuries later, on the slain Lamb of Revelation 5:6. Sprout (v8), still unexplained, crowned outright at 6:12. And the FIFTH night vision waiting one chapter ahead: the golden lampstand fed by two olive trees, and the word that answers every discouraged builder — “Not by might, nor by power, but by my Spirit.”
