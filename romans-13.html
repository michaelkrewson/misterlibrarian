The same letter, the same dictation. Chapter 12 ended on the instruction to overcome evil with good and to leave vengeance to God, and chapter 13 turns to the public life of the congregation: the magistrates, taxes, the debt that never ends, and the night that is far gone. It is the chapter the state has always been ready to quote, and the chapter whose plain meaning has been argued over ever since.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 12. The chapter names no date and no event. Its time-phrases are “the season” and “the hour” (verse 11), and its nearest clock is the sentence that salvation is nearer than when the readers first believed.

Romans 13 — The Mister Translation

1
Πᾶσα ψυχὴ ἐξουσίαις ὑπερεχούσαις ὑποτασσέσθω, οὐ γὰρ ἔστιν ἐξουσία εἰ μὴ ὑπὸ θεοῦ, αἱ δὲ οὖσαι ὑπὸ θεοῦ τεταγμέναι εἰσίν.
Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities; for there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been appointed by God.note
Toda persona esté sujeta a las autoridades superiores; porque no hay autoridad sino de Dios, y las que existen han sido establecidas por Dios.
2
ὥστε ὁ ἀντιτασσόμενος τῇ ἐξουσίᾳ τῇ τοῦ θεοῦ διαταγῇ ἀνθέστηκεν, οἱ δὲ ἀνθεστηκότες ἑαυτοῖς κρίμα λήμψονται.
So the one who sets himself against the authority has opposed the ordinance of God, and those who have opposed will receive judgment upon themselves.note
Así que el que se opone a la autoridad se ha rebelado contra el orden de Dios, y los que se han opuesto recibirán juicio sobre sí mismos.
3
οἱ γὰρ ἄρχοντες οὐκ εἰσὶν φόβος τῷ ἀγαθῷ ἔργῳ ἀλλὰ τῷ κακῷ. θέλεις δὲ μὴ φοβεῖσθαι τὴν ἐξουσίαν; τὸ ἀγαθὸν ποίει, καὶ ἕξεις ἔπαινον ἐξ αὐτῆς·
For rulers are not a terror to good conduct, but to bad. Do you want to have no fear of the authority? Do what is good, and you will have its praise;note
Porque los gobernantes no son terror para la buena conducta, sino para la mala. ¿Quieres no tener temor de la autoridad? Haz lo bueno, y tendrás su alabanza;
4
θεοῦ γὰρ διάκονός ἐστιν σοὶ εἰς τὸ ἀγαθόν. ἐὰν δὲ τὸ κακὸν ποιῇς, φοβοῦ· οὐ γὰρ εἰκῇ τὴν μάχαιραν φορεῖ· θεοῦ γὰρ διάκονός ἐστιν, ἔκδικος εἰς ὀργὴν τῷ τὸ κακὸν πράσσοντι.
for it is God’s servant to you for good. But if you do what is bad, be afraid; for it does not bear the sword in vain: it is God’s servant, an avenger for wrath on the one who practices what is bad.note
porque es servidor de Dios para ti, para tu bien. Pero si haces lo malo, teme; porque no en vano lleva la espada: es servidor de Dios, vengador para castigo del que practica lo malo.
5
διὸ ἀνάγκη ὑποτάσσεσθαι, οὐ μόνον διὰ τὴν ὀργὴν ἀλλὰ καὶ διὰ τὴν συνείδησιν,
Therefore there is necessity to be subject, not only because of the wrath but also because of conscience.note
Por eso es necesario estar sujetos, no solo por el castigo, sino también por la conciencia.
6
διὰ τοῦτο γὰρ καὶ φόρους τελεῖτε, λειτουργοὶ γὰρ θεοῦ εἰσιν εἰς αὐτὸ τοῦτο προσκαρτεροῦντες.
For this is also why you pay taxes, for they are God’s public servants, devoting themselves to this very thing.note
Por esta misma razón pagan también impuestos, pues son servidores públicos de Dios, dedicados precisamente a esto.
7
ἀπόδοτε πᾶσι τὰς ὀφειλάς, τῷ τὸν φόρον τὸν φόρον, τῷ τὸ τέλος τὸ τέλος, τῷ τὸν φόβον τὸν φόβον, τῷ τὴν τιμὴν τὴν τιμήν.
Render to all what is owed: tax to whom tax is owed, duty to whom duty, fear to whom fear, honor to whom honor.note
Den a todos lo que se les debe: el impuesto a quien se debe impuesto, el tributo a quien tributo, el temor a quien temor, la honra a quien honra.
8
Μηδενὶ μηδὲν ὀφείλετε, εἰ μὴ τὸ ἀλλήλους ἀγαπᾶν· ὁ γὰρ ἀγαπῶν τὸν ἕτερον νόμον πεπλήρωκεν.
Owe no one anything, except to love one another; for the one who loves the other has fulfilled the law.note
No deban nada a nadie, sino el amarse unos a otros; porque el que ama al otro ha cumplido la ley.
9
τὸ γάρ· Οὐ μοιχεύσεις, Οὐ φονεύσεις, Οὐ κλέψεις, Οὐκ ἐπιθυμήσεις, καὶ εἴ τις ἑτέρα ἐντολή, ἐν τῷ λόγῳ τούτῳ ἀνακεφαλαιοῦται, ἐν τῷ· Ἀγαπήσεις τὸν πλησίον σου ὡς σεαυτόν.
For the commandments, “You shall not commit adultery, you shall not murder, you shall not steal, you shall not covet,” and any other commandment there is, are summed up in this word: “You shall love your neighbor as yourself.”note
Porque los mandamientos: “No cometerás adulterio, no matarás, no robarás, no codiciarás”, y cualquier otro mandamiento que haya, se resumen en esta palabra: “Amarás a tu prójimo como a ti mismo.”
10
ἡ ἀγάπη τῷ πλησίον κακὸν οὐκ ἐργάζεται· πλήρωμα οὖν νόμου ἡ ἀγάπη.
Love does no harm to a neighbor; so love is the fulfillment of the law.note
El amor no hace daño al prójimo; así que el amor es el cumplimiento de la ley.
11
Καὶ τοῦτο εἰδότες τὸν καιρόν, ὅτι ὥρα ἤδη ὑμᾶς ἐξ ὕπνου ἐγερθῆναι, νῦν γὰρ ἐγγύτερον ἡμῶν ἡ σωτηρία ἢ ὅτε ἐπιστεύσαμεν.
And this, knowing the time: that it is already the hour for you to wake from sleep, for salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed.note
Y esto, sabiendo el tiempo: que ya es la hora de que ustedes despierten del sueño; porque ahora la salvación está más cerca de nosotros que cuando creímos.
12
ἡ νὺξ προέκοψεν, ἡ δὲ ἡμέρα ἤγγικεν. ἀποβαλώμεθα οὖν τὰ ἔργα τοῦ σκότους, ἐνδυσώμεθα δὲ τὰ ὅπλα τοῦ φωτός.
The night has advanced, and the day has drawn near. So let us throw off the works of darkness, and let us put on the armor of light.note
La noche ha avanzado, y el día se ha acercado. Así que despojémonos de las obras de las tinieblas, y vistámonos con las armas de la luz.
13
ὡς ἐν ἡμέρᾳ εὐσχημόνως περιπατήσωμεν, μὴ κώμοις καὶ μέθαις, μὴ κοίταις καὶ ἀσελγείαις, μὴ ἔριδι καὶ ζήλῳ,
Let us walk properly, as in the day: not in revels and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and debauchery, not in strife and jealousy.note
Andemos honestamente, como de día: no en orgías y borracheras, no en lujuria y desenfreno, no en contienda y celos.
14
ἀλλὰ ἐνδύσασθε τὸν κύριον Ἰησοῦν Χριστόν, καὶ τῆς σαρκὸς πρόνοιαν μὴ ποιεῖσθε εἰς ἐπιθυμίας.
But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh to stir up its desires.note
Pero revístanse del Señor Jesucristo, y no hagan provisión para la carne para despertar sus deseos.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the versions on the shelf. Quotations from the NWT are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV and ASV are public domain. Shelf wording was read from the published pages (NWT 1984 and 2013, ASV, KJV), not recalled.

Verses 1–2 · Πᾶσα ψυχὴ ἐξουσίαις ὑπερεχούσαις ὑποτασσέσθω … τεταγμέναι … ἀντιτασσόμενος … διαταγῇ … κρίμα λήμψονται Pasa psychē exousiais hyperechousais hypotassesthō … tetagmenai … antitassomenos … diatagē … krima lēmpsontai — Romans 13:1–2: “Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities”; the verb tassō and its compounds

“Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities” (Pasa psychē exousiais hyperechousais hypotassesthō, Romans 13:1). Psychē, soul, is the Greek for the living person; “every soul” is a way of saying every human being, and it is a Hebrew turn of phrase (nefesh) carried into Greek. The verb hypotassō, to be subject or put under rank, is in the imperative: KJV “Let every soul be subject” and ASV “Let every soul be in subjection” keep the word, and NWT 1984 “Let every soul be in subjection” keeps it too, and the NWT 2013 revision trades soul for “person” (“Let every person be in subjection”). Exousiais hyperechousais, “the authorities that are over,” name the superior powers; the adjective hyperechō means to rise above or stand over, and the rendering “higher powers” (ASV) and “superior authorities” (NWT) are both close to it. This translation says “governing authorities”: it reads the phrase as the ranks that govern a city or a state, which is what the context of taxes in verses 6–7 requires.

“for there is no authority except from God, and those that exist have been appointed by God” (ou gar estin exousia ei mē hypo theou, hai de ousai hypo theou tetagmenai eisin). Tetagmenai, “arranged,” is the perfect passive of tassō, to put in order. The root is the same word-family as antitassomenos in verse 2, “the one who sets himself against,” and as diatagē, the “ordinance” he sets himself against. The chapter gets its argument from one root used three times in two verses: a ruler is placed in order by God, a rebel sets himself against that placing, and the rebel has opposed a divine arrangement. The KJV has “ordained,” the ASV “ordained of God,” and the NWT “stand placed in their relative positions by God.” The NWT’s longer phrase does what the Greek does with a single perfect verb: it names the arrangement and not the people who hold it.

“so the one who sets himself against the authority has opposed the ordinance of God” (hōste ho antitassomenos tē exousia tē tou theou diatagē anthestēken, Romans 13:2). The verb anthistēmi means to stand against, and its perfect form, “has taken a stand,” is what the NWT carries as “has taken a stand against the arrangement of God.” The noun krima in the last clause is the word for a judicial sentence, and the Greek says the rebels will receive one “for themselves,” The KJV “shall receive to themselves damnation” reads krima as condemnation; the ASV and NWT “judgment” keep it neutral, and this translation does too, because verse 3 will go on to say the judgment is on the wrongdoing and not on the office. The text does not say the authority is infallible, only that resisting its ordering is resisting God’s.

Verses 3–4 · οἱ γὰρ ἄρχοντες … φόβος … ἔπαινον … θεοῦ γὰρ διάκονός ἐστιν … ἔκδικος εἰς ὀργὴν … τὴν μάχαιραν φορεῖ hoi gar archontes … phobos … epainon … theou gar diakonos estin … ekdikos eis orgēn … tēn machairan phorei — Romans 13:3–4: rulers, fear, praise, God’s servant, the sword and the avenger

“For rulers are not a terror to good conduct, but to bad” (hoi gar archontes ouk eisin phobos tō agathō ergō alla tō kakō, Romans 13:3). Archontes, rulers, is the same word the Gospels use for the rulers of the Jews, and Paul uses it for the rulers of this age at 1 Corinthians 2:6–8 (not yet on these pages). Here it means the magistrates. Phobos, fear or terror, is set against agathos ergon, “good work,” the phrase Paul used at Romans 12:17 and in the list of gifts. The sentence is a general claim about how the powers normally behave: they are a fear to the bad and not to the good. It is not a promise that they always do so, and the Greek present tense (eisin, “are”) reads it as a description. The KJV, ASV and NWT render the sentence as one of fact; the NWT 2013 keeps it as a statement and adds “an object of fear.”

“Do you want to have no fear of the authority? Do what is good, and you will have its praise” (theleis de mē phobeisthai tēn exousian; to agathon poiei, kai hexeis epainon ex autēs). The question is in the second person, a rhetorical turn that puts the reader in the magistrate’s presence. The noun epainos, praise, is the word of Romans 2:29 (“his praise is not from men but from God”), a neat cross-reference: here the praise comes from the authority, and there it comes from God. The KJV “wilt thou then not be afraid of the power?” and ASV “wouldest thou have no fear of the power?” keep the word for the power; the NWT 1984 “Do you, then, want to have no fear of the authority?” and the NWT 2013 “Do you want to be free of fear of the authority?” differ only in how the wish is put.

“for it is God’s servant to you for good” (theou gar diakonos estin soi eis to agathon, Romans 13:4). Diakonos, servant or minister, is the word Paul uses of Christ at Romans 15:8 (not yet on these pages); the Greek sets the magistrate in the position of a servant of God, and then in the same verse uses the same title for the one who bears the sword. The word is neutral in the Greek: it can mean an agent who carries out an order, as well as a waiter at a table. The KJV “the minister of God” and ASV “a minister of God” keep the older English word; the NWT “God’s minister” does the same. “Servant” is this translation’s choice, because it is the plainest English for an agent who answers to someone else.

“it does not bear the sword in vain: it is God’s servant, an avenger for wrath on the one who practices what is bad” (ou gar eikē tēn machairan pherei; theou gar diakonos estin, ekdikos eis orgēn tō to kakon prassonti). Machaira, sword, is the weapon the state holds, and eikē, “in vain,” is the word for a thing without a reason. The sword is not decorative. Ekdikos, avenger, is built on dikē, justice, and is the noun form of the verb in the dictionary entry on vengeance, the word Paul forbade to his readers at Romans 12:19. The magistrate is given the office that the believers are told to leave to God, and the chapter depends on the difference between the two: the state may carry out justice against wrongdoing, and the believer may not carry out vengeance against a wrong done to him. Orgē, wrath, is the word of Romans 12:19 too, “give place to the wrath.” The KJV “a revenger to execute wrath” and the ASV “an avenger for wrath” both keep the noun; the NWT 2013 “an avenger to express wrath” uses the word for the state’s acting, and this translation follows that.

Verses 5–7 · διὸ ἀνάγκη ὑποτάσσεσθαι … διὰ τὴν συνείδησιν … φόρους τελεῖτε … λειτουργοὶ θεοῦ … ἀπόδοτε πᾶσι τὰς ὀφειλάς … τὸ τέλος τὸ τέλος dio anankē hypotassesthai … dia tēn syneidēsin … phorous teleite … leitourgoi theou … apodote pasi tas opheilas … to telos to telos — Romans 13:5–7: necessity, conscience, taxes, public servants, and the four debts

“Therefore there is necessity to be subject, not only because of the wrath but also because of conscience” (dio anankē hypotassesthai, ou monon dia tēn orgēn alla kai dia tēn syneidēsin, Romans 13:5). Anankē, necessity, is a strong word: the reader is under compulsion, and the verse gives two grounds for it. Syneidēsis, conscience, is a word Paul brings in at Romans 2:15, where the law is written on the heart and the conscience bears witness with it, and again at Romans 9:1. The sentence says that obedience to the state is not only a fear of punishment but a matter of conscience. The KJV “for conscience sake” and ASV “for conscience’ sake” keep the phrase; the NWT 2013 “on account of your conscience” reads the phrase as a ground of obedience, and this translation does too.

“For this is also why you pay taxes, for they are God’s public servants” (dia touto gar kai phorous teleite, leitourgoi gar theou eisin, Romans 13:6). Teleite, pay, is the verb of the tax, and it is the same root as the noun telos in verse 7. Leitourgos, public servant, is a word the Greek Old Testament uses for the priests and the ministers of the sanctuary (its verb leitourgeō is used of the priestly service in the Greek of Exodus), and Paul uses it here for the officials who collect the state’s money. The verse makes the tax a service to God, which is a striking claim about a payment the state collected. The KJV “for they are God’s ministers” and the ASV “they are ministers of God’s service” both keep the word for a servant; the NWT “public servants” gives the sense of a civic office, and this translation adopts it.

“Render to all what is owed: tax to whom tax is owed, duty to whom duty, fear to whom fear, honor to whom honor” (apodote pasi tas opheilas, tō ton phoron ton phoron, tō to telos to telos, tō ton phobon ton phobon, tō tēn timēn tēn timēn, Romans 13:7). The sentence has four datives, each followed by its own object in the same form, and English can only keep the repetition by repeating the word. Phoros is the direct tax, the one levied on persons and property, and telos is the duty or toll on goods, the customs. The distinction is one the Greek lexicons draw and that the ancient tax system drew, and it is the reason this translation keeps both words. The KJV “tribute to whom tribute is due; custom to whom custom” and the ASV “tribute to whom tribute is due; custom to whom custom” keep both as English words; the NWT 1984 “to him who [calls for] the tax, the tax; to him who [calls for] the tribute, the tribute” uses the bracketed “calls for” to name the collector. The verb “render” is apodidōmi, to give back what is due, and the noun for debt, opheilē, is the noun the next verse turns into a single debt that never ends: owing love.

Verses 8–10 · Μηδενὶ μηδὲν ὀφείλετε … ἀγαπῶν τὸν ἕτερον νόμον πεπλήρωκεν … οὐ μοιχεύσεις … ἀνακεφαλαιοῦται … ἀγαπήσεις τὸν πλησίον σου ὡς σεαυτόν … πλήρωμα οὖν νόμου Mēdeni mēden opheilete … agapōn ton heteron nomon peplērōken … ou moicheuseis … anakephalaioutai … agapēseis ton plēsion sou hōs seauton … plērōma oun nomou — Romans 13:8–10: the debt that never ends, the commandments summed up, love as the fulfillment of the law

“Owe no one anything, except to love one another” (Mēdeni mēden opheilete, ei mē to allēlous agapan, Romans 13:8). The verb opheilō is the verb of the debt in verse 7, and the sentence turns the ledger over: the debts of verse 7 are paid in full, and what remains is a debt that cannot be paid off, the debt of love. The phrase has no verb of its own in the first clause; English has to supply “owe.” The KJV “Owe no man any thing, but to love one another” and the ASV “Owe no man anything, save to love one another” keep the sentence as a sentence of its own; the NWT 1984 “Do not YOU people be owing anybody a single thing, except to love one another” ties it to the list of verse 7 by repeating the verb, and the 2013 revision “Do not owe anything to anyone except to love one another” does the same.

“the one who loves the other has fulfilled the law” (ho gar agapōn ton heteron nomon peplērōken, Romans 13:8). The participle agapōn is the verb agapaō, to love, and the object is ton heteron, “the other,” a word Paul uses as a neighbor, a fellow human being. The verb plēroō, to fill or complete, is the verb of Matthew 5:17, where Jesus says he came not to abolish the law but to fulfill it; it is the verb the letter uses in Romans 8:4 for the law’s requirement being fulfilled in us. The KJV “hath fulfilled the law” and the NWT “has fulfilled the law” keep the verb. The noun plērōma, fulfillment, comes back at verse 10. The word “law” here is nomos, the Torah, and the chapter applies it to the whole of the commandments that follow.

“For the commandments… are summed up in this word: ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself’” (to gar… ou moicheuseis, ou phoneuseis, ou kleipseis, ouk epithumēseis… en tō logō touto anakephalaioutai, en tō… Agapēseis ton plēsion sou hōs seauton, Romans 13:9). Paul gives four of the Ten Commandments in the order adultery, murder, theft, coveting, and the order is not the order of the Greek Exodus, which puts murder first (Exodus 20:13–17, on these pages). The list leaves out the commandment against false witness, which the Greek Exodus includes. The text does not say why; the list is a selection, introduced by “and whatever other commandment there is.” The commandments are summed up by the verb anakephalaioō, to sum up or gather under one head, which the letter’s readers will know from Ephesians 1:10, where God gathers all things under Christ. The love command is Leviticus 19:18, “You shall love your neighbor as yourself,” a verse on these pages at Leviticus 19. The KJV “if there be any other commandment, it is briefly comprehended in this saying” and the ASV “it is summed up in this word” both keep the sense of summary; the NWT 2013 “is summed up in this saying” does the same, and the NWT 1984 says “word.”

“Love does no harm to a neighbor; so love is the fulfillment of the law” (hē agapē tō plēsion kakon ouk ergazetai; plērōma oun nomou hē agapē, Romans 13:10). The verb ergazomai, to work, is used in the active sense: love does not work evil. Romans 12:17 has the related idea of repaying evil, in a different verb (12:17). The verse is a definition of love that is a negative as well as a positive: it does not harm, and in that refusal it fulfils the law. The noun plērōma is the noun of the fulfillment in verse 8, and the chapter has returned to its opening verb with a different noun. The KJV “worketh no ill to his neighbour” and the NWT “does not work evil to one’s neighbor” keep the verb in the active; the NWT 2013 “therefore, love is the law’s fulfillment” keeps the noun.

Verses 11–12 · Καὶ τοῦτο εἰδότες τὸν καιρόν … ὥρα ἤδη … ἐξ ὕπνου ἐγερθῆναι … ἐγγύτερον ἡμῶν ἡ σωτηρία … ἡ νὺξ προέκοψεν … ἀποβαλώμεθα … ἐνδυσώμεθα … ὅπλα τοῦ φωτός Kai touto eidotes ton kairon … hōra ēdē … ex hypnou egerthēnai … engyteron hēmōn hē sōtēria … hē nyx prokopsen … apobalōmetha … endysōmetha … ta hopla tou phōtos — Romans 13:11–12: the time, the hour, the sleep, the nearer salvation, the night and the day, and the armor

“knowing the time: that it is already the hour for you to wake from sleep” (touto eidotes ton kairon, hoti hōra ēdē hymas ex hypnou egerthēnai, Romans 13:11). Kairos, the time or the season, is a word for a fitting or decisive moment; see the dictionary entry. Hōra, hour, is the word the Gospels use for the hour of Jesus’ betrayal (Matthew 26:45, on these pages). The sleep image, ex hypnou egerthēnai, “to be woken from sleep,” is the image of an alarm, and the readers are told to wake up as the day approaches. The KJV “knowing the time, that now it is high time to awake out of sleep” and the ASV “knowing the season, that already it is time for you to awake out of sleep” keep the verb; the NWT 2013 “you know the season, that it is already the hour for you to awake from sleep” keeps season, which is what kairos means here.

“for salvation is nearer to us now than when we first believed” (nyn gar engyteron hēmōn hē sōtēria ē hote episteusamen, Romans 13:11). Sōtēria, salvation, is the word of the chapter’s thesis at Romans 1:16, the power of God for salvation. The comparative engyteron, nearer, carries the same claim as the image of the day in verse 12. The aorist episteusamen, “when we believed,” refers to a single moment in the past, the believers’ first faith, and the salvation the letter describes is still ahead in time. The clause is an argument from a future that is closer than it was, and it is the reason for the command in the verse that follows.

“The night has advanced, and the day has drawn near” (hē nyx prokopsen, hē de hēmera ēngiken, Romans 13:12). Prokoptō, to advance, is the word for a night that is well along, and engizō, to draw near, is the verb of the day coming. The two verbs have the same grammatical form, and the sentence puts both clocks at once. The metaphor is the one the New Testament uses for the coming of the Lord and the coming of the day together. The KJV “The night is far spent, the day is at hand” and the ASV “The night is far spent, and the day is at hand” keep both verbs as clauses; the NWT 1984 “The night is well along; the day has drawn near” keeps “drawn near.”

⚠ “So let us throw off the works of darkness” (apobalōmetha oun ta erga tou skotous, Romans 13:12). The SBLGNT marks this verb as one with a variant reading. The printed text has apobalōmetha, “let us throw off,” the verb of taking off a garment. I did not check the witnesses for the alternative, so the alternative is reported, not checked. The variant does not change the metaphor, which is clothing: the works are taken off like a garment, and the armor is put on. The KJV “cast off the works of darkness” and the ASV “cast off the works of darkness” keep the verb of casting off; the NWT 1984 “put off the works belonging to darkness” and the 2013 “throw off the works belonging to darkness” carry the same image.

“and put on the armor of light” (endysōmetha de ta hopla tou phōtos, Romans 13:12). Hopla, weapons or armor, is a word for the equipment of a soldier, and the verb endyō, to put on, is the verb of the clothing. The armor is a soldier’s equipment that is also a garment, so the image is double. The soldier’s armor returns in Ephesians 6:11–17 and in 2 Corinthians 6:7 and 10:4 (none yet on these pages). The phrase “armor of light” sets the ancient image of the warrior against the chapter’s image of the day, and the letter is careful to say that the light is what the readers are to wear. The KJV “put on the armour of light” and the ASV “put on the armor of light” keep the soldier’s word; the NWT 2013 “put on the weapons of the light” keeps weapons, which is the more literal sense.

Verses 13–14 · ὡς ἐν ἡμέρᾳ εὐσχημόνως περιπατήσωμεν … κώμοις καὶ μέθαις … κοίταις καὶ ἀσελγείαις … ἔριδι καὶ ζήλῳ … ἐνδύσασθε τὸν κύριον Ἰησοῦν Χριστόν … πρόνοιαν … εἰς ἐπιθυμίας hōs en hēmera euschēmonōs peripatēsōmen … kōmois kai methais … koitais kai aselgeiais … eridi kai zēlō … endysasthe ton kyrion Iēsoun Christon … pronoian … eis epithumias — Romans 13:13–14: walking decently, the vices of the night, and putting on the Lord Jesus Christ

“Let us walk properly, as in the day” (hōs en hēmera euschēmonōs peripatēsōmen, Romans 13:13). Peripateō, to walk, is the verb of the whole ethical section of the letter, and the adverb euschēmonōs, decently or becomingly, comes from the same root as schēma, the outward form, which the letter used at Romans 12:2. The sentence makes the day the standard for the way a person walks, and the night the standard for what is hidden. The KJV “Let us walk honestly, as in the day” and the ASV “Let us walk becomingly, as in the day” keep the sense of respectability; the NWT 2013 “Let us walk decently as in the daytime” keeps decently.

“not in revels and drunkenness, not in sexual immorality and debauchery, not in strife and jealousy” (mē kōmois kai methais, mē koitais kai aselgeiais, mē eridi kai zēlō, Romans 13:13). The list is three pairs, each in the dative with the word for “not.” Kōmos, revel, is the word for the drunken parties of the Greek world; methē, drunkenness, is the second half of the first pair. Koitē, bed or sexual intercourse, is a word that stands also for a bed and is paired with aselgeia, the lack of restraint, which Paul also uses in the vice list of Galatians 5:19 (not yet on these pages). Eris, strife, and zēlos, jealousy or zeal, are the two vices that stand in the list of Romans 1:29 as well, and the pair comes back in 1 Corinthians 3:3 (not yet on these pages). The list is a list of the night’s behavior. The KJV “Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying” and the ASV “not in revelling and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and jealousy” keep the older English words; the NWT 2013 “not in wild parties and drunkenness, not in immoral intercourse and brazen conduct, not in strife and jealousy” uses the modern words for them.

“But put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make no provision for the flesh to stir up its desires” (alla endysasthe ton kyrion Iēsoun Christon, kai tēs sarkos pronoian mē poieisthe eis epithumias, Romans 13:14). The verb endyō, to put on, returns from verse 12, where the armor was put on, and now the object is a person: the readers are to put on the Lord Jesus Christ as a garment. The image is the baptism of the letter’s sixth chapter, where the readers were buried with Christ, and the phrase “put on Christ” is the one Galatians 3:27 uses (not yet on these pages). Pronoia, provision or forethought, is the word of Romans 12:17, where the readers take thought for what is good: here the same care is turned to the flesh, to the desires it stirs up. The KJV “make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof” and the ASV “make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof” both render pronoia as the provision a person makes; the NWT 2013 “do not be planning ahead for the desires of the flesh” keeps the planning.
