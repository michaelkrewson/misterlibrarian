A few months after the first letter, Paul writes to the same Thessalonian congregation again — now to steady them under sharpening persecution, and to put down a rumour that the Day of the Lord had already come. Chapter 1 answers the persecution: God will repay the afflicters at the unveiling of the Lord Jesus 'in flaming fire'.
c. AD 50–51, from Corinth, close on the heels of 1 Thessalonians (the two share senders, situation and a great deal of wording). ⚠ 2 Thessalonians is the most disputed of the letters outside the Pastorals: a substantial body of scholarship holds it to be by a later hand writing in Paul's name, chiefly because its picture of the End — signs first, then the Day (ch. 2) — sits awkwardly beside the first letter's 'thief in the night' (1 Thess 5:2), and because its verbal debts to that letter are so heavy. The majority still read it as Paul's, written soon after. Both positions are reported.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · the same three names, and the greeting the first letter cut short

The senders are identical to the first letter — Paul and Silvanus and Timothy, three names and no title, exactly as at 1 Thessalonians 1:1. If the two letters are only months apart, as almost everyone thinks, this is the same team writing again to the same congregation, and the second letter keeps looking back at the first. One small change: here it is “God our Father” (the Greek adds hēmōn), where the first letter had simply “God the Father.”

⚠ And here is the half of the greeting the first letter left off. 1 Thessalonians stopped dead at “grace to you and peace” — four words, the shortest opening Paul ever wrote, breaking off exactly where his other letters run on. This one runs on: “grace to you and peace from God the Father and the Lord Jesus Christ,” the full formula, the standard shape. Read side by side the two greetings are a study in the same hand a little later, with the convention now settled — or, on the other reading, a study in a later writer working with the first letter open in front of him. ⚠ That is the question this book carries: 2 Thessalonians is the most disputed of the letters outside the Pastorals, and the greeting is the first of the small verbal overlaps that make the dispute. The book intro lays out the case on both sides; the notes here only mark where the seams show. The KJV, ASV and NWT all print the fuller greeting, as every text does — unlike at 1 Thessalonians 1:1, there is nothing here that later copyists needed to supply.

Verses 3–4 · a thanksgiving under obligation, and boasting to the other churches

⚠ “We OUGHT to give thanks.” The first letter opened with a plain “we give thanks” (eucharistoumen); this one opens with a sense of duty — eucharistein opheilomen, “we are bound to give thanks” — and drives it home with kathōs axion estin, “as is fitting.” Some readers hear a cooler, more formal note in that and file it among the reasons to doubt Paul wrote the letter. Others hear a man reassuring an anxious, harassed congregation that their growth does not merely please him but obliges the thanks — that he is stating their due, not flattering them. Both readings are on the page; the library reports them and does not vote. KJV “we are bound to thank God”; NIV “we ought always to thank God”; NWT “we are obligated to give God thanks.”

⚠ Two words for growth, both turned up as high as they go. The faith hyperauxanei — “grows beyond all measure,” a rare double-compound (hyper, “over,” on the ordinary verb “to grow”) that occurs nowhere else in the Greek Bible and may have been made for the sentence — and the love pleonazei, “overflows,” runs past the brim. The first letter had prayed that their love would “increase and abound” (1 Thess 3:12); here he reports back that it has. KJV “groweth exceedingly”; ASV the same; NIV “is growing more and more.”

“Every single one of you.” The Greek piles up the singulars — henos hekastou pantōn hymōn, “of one, of each, of all of you” — a slightly awkward heaping that is not decoration: he is refusing to praise the congregation in the abstract and insisting on it person by person, which is a pointed thing to do to a group under pressure to close ranks or scatter.

“We ourselves boast about you.” Enkauchasthai — a strengthened form of Paul’s favourite kauchaomai, and found only here in the New Testament — with autous hēmas, “we ourselves,” thrown in for emphasis: normally he tells congregations not to boast, and here he is the one doing it, to their credit, in the other “congregations of God.” And the ground of the boast is their endurance and faith “in all your persecutions (diōgmois) and the afflictions you are bearing” — present tense, still going on. The pressure the first letter described has not lifted between the letters; if anything it has sharpened into open persecution, and that is the situation the rest of the chapter exists to answer.

Verse 5 · the suffering itself is evidence, and the kingdom of God

A hanging noun with no verb attached. Endeigma — “a plain token, a piece of evidence”; it too occurs only here in the New Testament — stands loose in the accusative, pointing back at the whole scene just described. The claim is startling and easy to miss: the Thessalonians’ patient suffering is evidence “of the righteous judgment of God.” ⚠ Not that the suffering earns anything — the point is not merit — but that a community holding steady under undeserved pressure is itself a visible sign that the books are not yet balanced and that a God who judges rightly will balance them. The suffering is not evidence against God’s justice, the way it looks; it is evidence for it. KJV “a manifest token of the righteous judgment of God”; NIV “evidence that God’s judgment is right.”

The vocabulary of a courtroom starts here and runs to the end of the paragraph. Dikaia krisis, “righteous judgment,” is the first of a chain of justice-words — dikaion in verse 6, ekdikēsin in verse 8, dikēn tisousin in verse 9 — that turns the whole passage into a legal proceeding. The theme is not anger but a verdict.

⚠ “Counted worthy of the KINGDOM of God.” Kataxiōthēnai, to be reckoned fully worthy (the verb returns at verse 11) — and the object is one that stands out in a Pauline letter: the kingdom of God is the great phrase of the Gospels, on Jesus’ lips constantly, but Paul reaches for it only a handful of times. Its arrival here, hooked to suffering — “for which you are also suffering” — keeps it eschatological: the kingdom is the thing coming, the thing the endurance is toward. Douay “that you may be counted worthy of the kingdom of God, for which also you suffer.”

Verses 6–8 · the strongest page of retribution in Paul, and what it does and does not license

⚠ Affliction repaid to the afflicters — a deliberate turn of the same word. Antapodounai tois thlibousin hymas thlipsin: “to repay affliction to those afflicting you.” The noun and the participle are the same root (thlipsis, pressure), so the sentence closes an exact circle — the pressure comes back on the ones applying it — and to those under the pressure, “relief” (anesin, a loosening, the precise opposite of a squeeze). This is the most sustained statement of retributive judgment anywhere in the Pauline letters, and it has troubled readers for centuries.

⚠ But notice what it does not hand to the sufferers. It is often set against Romans 12:19 — “never avenge yourselves… ‘vengeance is mine, I will repay,’ says the Lord” — as though the two pull opposite ways. They do not. Both leave the repaying entirely to God, and at a fixed future moment; neither puts it in a human hand. What this passage offers a persecuted congregation is not permission to retaliate but the ground on which they need not: the account will be settled, and not by them. Whether that reads as comfort or as something harder is left to the reader — the letter plainly means it as comfort, addressed to people with no power to avenge anything.

“At the UNVEILING of the Lord Jesus.” Apokalypsis — the word behind “apocalypse,” an uncovering of what has been hidden. ⚠ Worth watching across this short letter: the coming is an apokalypsis here, and at 2:1 and 2:8 a parousia, an arrival-and-presence — the same event under two images, a curtain drawn back and a monarch arriving. The first letter used parousia; this one adds the uncovering. “With the angels of his power” (met’ angelōn dynameōs autou) — we keep the genitive rather than flatten it to “his mighty angels.”

⚠ “In flaming fire” — the whole scene is quarried out of Isaiah. En phlogi pyros, “in a flame of fire,” and the descent that surrounds it — the Lord revealed from heaven, fire, vengeance rendered — is stitched together from the Old Testament theophany of judgment, above all Isaiah 66:15 (“the Lord will come in fire… to render his anger in fury, and his rebuke with flames of fire”). Didontos ekdikēsin is “dealing out vengeance” — and ekdikēsis is not private revenge but the carrying-out of justice, a setting-right by the proper authority, which is why the courtroom word belongs with the fire. ⚠ The two targets — “those who do not know God” and “those who do not obey the gospel” — echo the cry of Jeremiah 10:25 and Psalm 79:6 (“pour out your wrath on the nations that do not know you”); whether Paul names two groups, Gentiles who never knew God and hearers who refused the message, or one group described twice, is genuinely disputed. KJV “in flaming fire taking vengeance”; Geneva “in flaming fire, rendering vengeance”; NWT “as he brings vengeance.”

Verses 9–10 · “away from the face of the Lord” — the crux, and the mutual glory

A phrase out of the law courts and the tragedians. Dikēn tisousin, “they will pay the penalty” — dikē is Justice herself, and tinō is to pay a debt or a fine; the pairing is a fixed classical expression for paying a legal price, and it appears only here in the New Testament. The register lifts, for a moment, into formal Greek. What is paid is olethron aiōnion, “eternal destruction.”

⚠ And the whole weight of the verse hangs on one preposition. Apo prosōpou tou kyriou — “from the face of the Lord” — can be read two ways, and English is forced to choose where Greek is not. (1) SEPARATION: the destruction consists of being shut out from the Lord’s presence — “away from,” banishment — which is how the NIV takes it, “shut out from the presence of the Lord.” (2) SOURCE: the destruction comes from the Lord’s presence — and here the deciding fact is that the phrase is a near-quotation of Isaiah 2:10, 19, 21, where people hide “from before the face of the Lord and from the glory of his majesty” as he rises to shake the earth; on that reading the presence is the origin of the ruin, not its content. The KJV and ASV keep the bare “from the presence of the Lord,” which leaves the ambiguity open; the NIV resolves it toward separation. ⚠ Underneath sits the older question of olethron aiōnion itself — an unending conscious ruin, or a destruction whose effect is eternal and final (the annihilationist reading) — on which serious readers have divided for a very long time. The library keeps “away from the face of the Lord,” which preserves what the Greek preserves, and lays out the readings without casting a vote.

“Glorified IN his holy ones.” Not glorified by them but in them (en tois hagiois): they are the place the glory is displayed, the surface it shows on. Beside it stands thaumasthēnai, “to be marvelled at, wondered at,” among “all who believed.” ⚠ Then the sentence breaks off into a sudden aside — “because our testimony to you was believed” — which drops the anxious reader straight back into the picture: you are among those who believed, so you stand inside this scene, not outside its verdict. It closes “on that day” — the Day the first letter called the day of the Lord.

Verses 11–12 · the prayer, in the first letter’s own words, and a famously ambiguous last line

⚠ The prayer reaches straight back into 1 Thessalonians for its phrases. “Work of faith” (ergon pisteōs) is lifted verbatim from 1 Thessalonians 1:3, and “with power” (en dynamei) from 1:5 — the two letters’ opening thanksgivings resurfacing here in a single line. The overlap is close enough to be deliberate, and it is the same kind of verbal debt that runs through the whole letter: either a writer quoting himself, or a writer quoting the first letter. Axiōsē tēs klēseōs, “count you worthy of the calling,” picks up kataxiōthēnai from verse 5; and God is asked to “bring to fullness every good resolve of goodness” — eudokia, the settled good-pleasure of the will.

⚠ “The grace of our God and the Lord Jesus Christ” — one person, or two? The Greek is tou theou hēmōn kai kyriou Iēsou Christou: a single article governing two nouns joined by kai. On the strict form of the so-called Granville Sharp rule, that construction points to one referent — “our God and Lord, Jesus Christ,” a high title for Jesus alone. Most grammarians decline to press the rule here, because kyrios, “Lord,” behaves almost as a proper name and the rule is not usually applied to a word that has hardened into one; so the standard reading keeps two persons, “our God, and the Lord Jesus Christ,” matching the two-person greeting of verse 2. ⚠ But the ambiguity is real and old, and the same grammatical shape at Titus 2:13 and 2 Peter 1:1 is read the other way by nearly everyone. We print the two-person reading and mark the one-person possibility; the library reports the grammar and does not adjudicate it. Every English shelf here — KJV, ASV, NIV, NWT — reads two.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “we ought to give thanks” for opheilomen, the note of obligation the first letter did not have; “grows beyond all measure” for hyperauxanei, the made-for-the-sentence compound; the affliction / afflicting wordplay of verse 6 kept as one root repaid on itself; “unveiling” for apokalypsis, distinct from the parousia of chapter 2; “dealing out vengeance” for ekdikēsis kept as the carrying-out of justice, not private revenge; “away from the face of the Lord” for apo prosōpou, where “shut out from” would resolve an ambiguity the Greek leaves open; “will pay the penalty” for dikēn tisousin, the courtroom idiom found only here; and the two-person reading of verse 12, with the one-person Granville-Sharp possibility flagged rather than buried.

Echoes paid. The completed greeting, filling in the half broken off at 1 Thessalonians 1:1; “work of faith” and “with power” lifted verbatim from 1 Thessalonians 1:3 and 1:5; endurance under affliction, praised in the first letter (1 Thess 1:6) and boasted of to other congregations here; the coming wrath of 1 Thessalonians 1:10 and Zephaniah 1:15, given its fullest and most frightening description in this chapter; and the theophany of Isaiah — the fire of Isaiah 66:15 and the hiding “from the face of the Lord” of Isaiah 2 — standing behind the whole descent.

Echoes planted. The unveiling named here becomes the letter’s real business in chapter 2, where the parousia of the Lord is set against the parousia of the “man of lawlessness”; the Day the Thessalonians are told is still to come corrects a rumour — addressed at 2:2 — that it had already arrived; and the retribution named here and left with God frames the letter’s closing turn to the everyday, down to the sharp instruction that anyone unwilling to work should not eat (3:10).

2 Thessalonians is new to the library. Next in this book: chapter 2 — the letter’s most disputed and most influential page. The Day of the Lord “has not come,” against a rumour that it had; first the rebellion, and the revealing of “the man of lawlessness… who sets himself up in the temple of God, proclaiming himself to be God”; the mysterious “restrainer” who holds him back for now; and “the mystery of lawlessness” already at work.
