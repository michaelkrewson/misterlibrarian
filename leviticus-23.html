🕰
The Torah's full festival calendar, seven appointed times in one chapter: the weekly Sabbath; Passover and the seven-day Feast of Unleavened Bread in the first month; the firstfruits sheaf and the fifty-day count to the wheat-harvest offering (with the poor-and-foreigner gleaning law dropped into the middle of it); the Feast of Trumpets and the Day of Atonement in the seventh month; and the seven-day Feast of Booths closing the agricultural year. Each festival gets its own date, its own required rest, and its own offering — the same calendar still kept, in outline, by religious Jews today.
⚠ Undated within the chapter itself beyond the festival dates it fixes for every future year — the one chapter in Leviticus that is, by its own content, permanently forward-looking rather than tied to a single moment in the wilderness. Still spoken at Sinai in the same window as the rest of this block (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

2
דַּבֵּר אֶל-בְּנֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, וְאָמַרְתָּ אֲלֵהֶם, מוֹעֲדֵי יְהוָה, אֲשֶׁר-תִּקְרְאוּ אֹתָם מִקְרָאֵי קֹדֶשׁ--אֵלֶּה הֵם, מוֹעֲדָי.

Habla a los hijos de Israel, y díles: Los tiempos señalados de Jehová, que proclamaréis como convocaciones santas —estos son mis tiempos señalados.
3

Six days work shall be done, but on the seventh day is a sabbath of solemn rest, a holy convocation; you shall do no work at all; it is a sabbath to Jehovah in all your dwellings. {פ}note
Seis días se hará trabajo, pero el séptimo día es un sábado de reposo solemne, una convocación santa; no haréis ningún trabajo; es un sábado para Jehová en todas vuestras moradas.
4
אֵלֶּה מוֹעֲדֵי יְהוָה, מִקְרָאֵי קֹדֶשׁ, אֲשֶׁר-תִּקְרְאוּ אֹתָם, בְּמוֹעֲדָם.
These are the appointed times of Jehovah, holy convocations, which you shall proclaim at their appointed time.note
Estos son los tiempos señalados de Jehová, convocaciones santas, que proclamaréis en su tiempo señalado.

In the first month, on the fourteenth day of the month, at twilight, is the Passover to Jehovah.note
En el mes primero, a los catorce días del mes, al atardecer, es la Pascua para Jehová.

Leviticus 23 meaning — why the weekly Sabbath opens the festival calendar

Before naming a single annual feast, this chapter's list of Jehovah's mo'adim, ‘appointed times,’ opens with the weekly Sabbath (v3) — a structural claim, not an afterthought: the seven-day rhythm every Israelite keeps fifty-two times a year belongs to the same category as Passover or the Day of Atonement, not a lesser or separate thing. ‘A sabbath of solemn rest’ repeats the exact phrase this project has already met at Exodus 35:2 (already on these pages). ⚠ KJV and ASV both translate mo'adim as ‘feasts’ throughout this chapter, a word that fits Passover or Tabernacles but sits oddly on a weekly Sabbath; NIV's ‘appointed festivals’ has the identical problem. This translation keeps ‘appointed times’ consistently, precisely because the list itself insists a Sabbath belongs in the same category as a festival.

Passover and Unleavened Bread — and the work-category the calendar quietly splits in two

The Passover (v5) gets a date and a name but no ‘holy convocation’ of its own — a single evening rite, not a day of rest; Exodus 12 (already on these pages) already supplied its full ritual. Unleavened Bread (v6–8), the seven-day festival that follows it, is where the convocation language begins.

⚠ V7's ‘laborious work’ (melechet avodah) is a narrower category than the Sabbath's own ‘work at all’ (kol melachah, v3) — a distinction this chapter draws consistently and never explains, but later practice built an entire legal category on it: food may be prepared on a festival day in a way it may not be on the Sabbath or the Day of Atonement (v28, kol melachah again, the stricter standard). The chapter's own vocabulary is where that split begins.

Firstfruits and the Omer — the calendar two chapters promised and finally deliver

This is where two of this project's own forward-plants resolve at once: Leviticus 2's note on an individual firstfruits gift, and Exodus 23's own sketch of the three pilgrimage festivals (both already on these pages), each said the full ritual detail would come ‘at Leviticus 23’ — it has now arrived. The omer named here (v10) is the same word already fixed at Exodus 16 (already on these pages) for the daily manna measure — one word doing double duty as a sheaf of grain and a unit of volume, the calendar borrowing its name from the wilderness ration.

Counting to Pentecost, and the gleaning law that interrupts a festival calendar

V22's gleaning law — leave the field's edge and the fallen grain for the poor and the foreigner — restates Leviticus 19:9–10 (already on these pages) almost word for word, dropped into the middle of a ritual calendar with no ritual content of its own. The placement is not a slip: this is the HARVEST festival, and the law belongs exactly here, at the one moment of the year when a landowner's own grain and a poor family's next meal occupy the same field at the same time.

The fifty-day count from the wave-sheaf (vv15–16) is what later tradition calls Shavuot, and what this project's own Pentecost entry (already on these pages) already named as the Greek word for ‘fiftieth’ — the same count that lands, centuries later, on the day Acts 2 (already on these pages) reports a crowd ‘from every nation under heaven’ standing in Jerusalem, drawn there for exactly this festival's own pilgrimage requirement.

Trumpets — the one festival this chapter barely describes

The memorial of blowing (v24) gets two verses and no specified ritual beyond a rest and an offering — the least detail of any festival in this chapter, for the day later Judaism will develop into Rosh Hashanah, the new year. Whatever ceremony eventually attached to it, this text does not yet supply it.

The Day of Atonement, dated and staked

Leviticus 16 (already on these pages) already gave this day its full ritual — the two goats, the blood carried past the veil. This chapter gives it a place on the calendar (the tenth of the seventh month) and, for the first time, an explicit penalty: karet, being cut off, for anyone who does not afflict himself (v29), and destruction for anyone who works at all on it (v30) — the only appointed time in this whole chapter where failing to observe carries a stated consequence this severe.

Booths — a festival that explains its own reason for existing

Sukkot, seven days living in temporary shelters, is the only festival in this chapter that states its own pedagogical purpose outright: ‘so that your generations may know that I made the children of Israel dwell in booths’ (v43) — not a harvest rite alone but a deliberately staged history lesson, repeated every year specifically so it will not be forgotten. V40's ‘fruit of majestic trees, branches of palm trees… and willows of the brook’ becomes, in later practice, the four species of the lulav and etrog — not named as a fixed ritual object here, only as raw material for rejoicing.

The eighth day (v36, v39) is called atzarah, a solemn assembly — technically outside the seven days of Sukkot proper, the festival's own coda. Sukkot is where this project's own John 7 and Nehemiah 8 (neither yet on these pages) will eventually land, the Feast of Tabernacles supplying the setting for one and the occasion for a public reading of this very Law in the other.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 23 both run 1–44 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Seven Masoretic paragraphs divide the chapter, matching its seven festival sections exactly: closed after v3, open after v8, closed after v14, open after v22, closed after v25, open after v32, open after v44. ‘Laborious work’ (melechet avodah) is newly entered, distinguished from the Sabbath's stricter ‘work at all’ (kol melachah). ‘Appointed times’ renders mo'adim throughout rather than the more common ‘feasts,’ since the list opens with the weekly Sabbath, which no version calls a feast.

Echoes paid. Leviticus 2 and Exodus 23 (both already on these pages), each of which promised this chapter's own full ritual detail by name; Exodus 12's Passover (already on these pages), Exodus 16's omer (already on these pages), Exodus 35:2's sabbath of solemn rest (already on these pages), Leviticus 19:9–10's gleaning law (already on these pages), this project's own Pentecost, teruah, sukkah, and atzarah dictionary entries (all already on these pages), Acts 2's Pentecost crowd (already on these pages), and Leviticus 16's own Day of Atonement ritual (already on these pages), now given its calendar date and stated penalty.

Echoes planted. John 7 and Nehemiah 8 (neither yet on these pages), both set against the Feast of Tabernacles this chapter institutes.
