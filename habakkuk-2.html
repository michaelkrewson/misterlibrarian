The answer, and it begins with a watchman: the prophet posts himself on the rampart to wait, and is told to write the vision on tablets, plainly, because it will come late and will not lie. The proud man’s soul is puffed up, ‘but the righteous will live by his faithfulness’ (the line Paul quotes twice and Hebrews once). Then five woes against the plunderer, each in the mouths of his own victims — the creditor who is a biter, the nest on high, the city built on blood, the neighbor made drunk, the god of wood — with the whole earth filled with the knowledge of Jehovah’s glory in the middle and a single command at the end: hush.
c. 605 BC, the same moment as chapter 1: the Chaldeans are rising, Jerusalem is still standing, and its temple is the place where Jehovah is said to be (2:20). Nothing in the chapter dates the oracle more closely; its taunts are aimed at an unnamed plunderer whose empire the prophet has already named, in 1:6.

Translator’s Notes

Verse 1 · Habakkuk 2:1 — the watchpost: is the prophet waiting to answer a complaint, or a rebuke?

⚠ The prophet turns sentry. Chapter 1 ended on a question left in the air (1:17), and the chapter opens with the man who asked it posting himself to wait. Mishmeret is a watch, the post a sentry keeps, and matsor is a fortified place, a rampart or tower; the picture is of a guard who will not leave his station until something comes up the road. Habakkuk is not told to wait. He decides to, which is a quiet reversal: the man who cried “how long?” now stands still.

⚠ The last word of the verse will not settle. Tochachti is the noun tochachah, “reproof, rebuke, argument,” with my on it, and the suffix can point either way: the reproof I have delivered (my complaint) or the reproof aimed at me. The shelf splits down the middle. ASV “concerning my complaint,” NIV “this complaint,” TLB “my complaint”; KJV “when I am reproved,” DRB “to him that reproveth me,” Geneva “to him that rebuketh me,” NWT 1984 “at the reproof of me.” This translation prints the second, which keeps the Hebrew’s own openness: whichever way the suffix points, the prophet is waiting to hear how his complaint will be received.

“What he will say to me” is bi, literally “in me,” and the shelf takes it as “to me” (KJV “what he will say unto me”), “with me” (ASV “what he will speak with me”), or “by means of me” (NWT 1984 “what he will speak by me”). The first is the smoothest English and loses nothing the Hebrew insists on.

Verses 2–3 · Habakkuk 2:2–3 — “write the vision … so that one who reads it may run”: who runs, and why a vision that is late?

⚠ The answer begins with an order to write. Before God says one word of what the vision is, he tells the prophet how to keep it: ketov chazon, “write the vision,” and chazon is the noun for what the book’s own title says Habakkuk saw (chazah, 1:1). The vision that arrived as an argument is to be set down so that it outlasts the argument.

⚠ “Make it plain” is a verb with only three homes. Ba’er, to make plain, is the verb of two verses in Deuteronomy: Moses “undertook to make this instruction plain” (1:5), and the law is to be written on the stones of Ebal “plainly and thoroughly” (27:8); this verse is the third and last. The letters also spell be’er, a well, which is why a consonantal search returns dozens of verses; only these three are the verb, read hit by hit. Moses’s verb for expounding the law now lands on a prophet’s tablets. KJV and DRB “make it plain upon tables,” NWT 1984 “set [it] out plainly upon tablets.”

“The tablets” is ha-luchot, with the article, the plural of the noun that names the stone tablets Moses is promised at Sinai (luchot ha-even, Exodus 24:12). The article may only mean “the tablets one writes on,” and the Hebrew does not say they are those; but the word is the same, and a reader who has come through the Torah will hear it.

⚠ “So that one who reads it may run” says less than it seems to. The Hebrew is yarutz qore bo, “may run, one reading in it,” and the sentence does not say whether the runner is the reader or the courier. Three readings are on the shelf. The reader runs as he reads, meaning the message is large and clear enough to take in at a run: KJV “that he may run that readeth it,” and the NWT 1984 “the one reading aloud from it may do so fluently.” The reader runs with it as a herald: NIV “so that a herald may run with it.” Or both: TLB “so that anyone can read it at a glance and rush to tell the others.” This translation prints the Hebrew as it stands and does not choose.

⚠ “It speaks of the end and does not lie”: a witness’s verb. Yafeach is built on puach, “to breathe out,” and elsewhere it is what a witness does with his breath: yafiach. Ed emunim lo yechazzev, veyafiach kezavim ed shaqer, Proverbs 14:5: a faithful witness does not lie, a false witness breathes out lies; and Proverbs 12:17 has yafiach emunah, “he breathes out faithfulness”. Habakkuk uses the same pair in the next breath: the vision yafeach and lo yechazzev, “does not lie,” and in verse 4 the word emunah arrives. The vision is being described in a witness’s vocabulary. KJV “at the end it shall speak, and not lie,” NIV “it speaks of the end and will not prove false”; ASV “it hasteth toward the end” and NWT 1984 “it keeps panting on to the end” take the breath as a runner’s, which suits the running of verse 2; DRB “it shall appear at the end.”

⚠ “The appointed time” and the linger. The vision is still for the mo’ed, the appointed time, this translation’s fixed rendering, and it will not be rushed. Yitmahmah, “it lingers,” is the verb of Lot at the gates of Sodom, who “lingered” until the men took him by the hand (Genesis 19:16). The command that follows is two words, chakkeh lo, “wait for it,” and the promise is a doubled verb, bo yavo, “coming, it will come.” Hebrews 10:37 adds an article to the Greek of that doubled verb, so that the vision becomes a person, “the one who is coming,” and the note at Hebrews 10 lays out the three changes the Greek makes to these two verses.

Verse 4 · Habakkuk 2:4 — “the righteous shall live by faith”: what the Hebrew emunah means, and what Paul’s Greek changes

⚠ The verse is a contrast, and its first half is a rare verb. “Look — puffed up, not upright, is his soul within him.” Upplah is the passive of ofal, to swell up; as a verb it is found in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and Numbers 14:44, where Israel, told to turn back, “pushed on” up the mountain without the ark. The consonants turn up in sixteen verses, but fourteen are two nouns, the Ophel, a fortified mound (Jerusalem’s in most of its verses), and the tumours of Deuteronomy 28 and 1 Samuel; the hits were read one by one. The swollen soul is the same nefesh that will be opened as wide as Sheol in the next verse and that the builder of verse 10 sins against: the chapter follows one soul from swelling to appetite to loss.

⚠ “Faithfulness” is the sense of emunah, and the shelf is split on it. The word comes from aman, to be firm, the root of “amen,” and it means steadiness before it means belief. Its most visible picture is Moses on the hill at Rephidim: when Aaron and Hur held up his hands, “his hands were emunah until the sun went down” (Exodus 17:12), steady, unwavering, still up. In Habakkuk the verse sits between a vision that tarries (verse 3, “wait for it”) and a proud man who will not wait; the righteous one is the one whose hands stay up. NIV “the righteous person will live by his faithfulness” and NWT 1984 “by his faithfulness he will keep living” keep the word; KJV “the just shall live by his faith,” ASV “the righteous shall live by his faith,” Geneva “the just shall live by his faith,” DRB “the just shall live in his faith” give the familiar form; TLB “the righteous man trusts in me and lives” is a paraphrase that changes whose trust it is.

⚠ Four texts, four pronouns. The Hebrew has be’emunato, “by his faithfulness,” the righteous person’s own. The Greek Old Testament has “by my faith” (ek pisteos mou), and the notes at Galatians 3:11 and Hebrews 10:38 lay out what each does with it: Paul quotes the line with no pronoun at all, at Galatians 3:11 and at Romans 1:17, and Hebrews moves the “my” onto the righteous one. Whether “by faith” belongs with “the righteous” or with “will live” is a question the Greek leaves open and the Hebrew does not raise: the Hebrew order puts by his faithfulness before will live, and that is the order printed here.

⚠ A second ancient reader. The Dead Sea commentary on Habakkuk (1QpHab) is reported, in the standard translations, to quote this line in column 7 and to say in column 8 that it concerns those who keep the law in the house of Judah, whom God will rescue because of their toil and their faith in the Teacher of Righteousness. Read that way, the faithfulness is loyalty to a person and a community. Paul, in the next century, reads the same four words as the ground of belonging to God before the law. The Hebrew sentence is short enough that both readers found a place to stand.

The scroll sets this verse apart. A closed paragraph break (setumah) falls right after it, closing the first speech; the woes begin after the break.

Verses 5–8 · Habakkuk 2:5–8 — “wine betrays”, an appetite as wide as Sheol, and the first woe

⚠ “Wine betrays” borrows the prophet’s own word. Boged, treacherous, is the word of Habakkuk’s accusation at 1:13, “why do you look on the bogedim”; the drink here is a traitor the way the Chaldeans were. NWT 1984 “because the wine is dealing treacherously,” NIV “wine betrays him,” DRB “as wine deceiveth him that drinketh it.” Scholars report that the Qumran commentary has hon, “wealth,” where the Masoretic text has yayin, “wine,” and that the Septuagint and the Peshitta do not mention wine either; the reading printed here is the Masoretic one, and the arrogant man who is made unsteady by wine fits the drinking scenes at the end of the chapter.

The arrogant man “never settles.” Yahir, arrogant, haughty, is found in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and Proverbs 21:24 (“proud, haughty, scoffer is his name,&rdquo). Yinveh is the harder word, from navah, to dwell or to be at home, and the shelf takes it three ways: KJV “neither keepeth at home” and ASV “that keepeth not at home” read it literally; NIV “never at rest” reads it as restlessness; NWT 1984 “he will not reach his goal.” This translation prints “never settles,” which holds the first two.

⚠ An appetite as wide as Sheol, and the net of chapter 1 again. The man “has opened wide his nefesh” like Sheol: the same two words that Isaiah 5:14 puts the other way round, hirchivah she’ol nafshah, “Sheol has opened wide her appetite.” Nefesh is the soul that swelled in verse 4 and also the throat and the hunger; the proud man is a throat. And “he gathers to himself all the nations” is va-ye’esof, the verb of the net at 1:15, where the fisherman “gathers them in his dragnet.” NWT 1984 “made his soul spacious just like Sheol”; KJV “enlargeth his desire as hell”; NIV “as greedy as the grave.”

⚠ Three words for a taunt. Mashal, melitsah, chidot: a proverb, a taunt, riddles. Proverbs 1:6 lists the same three among wisdom’s tools, “to understand a proverb and a parable, the words of the wise and their riddles” (the “parable” there is melitsah, a figurative saying), and here the same equipment is turned into a jeer. What they say is a hoy, a woe, and the woe begins a list: the chapter has five, at verses 6, 9, 12, 15 and 19. And inside the first the victims ask a question: ad-matai, “how long!” It is not Habakkuk’s own ad-anah of 1:2, but it is the same question, now in the mouths of the plundered.

“Debts” is one word, found once. Avtit stands in this verse only. One traditional reading divides it av-tit, “thick clay,” and the KJV, Geneva and DRB have “thick clay”; the ASV “pledges” and the NWT 1984 “making debt heavy against himself” read it from the verb avat, to take a pledge, as in Deuteronomy 15:6, where Israel will “lend to many nations” with a verb from the same root, the pledge-taker’s verb. Avtit is printed as debts because the next verse brings the lenders.

⚠ “Those who bite you.” Noshekhekha is the participle of nashakh, to bite, and the noun from that root is neshekh, interest, which the law forbids charging a brother (Deuteronomy 23:20). The prophet is punning on a creditor and a snake, and the English has to choose. KJV, ASV and DRB “bite thee” keep the animal, and the NIV “your creditors” and NWT 1984 “those claiming interest of you” keep the loan; this translation prints the bite and trusts the reader to hear the debt. The verb that follows, mezaz’ekha, “those who shake you violently,” is the creditors’ other hand.

Plunder for plunder. Shalota and yeshaluka are one verb on both sides of the sentence, “you plundered … they will plunder you,” and that cognate is kept. Yeter is “what is left over”: all the peoples the conqueror left standing. Then the line that closes the first woe: mi-dmei adam va-chamas-eretz, qiryah ve-khol-yoshvei vah, “because of the blood of humankind and the violence done to the land, to the city, and to all who live in it.” It is chamas, the violence that filled the earth before the flood and that Habakkuk accused his own Judah of at 1:2, and it will come back word for word at verse 17.

The scroll’s paragraph breaks. After this woe falls an open break (petuchah), and the Masoretic breaks of the chapter then fall after verses 11, 14, 18 and 20, each time at the end of a closing verdict: the stone that cries out, the earth filled with knowledge, the idol that profits nothing, the earth made silent.

Verses 9–11 · Habakkuk 2:9–11 — “woe to him who sets his nest on high”, and the stone that cries out from the wall

⚠ “Gains evil gain” is a proverb’s own phrase. Botse’a betsa is the cognate pair Proverbs uses for a man who profits by cutting: “such are the paths of everyone greedy for gain: it takes away the life of its owner” (Proverbs 1:19), and Proverbs 15:27 has the same two words with a house in them: okher beito botse’a betsa, “he who gains gain troubles his own house.” Habakkuk’s phrase lands in the middle: the gain is “for his house,” and the verdict, in verse 10, is shame “for your house.” KJV “coveteth an evil covetousness,” ASV “getteth an evil gain,” NIV “builds his house by unjust gain.”

⚠ The nest on high is Edom’s. Lasum bamarom qinno, “to set his nest on high,” is almost the phrase of Obadiah 4, where Edom is told that if it sets its nest among the stars, “from there I will bring you down.” The man who wants to be “saved from the grasp of evil” by building high is the cliff-dweller of Obadiah, and the same answer waits for him.

“You sin against your own soul.” Chote nafshekha is the third time the chapter turns on nefesh: swollen (verse 4), opened wide as Sheol (verse 5), and now forfeited. Chata is the verb of sinning, and also of missing a mark; the man who counseled shame for his house has missed his own life. KJV “hast sinned against thy soul,” NWT 1984 “your soul is sinning,” NIV “forfeiting your life.” Qetzot ammim rabbim, “cutting off many peoples,” can also be read “the ends of many peoples”; the verb sense is the one the translations take.

⚠ The stone cries out, and the beam answers. Tiz’aq, “it will cry out,” is the verb of the prophet’s own complaint at 1:2, ez’aq elekha chamas, “I cry to you, ‘Violence!’” — and what he heard back was “you do not hear.” Now the house itself cries out in the same verb, and something does answer: ya’anenah, the verb of the first line of God’s reply at verse 2. The cry from the wall is not Abel’s blood crying from the ground (Genesis 4:10 uses a different verb, tsa’aq), but it is its nearest relative: an outcry of the wronged, now given to the building materials. Kafis, the “rafter,” stands in this verse only; the shelf gives it as a beam, a rafter, or the timber itself: KJV “the beam out of the timber,” NWT 1984 “a rafter itself will answer it,” NIV “the beams of the woodwork will echo it,” DRB “the timber that is between the joints of the building.”

Luke gives the stones a different cry. At Luke 19:40 Jesus answers the Pharisees who want his disciples silenced: if they were quiet, the stones would cry out. In Habakkuk the stones testify against a man who built his house on violence; in Luke they would testify for the one who comes in Jehovah’s name. The image is the same and the verdict is reversed.

Verses 12–14 · Habakkuk 2:12–14 — “woe to him who builds a city with bloodshed”, and “the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of Jehovah”

⚠ Micah’s line, with the city left unnamed. Boneh ir bedamim, vechonen qiryah be’avlah, “builds a city with bloodshed, and founds a town on injustice,” is almost word for word Micah 3:10, boneh Tsiyon bedamim, vi-Yerushalayim be’avlah, “who build Zion with bloodshed and Jerusalem with injustice.” Micah names the city; Habakkuk, with the Chaldeans in view, leaves it unnamed, and the unnamed city is large enough to be Babylon or Jerusalem. Damim is the plural of dam, blood, and in this sense means bloodshed. KJV “buildeth a town with blood, and stablisheth a city by iniquity,” ASV the same with “establisheth.”

⚠ “Is it not from Jehovah of hosts”: the empire’s labor goes up in smoke. The Hebrew has yig’u ammim bedei-esh, ul’ummim bedei-riq ye’afu, “peoples labor bedei fire, and nations bedei nothing grow weary.” Dei is “enough”: they labor only as much as the fire needs, for nothing more than fuel. The line returns in Jeremiah 51:58 with its two halves swapped, bedei-riq … bedei-esh, and there the fire is Babylon’s own, the broad wall leveled and the high gates burned. NWT 1984 “peoples will toil on only for the fire,” ASV “the peoples labor for the fire,” NIV “the people’s labor is only fuel for the fire,” KJV “shall labour in the very fire.” The word hinneh (“look”) turns the sentence into a pointing finger, and the pointing is at Jehovah of hosts: the nations’ labor for the fire is not an accident of empire but something done under his hand.

⚠ The verse that fills the earth. Ki timmale ha’aretz lada’at et-kevod Yehovah, kamayim yekhassu al-yam: “the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of Jehovah, as the waters cover the sea.” Two earlier verses are in it. In Numbers 14:21, after the scouts’ rebellion, the words as I live are joined to the glory of Jehovah filling all the earth; it comes at the worst moment of the wilderness and promises the opposite of its ruin. And Isaiah 11:9 has the earth is full of the knowledge of Jehovah, as the waters cover the sea. Habakkuk puts the glory and the knowledge and the water together, and puts them in the middle of five woes against a bloody empire. The Hebrew has an infinitive, “filled to know,” so the earth fills in order to know the glory (kavod, weight); knowledge (da’at) is the word Isaiah uses. NWT 1984 “filled with the knowing of the glory of Jehovah,” DRB “filled, that men may know the glory of the Lord,” ASV “filled with the knowledge of the glory of Jehovah, as the waters cover the sea.”

Verses 15–17 · Habakkuk 2:15–17 — the cup that comes around: “drink, you too”, the nakedness of the drunk, and the violence done to Lebanon

⚠ The drunk, uncovered. Noah “drank some of the wine and got drunk, and he lay uncovered inside his tent” (Genesis 9:21), and what his son did with the sight is the story. Habakkuk’s woe is about a man who engineers the scene: he gives his neighbor drink, makes him drunk, “so as to look on their nakedness” (me’oreihem, a form found only in this verse). The grammar slips from “him” to “their” mid-sentence, and the translation does not smooth it. ASV and KJV “that thou mayest look on their nakedness.”

⚠ “Your venom” is the word for rage. Chamatkha is chemah, heat, fury, and also poison: in the Song of Moses the wine of the wicked “is the venom of serpents” (chamat) (Deuteronomy 32:33). The man is not only pouring wine; he is pouring his own fury into it. The shelf does not agree on what: ASV “addest thy venom,” NWT 1984 “attaching [to it] your rage and anger,” DRB “presenteth his gall,” KJV “puttest thy bottle to him” and NIV “pouring it from the wineskin” read a skin or flask instead of rage. Venom is printed because the Song of Moses supports it and the context is poisoned drink.

⚠ “Sated with dishonor instead of glory”: weight and lightness. Qalon, dishonor, is built on qalal, to be light, and it is set against kavod, glory, which is built on kaved, heavy. The verse swaps a heavy name for a light one. Then the sentence turns on the drunk himself: shteh gam-atta ve-he’arel, “drink, you too, and show yourself uncircumcised!” Arel is the word the books of Judges and Samuel use for the Philistines, the neighbor that did not practise circumcision, and the command is to be exposed as exactly that. KJV “let thy foreskin be uncovered,” ASV “be as one uncircumcised,” NWT 1984 “be considered uncircumcised”; NIV “let your nakedness be exposed.” Scholars report that the Qumran commentary (column 11) quotes the word as ve-hera’el, “and stagger,” with two letters transposed, and that the Septuagint reads “be shaken, and tremble”; the Masoretic reading is printed here, and the staggering is the sense the next clause confirms.

⚠ The cup comes around. Kos yemin Yehovah, “the cup of Jehovah’s right hand,” tissov alekha, goes around, as a cup passes at a table, until it reaches the one who passed it to others. It is the cup of Jeremiah 25:15, the cup of the wine of fury (ha-chemah) that Jeremiah is told to make all the nations drink; at 25:27 they are told to drink, and be drunk, and vomit (uqyu), and fall. The cup that the drunken conqueror forced on his neighbors has become the cup Jehovah passes to him.

⚠ “Shameful vomit” is a word found once. Qiqalon stands in this verse only. It is either qi, vomit, plus qalon, shame (a drunk’s word, as in Jeremiah 25:27 above and Isaiah 28:8, “all the tables are full of vomit,&rdquo), or qalon doubled for emphasis, “shame upon shame.” Three versions on the English shelf read the vomit: KJV and Geneva “shameful spewing,” DRB “shameful vomiting”; the ASV “foul shame,” NWT 1984 “disgrace” and NIV “disgrace” read the shame alone. The word is placed to answer “sated with qalon” at the start of the verse, and in a chapter of drink the vomit is the likelier sense.

⚠ Lebanon, and a line said twice. Chamas Levanon, “the violence done to Lebanon,” is a deforestation: Lebanon is the land of the cedar, the timber felled for Solomon’s temple (1 Kings 5), and the violence done to it is the felling of its forests. The verb is yekhasekha, “will cover you,” the same verb as the waters that “cover” the sea at verse 14: the knowledge of God covers the earth as water does, and the violence the empire has done covers its doer the same way. The second half, yechitan, is ambiguous in its pronoun: “will terrify them,” the beasts the empire hunted (KJV “which made them afraid,” NWT 1984 “that terrifies them”), or “will terrify you” (NIV “your destruction of animals will terrify you”). And the line that closes verse 17 is the line that closed verse 8, word for word: mi-dmei adam va-chamas-eretz, qiryah ve-khol-yoshvei vah. The refrain at verses 8 and 17 brackets the three woes between them.

Verses 18–20 · Habakkuk 2:18–20 — an idol as “a teacher of lies”, and “Jehovah is in his holy temple: hush”

⚠ The idol is a teacher. Moreh sheqer, “a teacher of lies,” is built on the root of torah, “teaching,” and the next verse asks hu yoreh, “can it teach?” The question lands where chapter 1 left a wound: there, “the law grows numb” (1:4); here, the nation’s teacher is a carved and cast block. KJV “a teacher of lies,” NWT 1984 “an instructor in falsehood,” NIV “an image that teaches lies”; and at verse 19 ASV “Shall this teach?” and NWT 1984 “It itself will give instruction.”

⚠ “The maker trusts in what he has made”: two words from one verb. Yotser yitsro is a pun: the one who forms trusts in the thing he has formed. Yatsar is the verb of the potter, and the verb that formed the human from the dust of the ground (Genesis 2:7), where the next thing God does is breathe. The idol-maker uses the same verb and cannot do the next thing: verse 19 ends on ruach, “breath,” and says there is none in it.

“Worthless gods that cannot speak.” Elilim illemim is a sound-pun on the whole phrase. Elil is a worthless thing, a nothing, and sounds like elohim, “gods”; illem is dumb. The shelf: KJV and ASV “dumb idols,” NWT 1984 “valueless gods that are speechless,” NIV “idols that cannot speak.”

Waking a block of wood. Haqitsah, “wake up!” is from the verb of verse 7, where the creditors “wake” on their own (ve-yiqtsu). The god of wood has to be woken by a man who then expects it to teach. And tafus zahav vachesef, “plated with gold and silver,” says the god is a coating over a core with no breath in it.

⚠ “Hush”: the silence of the stone and the silence of the earth. The idol is dumam, silent stone, and the last line tells all the earth to be silent. Has is the bare hush a person says with a finger to the lips, and it is found as a bare interjection in six verses of the Hebrew Bible, three of them prophets telling a room to be quiet before Jehovah: here, Zephaniah 1:7 (“Hush before the Lord Jehovah! For the day of Jehovah is near”), and Zechariah 2:17 (“Be silent, all flesh, before Jehovah,” with the same word). The others are the king in Judges 3:19, sending his attendants out of the room before the assassin’s secret message, and Amos 6:10 and 8:3. Two silences stand side by side: a block that cannot speak, and a world that is told to stop. NWT 1984 “Keep silence before him, all the earth!”, KJV “let all the earth keep silence before him.”

Heikhal, “temple,” is also the word for a palace. The line says Jehovah is in his heikhal qodsho, his holy palace, and the picture is a throne room whose doors have just opened. The Jerusalem temple was standing when Habakkuk wrote (the first temple), and the Chaldean armies of chapter 1 were on their way.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “make it plain” for ba’er (v2), the verb of Moses’s speech and Ebal’s stones; “the appointed time” for mo’ed (v3); “puffed up” for upplah (v4), the swollen soul of Numbers 14:44; “faithfulness” for emunah (v4), the steadiness of Moses’s raised hands; “soul” for nefesh in verses 4 and 10, with the appetite of verse 5; “those who bite you” for noshekhekha (v7), the creditor-snake; “plunder / plunder you” for shalal kept as one verb (v8); “violence” for chamas (vv8, 17); “cover” for kasah (vv14, 17); “woe” for hoy at its five turns (vv6, 9, 12, 15, 19); “a teacher of lies” and “can it teach?” for moreh and yoreh (vv18–19); and “hush” for has (v20).

Echoes paid. The question left hanging at the end of chapter 1 now has a watchman waiting for its answer. Boged, “treacherous,” of 1:13, now the wine; the net’s asaf of 1:15, now the appetite that gathers all the nations; the prophet’s ez’aq of 1:2, now the stone in the wall; the numb torah of 1:4, now an idol that is called a teacher. Out of earlier books: ba’er of Deuteronomy 1:5 and 27:8; the verb of Numbers 14:44 set against emunah; the glory that fills the earth in Numbers 14:21; the nest on high of Obadiah 4; the gain that takes the life of its owners in Proverbs 1:19; the bite of Deuteronomy 23:20; Noah’s tent in Genesis 9:21; the venom of Deuteronomy 32:33; the cup of Jeremiah 25; the hush of Zephaniah 1:7 and Zechariah 2:17; and the three New Testament quotations at Romans 1:17, Galatians 3:11 and Hebrews 10:38, with the stones of Luke 19:40.

Echoes planted. Chapter 3, the prayer of Habakkuk, which says “Jehovah, I have heard your report, and I fear” and ends on the prophet standing in the dark with an empty fig tree and exulting anyway. Micah 3:10, the city built on bloodshed; Jeremiah 51:58, the peoples who labor for the fire; Isaiah 11:9, the knowledge of Jehovah as the waters cover the sea; Isaiah 5:14, Sheol opening wide her appetite; Amos 6:10 and 8:3, the other “hush”; and Proverbs 14:5 with its faithful witness who does not lie.
