Moses turns the forty years in the wilderness into an argument: Jehovah led, humbled and tested Israel with hunger and manna, so that the good land ahead — wheat and barley, vines and figs and pomegranates, iron in the hills and copper to mine — would be received as gift rather than achievement. A warning follows, structured as a trap: eat, be satisfied, build good houses, watch herds and silver multiply, and only then does the heart grow high and forget the God who gave it all, exactly as the nations before them are being destroyed for the same forgetting.
Still the plains of Moab, in the fortieth year (1:3); no new narrated event — the chapter continues Moses' second address without a break, immediately after chapter 7's command to devote the seven nations to destruction, and re-narrates the forty years now closing rather than reporting a new one.

Translator’s Notes

Verse 1 · every commandment, singular — a shelf split on the chapter’s very first word

★ The Hebrew opens kol ha-mitzvah, ‘the WHOLE commandment’ — a singular collective covering everything Moses is about to say, the same singular already governing Deuteronomy 6:1’s ‘this is the commandment’ (already on these pages). Reading straight down the shelf: ASV keeps the oddity, ‘All the commandment’; NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 both keep it singular, ‘every commandment’; so does NIV, ‘every command’. KJV pluralizes it outright, ‘All the commandmentS’, and Douay-Rheims and Geneva follow it there. ⚠ On the Spanish shelf the same seam runs through TIME rather than across versions: TNM 1987 keeps the collective, «todo mandamiento», and TNM 2019 moves to a plural partitive, «cada uno de los mandamientos» — naming the plural noun the 1987 edition kept folded into one. Five verbs follow in a chain — keep, do, live, multiply, go in and possess — the same live/multiply/possess sequence 6:3 already used.

Verses 2–3 · humbled and tested, twice over — and the sentence Jesus quotes back word for word

★ Two verbs, paired, and paired again fourteen verses later. Le-ma‘an annotkha le-nassotkha — ‘to humble you, to test you’ — is not one verb doing two jobs but two distinct roots standing side by side, the same pairing this translation has already met separately: anah is Egypt’s own affliction verb, run backward on the wilderness itself (Genesis 15:13, already on these pages); nasah is the root behind the place-name Massah, ‘Testing’ (Deuteronomy 6:16, already on these pages). Here the pair is not a place-name at all, just the plain verbs, side by side — and they recur, nearly verbatim, at v16 below.

★ ‘Not by bread alone does the human live’ — the most quoted sentence in the book so far. The Hebrew word is ha-adam, the human, humankind — rendered that way on this site since Genesis 1, and this is exactly the sentence Matthew 4:4 (already on these pages) has Jesus quote back at the tempter in the wilderness, forty days into his own fast. ⚠ One honest wrinkle, already flagged when that Gospel chapter shipped: the Hebrew says ‘by everything that goes out of the mouth of Jehovah’ — there is no noun ‘word’ anywhere in the clause, only the verb yatza, to go out, turned into the noun motza, an issuing, an utterance. Reading the shelf for exactly that noun: English splits three ways — KJV, NIV and Douay-Rheims all supply ‘word’ (the Douay by way of the Vulgate, itself following the Greek Old Testament’s rhēmati — the very noun Matthew’s own Gospel then quotes); NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 supply a different noun, ‘expression’; only ASV keeps the Hebrew noun-less, ‘everything that proceedeth’. ⚠ The Spanish shelf leans further toward the Hebrew’s own bareness than the English one does: RV 1909, RV60 and NVI all read «todo lo que sale de la boca», no noun at all — and TNM 2019 now joins them, dropping the noun TNM 1987 had supplied, «toda expresión de la boca». Another instance of the pattern the closing note below is tracking, though this one runs the OTHER direction — the later edition converging on the wider Spanish shelf’s own bareness rather than clarifying an image away. This translation follows the Hebrew’s own noun, motza, an issuing, rendered ‘utterance’ — matching Matthew 4’s own note on the same verse.

⚠ The Douay-Rheims takes the humbling a step further than any other witness on the shelf: ‘He afflicted thee WITH WANT’ — naming the hunger as the affliction’s own instrument, where the Hebrew simply runs three verbs in a row (he humbled you, and he made you hunger, and he fed you). A small addition, and it happens to be exactly right about the mechanism the sentence goes on to describe.

Verses 4–5 · a garment that did not wear out, and the verb behind a whole vocabulary of discipline

Two physical claims, stated flatly and never explained: the clothing did not age, the feet did not swell, across forty years of walking. Twelve of the thirteen witnesses stay just as plain; the one exception is TLB, which adds a detail the Hebrew does not supply — ‘your feet haven’t been BLISTERED or swollen’ — the paraphrase’s own habit of filling in a physical picture nothing else on the shelf offers.

★ Yeyasser…meyasserkha — ‘disciplines…disciplines you’ — is the verb behind this site’s own musar entry, written for Proverbs 1:2 and grounded there in exactly this image: ‘he who spares the rod hates his son’ (Proverbs 13:24, not yet on these pages), ‘do not despise the discipline of Jehovah’ (Proverbs 3:11, not yet on these pages, and the verse that supplies the parent for Proverbs’ own noun). This is the one place in the Torah’s own narrative where the metaphor Proverbs will build a whole vocabulary out of — a father’s corrective hand, aimed at a son — is spoken about God directly, in the plain past tense, of forty real years. ⚠ The shelf divides by REGISTER more than by meaning: KJV and ASV ‘chasteneth’, matching RV60’s «castiga» and RV 1909’s identical «castiga» — all four keep the severity; Douay-Rheims softens to ‘traineth up…trained thee up’ and Geneva to ‘nurtureth’, both losing the correction; NIV and NWT land in the middle, ‘disciplines’/‘corrects’; and TLB paraphrases furthest, ‘punishes you to help you’, supplying a purpose clause the Hebrew leaves the reader to infer.

Verses 6–9 · the seven species, and a metal that will meet a serpent nine verses on

★ Wheat, barley, vine, fig, pomegranate, olive oil, honey — this verse is the source text for what later Jewish tradition names the shivat ha-minim, the ‘seven species’ native to the land, still cited today (first-fruits liturgy, blessings recited over food, even the design of Israeli coinage). Nothing later is read back into the translation; the list is simply named here, once, in this order, and everything downstream takes its cue from these seven nouns.

⚠ Nechoshet, the metal dug from the hills at v9, splits the shelf three ways. Nine of the thirteen read COPPER: ASV, NWT (both editions), NIV, TNM (both editions), TLB, RV60 and NVI all agree. Three read BRASS: KJV, Douay-Rheims and Geneva — brass being an alloy with no clear attestation this early. And one, RV 1909, stays generic, ‘metal’ («cortarás metal»), naming neither. This translation follows the nine-witness majority. The same word gets a second life nine verses on: at v15 below the danger in that same wilderness is a saraph, a burning serpent — and the rescue Israel is eventually given for exactly that danger is a serpent made of this exact metal (Numbers 21:9, already on these pages, where the pun — nachash nechoshet, ‘a copper serpent’ — is in the consonants themselves). The blessing of v9 and the danger of v15 are made of the same word.

Verse 10 · eat, be satisfied, and BLESS — a blessing said after the meal, not before it

★ This verse is the source text later Jewish law cites for the obligation to say a blessing AFTER eating — reported, not verified from this project’s own sources: the Talmud, tractate Berakhot 48b, is standardly cited as deriving the obligation from here directly — Birkat Hamazon, ‘the Blessing of the Food’, said once the meal is over rather than the more familiar blessing spoken before it. ⚠ The verb matters to that tradition: it is barakh, ‘to bless’, the same verb this project has tracked since Genesis, and reading the shelf for it specifically shows a real divide. KJV, ASV, Douay-Rheims, Geneva, TLB, RV 1909 and RV60 all keep ‘bless’/«bendecirás»; NIV, NVI, NWT 2013 and TNM 2019 all move to ‘praise’/«alabarás» — where NWT 1984 and TNM 1987 both still read ‘bless’/«bendecir». Another instance, on both shelves at once, of the pattern the closing note below is tracking — and here the swap has a real cost: the technical name of the tradition this verse anchors is literally ‘the BLESSING of the food’, not its praise.

Verses 11–14 · forget, four times in three verses — matching the rest of the book so far, combined

★ Shakach, ‘to forget’, checked across the whole archived Hebrew of Deuteronomy, stands in thirteen verses total — and two of its occurrences land in these four verses alone, at v11 and v14. Before this chapter the book had used it exactly four times, once each (4:9, 4:23, 4:31, 6:12, all already on these pages). Verse 19 below adds a doubled third occurrence, an emphatic Hebrew construction (‘if FORGETTING you forget’), discussed in its own note — so this one chapter, in three verses total, matches the entire rest of the book’s use of the word up to here.

★ The structure is a trap sprung in slow motion. V11 states the danger; vv12–13 list, in careful sequence, exactly what triggers it — eating and being satisfied, building good houses, herds and flocks multiplying, silver and gold multiplying — and only then, once every condition is met, v14 springs the verb: ‘then your heart grows high, and you forget’. The warning is not against poverty. It is against the exact prosperity the chapter has just spent nine verses promising.

⚠ Ve-ram levavekha, ‘your heart grows high’, is rendered literally here to preserve an image the later editions on both shelves let go. KJV, ASV, Douay-Rheims and NWT 1984 all keep the heart ‘lifted up’; NWT 2013 moves to ‘do not let your heart become PROUD’, and TNM 2019 matches it, «no dejes que tu corazón se vuelva ORGULLOSO», against TNM 1987’s still-literal «tu corazón realmente se eleve». Another instance of the pattern below, on both shelves at once. Even the older Spanish witnesses split on the same seam a generation earlier than the TNM did: RV 1909 keeps the image, «se eleve luego tu corazón», where RV60 already reads «se enorgullezca tu corazón» — the same clarifying move, a full half-century before either NWT/TNM edition made it.

Verses 15–16 · the chapter quotes itself

★★ Read against vv2–3, these two verses are close to a word-for-word repeat of the chapter’s own opening. V2–3: le-ma‘an annotkha le-nassotkha…va-ya‘akhelkha et ha-man asher lo yadata ve-lo yad‘un avotekha — ‘to humble you, to test you…and he fed you the manna, which you did not know and your fathers did not know’. V15–16: ha-ma‘akhilkha man ba-midbar asher lo yad‘un avotekha, le-ma‘an annotkha u-lema‘an nassotekha — ‘who fed you manna in the wilderness, which your fathers did not know, to humble you and to test you’. Same two verbs, same withheld knowledge, same food, the order of clauses reshuffled and ‘which YOU did not know’ quietly dropped the second time. The chapter that opened by telling Israel to remember the wilderness closes by re-narrating it, in its own earlier words, as Exhibit A against forgetting — the form of the sentence enacting the content of the warning.

★ Nachash saraph, ‘fiery serpent’, at v15 — checked against the whole archived Hebrew Bible, this exact two-word collocation occurs in exactly one verse: this one. Its only relative is the definite plural at Numbers 21:6 (already on these pages), ‘the burning serpents’ Jehovah sends among the people for their own grumbling — the incident this verse is looking back on. This translation keeps ‘fiery’ specifically to preserve that link; the shelf splits on exactly this word. KJV, ASV, Geneva and RV60 («serpientes ardientes») all keep the burning image; NWT and TNM both move to ‘poisonous’/«venenosas» — a real hazard, but a different one, and it severs the very echo this note is built on; TLB drops the image entirely, ‘dangerous snakes’. ⚠ The Douay-Rheims goes furthest of all, translating the Vulgate’s own elaboration rather than the Hebrew: ‘the serpent BURNING WITH HIS BREATH’ — a detail neither the Hebrew nor any other witness on either shelf supplies.

Verses 17–18 · ‘my own power’ — and the verb Jehovah takes back

★ The error the chapter is built to head off is stated in one sentence and answered in the next: ‘My power and the strength of my hand have made me this wealth’ (v17), against ‘it is HE who gives you power to make wealth’ (v18). The Hebrew repeats koach, ‘power/strength’, in both verses — the man claims it as his own possession, and the text hands the same noun straight back to its source without ever disputing that the power is real. Nothing in the rebuttal denies the wealth was earned by real strength; it denies only where that strength came from.

⚠ The purpose clause closing v18 — ‘so that he may establish his covenant…as at this day’ — makes the whole forty-year discipline retroactively purposeful: the humbling of vv2–3 and the manna of v16 were not punishment for its own sake but the mechanism by which a people who might otherwise credit themselves are kept able to receive a promise that was never theirs to earn.

Verses 19–20 · perish, four times over — and a sentence this book has used before, almost word for word

★★ The root avad, ‘to perish, to destroy’, is doubled twice in two verses — avod tovedun at v19 (an infinitive absolute plus the verb, ‘perishing you shall perish’) and ma‘avid…tovedun at v20 — four occurrences in two verses. And v19’s exact wording is not new to this book: ha‘idoti vakhem hayom, ki avod tovedun, ‘I bear witness against you today that you shall surely perish’, is close to verbatim what Deuteronomy 4:26 (already on these pages) already said, with exactly one change: that verse calls heaven and earth to witness (‘I call the heavens and the earth to witness against you today’) and adds ‘quickly’; this one drops both, Moses himself standing as the sole witness this time. The same formula, stripped once.

★ Eqev, ‘on the heel of’, closes the chapter exactly where the last one opened it — only inverted. Checked against the whole archived Hebrew of Deuteronomy for the word itself (not merely the letters, which also spell ‘Jacob’), eqev stands as a bare word in exactly two verses in this book: Deuteronomy 7:12, ‘on the heel of your listening’ — the verse that names the week of the Torah’s annual reading cycle, parashat Eikev, which runs from that verse through this one and beyond — and this verse, ‘on the heel of your NOT listening’. The last chapter promised the covenant would follow obedience like a heel follows a foot; this one closes the same image in reverse, inside the very reading-week the word names.

⚠ This is not the last time the doubled ‘surely perish’ formula recurs in the book (it returns again at Deuteronomy 30:18, not yet on these pages) — but the pairing with Deuteronomy 4:26 is the tightest, sharing the largest block of identical wording of any two instances checked.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew, English and Spanish all number this chapter identically at 20 verses — checked against every fetched witness on both shelves, with one merge and no splits anywhere: TLB folds vv12–13 into one printed unit, ‘12–13’, the same habit this project has already noted in that paraphrase elsewhere. Three renderings are departures worth naming in their own notes: ‘the human’ for the generic adam at v3, matching the rendering already fixed at Genesis 1 and quoted back at Matthew 4:4; ‘utterance’ for motza at the same verse, keeping the Hebrew’s own noun rather than importing ‘word’ from the Greek New Testament; and ‘your heart grows high’ at v14, printing the literal image most of the shelf’s later editions clarify into ‘proud’. ‘Jehovah’ for the divine name, ‘statutes and ordinances’ for the paired legal nouns, and ‘fear’ for yirah all hold from earlier chapters unchanged. Verse and note anchors follow the canonical v8-N/n8-N form.

Library. Three new dictionary entries, each in both languages: shakach (‘to forget’, the chapter’s own central verb), abad (‘to perish’, distinguished from shamad and cherem, both already entered from Deuteronomy 7), and shivat ha-minim, the seven species named at v8. Five existing entries extended, and two long-standing Spanish parity gaps closed in the same pass: anah and massah-meribah (the humble/test doublet of v2, recurring at v16); musar, whose noun-form entry (written for Proverbs) now carries the verb it comes from, spoken here directly of God; man, whose Exodus 16 entry now carries this chapter’s own theological reinterpretation of the same substance; and eqev, whose Deuteronomy 7:12 entry now carries this chapter’s own inverted echo. Anah and musar had English entries with no Spanish twin at all until now — both closed in passing, not created as separate work. No new encyclopedia entries: this chapter names no new person or place, so VIDEO_QUEUE was not checked.

Echoes paid. Deuteronomy 7:12’s eqev, answered at v20 with the direction of the listening reversed (n8-19) — the payoff Deuteronomy 7’s own closing note asked to be watched for. Deuteronomy 6:16’s massah, its third appearance on these pages (n8-2). Deuteronomy 4:26’s doubled ‘surely perish’, repeated at v19 minus the summoned heavens and earth (n8-19). Exodus 16’s manna, reinterpreted here as a deliberate test rather than a simple provision (n8-2, n8-15). Numbers 11:7–9’s physical description of the manna, presupposed by this chapter’s theological one (n8-15). Numbers 21:6’s fiery serpents, recalled at v15 in the one other verse in the Hebrew Bible that pairs the same two words (n8-15). Numbers 21:9’s copper serpent, sharing its metal with the mining promised at v9 (n8-6). And Matthew 4:4, which quotes v3 directly — the cross-reference now runs both ways, and Matthew 4’s own note, which had cited ‘Deuteronomy 8:2’ and ‘8:3’ as bare references, now links them.

Patterns worth carrying forward. ★ The self-quotation at the chapter’s own center is the best find of this chapter, and it is a new SHAPE for an old habit. Deuteronomy 7’s closing note tracked the book quoting OTHER passages at close range, changing exactly one thing each time. This chapter does that too — v19 restages Deuteronomy 4:26 minus the summoned heavens and earth — but its sharper instance is quoting ITSELF: vv15–16 restage vv2–3 almost word for word, reshuffled and thinned by exactly one clause (‘which YOU did not know’ dropped the second time). Worth watching whether a chapter quoting its own opening at its close turns out to be a Deuteronomy habit or a one-off. ⚠ The shelf’s 1984/1987-versus-2013/2019 split, tracked deliberately since Deuteronomy 6, added FOUR more instances here: the singular/plural of ‘commandment’ at v1 (Spanish only); ‘bless’ becoming ‘praise’ at v10 (both shelves, and NVI makes the same move independently); ‘your heart grows high’ becoming ‘proud’ at v14 (both shelves, and RV60 made the identical move against RV 1909 half a century earlier); and, running the OTHER direction, the bread-alone clause at v3, where TNM 2019 drops a supplied noun its own 1987 edition had added, converging toward the wider Spanish shelf’s bareness rather than away from an image. Running total across the three chapters that have counted this deliberately: fifteen places — Deuteronomy 6’s four, Deuteronomy 7’s seven, and this chapter’s four. ★ Deuteronomy 7 asked for one more chapter’s count before calling the pattern settled; this is that chapter, and the honest verdict is a real majority with two real exceptions now on the books, not an exceptionless rule: Deuteronomy 7 already found the 2013/2019 editions closer to the Hebrew at natan (v2, v23) and in full agreement with the rest of the shelf at v20; this chapter adds the bread-alone clause as a second counter-example, and on a headline verse, not a minor one. The pattern is real and worth continuing to name case by case — but it is a tendency the later editions show, not a rule they follow.

Next installment in this book: Deuteronomy 9 — the sermon turns from warning to correction. Moses tells Israel plainly that the land is not being given for their own righteousness but for the wickedness of the nations they are displacing and the oath sworn to the patriarchs, then retells the one story that proves it: the golden calf, the tablets broken at the foot of the mountain, and a fast of his own that runs forty days and forty nights without bread or water — the same formula this chapter’s own bread-alone verse will meet again, this time on the mountain rather than in the wilderness.
