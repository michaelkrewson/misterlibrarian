Translator’s Notes

Verse 1 · masters, what is just and equal, and a Master in heaven

⚠ “Masters, grant your slaves what is just and equal.” This verse ends the household instructions of 3:18–25; the chapter division, added many centuries later, falls in the middle of them, so the slaves’ instruction and the masters’ stand on different pages of most Bibles. “Masters … a Master in heaven” is one Greek word, kyrios, used of both, as at Ephesians 6:9, “both their Master and yours is in the heavens.” Isotēs, “equal,” stands in three verses: 2 Corinthians 8:13 and 2 Corinthians 8:14, “by equality … and there may be equality,” and this one (dictionary). Most readers take it here as fairness, fair treatment; others hold to “equality,” the standing of men who serve one Master. This translation keeps “equal,” which can carry either, and does not choose. For how the household verses have been used, see the notes at Colossians 3:22 and Ephesians 6:5. The KJV reads “give unto your servants that which is just and equal”; the NIV reads “provide your slaves with what is right and fair”; the NWT 1984 reads “keep dealing out what is righteous and what is fair to [your] slaves.”

Verses 2–6 · persevere in prayer, a door for the word, chains, buying out the time, and speech seasoned with salt

“Persevere in prayer, keeping watch in it with thanksgiving.” Proskartereō is the “persevering in prayer” of Romans 12:12 and the “devoting themselves … to prayer” of Acts 1:14 (dictionary). “Keep watch” is Jesus’ word in Gethsemane, “Keep watch and pray” (Mark 14:38). Thanks comes back again, after 3:15–17. The KJV reads “Continue in prayer, and watch in the same with thanksgiving”; the NIV reads “Devote yourselves to prayer, being watchful and thankful”; the NWT 1984 reads “Be persevering in prayer, remaining awake in it with thanksgiving.”

“That God may open to us a door for the word … for which I am also in chains.” The open door is Paul’s figure at 1 Corinthians 16:9, “a great and effective door has opened to me,” and 2 Corinthians 2:12; at Acts 14:27 God “opened to the nations a door of faith.” He asks for a door while he sits behind one. “The mystery of Christ” is the mystery “made plain” at Colossians 1:26, and “make it plain” in v4 is the same verb. “I am in chains” (dedemai, “I have been bound”) and “my chains” in v18 (desmoi) are one root. The KJV reads “a door of utterance”; the ASV reads “a door for the word”; the NIV reads “a door for our message.”

“Walk in wisdom toward those outside, buying out the time.” “Buying out the time” is the phrase of Ephesians 5:16 (dictionary); kairos is the right moment rather than time in general. “Those outside” are those not in the congregation, as at 1 Corinthians 5:12, “to judge those outside.” The KJV reads “redeeming the time”; the NWT 1984 reads “buying out the opportune time for yourselves”; the NIV reads “make the most of every opportunity.”

“Let your speech always be with grace, seasoned with salt.” Artyō, “season,” stands in three verses: Mark 9:50, “with what will you season it?”, Luke 14:34, and this one (dictionary). Readers take the salt as the savour that makes speech worth hearing, or as what keeps it from going bad, the “rotten word” of Ephesians 4:29; or both. “Grace” is the word of the letter’s greeting and its last line. The KJV reads “Let your speech be alway with grace, seasoned with salt”; the Geneva reads “powdered with salt”; the TLB reads “Let your conversation be gracious as well as sensible.”

Verses 7–9 · Tychicus, a passage shared with Ephesians, and Onesimus, “one of you”

“Tychicus, the beloved brother and faithful servant and fellow slave in the Lord.” These two verses and Ephesians 6:21 and Ephesians 6:22 say the same thing in the same words: apart from “and fellow slave,” which only Colossians has, twenty-nine Greek words run the same in both letters, and v8 is word for word Ephesians 6:22 (encyclopedia). Readers explain it as two letters carried on one journey by one man, or as one letter written with the other open beside it. “Fellow slave” is the title given to Epaphras at Colossians 1:7 (dictionary). The Byzantine text reads “that he may know your affairs,” and so the KJV: “that he might know your estate, and comfort your hearts”; the ASV reads “that ye may know our state, and that he may comfort your hearts.”

“With Onesimus, the faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you.” If he is the Onesimus of the letter to Philemon, as is usually held, he is the slave who left his master in Colossae and is now sent home as “a beloved brother” (Philemon 1:16; encyclopedia). Here nothing is said of his condition: he is “one of you,” the phrase used of Epaphras in v12, and he carries the news with Tychicus. Ignatius of Antioch, writing about 110, names a bishop of Ephesus called Onesimus; whether he is the same man is argued. The KJV reads “a faithful and beloved brother, who is one of you”; the NWT 1984 reads “my faithful and beloved brother, who is from among YOU”; the NIV reads “our faithful and dear brother”; each supplies a pronoun the Greek does not have.

Verses 10–11 · Aristarchus, Mark the cousin of Barnabas, Jesus called Justus, and a comfort

“Aristarchus my fellow prisoner.” The Macedonian of Thessalonica seized in the theatre at Ephesus (Acts 19:29) and on board with Paul at Acts 27:2 (encyclopedia). Synaichmalōtos stands in three verses: Romans 16:7, of Andronicus and Junia, this one, and Philemon 1:23, where the title goes to Epaphras and Aristarchus is listed among the “fellow workers” (dictionary). It is built on aichmalōtos, a captive taken in war. Readers take it as men who shared Paul’s confinement, perhaps by turns, or as a figure for the war they served in together. The KJV reads “Aristarchus my fellowprisoner saluteth you”; the NIV reads “My fellow prisoner Aristarchus sends you his greetings.”

“Mark the cousin of Barnabas.” Anepsios stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). The Greek Old Testament uses it at Numbers 36:11 for the husbands of Zelophehad’s daughters, “the sons of their uncles,” that is, cousins; later Greek also used it for a nephew, and so some older versions. This is the Mark over whom Paul and Barnabas parted at Acts 15:39, “a sharp disagreement arose” (encyclopedia); here he is with Paul again, and at Philemon 1:24 he is a “fellow worker.” When or how the Colossians “received instructions” about him is not said. The KJV reads “Marcus, sister’s son to Barnabas”; the ASV reads “Mark, the cousin of Barnabas”; the Douay reads “the cousin german of Barnabus.”

“Jesus who is called Justus … these alone are fellow workers … a comfort to me.” “Jesus,” the Greek form of Joshua, was a common Jewish name (encyclopedia). Ioustos, the Latin name “Justus,” stands in three verses: Acts 1:23, the surname of Joseph Barsabbas, Acts 18:7, Titius Justus at Corinth, and this one. “Those who are of the circumcision” are the Jews among Paul’s companions, and the sentence is usually read to mean that Aristarchus, Mark and Justus were the only Jewish fellow workers with him. On that reading the three named next, Epaphras, Luke and Demas, were Gentiles, and Luke is the one writer of the New Testament who was not a Jew; others think the sentence does not settle it. Parēgoria, “comfort,” stands in one verse (dictionary); its family gave English “paregoric,” a soothing medicine, and three verses on a physician sends his greetings. The KJV reads “These only are my fellowworkers unto the kingdom of God, which have been a comfort unto me”; the NIV reads “These are the only Jews among my co-workers”; the NWT 1984 reads “a strengthening aid to me.”

Verses 12–14 · Epaphras struggling in prayer, great pains for three towns, Luke the physician, and Demas

“Epaphras … always struggling for you in his prayers, so that you may stand mature and fully convinced.” The founder of the congregation (encyclopedia), named at Colossians 1:7. His prayer uses Paul’s own words in this letter: “struggling,” as at Colossians 1:29, “mature,” as at Colossians 1:28 (dictionary), and “fully convinced,” the verb of Romans 4:21 and the “full assurance” of Colossians 2:2. The text this translation follows reads “a slave of Christ”; Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland add “Jesus.” The KJV reads “a servant of Christ … that ye may stand perfect and complete in all the will of God”; the ASV reads “a servant of Christ Jesus … that ye may stand perfect and fully assured”; the NWT 1984 reads “complete and with firm conviction.”

“He takes great pains for you and for those in Laodicea and those in Hierapolis.” Ponos stands in four verses: this one, two in Revelation 16, and Revelation 21:4, “nor pain any more” (dictionary); “takes great pains” keeps the English word. The Byzantine text reads “great zeal,” and so the KJV: “he hath a great zeal for you”; the ASV reads “he hath much labor for you.” The three towns stood within some fifteen miles of one another in the Lycus valley. Laodikeia is named in six verses, four of them in this letter and two in Revelation, among them Revelation 3:14 (encyclopedia); Hierapolis, “holy city,” stands in one verse (encyclopedia).

“Luke the beloved physician greets you, and Demas.” This is the one place where Luke is called a physician (encyclopedia). Iatros stands in seven verses, among them the proverb “Physician, heal yourself” at Luke 4:23. Since the second century he has been held to be the author of the Gospel of Luke and Acts. W. K. Hobart argued in 1882 that the Gospel’s vocabulary is a physician’s; H. J. Cadbury answered that most of the same words stand in writers who were not physicians, and the argument is no longer widely relied on. Readers still point to one difference: where Mark says the woman with the flow of blood “had suffered much under many physicians … but rather grew worse” (Mark 5:26), Luke says only that she “had spent all her living on physicians” (Luke 8:43). Demas sends greetings here and at Philemon 1:24; at 2 Timothy 4:10 he is the one who “deserted” Paul (encyclopedia). The KJV reads “Luke, the beloved physician, and Demas, greet you”; the Douay reads “Luke, the most dear physician”; the TLB reads “Dear Doctor Luke sends his love.”

Verses 15–18 · Nympha and the congregation at her house, the letter from Laodicea, Archippus, and the greeting in Paul’s own hand

⚠ “Nympha and the congregation at her house.” The manuscripts divide. Codex Vaticanus reads “her house,” Codex Sinaiticus “their house,” and the Byzantine text “his house.” The name in the accusative, Nymphan, can be the woman’s name Nympha or a man’s name Nymphas, and only the accent tells them apart; the oldest manuscripts are written without accents (encyclopedia). The editors of the United Bible Societies’ text print “her” and mark the choice as difficult. Readers who prefer “her” hold that a copyist was more likely to change a woman to a man than the other way; readers who prefer “his” point to the wider spread of that reading. This page sets out both and does not choose. Congregations met in houses, as at Romans 16:5, 1 Corinthians 16:19 and Philemon 1:2. The KJV reads “and Nymphas, and the church which is in his house”; the ASV reads “and the church that is in their house”; the NIV reads “and to Nympha and the church in her house”; the NWT 1984 reads “and to the congregation at her house.”

⚠ “And that you also read the one from Laodicea.” No letter of Paul to Laodicea is known, and readers have proposed three. It may be a letter now lost. It may be Ephesians: the earliest manuscripts of Ephesians lack “in Ephesus” at 1:1, and Tertullian reports that Marcion, in the second century, called it the letter “to the Laodiceans” (see the note at Ephesians 1:1). A few have proposed Philemon. A short Latin “Letter to the Laodiceans,” twenty verses gathered mostly from Philippians, is first found in Codex Fuldensis, copied in 546, and stood in many Latin Bibles for centuries; it is generally held to be a later composition prompted by this verse. “The one from Laodicea” may mean a letter coming on from there, not one written to that town. The KJV reads “read the epistle from Laodicea”; the Douay reads “that you read that which is of the Laodiceans”; the TLB reads “And read the letter I wrote to them,” which settles what the Greek leaves open.

“Say to Archippus, ‘See to the service you received in the Lord, that you fulfill it.’” Archippos stands in two verses: this one and Philemon 1:2, “Archippus our fellow soldier” (encyclopedia). What his “service” was is not said; whether the words are encouragement or a pointed reminder is read both ways. “Fulfill” is plēroō, as at Colossians 1:25, “to fulfill the word of God”: a letter that has spoken of all the fullness dwelling in Christ (1:19, 2:9) and of the readers “made full” in him (2:10) ends by telling one man to fill up his task. The KJV reads “Take heed to the ministry which thou hast received in the Lord, that thou fulfil it”; the NIV reads “See to it that you complete the ministry you have received in the Lord.”

“The greeting is in my own hand, Paul’s. Remember my chains.” The same Greek as 1 Corinthians 16:21; compare Galatians 6:11, “with what large letters I have written to you with my own hand,” and Philemon 1:19. Letters were written out by a scribe, as Tertius says at Romans 16:22, and the sender took the pen at the end. Those who hold that a follower of Paul wrote the letter read the line otherwise; readings differ. “Chains” is the root of v3. The Byzantine text adds “Amen,” and later manuscripts add “Written from Rome to the Colossians by Tychicus and Onesimus,” which the Geneva prints: “Written from Rome to the Colossians, and sent by Tychicus, and Onesimus.” The KJV reads “The salutation by the hand of me Paul. Remember my bonds. Grace be with you. Amen”; the NIV reads “I, Paul, write this greeting in my own hand. Remember my chains.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “Master” for the kyrios of the masters, as at Ephesians 6:9; “equal” for the “equality” of 2 Corinthians 8:14; “persevere in prayer,” as at Romans 12:12; “keep watch,” as at Mark 14:38; “make plain,” as at 1:26; “buying out the time,” as at Ephesians 5:16; “season,” as at Mark 9:50; the Tychicus verses word for word as at Ephesians 6:21–22; “fellow slave,” as at 1:7; “fellow prisoner” and “fellow workers,” as at Philemon 23–24; “struggling” and “mature,” as at 1:28–29; “fully convinced,” as at Romans 4:21; “congregation” for ekklēsia; “service,” as at Ephesians 4:12; “fulfill,” as at 1:25; and “the greeting is in my own hand, Paul’s,” as at 1 Corinthians 16:21.

Echoes paid. The masters’ instruction that closes Colossians 3:22; thanks, after Colossians 3:17; the mystery made plain at Colossians 1:26; the Tychicus of Ephesians 6:21; Epaphras, the fellow slave of Colossians 1:7, now struggling and praying for the mature of Colossians 1:28–29; the full assurance of Colossians 2:2; Laodicea, for which Paul struggled at Colossians 2:1; Onesimus of Philemon 1:10; Archippus of Philemon 1:2; and the fullness of Colossians 2:9, ending in one man told to fulfill his service.

Echoes planted. Laodicea, greeted here, receives a letter of its own at Revelation 3:14. Demas, who greets here, deserts at 2 Timothy 4:10, and Mark, a cousin with a past, is called “useful to me for service” at 2 Timothy 4:11 (both yet to come in this translation), with the word that Philemon 1:11 uses of Onesimus.

This is the last chapter of Colossians. The next letter is 1 Thessalonians 1, already on these pages.
