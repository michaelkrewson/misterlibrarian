Mary Magdalene finds the tomb open and tells Peter and the disciple whom Jesus loved, who race to it and find the linen cloths but no body; the other disciple 'saw and believed.' Mary, left weeping outside, sees two angels and then Jesus, whom she takes for the gardener until he speaks her name; he tells her not to cling to him and sends her to his brothers. That evening Jesus stands among the disciples behind locked doors, shows his hands and side, sends them as the Father sent him, and breathes on them: 'Receive the Holy Spirit.' A week later he appears again for Thomas, who had refused to believe without seeing and touching, and Thomas answers 'My Lord and my God.' The chapter closes with the book's own statement of its purpose.
c. AD 30, the first day of the week after the crucifixion — the empty tomb before dawn and the appearance that evening — and the first day of the week after, eight days later, for Thomas.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–2 · ⚠ four Gospels, four dawn markers, one woman named in all of them, and a ‘we’ spoken by someone alone

⚠ The four Gospels give four different markers for the hour the women reached the tomb — Matthew's ‘as it was dawning toward the first day of the week’ (28:1), Mark's ‘very early… when the sun had risen’ (16:2), Luke's ‘at early dawn’ (24:1), all already on these pages, and this Gospel's ‘while it was still dark’ (v1) — the earliest of the four, and the only one that puts the arrival in the dark. Mary Magdalene is the one woman named in all four accounts (Matthew 28:1; Mark 16:1; Luke 24:10, already on these pages), and here she is the only one named at all. The phrase this translation prints as ‘the first day of the week’ opens this verse with exactly the five Greek words that open Luke 24:1 (tē de mia tōn sabbatōn, literally ‘on the one of the sabbaths’); see the dictionary entry.

‘We do not know where they have laid him’ (v2) is said by a woman who, on this Gospel's own account, came to the tomb alone — a plural the text never explains. The usual reading hears in it the other women of the Synoptic accounts (Mark 16:1; Luke 24:10, already on these pages), left just offstage. Whatever the reason, her conclusion is a removal, not a resurrection: ‘they have taken the Lord out,’ by someone she does not name. She says almost the same sentence again to the angels at v13, with two small changes (see the note there).

⚠ The disciple ‘whom Jesus loved’ is described here with a different verb of love: ephilei (from phileō), where the other four verses that describe him this way all use ēgapa (from agapaō) — 13:23 and 19:26, already on these pages, and 21:7 and 21:20, not yet. This Gospel swaps the two verbs without comment elsewhere too: 11:3 and 11:5 (already on these pages) use one each of the identical relationship, and the dictionary entry says why the swap should not be made to carry more than it does. The disciple himself is anonymous throughout; see the encyclopedia entry for who he has been taken to be.

TLB resolves the anonymity inside the text itself: it prints ‘Simon Peter and me’ at v2 and ‘I went in too’ at v8, turning this Gospel's third-person disciple into a first-person narrator — a traditional identification printed as though it stood in the Greek, which says neither.

Verses 3–10 · a race to the tomb, three verbs of seeing, a cloth wrapped up apart, and a belief the text declines to describe

⚠ The race has a winner and a surprise: the other disciple outruns Peter (v4) but stops at the entrance, and Peter, arriving second, is the one who goes in first (v6). Luke has Peter alone run to the tomb, stoop in, and see ‘the linen wrappings alone’ (Luke 24:12, already on these pages, where a note takes up the manuscript question that verse raises); this Gospel's longer scene has two men and keeps their roles apart. The Greek uses three different verbs for what they do with their eyes — blepei at v5, theōrei at v6, eiden at v8 — and this translation prints ‘saw,’ ‘looked closely at,’ ‘saw.’ NWT 1984 prints three different words as well (‘beheld,’ ‘viewed,’ ‘saw’), as does the ASV (‘seeth,’ ‘beholdeth,’ ‘saw’). The same verb theōrei is used of Mary at vv12 and 14, where nothing is being inspected, and there this translation says plainly ‘saw.’

The ‘linen cloths’ (othonia, vv5–7) are the same wrappings that bound the body in 19:40 (already on these pages); the separate ‘cloth’ (soudarion) is the one that had been ‘on his head.’ It is the word this Gospel used of Lazarus, who came out of his tomb with ‘his face wrapped around with a cloth’ and had to be freed by others (11:44, already on these pages). Jesus's cloth is simply left behind, in a place by itself, and the text draws no comparison of its own.

⚠ ‘Wrapped up’ (entetyligmenon, v7) is the same verb Matthew and Luke use when Joseph of Arimathea wraps the body (Matthew 27:59; Luke 23:53, already on these pages) — and the Greek says the cloth was wrapped up in one place, not that it was folded. ASV reads ‘rolled up in a place by itself’; KJV and GNV read ‘wrapped together in a place by itself’; DRA reads ‘wrapped up into one place’; NIV keeps the separation and drops the wrapping altogether: ‘still lying in its place, separate from the linen.’ What the verse stresses is the contrast — not lying with the linen cloths, but apart.

⚠ What the other disciple ‘believed’ (v8) the text never says. The next sentence (v9) explains that they did not yet know the scripture that he must rise — the same ‘must’ (dei) the risen Jesus uses in Luke, ‘Was it not necessary for the Christ to suffer these things…?’ (Luke 24:26, already on these pages) — and which scripture is meant this Gospel does not say. The verse can be read as the reason belief in a resurrection was not yet possible, or as a comment on a belief that came without scripture's help; this translation leaves ‘believed’ as bare as the Greek does. TLB alone supplies an object: ‘believed that he had risen.’

‘Went back to their own homes’ (v10) renders pros hautous, literally ‘to themselves,’ an idiom for returning to one's own place — the same idiom Luke uses of Peter alone (Luke 24:12, already on these pages). NIV reads ‘to where they were staying’ and TLB ‘went on home’; the ASV keeps ‘unto their own home.’

Verses 11–15 · four weepings in five verses, two angels no one else sees, a question asked twice, and a gardener in a garden

The verb klaiō, ‘weep,’ is said four times in these five verses (twice in v11, once each in vv13 and 15), and ‘Woman, why are you weeping?’ is asked twice in identical words — first by the angels (v13), then by Jesus (v15). Mary's answer to the angels is her message to Peter (v2) with two small changes: ‘we do not know’ has become ‘I do not know,’ and ‘the Lord’ has become ‘my Lord.’ The report has turned into a grief that belongs to one person.

⚠ The four Gospels name the figures at the tomb four ways — Mark's ‘young man’ (16:5), Matthew's ‘messenger of Jehovah’ (28:2), Luke's ‘two men’ in dazzling clothing (24:4), all already on these pages — and this Gospel's ‘two angels in white’ (v12), whom only Mary sees. The two disciples who went in earlier saw cloths and nothing else (vv5–8); the text reports no angels until Mary stoops to look. Sitting ‘one at the head and one at the feet’ of the place where the body lay, they have reminded some readers of the two golden cherubim at the two ends of the ark's cover (Exodus 25:18–19, already on these pages); the text itself draws no such comparison. In this Gospel the angels say nothing about a resurrection — their only words are the question of v13.

Mary does not recognise Jesus (v14), and the text gives no reason. Luke's Emmaus pair fail to recognise him too, though Luke does say why: ‘their eyes were kept from recognizing him’ (Luke 24:16, already on these pages). Her guess — kēpouros, ‘gardener,’ a word found in this one verse of the New Testament — is a fair one: she is standing in the ‘garden’ of 19:41 (already on these pages), where the note on the previous chapter said this book's next scene would open. Her ‘Sir’ (kyrie) is the identical word she used of Jesus two verses before as ‘my Lord’ (v13); this translation reads it ‘Sir’ because she means a stranger by it, and leaves the reader to hear what she cannot. NWT 1984 ASV KJV NIV DRA GNV and TLB all print ‘Sir.’

Verses 16–18 · ⚠ one word that answers a shepherd's promise, ‘Rabbouni,’ a verb that means touch or cling, and ‘my brothers’

⚠ Jesus says one word, her name, and she knows him — the payoff of a promise this Gospel made in its shepherd chapter: the shepherd ‘calls his own sheep by name’ (10:3) and ‘my sheep listen to my voice’ (10:27, both already on these pages). The name is given in its Semitic form, Mariam (Miriam), where the narrator writes Maria at vv1 and 11; the printed Greek has the narrator adopt Mariam himself at v18, once she has been called by it, though the manuscripts differ there.

She answers ‘in Hebrew’ — the same word (Hebraisti) flagged at 19:13, which most modern scholarship takes to mean Aramaic — and the shelf splits over whether the phrase belongs here at all: ASV and NWT 1984 print ‘in Hebrew,’ NIV prints ‘in Aramaic,’ and KJV, DRA and GNV leave it out, though the critical Greek printed here has it (with a marked variant). Rabbouni is used in only two places in the whole New Testament, both on these pages (Mark 10:51; here). Mary also turns twice: the verb of v14 (‘she turned around’) comes back at v16 (‘she turned’), and the text does not say why; one reading has her turn back toward the tomb in between, another takes the second turn as the movement of recognition.

⚠ ‘Do not cling to me’ (v17) translates mē mou haptou, the present imperative of haptomai, a verb that means both ‘touch’ and ‘take hold of.’ With mē, a present imperative is most naturally ‘do not keep doing it’ or ‘stop,’ and the text does not say whether she had already taken hold. The shelf splits on the verb: ASV KJV and GNV print ‘Touch me not’; DRA prints ‘Do not touch me’; TLB prints ‘Don't touch me’; while NWT 1984 reads ‘Stop clinging to me’ and NIV reads ‘Do not hold on to me.’ The risen Jesus is not always so careful about being held: Matthew's women ‘took hold of his feet’ (Matthew 28:9, a different verb, krateō) and are not stopped; Luke's Jesus says ‘Touch me and see’ (Luke 24:39, another verb again, psēlaphaō); and Thomas is invited at v27 to put his hand into the wound (pherō, ballō) — all already on these pages. The reason given here is the one that matters: ‘I have not yet ascended to the Father,’ the going-up this Gospel has spoken of since 3:13 and 6:62 (already on these pages), now put in the present tense: ‘I am ascending.’

⚠ ‘My brothers’ is the only time in this Gospel that Jesus calls his disciples brothers — elsewhere in this book the word is his own family (2:12; 7:3, 5, 10, already on these pages) or the narrator's word for the community (21:23, already on these pages). Matthew's risen Jesus sends the women with nearly the same words: ‘report to my brothers’ (Matthew 28:10, already on these pages). He does not say ‘our Father’: it is ‘my Father and your Father, my God and your God’ — one relationship, shared but not identical. The phrase ‘my God’ (theos mou) stands in exactly two verses of this Gospel, both in this chapter: here, of the Father, on Jesus's lips, and at v28, of Jesus, on Thomas's. Traditions that confess the Son to be fully God read Jesus's ‘my God’ as the Son speaking, in his humanity, to the Father; traditions that do not read it as showing that the Father is God in a sense the Son is not; this translation prints the words and does not vote.

Mary ‘announced’ (angellousa) the news — a verb from the same root as the ‘angels’ (angeloi, ‘messengers’) of v12. In this Gospel the angels carry no message; the first announcement of the resurrection is made by a woman, ‘I have seen the Lord’ (heōraka, a perfect tense: I have seen, and it still holds). The other accounts record how a report like hers was received: Luke says the women's words ‘appeared before them like nonsense’ (24:11), and the bracketed ending of Mark says the disciples ‘did not believe it’ (16:11, both already on these pages); this Gospel narrates no reaction at all and cuts straight to the evening (v19).

Verses 19–21 · locked doors, ‘Peace to you’ said three times in the chapter, wounds shown, and a sending in two different verbs

The doors are locked ‘for fear of the Jewish leaders’ (v19) — the identical clause that explained Joseph of Arimathea's secrecy in 19:38 (already on these pages). The same fear that kept one disciple silent now keeps them all indoors, and hoi Ioudaioi stays ‘the Jewish leaders’ here as it has since chapter 18, for the hostile authority these disciples fear. The text says the doors were locked and that Jesus ‘came and stood in their midst’; it gives no account of how he entered, here or when the circumstance is repeated at v26 (see the dictionary entry).

‘Peace to you’ is said three times in this chapter (vv19, 21, 26) — the Greek counterpart of the Hebrew and Aramaic greeting shalom — and it is also the thing Jesus had promised. ‘Peace I leave with you’ (14:27) and ‘in me you may have peace’ (16:33, both already on these pages) are given here in person; see the dictionary entry. The Greek has no verb in the greeting, and six of the seven shelf versions supply one: ASV KJV and GNV print ‘Peace be unto you,’ DRA ‘Peace be to you,’ NIV ‘Peace be with you!’ and NWT 1984 ‘May YOU have peace’ — while TLB drops the greeting at v19 (‘After greeting them’) and again at v21. This translation prints it as the Greek has it, with none.

‘He showed them his hands and his side’ (v20): the side is the spear wound of 19:34 (already on these pages; the same word, pleura), a detail only this Gospel narrates. Luke's risen Jesus tells them to look at ‘my hands and my feet’ (24:39), and the next verse, printed in brackets on these pages, says he showed them ‘his hands and his feet’ (24:40): Luke has the feet where John has the side. The disciples ‘rejoiced when they saw the Lord’ — joy and seeing together, exactly as the farewell promise had put it: ‘I will see you again, and your heart will rejoice’ (16:22, already on these pages; compare 16:20).

⚠ ‘As the Father has sent me, I also am sending you’ (v21) uses two different verbs for the two sendings — apostellō (perfect, ‘has sent’) for the Father's, pempō (present, ‘am sending’) for Jesus's — where the prayer of 17:18 (already on these pages) had used a single verb, apostellō, for both. This Gospel uses both verbs of the Father sending the Son (pempō in ‘the one who sent me,’ as at 4:34; apostellō throughout chapter 17), so the switch is not obviously a distinction; this translation prints ‘sent’ and ‘sending’ and flags the change. And although the verb behind ‘apostle’ (apostolos) is the first of the two, nobody in this Gospel is ever called one: the noun occurs once, of any ‘one who is sent’ (13:16, already on these pages).

Verses 22–23 · ⚠ a breath, a verb from Genesis, no article on ‘Spirit,’ and forgiveness in the perfect tense

⚠ ‘Breathed on’ (enephysēsen, v22) occurs nowhere else in the New Testament — and it is the exact verb, in the exact form, that the Greek Old Testament uses where God breathes the ‘breath of life’ into the first human (Genesis 2:7, already on these pages, where the Hebrew has the breath go ‘into his nostrils’ and the Greek translation ‘into his face’). The same Greek translation uses the same verb in Ezekiel's valley of dry bones, where the breath is called to ‘breathe into these dead, and let them live’ (Ezekiel 37:9, not yet on these pages). The Gospel does not say it is recalling either scene; but a reader who knew the Greek Genesis would have heard it — a gift of life to a frightened room, made with the one verb the Greek Old Testament had already used for exactly that. And the gift itself is a pneuma, a word that means breath as well as spirit, so that act and gift are one gesture.

The Greek has no article on ‘Spirit’ (pneuma hagion, ‘holy spirit’), and the shelf divides on how much to make of that. NWT 1984 prints ‘Receive holy spirit,’ matching the Greek's bare noun; ASV and NIV print ‘the Holy Spirit’; KJV and DRA print ‘the Holy Ghost.’ This translation prints ‘the Holy Spirit,’ the standing English name, and flags that the Greek here is the bare form.

⚠ Two accounts, two moments. This Gospel has the Spirit given on Easter evening; Luke has the risen Jesus promise ‘what my Father promised’ and tell the disciples to wait in the city ‘until you are clothed with power from on high’ (Luke 24:49), and Acts places the arrival at Pentecost (Acts 2:1–4, both already on these pages). The Gospel itself had said that the Spirit ‘had not yet been given, because Jesus had not yet been glorified’ (7:39, already on these pages); this scene is where that sentence seems to arrive — if it is the same arrival. Readers have harmonised the two (a first gift now, a fuller one later), set them side by side as two separate events, or taken this scene as John's own telling of the Pentecost gift; this translation states the difference and does not choose.

⚠ Both halves of v23 are in the perfect tense: sins have been forgiven (apheōntai) and have been retained (kekratēntai), not ‘are’ forgiven and retained. Some manuscripts read a present form (aphientai) in the first half; the perfect is the reading printed here. NWT 1984 keeps the tense — ‘they stand forgiven’ and ‘they stand retained’ — where its own 2013 revision, NWT 2013, reads ‘they are forgiven’ and ‘they are retained’; ASV prints ‘they are forgiven’ and ‘they are retained,’ while NIV turns the second half into the absence of the first (‘if you do not forgive them, they are not forgiven’); TLB does the same (‘if you refuse to forgive them, they are unforgiven’).

Who is addressed is as open as the Greek leaves it. The ‘you’ is plural and the audience is ‘the disciples’ of vv19–20, with Thomas absent (v24); no list of names is given. Catholic and Orthodox traditions read the verse as the root of priestly absolution; many Protestant traditions read it as the community's authority to proclaim forgiveness, or its refusal, in the gospel's name. Matthew gives the same kind of authority in different words — ‘whatever you bind on earth will be bound in heaven’ — to Peter (16:19) and to the disciples as a group (18:18, both already on these pages). This translation prints the Greek and does not decide who is addressed or what the verse licenses.

Verses 24–27 · Thomas, absent the first time; the nails, named for the first time; and a Jesus who quotes him back

This Gospel gives Thomas a line in three scenes — ‘Let us also go, that we may die with him’ (11:16), ‘Lord, we do not know where you are going’ (14:5, both already on these pages), and this one — and never says why he is missing on the first evening. Didymos is Greek for ‘twin’; see the encyclopedia entry.

⚠ The word for ‘nails’ (hēloi, v25, said twice) stands in this one verse of the New Testament and nowhere else — none of the four crucifixion narratives uses it. They say only that Jesus was crucified (19:18, already on these pages); the nails enter the Gospels' record here, in Thomas's mouth, as the thing he must see. The mark they left is a typos. And ‘I will never believe’ is the Greek double negative ou mē, the strongest refusal the language has: ASV and KJV print the plain ‘I will not believe,’ NWT 1984 ‘I will certainly not believe,’ and its 2013 revision, NWT 2013, ‘I will never believe it.’

⚠ ‘Eight days later’ (v26) counts, in the ancient way, both ends of the span — so it is the next first day of the week, a week after v19. The text says only ‘eight days,’ and that the doors are locked again. Jesus's words to Thomas then repeat Thomas's own conditions nearly word for word — finger, hands, side (compare v25 with v27) — and the text does not say how he knew them. It also never says Thomas touched anything: v28 goes straight to the confession, and v29 names sight, not touch, as the ground of belief (‘Because you have seen me’). The long tradition of painting the scene puts a finger in the wound; the Gospel does not.

‘Unbelieving’ and ‘believing’ (apistos, pistos, v27) can also be ‘faithless’ and ‘faithful.’ ASV KJV and DRA print ‘faithless, but believing’; GNV prints ‘faithless, but faithful’; NWT 1984 keeps the verb of becoming (‘become believing’); and NIV reads ‘Stop doubting and believe’ — but doubt is a different thing from what Thomas actually said, a flat refusal, and ‘doubting Thomas’ is a title the text itself never gives him. This translation keeps the Greek's ‘do not become…,’ a change of state rather than a verdict on a character.

Verses 28–29 · ⚠ ‘My Lord and my God’ — a confession, an exclamation, or an address; a question or a statement; and a blessing for later readers

⚠ ‘My Lord and my God!’ (v28) is, in the Greek, two nominatives each with the article (ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou) used as address — and the article matters, because the note on the Gospel's opening claim that the Word was divine (1:1, already on these pages, still not in Spanish) turns on the fact that theos there has none. Here it has one. Six of the seven shelf versions print the same words; GNV alone supplies a verb, ‘Thou art my Lord, and my God,’ which turns an address into a statement of identity.

Readers divide on what the sentence asserts, and this translation prints the Greek and does not vote. One reading takes it as the plainest confession of Jesus's deity in the four Gospels, closing the frame the prologue opens; another hears an exclamation of wonder, spoken in Jesus's presence and directed to God; a third takes ‘God’ here as honoring the Father who is revealed in Jesus. The Greek says Thomas spoke ‘to him’ (autō), which makes the address Jesus's, and Jesus's reply in v29 neither corrects the words nor repeats them — it turns at once to belief and sight. The argument over the Gospel's opening claim, which this question runs through, is gathered in the Ask Mr. Librarian post on John 1:1; and the same two words, theos mou, ‘my God,’ are Jesus's own of the Father at v17, the only other verse where this Gospel has them.

⚠ Verse 29 is a question in the printed Greek — ‘Because you have seen me, have you believed?’ — and a statement in most of the shelf. NWT 1984 reads it as a question (‘Because you have seen me have you believed?’); ASV (‘Because thou hast seen me, thou hast believed’) and NIV (‘Because you have seen me, you have believed’) print statements, and KJV, DRA and GNV also add the name ‘Thomas,’ which the Greek printed here does not have. The question form leaves open whether Jesus is reproaching Thomas, testing him or smiling at him; the Greek does not say. ‘Happy’ (makarios) is this translation's fixed rendering of the beatitude word, as in the NWT 1984; ASV KJV NIV DRA GNV and TLB print ‘blessed.’

The blessing is for readers who come later — and the next two verses say so. It answers the prayer's widening to ‘those who will believe in me through their word’ (17:20, already on these pages), and the book then turns to explain that it was written for just such a reader (vv30–31, below).

Verses 30–31 · ⚠ the book states its own purpose, a verb tense the manuscripts disagree on, and an ending that is not yet the end

⚠ These two verses read like the Gospel's own closing — a note on what was left out, then a statement of purpose — yet a full chapter follows, and it ends with a second note of the same kind (John 21:25, already on these pages). The signs are this Gospel's own counted category: it numbered the first (2:11, ‘the beginning of the signs’) and the second (4:54, already on these pages) and here says that there were many more, unwritten; see the dictionary entry. The ‘book’ is a biblion, strictly a scroll — NWT 1984 prints ‘scroll’ where ASV KJV NIV DRA and GNV print ‘book.’

The stated purpose names the two titles Martha confessed, in the same order: ‘the Christ, the Son of God’ (11:27, already on these pages). The book's declared aim is to bring its reader to the confession one of its own characters had already made. NIV and TLB print ‘the Messiah’ where ASV KJV DRA and NWT 1984 print ‘the Christ’; this translation keeps ‘the Christ,’ as at 11:27 (see the dictionary entry). And the Gospel's last stated goal is ‘life’ — the thing Jesus told Martha he was, in the same chapter (11:25).

⚠ ‘May believe’ (pisteuēte) is a verb the manuscripts do not agree on: a present form (‘may go on believing’) or an aorist (‘may come to believe’), a difference of a single letter. The printed Greek here reads the present; the same variation stands at 19:35 (already on these pages). The argument is over whom the Gospel imagines reading it — people who already believe, or people who do not yet — and this translation's English ‘may believe’ is deliberately neutral between them; the Greek's own tense is the one thing that could tip it.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Hoi Ioudaioi stays ‘the Jewish leaders’ for the hostile authority the disciples fear (v19), as in the trial and burial chapters. Kyrios is ‘the Lord’ or ‘my Lord’ wherever the speaker means Jesus (vv2, 13, 18, 20, 25, 28), and ‘Sir’ once, where Mary means a stranger (v15). Angeloi stays ‘angels’ (v12) against Matthew's ‘messenger.’ Makarios keeps its fixed rendering ‘Happy’ (v29). Haptou is ‘cling to’ (v17), entetyligmenon is ‘wrapped up’ rather than ‘folded’ (v7), and pneuma hagion, though it has no article here, is printed ‘the Holy Spirit.’

Echoes paid. The linen cloths of 19:40 (vv5–7), the garden of 19:41 (v15) and the pierced side of 19:34 (vv20, 25, 27) all return, as the notes on the last chapter said they would. The shepherd who ‘calls his own sheep by name’ (10:3) does so at v16; the joy and peace of the farewell discourse (16:22; 14:27) arrive at vv19–20; ‘the Spirit had not yet been given’ (7:39) meets its scene at v22; Lazarus's face-cloth (11:44) meets Jesus's at v7; Thomas's two earlier lines (11:16; 14:5) meet his third; Martha's confession (11:27) becomes the book's stated purpose at v31; the prayer for those who will believe through the disciples' word (17:20) is answered by the blessing at v29; and the ‘sending’ of 17:18 is said again, in different verbs, at v21 (all already on these pages).

Echoes planted. The charcoal fire of 18:18 returns at Peter's restoration (John 21:9, already on these pages). Thomas appears once more, among the disciples at the lake (21:2); the disciple whom Jesus loved is named again at 21:7 and 21:20, and the Gospel's closing statement that he is the one who ‘testifies about these things’ (21:24) is on these pages too; and the ‘many other things’ Jesus did are said again, at greater length, at 21:25.

This chapter completes the resurrection-day and eighth-day appearances and the book's stated purpose; the book's own sequence runs unbroken from chapter 1 through this chapter — next in sequence is John 21, the last chapter: a breakfast on the shore, the charcoal fire, and the restoration of Peter.
