Mary Magdalene finds the stone removed from the tomb and runs to Peter and the disciple Jesus loved; the two race to the tomb and find the grave-cloths lying there, the head-cloth rolled up apart, and the other disciple 'saw and believed.' Mary, weeping outside, sees two angels and then takes Jesus for the gardener until he says her name; he tells her not to cling to him and sends her to his 'brothers.' That evening Jesus stands among the disciples behind locked doors, shows his hands and side, sends them as the Father sent him, and breathes on them with 'Receive holy spirit.' Thomas, who was absent, refuses to believe unless he sees and touches the wounds; eight days later Jesus comes again, and Thomas answers, 'My Lord and my God.' The chapter closes with the Gospel's statement of its own purpose.
c. AD 30, Sunday, the day of the resurrection from before dawn through that same evening, then the following Sunday ('eight days later,' counted inclusively) for the appearance to Thomas.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–10 · ⚠ the empty tomb of John 20: ‘the first day of the week,’ a plural ‘we’ in Mary’s report, a footrace, grave-cloths that answer Lazarus’s — and a disciple who ‘saw and believed’: believed what?

⚠ ‘The first day of the week’ opens this chapter in the same four Greek words that open Luke’s resurrection chapter (tē de mia tōn sabbatōn, Luke 24:1, already on these pages); Mark has the same phrase without the de (Mark 16:2), and only Matthew builds it differently, eis mian sabbatōn (Matthew 28:1, both already on these pages). Literally the phrase is ‘the first of the sabbaths’ — see the dictionary entry — and this Gospel uses it twice, here for the dawn and again, in a shorter form, for the evening of the same day (v19).

⚠ Mary’s report has a plural the scene never explains: ‘we do not know where they have laid him’ (v2, oidamen), though she is the only woman John names and her own words to the angels are singular, ‘I do not know’ (v13, oida). The other Gospels send several women to this tomb (Mark 16:1; Luke 24:10, both already on these pages), and the plural may be a trace of them; the text does not say. ASV and KJV both read ‘we know not where they have laid him’, the NWT 1984 reads ‘we do not know where they have laid him’, and the NIV reads ‘we don’t know where they have put him’; the TLB alone flattens it to ‘I don’t know where they have put him’, which removes the question.

The race is told with a winner, and the winner is not Peter. ‘The other disciple’ — the one Jesus loved, as v2 names him — ‘ran ahead, faster than Peter’ (v4) and is ‘the one who had come first’ again at v8, yet he stops at the threshold; Peter, behind him, goes in first. The verb for the stooping glance, parakyptō (v5), is the one used of Mary at v11 and the one Luke gives Peter alone (Luke 24:12, already on these pages).

⚠ The grave-cloths are described twice over, with a precision that answers an earlier chapter. There are ‘the linen cloths’ (othonia, vv5–7), the same word as the wrappings of 19:40 (already on these pages), and a separate soudarion that had been on his head, ‘rolled up separately in one place’ (v7). John has used that second word once before, of Lazarus, who walked out of his tomb still bound hand and foot, ‘his face wrapped around with a cloth’, and who needed others to untie him (11:44, already on these pages); here the cloths are simply left behind and the head-cloth is set apart. The word stands in only four verses of the New Testament, and these two Johannine verses are two of them. Its verb in v7, entylissō, is the one Matthew and Luke use for what Joseph did to the body itself (Matthew 27:59; Luke 23:53, both already on these pages) — three verses in all — and here it is said of a cloth with no body in it. The shelf: ASV, KJV and Douay call it ‘the napkin’; Geneva has ‘the kerchief’; the NIV reads ‘the cloth that had been wrapped around Jesus’ head’ and the NWT 1984 ‘the cloth that had been upon his head’, the two nearest to this translation.

⚠ ‘He saw and believed’ (v8) is followed at once by ‘for they did not yet understand the scripture’ (v9), and the Greek never says what the other disciple believed. The TLB supplies an object — ‘believed that he had risen’ — where the ASV, KJV and NIV all leave the verb bare, as the Greek does (‘he saw, and believed’; ‘He saw and believed’). A reader can take it as the first glimmer of resurrection faith, or as belief that Mary’s report was true and the body really was gone; the verse that follows, and the two men going home, count against the fuller reading, and this translation does not choose. Which scripture is meant, the Gospel does not say either.

Verses 11–18 · ⚠ ‘Do not touch me’ or ‘do not cling’? Mary Magdalene and the risen Jesus in the garden: two angels who announce nothing, a ‘gardener,’ a name spoken, ‘Rabbouni,’ and ‘my God and your God’

Two angels in white say one line and let Mary answer. They sit ‘one at the head and one at the feet’ of the empty place (v12) and ask only ‘Woman, why are you weeping?’ (v13) — the same question, word for word, that the figure behind her asks next (v15), adding ‘Whom are you looking for?’ Matthew’s messenger and Mark’s young man announce the resurrection outright (Matthew 28:6; Mark 16:6, both already on these pages); Luke, like John, has two (Luke 24:4, already on these pages); John’s angels announce nothing, and the news will come from Jesus’s own mouth. Some readers have heard the two cherubim who face each other over the mercy seat (Exodus 25:18–20, already on these pages) in a pair seated at the two ends of the place where the body lay; the text draws no such line.

⚠ ‘The gardener’ (v15, kēpouros) stands only here in the New Testament, and it completes a word that 19:41 planted: the tomb lay in a kēpos, a garden, and the garden’s keeper is the first person Mary takes Jesus to be. Her ‘Sir’ is kyrie, the same word (kyrios) she has just used of him as ‘the Lord’ (vv2, 13; see the dictionary), so one word carries both her mistake and the truth. Genesis puts the first human in a garden ‘to work it and watch over it’ (Genesis 2:15, already on these pages); this Gospel does not draw that line, and this translation does not claim it as the text’s argument, only notes where the garden word returns.

‘Mary.’ (v16) is a single word, and it is the whole recognition. Jesus has said in this Gospel that the shepherd ‘calls his own sheep by name’ and that ‘the sheep follow him, because they know his voice’ (10:3–4, already on these pages); here it is acted out in one name. In the Greek printed on this page the narrator writes her name Maria (vv1, 11), while the word Jesus speaks is spelled Mariam, the Semitic form, which the narrator himself then uses at v18; the printed text marks both spellings as points where manuscripts differ. She answers in the language of that form: ‘in Hebrew, Rabbouni.’ ⚠ Hebraisti most likely means Aramaic here, as at 19:13 (already on these pages), and the shelf splits on the word: the ASV and the NWT 1984 print ‘in Hebrew’; the NIV prints ‘in Aramaic’; the KJV, Geneva and Douay leave the phrase out altogether and print just ‘Rabboni’, and the printed Greek marks Hebraisti as one of the places where manuscripts differ.

⚠ ‘Do not cling to me’ (v17, mē mou haptou) is a present-tense command — ‘stop holding on,’ not a ban on ever being touched — and the shelf splits three ways. The ASV, KJV and Geneva read ‘Touch me not’; the Douay reads ‘Do not touch me’ and the TLB ‘Don’t touch me’ (the Latin noli me tangere, which named the scene for Western painters); the NIV reads ‘Do not hold on to me’; and the NWT 1984 reads ‘Stop clinging to me’, the rendering this translation follows (see haptomai). Touch itself cannot be the problem: in Matthew’s account the women ‘took hold of his feet’ the same morning and he does not stop them (Matthew 28:9), Luke’s Jesus says ‘Touch me and see’ (Luke 24:39), and Thomas is invited to put in his finger (v27) — though none of the three uses this verb. The reason Jesus gives is a departure still ahead: ‘I have not yet ascended,’ and then, in the present tense, ‘I am ascending’, the movement 6:62 had promised: ‘the Son of Man ascending to where he was before’ (already on these pages).

‘My brothers’ (v17) is the only place in this Gospel where Jesus says it of his disciples — the same word Matthew’s risen Jesus uses to the women that same morning (Matthew 28:10, already on these pages) — and he follows it with a sentence in which every possessive is doubled: ‘my Father and your Father, my God and your God.’ ‘My God’ on Jesus’s lips is rare in the four Gospels: in these forms it stands only in the cry from the cross (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34, both already on these pages), here, and then in Thomas’s mouth, addressed to Jesus himself, eleven verses later (v28).

Verses 19–23 · ⚠ ‘Peace be with you’ behind locked doors, ‘as the Father has sent me,’ the only breath in the New Testament, and John’s forgive-or-retain verse

⚠ ‘Peace to you’ (vv19, 21, 26) is the delivery of a promise: eirēnē stands in only five verses of this Gospel, and the other two are ‘Peace I leave with you; my peace I give you’ (14:27) and ‘I have said these things to you so that in me you may have peace’ (16:33, both already on these pages). It is the ordinary Hebrew greeting (shalom; see the dictionary), spoken to men behind locked doors ‘for fear of the Jewish leaders’ — the fear that made Joseph of Arimathea a secret disciple (19:38, already on these pages) — so it arrives exactly where 14:27’s ‘do not let your heart be troubled, and do not be afraid’ was needed.

‘The disciples rejoiced when they saw the Lord’ (v20) lands a promise from the farewell speech with the same root and the same pairing of seeing and rejoicing: ‘I will see you again, and your heart will rejoice, and no one takes your joy away from you’ (16:22, already on these pages; charēsetai there, echarēsan here). What he shows them is ‘his hands and his side’, the side being the spear wound of 19:34 (already on these pages); the word for the side stands in none of the other three Gospels, and Luke’s Jesus shows ‘hands and feet’ instead (Luke 24:40, a verse this translation prints in double brackets, already on these pages).

⚠ ‘As the Father has sent me, I also send you’ (v21) changes verbs mid-sentence — the Father has sent (apestalken) but Jesus sends (pempō) — and the Gospel gives no sign that the switch means anything. It uses both verbs of the Father’s sending of the Son throughout, and the prayer of chapter 17 had the first one twice in a single verse: ‘Just as you sent me into the world, I also have sent them into the world’ (17:18, already on these pages). This translation prints ‘sent’ and ‘send’ and builds no distinction the Gospel does not draw; the commission that was a prayer in chapter 17 is now a sentence spoken to the room.

⚠ ‘He breathed on them’ (v22, enephysēsen) is the only occurrence of the verb in the entire New Testament, and it is the exact form the Greek translation of Genesis uses when God breathes into the first human: ‘and he breathed (enephysēsen) into his face the breath of life’ (Greek Genesis 2:7; the English of Genesis 2:7 on these pages, translated from the Hebrew, has ‘breathed into his nostrils’). The Greek version of Ezekiel 37:9 uses the related command to the wind over the dead bones, ‘breathe into these dead’ (emphysēson; Ezekiel 37, not yet on these pages). The Gospel does not point back to either verse; the shared verb is the evidence, and what it sets beside the gift of ‘spirit’ is that the one word pneuma also means breath and wind (pneuma). It also answers 7:39 — ‘For the Spirit had not yet been given, because Jesus had not yet been glorified’ (already on these pages) — with the first scene after the cross and the tomb. See the dictionary entry.

⚠ ‘Receive holy spirit’ has no article in the Greek (pneuma hagion), and this translation prints it as the Greek does, in lowercase, as at Luke 2:25 (already on these pages), keeping ‘the Holy Spirit’ for where the article stands, as for the Advocate at 14:26 (already on these pages). The ASV prints ‘Receive ye the Holy Spirit’ and the NIV ‘Receive the Holy Spirit’; the KJV and Douay have ‘Receive ye the Holy Ghost’; the NWT 1984 alone matches the Greek’s bare ‘Receive holy spirit.’ This is a point about the Greek’s grammar, not a verdict on doctrine.

⚠ v23 is a pair of perfect passives — ‘they have been forgiven them’ (apheōntai), ‘they have been retained’ (kekratēntai) — and the shelf splits on the second half. The ASV, KJV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 all keep a verb of holding — ‘retain’, ‘retained’ — while the NIV turns it into a negative, ‘if you do not forgive them, they are not forgiven’, and the TLB into ‘If you refuse to forgive them, they are unforgiven.’ The Greek verb is krateō, ‘to hold fast, to seize’, and it stands nowhere else in this Gospel; ‘retain’ keeps it, where the negative versions get a smoother sentence by losing it. The printed Greek also marks the first verb as a point where manuscripts differ. Matthew’s nearest parallels use other verbs, ‘bind’ and ‘loose’ (Matthew 16:19; 18:18, both already on these pages), and this translation does not equate them.

Verses 24–29 · ⚠ Doubting Thomas and ‘my Lord and my God’: the Twin who was not there, a touch the text never reports, an ‘unbelieving’ that is not doubt, and a blessing planted three chapters ago

Thomas arrives already described: ‘one of the Twelve, called the Twin’ (v24), the same epithet John gave him at 11:16, where he said ‘Let us also go, that we may die with him’ (already on these pages). The man who offered to die with Jesus is the one who was not in the room. The ASV, KJV and Geneva print the Greek name, ‘called Didymus’; the NIV prints ‘also known as Didymus’; the TLB and the NWT 1984 translate it, ‘The Twin’, as this translation does (see Thomas).

⚠ Thomas’s terms are exact — the mark of the nails, a finger into it, a hand into the side (v25) — and Jesus gives them back to him in v27 nearly item for item, though the Gospel never says how he knew. The ASV and KJV call it ‘the print of the nails’; the NIV ‘the nail marks’; the TLB ‘the nail wounds’. His refusal uses the emphatic double negative (ou mē pisteusō), hence ‘never’. And the text never says he did it. After v27 the next thing Thomas does is speak (v28), and Jesus’s answer is ‘Because you have seen me’ (v29), not ‘touched’. The painters’ finger entering the wound is the tradition’s addition; the Greek leaves it out.

The Greek word for Thomas is apistos, ‘unbelieving’, set against pistos, ‘believing’ (v27) — a refusal, not a wavering — and ‘doubting Thomas’, a nickname the Gospel never uses, rests on this one scene. The Greek verb for real wavering is a different one, distazō, and it stands in only two verses of the New Testament, both in Matthew (14:31; 28:17, both already on these pages), neither of them here. The NIV reads ‘Stop doubting and believe’, turning the adjective pair into a verb of doubt; the ASV, KJV and Douay keep the adjectives (‘be not faithless, but believing’), and the NWT 1984 keeps the verb of becoming too (‘stop being unbelieving but become believing’). The Greek says ginomai, ‘become’, and this translation keeps ‘do not become’.

⚠ ‘My Lord and my God!’ (v28, ho kyrios mou kai ho theos mou) is said to him — the Greek says Thomas ‘said to him’ — and both nouns belong to the Greek Bible’s standing vocabulary for the God of Israel. Kyrios is the word that stands for the divine name (dictionary), and the psalmist’s cry to Jehovah to wake and judge is ‘my God and my Lord’ (Psalm 35:23, not yet on these pages; the Greek version of that verse, Psalm 34:23 in its numbering, puts the article before both nouns, as here). The Gospel opened by saying the Word ‘was God’ (1:1, already on these pages); this is the point at which someone in the story says it aloud to him, and it stands directly before the Gospel’s statement of its own purpose (v31). Of the seven versions on the English shelf, Geneva alone supplies a verb: ‘Thou art my Lord, and my God.’ The other six print the plain exclamation.

⚠ v29 carries three small differences the shelf splits on. The Greek printed here ends the first clause with a question mark — ‘Because you have seen me, have you believed?’ — and the NWT 1984 reads it so (‘Because you have seen me have you believed?’), where the ASV and KJV make it a flat statement (‘thou hast believed’), as does the NIV (‘you have believed’). The KJV and Geneva add the name, ‘Thomas, because thou hast seen me’, and the Douay adds it after (‘Because thou hast seen me, Thomas’), which the Greek printed here does not have; the ASV, NIV and TLB omit it. And makarioi is ‘Happy’ here, as in the Beatitudes (dictionary), where the ASV, KJV and NIV read ‘blessed’. The blessing answers a clause planted three chapters back: Jesus’s prayer for ‘those who will believe in me through their word’ (17:20, already on these pages) — the people v29 speaks over are, on the Gospel’s own terms, its readers.

Verses 30–31 · ⚠ why was the Gospel of John written? John 20:31 — a purpose statement in Martha’s words, a book that admits it is a selection, and a verb that manuscripts spell two ways

The Gospel states its own purpose, and does it in Martha’s words. ‘That Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God’ (v31) is the same two titles in the same Greek (ho christos ho huios tou theou) as her confession at 11:27 (already on these pages), which that chapter’s closing note planted as the Gospel’s thesis; and ‘life in his name’ closes a ring with the prologue’s ‘to those who believe in his name’ (1:12, already on these pages).

⚠ ‘Many other signs’ that are not ‘written in this book’ (v30) is the Gospel’s own account of itself as a selection. Sēmeion is its standing word for the miracles (dictionary): Cana was ‘the beginning of the signs’ (2:11, already on these pages), and this verse says the count was never complete. Chapter 21 will say the same thing again, at greater length, with a world that could not hold the books (John 21:25, already on these pages).

⚠ The verb pisteuēte, ‘you may believe’, is a point where the manuscripts differ by a single letter: the printed Greek has the present subjunctive, ‘that you may [go on] believing’, where other manuscripts have the aorist, ‘that you may come to believe’, and the difference has been argued to bear on whether the Gospel was written to win outsiders or to hold believers. Scholars do not agree that the tense alone can settle it, and the English renderings do not show the difference: the ASV reads ‘that ye may believe’, the KJV ‘that ye might believe’, the NIV ‘that you may believe’. This translation prints ‘may believe’ and leaves the question open.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Hoi Ioudaioi continues the split of the last two chapters: ‘the Jewish leaders’ for the fear behind the locked doors (v19). Mia tōn sabbatōn is ‘the first day of the week’. Othonia is ‘linen cloths’ and soudarion ‘cloth’ (see the dictionary entry). Mē mou haptou is ‘Do not cling to me’. Pneuma hagion without the article is ‘holy spirit’. Typos is ‘mark’, Didymos is ‘the Twin’, and makarioi is ‘Happy’, as in the Beatitudes. Rabbouni is left as the Aramaic it is, with the Gospel’s own gloss.

Echoes paid. Lazarus’s face-cloth (11:44) is answered by the head-cloth set apart in v7. The garden of 19:41 gets its gardener at v15. The shepherd who ‘calls his own sheep by name’ (10:3) does it at v16. The peace of 14:27 and 16:33 arrives at vv19, 21 and 26; the joy promised at 16:22 at v20; the sending of 17:18 at v21; and ‘the Spirit had not yet been given’ (7:39) at v22. Thomas’s 11:16 scene is finished in v24–28; ‘those who will believe through their word’ (17:20) are called happy in v29; and Martha’s confession (11:27) becomes the Gospel’s own purpose at v31, all already on these pages.

Echoes planted. The disciple Jesus loved, who outruns Peter and ‘saw and believed’ here (vv2–8), is the one who says ‘It is the Lord’ from the boat and is seen following at the end (John 21:7, 20, already on these pages), and chapter 21 names him as the witness who wrote these things (21:24). Thomas appears once more among the group at the lake (21:2), and the books the world could not contain close the Gospel (21:25), all already on these pages.

This chapter ends the Gospel’s first ending; the book’s own sequence runs unbroken from chapter 1 through this chapter — next in sequence is John 21, the morning on the lake shore, the charcoal fire and Peter’s restoration.
