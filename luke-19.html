At Jericho, Jesus calls down a short, rich chief tax collector named Zacchaeus who has climbed a tree to see him, and tells him salvation has come to his house. Because the crowd expects the kingdom of God to appear at once, Jesus tells a parable found only in this Gospel: a nobleman travels to secure a kingship his own citizens oppose, leaving ten slaves ten identical minas to trade with until he returns, then rewards or punishes each by what they made of it and orders his rebellious citizens slaughtered before him. The road to Jerusalem finally ends: the disciples fetch a colt no one has ridden, spread their cloaks before Jesus, and hail him as king, to the objection of some Pharisees in the crowd; Jesus answers that the stones would cry out, then weeps over the city and foretells its coming destruction in specific, siege-shaped detail before entering the temple, driving out the sellers, and teaching there daily while the chief priests and scribes look for a way to destroy him.
c. AD 30, the last stage of the same journey-to-Jerusalem sequence opened at 9:51, now arriving at its destination; the Zacchaeus episode and the parable of the minas are found only in Luke (the minas share a structure, but not a currency or a distribution, with Matthew's separate parable of the talents), while the triumphal entry and the temple's clearing run in parallel with Matthew 21 and Mark 11, and the lament over Jerusalem (vv41-44) is unique to this Gospel.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–10 · ἀρχιτελώνης … υἱὸς Ἀβραάμ architelōnēs … huios Abraam — a job title found nowhere else in Greek literature, a tree this Gospel has already flagged for its own sake, and a phrase spoken to exactly one other outsider in the whole New Testament

⭐⭐⭐ “Chief tax collector” is a genuine hapax — the only occurrence of this word in the whole New Testament, and no clearly earlier example survives in Greek outside it. See the architelōnēs dictionary entry: an ordinary telōnēs bid for the right to collect a toll at one post; an architelōnēs oversaw a whole district of them, which is exactly the kind of post that would put a small city like Jericho — sitting on the balsam and date-palm trade at the edge of the Jordan valley — in reach of real wealth. Zacchaeus’ own report of his fortune two verses later is not a boast invented for the story; the title has already told the reader where the money came from.

⭐⭐ The tree Zacchaeus climbs is the same word this library already singled out for standing apart from a very similar-sounding one. See the sykomorea dictionary entry: this fig-mulberry is a different tree from the sykaminos, the black mulberry Jesus names one chapter earlier as the thing faith could uproot and replant in the sea (17:6, already on these pages) — two similar Greek words, two different trees, one chapter apart. A short man unable to see over a crowd is also, on its own, an echo of the very first verse this Gospel ever wrote about seeing a king: the wise men’s star and the shepherds’ sign both find Jesus without difficulty; here it is a rich man who has to leave the ground to get a look at him.

⭐⭐⭐ “A son of Abraham” is spoken to exactly one other person in the whole New Testament, and both times the hearer is someone the audience would not expect to hear it. See the huios Abraam dictionary entry: the only other time this Gospel — or any New Testament book — calls a living, named individual a child of Abraham to their face is Luke 13:16 (already on these pages), where Jesus calls the crippled woman he heals on the sabbath ‘a daughter of Abraham,’ the only such address in the whole New Testament. A collaborator with Rome and a disabled woman bent double for eighteen years: neither looks, by the standards of the crowd muttering in v7, like someone the covenant obviously belongs to. This translation counted both occurrences directly against the Greek text rather than trusting memory for a claim this absolute.

⚠ Zacchaeus’ declaration in v8 is most naturally read as a present-tense description of an existing practice, not a new promise. The Greek verbs (didōmi, apodidōmi) are plain present-tense forms, which several modern readers take as Zacchaeus defending an already-generous life against the crowd’s assumption that all tax collectors are thieves, rather than announcing a conversion on the spot — though most English versions, including this one, render it as a resolve made in the moment, since the surrounding narrative reads naturally either way and Jesus’ answer in v9 treats something as having genuinely changed today.

⭐ “Seek out and save the lost” is this Gospel’s own summary of an idea it has spent five chapters dramatizing. The shepherd who leaves ninety-nine sheep for one, the woman who sweeps her house for one coin, and the father who runs to meet one returning son (Luke 15, already on these pages) are all told without this sentence attached to any of them; here, for the only time, the narrator lets Jesus state the principle those three parables performed. ⚠ The shelf stays close to the Greek here: the KJV and ASV both read ‘to seek and to save that which was lost,’ and the NWT 1984 keeps the same verbs with one small modernization, ‘to seek and to save what was lost’ — three very different eras of English translation, with no real disagreement to report.

Verses 11–27 · δέκα δούλοις … δέκα μνᾶς … Εὖγε deka doulois … deka mnas … Euge — ten slaves given one identical coin each, a word of praise the New Testament uses nowhere else, and a parable this library has already met once before under a different name

⭐⭐⭐ Every servant starts with the identical stake — a detail the arithmetic of v13 settles and Matthew’s own version of this story does not share. See the mna dictionary entry: ten slaves receive ten minas between them, one mina each, which is why the first two servants each report on ‘YOUR mina’ in the singular (vv16, 18) rather than a plural sum. Matthew’s parable of the talents (25:14–30, already on these pages) gives its three slaves unequal starting capital — five, two, and one talent, deliberately ‘according to each one’s own ability’ — and a talent is worth roughly sixty times a mina, so Matthew’s smallest gift already dwarfs Luke’s largest. Two parables, told to make a similar point about faithfulness with what is entrusted, and neither their capital nor their distribution matches: one story is about equal opportunity and unequal industry, the other about unequal gifts and a single fixed standard.

⭐⭐⭐ “Well done” translates a Greek word used exactly once in the entire New Testament. See the euge dictionary entry: euge is an intensified form of the ordinary eu, and it appears in no other verse of the New Testament at all — not even in Matthew’s own version of this scene, whose master instead says eu, doule agathe kai piste, ‘well [eu], good and faithful slave’ (Matthew 25:21, 23), a different word and a different word order (‘good AND faithful’ there; ‘good’ alone, with faithfulness proved rather than named, here). A single emphatic syllable of praise, spoken once in the whole Bible, to a servant who turned one coin into eleven.

⚠ The setup’s own political shape has a specific and ugly First-century echo that the parable never explains, because its first hearers would not have needed it explained. A nobleman traveling to a distant land to be confirmed as king, opposed by a delegation of his own citizens, is close to the history of Herod the Great’s son Archelaus (see that encyclopedia entry) — who traveled to Rome to have his father’s will confirmed shortly before this same road to Jerusalem was walked, in years within living memory of some of Jesus’ own hearers, while a Jewish delegation followed him to Rome specifically to oppose the appointment. Whether Jesus intends the parallel as a pointed contrast (a king unlike that one) or simply borrows a scenario everyone in the crowd would recognize is a question this translation reports rather than settles; either way, v27’s closing order to slaughter the rebellious citizens in front of the king is a harder image than the parable’s usual reading as an allegory for the return of Christ comfortably explains on its own.

⚠ Verse 11 states its own reason for existing, directly: the parable answers a specific, mistaken expectation — that because Jesus was near Jerusalem, the kingdom of God was about to appear immediately. The nobleman’s own journey, delay and return builds an interval into the story on purpose, against that expectation, before a word of the parable itself is spoken.

Verses 28–36 · πῶλον … ἐφʼ ὃν οὐδεὶς πώποτε ἀνθρώπων ἐκάθισεν pōlon … eph’ hon oudeis pōpote anthrōpōn ekathisen — a young animal no one has ever ridden, and the same two-word password given to two different pairs of disciples one chapter apart

⭐⭐ “On which no one has ever yet sat” states, without naming it, the ancient qualification for an animal fit for sacred use. An animal that had never been put to ordinary work was the standard requirement for a beast used in a solemn or cultic act across the ancient Near East (compare Numbers 19:2 and Deuteronomy 21:3, both already on these pages, of the heifers used in Israel’s own purification rites) — Luke states the fact about the colt and never draws the comparison for the reader himself, leaving the qualification to do its own quiet work.

⚠ The password the disciples are given — “The Lord needs it” — is the identical Greek sentence in all three Synoptic accounts of this same event, with one telling difference. Ho kyrios autou chreian echei here (vv31, 34) matches Mark’s own version of this scene letter for letter (Mark 11:3, already on these pages). Matthew’s parallel changes exactly one word: autōn, ‘THEIR need,’ plural, instead of autou, ‘his’ (Matthew 21:3, already on these pages) — consistent with Matthew’s own two donkeys against Luke and Mark’s one. A short, sufficient claim of authority, worded almost identically by two Gospel writers and adjusted by exactly the pronoun a third writer’s own extra animal requires.

⚠ This scene supplies no palm branches at all — only cloaks spread on the road, the detail Matthew and Mark also keep. The palm branches belong to John’s own account of this same entry (John 12:13, not yet on these pages), not to any of the three Synoptic Gospels.

Verses 37–40 · ἐν οὐρανῷ εἰρήνη καὶ δόξα ἐν ὑψίστοις en ouranō eirēnē kai doxa en hypsistois — the one Synoptic account of this scene with no “Hosanna” in it at all, and an angels’ song from this Gospel’s own first chapters, run in reverse

⭐⭐⭐ Luke is the only Gospel account of the triumphal entry that never once uses the word “Hosanna” — a fact this translation checked directly against the Greek rather than assuming. The word occurs exactly five times in the whole New Testament, and all five are in the other three accounts: twice in Matthew (21:9, 15, both already on these pages), twice in Mark (11:9–10, already on these pages), once in John (12:13, not yet on these pages). Luke’s crowd shouts something else entirely.

⭐⭐⭐ What Luke’s crowd shouts in its place is not new to this Gospel — it is the birth angels’ own song, with its two halves swapped. Compare Luke 2:14 (already on these pages): ‘Glory to God en hypsistois [in the highest], and on earth peace among people he has favored.’ Here at v38: ‘Peace in heaven, and glory en hypsistois [in the highest]’ — the identical rare phrase en hypsistois anchoring both lines, and the same two nouns, glory and peace, but heaven and earth have changed places. At the birth, glory stayed above while peace came down to earth; here, entering Jerusalem for the last time, the crowd’s peace is relocated back up to heaven — which the following section (vv41–44) makes an ache rather than a coincidence: the peace this Gospel promised the earth in chapter 2 is precisely what the city below is about to fail to recognize. ⚠ Matthew and Mark both instead close their crowd’s own shout with ‘Hosanna en tois hypsistois’ (Matthew 21:9; Mark 11:10) — the identical phrase ‘in the highest,’ filled with a different word.

⭐⭐ “The stones would cry out” is this Gospel’s own line alone among the four Gospels, and it reaches for an image the Hebrew prophets had already used for a very different kind of cry. Habakkuk 2:11 (not yet on these pages) has ‘the stone will cry out from the wall’ as evidence AGAINST a man who has built his house on violence and unjust gain — there, the stones testify to a wrong. Jesus turns the same image toward praise: if human voices fell silent, creation itself would not let this moment go unmarked either way.

⚠ ‘King’ here is the crowd’s own addition to Psalm 118:26’s ‘Blessed is he who comes in the name of Jehovah’ (not yet on these pages, quoted without it by Matthew, Mark and John alike) — the only one of the four Gospel accounts of this scene to insert the royal title directly into the psalm quotation itself, rather than leaving it implied by context.

Verses 41–44 · ἔκλαυσεν … τὸν καιρὸν τῆς ἐπισκοπῆς σου eklausen … ton kairon tēs episkopēs sou — a louder verb for weeping than the New Testament’s most famous tear, and a siege described in enough plain detail that a reader can watch it happen forty years later

⭐⭐⭐ “Wept” here is a loud, audible verb — not the quiet, single-tear verb this library has already flagged at its one other New Testament appearance. See the klaiō dictionary entry: eklausen is the same verb Peter uses when he ‘wept bitterly’ after denying Jesus three times, later in this Gospel (22:62, already on these pages) — open, audible grief, the kind Jesus himself says will be reversed for ‘you who weep now’ (6:21, already on these pages). John’s Gospel instead uses edakrysen of Jesus at Lazarus’ tomb (John 11:35, not yet on these pages), built on the noun for a single tear — a real distinction in the underlying Greek that the shared English verb ‘wept’ quietly erases in both places.

⭐⭐⭐ The siege Jesus describes matches the actual Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70 in enough specific, ordinary detail that this passage is a standing test case in how a reader dates this Gospel. A barricade thrown up around the city, encirclement from every side, the city leveled with its children inside it, not one stone left on another — all four details are attested features of the historian Josephus’ own eyewitness account of Titus’ siege, a full generation after Jesus is reported to have said this. Readers who take these verses as a genuine, specific prophecy read them as exactly that; readers who date Luke’s Gospel after AD 70 read the passage’s precision as a writer describing an event that had already happened. Both readings are reported here; the text itself supplies no date for when it was written down.

⚠ “The time of your visitation” carries a word this translation keeps deliberately ambiguous, because the Greek itself is. Episkopē can name either a gracious visit — God drawing near to help, the sense in Luke 1:68 and 1:78 (already on these pages, of Zechariah’s prophecy that God ‘has visited and redeemed his people’) — or an inspection that judges, the sense that fits a passage about a coming disaster for not recognizing what has arrived. Jerusalem's own visitation, this chapter's own bookend suggests, was Jesus himself, entering the city three verses earlier to a crowd that (mostly) knew what it was watching, while the city's own leadership did not.

Verses 45–48 · οἶκος προσευχῆς … σπήλαιον λῃστῶν oikos proseuchēs … spēlaion lēstōn — the New Testament’s briefest account of the clearing of the temple, and a scripture quotation Mark completes that Luke leaves cut short

⭐⭐⭐ Luke tells the whole clearing of the temple in two verses — the most compressed of any Gospel’s account of this scene. Matthew (21:12–13, already on these pages) and Mark (11:15–17, already on these pages) both specify overturned money-changers’ tables and the seats of those selling doves, and both describe the episode across several verses of action; Luke names no furniture, no specific merchandise, and no crowd reaction to the act itself, moving directly from ‘he began driving out those who were selling’ to the quotation that justifies it. The same compression runs through the whole chapter’s final scene: where Matthew and Mark both let the episode breathe, Luke closes it in four verses total (45–48) and immediately notes that Jesus kept teaching in the temple every day afterward, as if the confrontation itself were less the point than what continued after it.

⚠ The quotation Jesus makes is genuinely two Old Testament verses spliced into one sentence, and Mark keeps a clause of the first one that Luke drops. ‘A house of prayer’ is Isaiah 56:7 (not yet on these pages), which in its own context continues ‘for all peoples’ — a phrase Mark’s own version of this scene keeps in full, ‘a house of prayer for all the nations’ (Mark 11:17), and Luke omits entirely, printing only ‘a house of prayer’ with no further qualifier. ‘A den of robbers’ is Jeremiah 7:11 (not yet on these pages), spoken there against a temple whose worshippers thought sacrifice could paper over active injustice elsewhere in their lives — the accusation is not that money was being handled in the temple courts (some exchange and sale was a practical necessity for pilgrims bringing offerings from a distance), but that the concession to convenience had curdled into a hiding place for wrongdoing.

⚠ The chapter’s very last sentence supplies the reason the plot to destroy Jesus stalls for now: not a change of heart in the chief priests and scribes, but a practical problem — ‘all the people hung on his words, listening’ — the same crowd whose praise the Pharisees tried to silence twelve verses earlier is now the one thing standing between Jesus and his enemies’ stated intention to destroy him.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: minas for mna (vv13, 16, 18, 20, 24, 25), never conflated with the ‘talents’ of Matthew’s own, differently structured parable; wept for the loud, public verb klaiō (v41), flagged apart from John’s quieter dakryō; and chief tax collector for the hapax architelōnēs (v2), kept distinct from the ordinary telōnēs this Gospel has used of Levi and of the parable’s own praying tax collector.

Echoes paid. The sykomorea flagged apart from the sykaminos at 17:6 is finally climbed, by name, at v4. And the angels’ own song from Luke 2:14 — glory in the highest, peace on earth — returns at v38 with its two halves reversed: peace now in heaven, glory still in the highest.

Echoes planted. Asked by what authority he is doing all this, Jesus answers a hostile question with a counter-question about John's own baptism — heaven's authority or man's — that the chief priests and scribes find they cannot safely answer either way (20:1–8, not yet on these pages). And a parable told directly against those same leaders follows immediately: a vineyard, tenant farmers who beat and kill a whole series of the owner's servants, and finally his own son (20:9–19, not yet on these pages) — the plot this chapter's own last verse already named, now given its own story.

Next in sequence: Luke 20 — the chief priests and scribes demand to know by what authority Jesus is acting, and are trapped by his counter-question about John the Baptist; a landowner's tenant farmers beat and kill a series of servants sent to collect his share of the harvest, and finally his own son.
