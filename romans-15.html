The same letter, the same dictation, still from Corinth. Chapter 14 ended on faith and the sentence that whatever is not from faith is sin; chapter 15 begins with the strong bearing the weaknesses of the weak, then Paul turns to himself: a servant of Christ to the nations, hindered for years from coming to Rome, now on his way to Jerusalem with a gift from Macedonia and Achaia, and planning from there to go on to Spain.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 14. The chapter names no date and no event that fixes one. Its own clock is the journey: the next step is Jerusalem with the collection, and after that Paul plans to come to Rome and go on to Spain.

Verses 1–3 · ἡμεῖς οἱ δυνατοὶ τὰ ἀσθενήματα τῶν ἀδυνάτων βαστάζειν … ἑαυτῷ ἤρεσεν hēmeis hoi dynatoi ta asthenēmata tōn adynatōn bastazein … heautō ērese — Bearing the weak, and Christ who did not please himself — Romans 15:1–3
“We who are strong ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak” (opheilomen de hēmeis hoi dynatoi ta asthenēmata tōn adynatōn bastazein, Romans 15:1). The verb bastazō means to carry a load, to lift and bear what another cannot carry alone. The strong are the ones who carry, and the weak are the ones carried. The noun asthenēmata, weaknesses, is from the same root as asthenēs, weak, from the same root as the verb in 14:1 for the one who is weak in faith. Paul gives the strong the job of the porter, not the judge. The same verb is at Galatians 6:2 (not yet on these pages), where people are told to carry one another’s burdens. The shelf: NIV “bear with the failings of the weak,” KJV “bear the infirmities of the weak,” TLB “we must bear the “burden” of being considerate of the doubts and fears of others,” and NWT 1984 “bear the weaknesses of those not strong.”

“Christ did not please himself” (kai gar ho Christos ouch heautō ēresen, Romans 15:3). The verb areskō, to please, runs through both verses. Verse 2 tells each person to please the neighbor for his good, toward building up, and the word for building up is oikodomē, the word of 14:19. Verse 3 says Christ did not please himself, and the proof is a quotation from Psalm 69:9 (not yet on these pages): “The reproaches of those who reproached you have fallen on me.” In the Psalm the speaker suffers insults for God’s sake, and Paul puts Christ in that place. The shelf: KJV “For even Christ pleased not himself,” and for the quotation, NWT 1984 “those who were reproaching you” against NWT 2013 “those reproaching you.” The Greek is a present participle, so both English forms are possible; the 2013 revision is a choice about tense, not a different text.

Verses 4–6 · ὅσα γὰρ προεγράφη … ἡ ὑπομονή … ἡ παράκλησις … ὁμοθυμαδὸν ἐν ἑνὶ στόματι hosa gar proegraphē … hypomonē … paraklēsis … homothymadon en heni stomati — Written for our teaching, and one mouth — Romans 15:4–6
“Whatever was written beforehand was written for our teaching” (hosa gar proegraphē, Romans 15:4). The verb prographō means written before, in the older Scriptures, and Paul says those writings were written for us: eis tēn hēmeteran didaskalian, for our teaching. Two words carry the verse: hypomonē, endurance, and paraklēsis, encouragement. Verse 5 gives both to God. The shelf shows how the word for endurance is rendered: NIV “the endurance taught in the Scriptures and the encouragement they provide,” KJV “through patience and comfort of the scriptures,” NWT 2013 “through our endurance and through the comfort from the Scriptures.”

“With one mind and one mouth” (homothymadon en heni stomati, Romans 15:6). The adverb homothymadon, with one accord, is the word Luke uses for the early believers gathered together in prayer (Acts 2:46 and Acts 1:14 on these pages). Paul asks for the same unity, and the mouth is the one place where it is seen. The shelf: ASV “that with one accord ye may with one mouth glorify the God and Father,” KJV “with one mind and one mouth glorify God,” NIV “with one mind and one voice,” and NWT 2013 “so that unitedly you may with one voice glorify.” The versions differ on whether the accord is a mind, a purpose or a unity; the Greek word is the same in all of them.

Verses 7–9 · προσλαμβάνεσθε ἀλλήλους … ὁ Χριστὸς διάκονον … περιτομῆς … ἐξομολογήσομαί σοι ἐν ἔθνεσι proslambanesthe allēlous … Christon diakonon peritomēs … exomologēsomai soi en ethnesi — Welcome one another, the servant of the circumcision, and the nations glorifying God — Romans 15:7–9
“Welcome one another” (proslambanesthe allēlous, Romans 15:7). The verb is the one of 14:1 and 14:3, proslambanō, to take someone to oneself. In chapter 14 it was a rule about one case, the weak brother. Here it becomes a command for the whole congregation, and it is grounded in Christ’s own welcome, which was for God’s glory. The shelf: NIV “Accept one another, then, just as Christ accepted you,” KJV “Wherefore receive ye one another, as Christ also received us,” and NWT 2013 “So welcome one another, just as the Christ also welcomed you.”

“A servant of the circumcision” (Christon diakonon peritomēs gegenēsthai, Romans 15:8). Peritomē, circumcision, stands here for the Jewish people, as it does in the argument of Romans 4, where circumcision is the sign given to Abraham. Diakonos, servant, is the word for the servant of God who holds the sword in 13:4, and here it is used of Christ. The verse ties together two things: the promises made to the fathers, and the nations glorifying God in verse 9. The quotation at the end of verse 9 is from Psalm 18:49 (not yet on these pages): “Therefore I will acknowledge you among the nations, and sing to your name.” The shelf for the first clause: KJV “Jesus Christ was a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God,” NIV “a servant of the Jews on behalf of God’s truth,” and ASV “a minister of the circumcision for the truth of God.” NIV names the people where the Greek names the circumcision, and the note above says why.

Verses 10–12 · Εὐφράνθητε, ἔθνη … αἰνεῖτε … ἔσται ἡ ῥίζα τοῦ Ἰεσσαί Euphranthēte, ethnē … aineite … estai hē rhiza tou Iessai — Three quotations in three verses: rejoice, praise, the root of Jesse — Romans 15:10–12
“Rejoice, nations, with his people” (Euphranthēte, ethnē, meta tou laou autou, Romans 15:10). Paul introduces the line with kai palin, and again, and it is the Greek of Deuteronomy 32:43, the song of Moses, which is on these pages (Deuteronomy 32). The shelf: NIV “Rejoice, you Gentiles, with his people,” Douay-Rheims “Rejoice, ye Gentiles, with his people,” and NWT 2013 “Be glad, you nations, with his people.” The English versions say Gentiles where the Greek has ethnē, nations, and this translation keeps “nations” as it did in verse 9.

“Praise the Lord, all you nations” (Aineite, panta ta ethnē, ton kyrion, Romans 15:11) and “the root of Jesse” (estai hē rhiza tou Iessai, Romans 15:12). The second quotation is from Psalm 117:1 (Greek 116:1, not yet on these pages), and the third is from Isaiah 11:10 (not yet on these pages), which the Greek introduces as Isaiah’s word. Three books of the Hebrew Scriptures are quoted in three verses, and each one is about the nations: the Law, the Psalms and the Prophets. The verb of verse 12, elpiousin, to hope, closes the chain and goes back to the hope of verses 4 and 13. The shelf for verse 12: NIV “The Root of Jesse will spring up, one who will arise to rule over the nations; in him the Gentiles will hope,” NWT 2013 “on him nations will rest their hope.” For verse 11, Douay-Rheims “Praise the Lord, all ye Gentiles; and magnify him, all ye people,” and NIV “let all the peoples extol him.”

Verses 13–16 · ὁ θεὸς τῆς ἐλπίδος … πέπεισμαι … τολμηρότερον … λειτουργὸν … ἱερουργοῦντα ho theos tēs elpidos … pepeismai … tolmēroteron … leitourgon … hierourgounta — The God of hope, a priest of the good news, and the offering of the nations — Romans 15:13–16
“The God of hope” (ho de theos tēs elpidos, Romans 15:13). This blessing is the twin of verse 5. There it was the God of endurance and encouragement; here it is the God who gives elpis, hope, and fills the readers with joy and peace in believing. The result is that they abound in hope, and the power that makes them do so is the holy spirit, not their own resources. The shelf: NIV “overflow with hope by the power of the Holy Spirit,” and NWT 2013 “abound in hope with power of holy spirit.”

“A priest of the good news of God” (leitourgon Christou Iēsou … hierourgounta to euangelion tou theou, Romans 15:16). Paul calls himself a leitourgos, a public servant, of Christ Jesus to the nations, and then uses the verb hierourgeō, to perform sacred service, to act as a priest, for the work of the good news. The offering is the nations themselves, and it is made acceptable because it is sanctified by the holy spirit. The shelf: NIV “He gave me the priestly duty of proclaiming the gospel of God, so that the Gentiles might become an offering acceptable to God, sanctified by the Holy Spirit,” and NWT 2013 “I am engaging in the holy work of the good news of God, so that these nations might be an acceptable offering, sanctified with holy spirit.” Verse 15 says Paul has written boldly on some points, and the grace of God is the reason, which he names in the same breath.

Verses 17–21 · ἔχω οὖν τὴν καύχησιν … οὐ γὰρ τολμήσω … σημείων καὶ τεράτων … πεπληρωκέναι … οὐχ ὅπου ὠνομάσθη Χριστός echō oun tēn kauchēsin … ou gar tolmēsō … sēmeiōn kai terata … peplērōkenai … ouch hopou ōnomasthē Christos — A boast in Christ, Illyricum, and not on another’s foundation — Romans 15:17–21
“A boast in Christ Jesus” (echō oun tēn kauchēsin en Christō Iēsou, Romans 15:17). Paul’s boast is in what Christ has done through him, not in himself. Verse 18 names the thing: katērgasato, what Christ accomplished through him, to bring the nations to obedience by word and deed. In verse 19 the power is of signs and wonders, and the book of Acts gives an example: Acts 19:11 says God worked unusual miracles through Paul’s hands. The shelf: NIV “I will not venture to speak of anything except what Christ has accomplished through me,” KJV “I will not dare to speak of any of those things which Christ hath not wrought by me,” and NWT 2013 “I will not presume to speak about anything except what Christ has done through me.”

“From Jerusalem and round about to Illyricum” (apo Ierousalēm kai kyklō mechri tou Illyrikou, Romans 15:19). Illyricum was a Roman province along the eastern Adriatic coast, north of Greece. Paul does not say that he founded churches there, only that he has fully preached the good news from Jerusalem around to it. The shelf: KJV “I have fully preached the gospel of Christ,” ASV “I have fully preached the gospel of Christ,” and NWT 2013 “I have thoroughly preached the good news about the Christ.” Verse 20 says his aim was never to build on another person’s themelion, foundation, and verse 21 quotes Isaiah 52:15 (not yet on these pages): “Those to whom no news of him came will see.” The shelf for verse 21: NIV “Those who were not told about him will see, and those who have not heard will understand.”

Verses 22–24 · ἐνεκοπτόμην … ἐπιποθίαν … τὴν Σπανίαν enekoptomēn … epipothian … tēn Spanian — Hindered from coming, and the way to Spain — Romans 15:22–24
“I was often hindered from coming to you” (Dio kai enekoptomēn ta polla tou elthein pros hymas, Romans 15:22). The verb enkoptō is to cut in, to hinder. Romans 1:13 uses a related verb, kōlyō, to prevent, when Paul says he has often wanted to come and has been prevented. Verse 23 says that now there is no more place for him in these regions, and that he has longed to come to them for years. The shelf: NIV “This is why I have often been hindered from coming to you,” KJV “For which cause also I have been much hindered from coming to you,” and ASV “Wherefore also I was hindered these many times from coming to you.”

“When I go to Spain” (hōs an poreuōmai eis tēn Spanian, Romans 15:24). Spania is Spain, a province at the far western edge of the empire, and the chapter gives it no more than a clause. Paul hopes to see the Romans on the way and to be propemphthēnai, sent on, by them, after he has first enjoyed their company for a while. The plan is set against the collection in verses 25–28. The shelf: NWT 2013 “I hope that I will see you and be accompanied partway there by you,” NIV “I plan to do so when I go to Spain,” and KJV “Whensoever I take my journey into Spain, I will come to you.” The text does not say that Paul ever reached Spain, and the note does not claim it.

Verses 25–29 · νυνὶ δὲ πορεύομαι … διακονῶν τοῖς ἁγίοις … κοινωνίαν τινὰ … ὀφειλέται … σφραγισάμενος nyni de poreuomai … diakonōn tois hagiois … koinōnian tina … opheiletai … sphragisamenos — The collection for the holy ones, and the debt owed to Jerusalem — Romans 15:25–29
“I am going to Jerusalem to serve the holy ones” (nyni de poreuomai eis Ierousalēm diakonōn tois hagiois, Romans 15:25). The hagioi, holy ones, are the believers in Jerusalem, and the verb diakoneō, to serve, is the verb for the trip itself. Paul says in verse 26 that Macedonia and Achaia were pleased to make koinōnian tina, some sharing, for the poor among them; the word for sharing is koinōnia, the same word that means fellowship. The Greek of Acts shows the trip itself: Acts 20:4 names his companions from Macedonia and Asia, and Acts 24:17 has Paul say he came to Jerusalem to bring alms to his nation. The shelf for verse 26: NIV “Macedonia and Achaia were pleased to make a contribution for the poor among the Lord’s people in Jerusalem,” KJV “it hath pleased them of Macedonia and Achaia to make a certain contribution for the poor saints,” and NWT 2013 “have been pleased to share their things by a contribution to the poor among the holy ones in Jerusalem.”

“They owe it to them” (opheiletai eisin autōn, Romans 15:27). The debt is argued from the gift of the Gentiles. If the nations have shared in the spiritual things of the Jews, they owe them a share in material things. The verb leitourgeō, to serve, is used of the material part, and sphragisamenos, having sealed, in verse 28 is the word for handing over a sealed gift safely. The shelf for verse 27: NIV “they owe it to the Jews to share with them their material blessings,” KJV “their duty is also to minister unto them in carnal things,” and ASV “they owe it to them also to minister unto them in carnal things.” Verse 29 ends the section on a note of blessing: Paul expects to come with the plērōma, the fullness, of the blessing of Christ.

Verses 30–33 · παρακαλῶ δὲ ὑμᾶς … συναγωνίσασθαί μοι … ῥυσθῶ ἀπὸ τῶν ἀπειθούντων … ὁ θεὸς τῆς εἰρήνης parakalō de hymas … synagōnisasthai moi … rhysthō apo tōn apeithountōn … ho theos tēs eirēnēs — Strive together in prayer, rescued from the disobedient in Judea — Romans 15:30–33
“Strive together with me in prayers” (synagōnisasthai moi en tais proseuchais, Romans 15:30). The verb synagōnizomai, to fight alongside, to struggle together, is the verb of an athlete or a soldier. Paul asks for the readers to take part in his struggle, and the basis of the request is the Lord Jesus Christ and the love of the spirit. The shelf: NIV “I urge you, brothers and sisters, by our Lord Jesus Christ and by the love of the Spirit, to join me in my struggle by praying to God for me,” ASV “that ye strive together with me in your prayers to God for me,” and NWT 2013 “that you exert yourselves with me in prayers to God for me.”

“Rescued from the disobedient in Judea” (hina rhysthō apo tōn apeithountōn en tē Ioudaia, Romans 15:31). Paul expects danger in Judea. The verb rhyomai, to rescue, is the verb of Romans 7:24, “who will rescue me.” The chapter does not say what the danger was; the book of Acts shows that Paul was arrested in Jerusalem after his return (Acts 21). The shelf: NIV “Pray that I may be kept safe from the unbelievers in Judea,” KJV “That I may be delivered from them that do not believe in Judaea,” and ASV “that I may be delivered from them that are disobedient in Judaea.” Verse 32 hopes to come with joy and be refreshed together, and verse 33 ends with the God of peace.
