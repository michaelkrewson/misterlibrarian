Sixteen tight verses, all in a single unbroken paragraph like the chapter before it: every slaughter of an ox, lamb, or goat must be brought to the tent of meeting rather than killed anywhere in the open field, closing off sacrifice to the goat-demons some had drifted toward. Burnt-offerings and sacrifices generally get the same rule. Then the chapter turns to blood itself — never to be eaten, because the life is in the blood, and the blood belongs on the altar, making atonement. Hunted game's blood is poured out and covered with dust rather than carried to an altar at all. A carcass or an animal killed by a predator, if eaten, brings the same 'unclean until evening' as the rest of this block.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus. Its centralized-slaughter law (vv3-9) is explicitly tied to the wilderness camp's own geography — one tent at the center of a small, mobile community — a law this project expects to see revised once Israel is settled across a whole land (Deuteronomy 12, not yet on these pages). Still inside the same Sinai window as the rest of this block (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

Leviticus 17 meaning — why every slaughter had to happen at the tabernacle door

Verses 3–7 legislate something stricter than it first appears: an ox, lamb, or goat killed ANYWHERE — inside the camp or outside it — without being brought first to the tent of meeting counts as bloodshed, the same verb (dam yechashev, ‘blood shall be counted’) used elsewhere for murder. Read strictly, this requires every slaughter of these three species, not only sacrificial killing, to pass through the tent door as a peace-offering. That reading is not this project's invention: Deuteronomy 12:15, 20–24 (not yet on these pages) explicitly RELAXES it once Israel is scattered across a land too large for one central altar to reach every table — itself the clearest evidence that the strict version belonged to the wilderness camp's own geography, one tent literally at the center of every family's walk.

‘The goat-demons’ (v7, se'irim) resolves a promise this project's own Azazel dictionary entry made at Leviticus 16 (already on these pages): the case that Azazel names a being, not merely a place or an etymology, rested partly on this exact verse — Leviticus elsewhere legislating against sacrifice to goat-demons. That verse is this one. ⚠ The shelf splits three ways on the word: KJV and Geneva both import the Greco-Roman ‘satyrs,’ a mythological borrowing the Hebrew itself does not make; NIV reads ‘goat idols,’ naming the object but losing the demonic sense; ASV and NWT both keep the plainer ‘he-goats,’ declining to commit to either an idol or a demon. ‘Prostitute themselves’ (zonim achareihem) keeps the Torah's own blunt sexual-infidelity idiom for idolatry rather than softening it — the same verb this project will meet again wherever Israel is charged with chasing after other gods.

One altar, one law — burnt-offerings held to the same rule

Verses 8–9 extend vv3–7's rule beyond the three named animals to any burnt-offering or sacrifice at all, offered by native Israelite or resident foreigner alike — closing off, in two verses, any reading of the first law as narrowly about butchery rather than about worship. One tent, one altar, no exceptions by species, offering type, or the offerer's own origin.

Why the Bible bans eating blood — ‘the life is in the blood’

Verses 10–12 do not institute a new law so much as give Sinai's own legal teeth to one already stated at Genesis 9:4 (already on these pages) — ‘flesh with its life still in it, its blood, you must not eat’ — addressed there to Noah and all his descendants, before Israel existed as a people. The Torah is explicit, twice over (here and there), that this prohibition predates Sinai; Leviticus enforces what Genesis already announced.

⚠ V11's own wording is this project's clearest single proof text for its own nefesh entry (fixed at Genesis 1:20, already on these pages): ‘the life (nefesh) of the flesh is in the blood… it is the blood, by reason of the life (nefesh), that makes atonement.’ A nefesh that can be poured out, dashed against an altar, and physically located inside blood is not an immaterial soul housed in a body — it is the animating life itself, exactly the reading that dictionary entry argues from Psalm 23:3. This chapter is the Torah legislating its own vocabulary.

Trefah meaning — the ancient root of kosher's ‘treif’

Hunted game (v13) gets a different treatment than domestic slaughter: its blood is poured out and covered with dust, not carried to an altar at all, since a wild animal taken in the field was never brought live to the tent door in the first place. The requirement is the same principle in a different setting — blood always returned to the ground or the altar, never eaten — applied to a case the tent-door law (vv3–9) does not reach.

Trefah (v15, ‘what is torn by wild animals’) is the direct ancestor of the Yiddish and modern Hebrew ‘treif,’ still the standing word for anything not kosher — though the word's OWN sense in this verse is narrower than its later career, naming one specific way an animal's death disqualifies its meat. KJV and ASV both read ‘torn of beasts’; NIV's ‘torn by wild animals’ is the closer match to this project's own choice. Its partner nevelah, ‘what dies on its own,’ already governs TOUCHING a carcass at Leviticus 11:39 (already on these pages); this verse completes the law by legislating the consequence of actually EATING one.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 17 both run 1–16 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Like Leviticus 16 immediately before it, the chapter carries no internal paragraph break at all, a single Masoretic unit closed by one open break at v16 — the second consecutive chapter told in one unbroken breath. ‘The goat-demons’ renders se'irim plainly rather than importing KJV's Greco-Roman ‘satyrs,’ a mythological borrowing the Hebrew itself does not make. ‘The life of the flesh is in the blood’ is kept literal throughout, preserving the repeated nefesh this chapter leans on. ‘Trefah’ is newly entered, paired with the already-established nevelah.

Echoes paid. This project's own Azazel dictionary entry (already on these pages), which cited this chapter's se'irim in advance as grammatical evidence for reading Azazel as a being rather than merely a place; Genesis 9:4's Noahide blood law (already on these pages), now given Sinai's own legal form; and Leviticus 11:39's nevelah-touching law (already on these pages), completed here by nevelah's EATING law.

Echoes planted. Deuteronomy 12's own relaxation of this chapter's centralized-slaughter law (not yet on these pages) is named directly above, the clearest forward acknowledgment this project has made of a law it expects to see revised later in the very same Torah. And the Jerusalem council's letter to Gentile believers — abstain from food sacrificed to idols, from blood, and from what is strangled (Acts 15:20, 29, not yet on these pages) — draws on this chapter's own paired concerns, idol-sacrifice and blood, in that order.
