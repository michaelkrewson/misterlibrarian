David asks whether anyone is left of the house of Saul to whom he may do kindness for Jonathan’s sake; Ziba, a servant of that house, names Jonathan’s son, lame in his feet, in the house of Machir son of Ammiel at Lo-debar; David has him fetched, tells him not to be afraid, gives back all the field of Saul and sets him to eat at the king’s table continually; Ziba, his sons and his servants are to work the ground for him; Mephibosheth’s young son is Mica, and he lives in Jerusalem, lame in both feet.
After the wars of 2 Samuel 8; the chapter gives no date and does not say how long David has reigned. Mephibosheth was five at Gilboa (4:4) and now has a young son.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · Kindness for Jonathan’s sake — “the kindness of God”, Ziba, and a lame son in Lo-debar

Kindness for Jonathan’s sake. “Is there anyone still left of the house of Saul, that I may do kindness with him for Jonathan’s sake?”: chesed is the word, and the verb is the one Jonathan asked for in the field, “will you not — if I am still alive — do with me the kindness of Jehovah, so that I do not die?” and “you shall not cut off your kindness from my house forever” (1 Samuel 20:14–15, already on these pages), with the oath that sealed it, “Jehovah shall be between me and you, and between my offspring and your offspring, forever” (20:42). David swore a second oath of the same kind to Saul himself, that he would not cut off Saul’s seed or destroy his name out of his father’s house (1 Samuel 24:21–22, already on these pages). Abner had named the same deed from the other side, “today I do kindness with the house of Saul your father” (2 Samuel 3:8, already on these pages). “And I will do with him kindness” (ve’e‘eseh immo chesed) stands in two verses, the first and the third of this chapter, so the question is asked twice in the same words. The ASV has “that I may show him kindness for Jonathan’s sake”; the KJV “that I may shew him kindness”; the NIV “to whom I can show kindness for Jonathan’s sake”; the NWT 1984 “exercise loving-kindness toward him”; the Douay “shew kindness to him”; the Geneva “show him mercy”; and the TLB turns the deed into a promise, “as he had promised Prince Jonathan”. The Greek has eleos, “mercy”.

The kindness of God. “Is there not still a man of the house of Saul, that I may do with him the kindness of God?”: David asks a second time, and adds what the first question left out. “The kindness of God” stands in two verses. They are this one and Psalm 52:10. Either it is kindness of the kind God shows, undeserved and unrepaid, or kindness sworn in God’s name, the “kindness of Jehovah” that Jonathan asked for; the Hebrew does not say which, and the oath of 1 Samuel 20 is why both readings are good. The ASV and the KJV have “the kindness of God”; the NWT 1984 “the loving-kindness of God”; the NIV “God’s kindness”; the Douay and the Geneva “the mercy of God”; the TLB “to fulfill a sacred vow by being kind to him”. The Greek is eleos theou. The Hebrew of verse 1 asks “is there yet any who is left”, and of verse 3 “is there not yet a man”; the Greek turns both into “is there yet a man left”.

Ziba. “And the house of Saul had a servant whose name was Ziba; and they called him to David”: the Hebrew does not say who “they” are. The king’s answer to the question “Are you Ziba?” is “Your servant”, avdekha, the same word Mephibosheth will use of himself in verses 6 and 8. The NIV has “At your service”; the NWT 1984 “I am your servant”; the ASV “Thy servant is he”. The Greek has pais, “a servant”, and the name Siba. Ziba comes back with a pair of saddled donkeys when David flees Absalom (2 Samuel 16:1–4, now on these pages).

Lame in his feet. “There is still a son of Jonathan, lame in his feet”: “lame in his feet” (nekheh raglayim) stands in two verses. They are this one and 2 Samuel 4:4 (already on these pages), where the nurse fled with the five-year-old and he fell. Ziba does not give his name; David has to ask where he is. The ASV has “who is lame of his feet”; the KJV “which is lame on his feet”; the NWT 1984 “lame in the feet”; the Douay and the Geneva “lame of his feet”; the NIV “he is lame in both feet”, which is the wording of verse 13 and not of this verse; and the TLB “Jonathan’s lame son is still alive”. The Greek has peplēgōs tous podas, “struck in the feet”.

Machir son of Ammiel, in Lo-debar. “He is in the house of Machir son of Ammiel, in Lo-debar”: “Machir son of Ammiel” stands in three verses. They are verses 4 and 5, and the verse in which he brings beds and food to David at Mahanaim when David flees (2 Samuel 17:27, now on these pages). He is not the Machir son of Manasseh. Lo-debar is on the east side of the Jordan, near Mahanaim, where Ish-bosheth’s kingdom was made; its site is not known and the name is uncertain, “no word”, “nothing” or “no pasture”, and the prophet Amos has the same sounds in “you who rejoice in a thing of nothing” (lelo davar, Amos 6:13). The man who gave a house to Saul’s grandson is the same who gave beds to David. The NIV has “Makir” and “Lo Debar”; the KJV “Lodebar”; the Douay “Lodabar”; the TLB “At the home of Machir”. The Greek has Ladabar, and writes Machir for the man. Mephibosheth has been hidden there since he was five years old.

Verses 5–8 · Mephibosheth fetched — “do not be afraid”, Saul’s field given back, bread at the king’s table, and “a dead dog such as I”

Sent and took him. “And King David sent and took him from the house of Machir son of Ammiel, from Lo-debar”: the verb is vayyikkachehu, “and took him”. The ASV has “fetched him out of the house of Machir”; the NIV “had him brought”; the NWT 1984 “sent and took him”; the Douay “brought him out of the house of Machir”; the Geneva “took him out of the house of Machir”. The next verse gives him his whole line, “Mephibosheth son of Jonathan son of Saul”, as if he were being announced, and the TLB adds “Saul’s grandson” to say what the line means.

Fell on his face. “And fell on his face and prostrated himself”: “and fell on his face and prostrated himself” stands in one verse. It is the verb of the fall in 2 Samuel 4:4 (already on these pages), “he fell and became lame”: once he fell in a nurse’s haste; now he falls on purpose. The ASV has “did obeisance”; the KJV and the Geneva “did reverence”; the NIV “he bowed down to pay him honor”; the NWT 1984 “fell upon his face and prostrated himself”; the Douay “fell on his face and worshipped”; the TLB “bowing low before him”. David says his name, and he answers “Look — your servant”, hinneh avdekha, where the NIV has “At your service” and the NWT 1984 “Here is your servant”.

Do not be afraid. “Do not be afraid, for I will surely do kindness with you for Jonathan your father’s sake”: al tira is what Jonathan said to David at Horesh, “Do not be afraid, for the hand of Saul my father will not find you; and you will be king over Israel, and I will be second to you” (1 Samuel 23:17, already on these pages), so the words come back to the next generation in the same shape: do not be afraid, for. “I will surely do” is the doubled form asoh e’eseh. The ASV and the Geneva have “I will surely show thee kindness”; the NIV “I will surely show you kindness”; the NWT 1984 “without fail”; the Douay “I will surely shew thee mercy”; the TLB “because of my vow to your father Jonathan”. The Greek has poiōn poiēsō.

All the field of Saul your father. “And I will give back to you all the field of Saul your father”: the Hebrew says “father” for Saul, who is his grandfather; the Hebrew av is any forefather. The NIV has “your grandfather Saul”; the NWT 1984 “Saul your grandfather”; the ASV “Saul thy father”; the Greek spells it out, “Saul father of your father”. “Field” is singular, sadeh; the KJV and the ASV have “all the land”, the Geneva “all the fields”, the Douay “the lands”. Saul had asked his Benjaminites whether the son of Jesse would give them “fields and vineyards” (1 Samuel 22:7, already on these pages), and Samuel had warned that a king “will take” them (1 Samuel 8:14, already on these pages). Here the king gives a field back.

Bread at my table. “And you yourself shall eat bread at my table continually”: “bread at my table” stands in two verses, 7 and 10 of this chapter. Shulchan is the table, and the table has been in this story twice before: Jonathan “rose from the table in burning anger” when Saul’s plan against David was clear (1 Samuel 20:34, already on these pages), and David’s absence from “the king’s table” was explained away (20:29). The NWT 1984 has “eat bread at my table constantly”; the NIV “always eat at my table”; the TLB turns the table into a house, “you shall live here at the palace!”

A dead dog. “What is your servant, that you should turn toward a dead dog such as I?”: “the dead dog” stands in two verses. They are this one and the verse where Abishai calls Shimei “this dead dog” (2 Samuel 16:9, now on these pages). David used the word of himself before Saul, “after a dead dog — after a single flea” (1 Samuel 24:14, already on these pages); now Saul’s grandson says it to David. The verb is panita el, “turn toward”; the ASV has “look upon such a dead dog as I am”, and so has the KJV; the Geneva and the Douay have “look upon such a dead dog as I am” too; the NIV has “notice a dead dog like me”; the NWT 1984 “turned your face to the dead dog such as I am”; the TLB “show kindness to a dead dog like me”, which brings the chapter’s word into the verse. The Greek has ton kyna ton tethnēkota. See kelev.

Verses 9–11 · Ziba, Saul’s young man, put to work — “your master’s son”, the ground and the table, and a seam in verse 11

Saul’s young man. “And the king called Ziba, Saul’s young man”: the word is na’ar, a lad or attendant, not the eved, “servant”, of verse 2. The NIV has “Saul’s steward”; the NWT 1984 “Saul’s attendant”; the ASV and the KJV “Saul’s servant”; the TLB “Saul’s servant Ziba”. The Greek has paidarion, “a boy”. “All that belonged to Saul and to all his house I have given”: the verb is a completed act, “I have given”, so the grant is already made when Ziba is told. The NWT 1984 has “I do give”.

Your master’s son. The Hebrew has ben, “son”, in “your master’s son”, as it does for Saul as Mephibosheth’s “father”; the ASV and the KJV have “thy master’s son”, the Douay “thy master's son”, and the NIV “your master’s grandson”, as do the NWT 1984 (“the grandson of your master”) and the TLB (“your master’s grandson”). The Greek keeps “son”. This translation keeps “son” and says in the note what the NIV says in the text. Ziba is told that his master’s house now has a master again: his old master’s grandson, whom he must call by the word adon, the word he used for the king.

The ground and the table. “And you shall work the ground for him — you and your sons and your servants — and bring in the yield”: where verse 7 gave back a field, sadeh, this verse speaks of the ground, adamah, the word of the soil that is tilled; the verb “work” is avad, the root of “servant”, so Ziba and his servants serve the ground for Saul’s grandson. The Hebrew says “and you shall bring” and does not say what; the NIV has “bring in the crops”; the ASV and the KJV have “bring in the fruits”; the NWT 1984 “do the gathering in”, and the Douay “thou shalt bring in food”. The field feeds the household, and the king’s table feeds Mephibosheth himself; “but Mephibosheth your master’s son shall eat bread continually at my table” is said twice over, in this verse and in verse 7. Ziba’s house is counted, fifteen sons and twenty servants, so the reader knows how many hands now work Saul’s old ground. The field is not Ziba’s, and Ziba is not to be trusted with it by the time this book is over (2 Samuel 16:1–4, now on these pages; 19:25–31, now on these pages).

A seam in verse 11. “And Ziba said to the king: ‘According to all that my lord the king commands his servant, so your servant will do.’ ‘And Mephibosheth is eating at my table like one of the king’s sons.’” In the Hebrew there is no second “and the king said”: after Ziba’s promise the same speech goes on with “Mephibosheth is eating at my table”, and the table is the king’s in verse 13, not Ziba’s. The versions mend it three ways. The ASV and the KJV add the words, “As for Mephibosheth, said the king, he shall eat at my table, as one of the king’s sons”, making it David’s speech; the NIV turns it into narration, “So Mephibosheth ate at David’s table like one of the king’s sons”, as the Greek does (“at David’s table”); the NWT 1984 leaves it in Ziba’s mouth, “Mephibosheth is eating at my table like one of the sons of the king”. The Geneva binds it to Ziba’s promise, “so shall thy servant do, that Mephibosheth may eat at my table”, and the Douay makes it the next clause, “and Miphiboseth shall eat at my table”. The TLB has “Mephibosheth ate regularly with King David”. This translation prints the Hebrew as it stands, in a second set of quotation marks, and does not say whose words they are. The participle “is eating” is okhel, the participle of verse 13: the table is set while the speech is still being made.

Verses 12–13 · Mica and the house of Ziba — a lame man at the king’s table in Jerusalem, “lame in both his feet”

Mica. “And Mephibosheth had a young son, whose name was Mica”: the line of Jonathan goes on in this child, and the Chronicles carry it down, “the son of Jonathan was Merib-baal; and Merib-baal begot Micah”, with Micah’s four sons (1 Chronicles 8:34–35), where Saul’s fourth son is “Eshbaal” (8:33) and not Ish-bosheth. The name is spelled with an aleph (Mikha) here, as against the prophet Micah, whose name is spelled with a he. The ASV and the TLB have “Mica”; the KJV and the Geneva “Micha”; the NIV “Mika”; the Douay “Micha”. The Greek has Micha. “Young son”, ben qatan, is a small boy.

The house of Ziba. “And all who lived in the house of Ziba were servants to Mephibosheth”: the man whom Saul’s house sent for as a servant is now the head of a house of servants, and they serve Saul’s grandson. The KJV has “all that dwelt in the house of Ziba were servants unto Mephibosheth”; the NIV “all the members of Ziba’s household were servants of Mephibosheth”; the TLB “All the household of Ziba became Mephibosheth’s servants”; the Douay “all the kindred of the house of Siba served Miphiboseth”.

In Jerusalem, at the king’s table. “And Mephibosheth was living in Jerusalem, for he was eating continually at the king’s table”: “the king’s table” (shulchan hammelekh) stands in three verses. They are this one, the verse in which David’s empty place at Saul’s table is explained (1 Samuel 20:29), and the verse in which Solomon’s officers provision his (1 Kings 5:7). Mephibosheth lives in Jerusalem because he eats there; the city is the king’s table and the table is the city. The ASV has “he did eat continually at the king’s table”; the NIV “because he always ate at the king’s table”; the NWT 1984 “it was constantly at the table of the king that he was eating”; the TLB “moved to Jerusalem to live at the palace”.

Lame in both his feet. “And he was lame in both his feet”: the last words of the chapter are the Hebrew pisseach shtei raglav, and they are the first thing that the story of the kindness was about. The root of “lame” (pisseach) stands in five verses of 2 Samuel: the fall in 4:4, the Jebusites’ “blind and lame” and David’s hatred of them in 5:6 and 8 (“Blind and lame shall not come into the house”), this verse, and Mephibosheth’s own “your servant is lame” when he meets David again (2 Samuel 19:27, now on these pages). A lame man in the city David took, at David’s own table, is the answer to that proverb. The NIV has “he was lame in both feet”; the NWT 1984 “lame in both of his feet”; the Douay “lame of both feet”. The Greek has chōlos amphoterois tois posin.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “kindness” for chesed, with “do kindness with” as in 2 Samuel 3:8 and 1 Samuel 20:14–15; “the kindness of God”; “lame in his feet”, as in 2 Samuel 4:4; “Look —”; “fell on his face and prostrated himself”; “Do not be afraid”; “Saul your father” for Mephibosheth’s grandfather; “the field” and “the ground” kept apart; “bread at my table”; “a dead dog”; “young man” for na’ar and “servant” for eved; “your master’s son”; the seam in verse 11 left as it stands; “the king’s table”.

Echoes paid. Jonathan’s request for kindness and the oath between them (1 Samuel 20:14–15, 42); David’s oath to Saul (1 Samuel 24:21–22); Abner’s “I do kindness” (2 Samuel 3:8); the nurse’s fall and the lame son (2 Samuel 4:4); the Jebusites’ “blind and lame” (2 Samuel 5:6, 8); Jonathan’s “do not be afraid” at Horesh (1 Samuel 23:17); the fields and vineyards (1 Samuel 22:7; 8:14); Jonathan at the table and David’s empty place (1 Samuel 20:29, 34); David’s “dead dog” (1 Samuel 24:14).

Echoes planted. Ziba and Mephibosheth at Absalom’s revolt, and the land divided (2 Samuel 16:1–4, now on these pages; 19:25–31, now on these pages); Machir at Mahanaim (2 Samuel 17:27, now on these pages); “the dead dog” again (2 Samuel 16:9, now on these pages); David spares Mephibosheth because of the oath (2 Samuel 21:7, now on these pages); Barzillai’s sons at the table (1 Kings 2:7); Micah’s line (1 Chronicles 8:34–35); Psalm 52:10 and the kindness of God; Amos 6:13 and Lo-debar. None of the others is translated here yet.
