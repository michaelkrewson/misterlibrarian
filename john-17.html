Jesus turns from instructing the disciples to praying aloud to the Father — the High Priestly Prayer. He asks to be glorified with the glory he had before the world existed, defines eternal life as knowing the only true God and Jesus Christ, and prays first for the eleven ('keep them... that they may be one'), then for everyone who will ever believe through their word. He asks that his followers see his own glory, given him before the founding of the world, and the prayer ends without a closing amen.
c. AD 30, still the same night as chapters 13-16, the last words spoken before Jesus crosses the Kidron valley to the garden and the arrest.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–5 · ⚠ ‘the hour has come’ after three chapters of promising it, eternal life defined for the only time in this Gospel, and a glory claimed from before the world existed

⚠ ‘Father, the hour has come’ (v1) closes a ring this Gospel opened at the very start of the supper: the narrator's own ‘knowing that his hour had come to depart from this world to the Father’ (John 13:1, already on these pages) is now said again, in the identical perfect tense, but this time by Jesus himself, in prayer, rather than reported about him. The same declaration was also announced at the triumphal entry (‘the hour has come for the Son of Man to be glorified,’ 12:23, already on these pages) and reached its exact midpoint one chapter earlier, split across a single sentence — ‘an hour is coming, in fact it has come’ (16:32, already on these pages). Four statements, one claim, closing in from ‘coming’ to ‘has come’ across five chapters, until the clock Jesus has been watching finally agrees with the one the narrator started. The prayer that follows is addressed to the Father, not the disciples — the first time since this supper began that Jesus turns away from them to speak.

‘This is eternal life: that they may know you, the only true God, and the one you sent, Jesus Christ’ (v3) is the only place anywhere in this Gospel that bothers to DEFINE ‘eternal life,’ a phrase this book has already used FOURTEEN times across six earlier chapters without ever once saying what it consists of. The definition is relational, not durational — knowing, not merely lasting — and ‘the only true God’ is the fifth of this Gospel's nine uses of alēthinos, genuine as against counterfeit, joining the true light, the true worshippers, the true bread and the true vine (all already on these pages) as one more thing this book insists is the real version of something a reader might otherwise settle for an imitation of.

⚠ ‘The glory I had alongside you before the world existed’ (v5) states a claim to personal pre-existence as plainly as this Gospel ever states anything — not a glory given at some point in time, but one already possessed ‘before the world was,’ spoken in the same breath as the opening sentence's own ‘the Word was with God’ (John 1:1, already on these pages). NIV ‘before the world began’; NWT (1984 and 2013 alike) ‘before the world was’; ASV and KJV both the same, ‘before the world was’ — all five English-shelf versions checked here agree the claim is temporal priority, not merely rank.

Verses 6–10 · a name made known, a chiasm of shared possession, and a glory already claimed before the cross has happened

‘They have kept your word’ (v6) opens a four-time run of the verb tēreō across this chapter — here of what the disciples have done, then three times of what Jesus and the Father do FOR them (‘keep them,’ v11; ‘I kept them,’ v12; ‘keep them from the evil one,’ v15) — a single verb moving from the disciples' own small faithfulness to the much larger faithfulness holding them up.

‘All that is mine is yours, and what is yours is mine’ (v10) is a plain chiasm in the Greek word order — ta ema panta sa estin kai ta sa ema, mine-yours / yours-mine — stating total mutual possession between Father and Son in the same breath that the prayer turns, for the first time, to the disciples who are about to receive the benefit of it. ASV and KJV both preserve the mirrored word order in English. NIV smooths it to ‘all I have is yours, and all you have is mine,’ which states the content without the shape.

⚠ ‘I have been glorified in them’ (v10) is a perfect tense, stated of the disciples, before the cross that this book elsewhere treats as the moment of glorification (12:23, already on these pages) has even happened — the identical grammar this project's own note on John 16:33 already flagged, a victory declared settled in advance of the event that will visibly secure it.

Verses 11–16 · ⚠ ‘that they may be one’ for the first of five times in thirteen verses, Judas named without a name, and the identical fulfillment formula already used of him three chapters earlier

⚠ ‘That they may be one’ (v11) is the first of FIVE times the word hen, ‘one,’ is said across the span from v11 to v23 (11, 21, 22 twice, 23) — a count any reader can run for themselves — and each repetition adds a clause the one before it did not have, building toward the fullest statement at v23. This translation keeps the plain ‘one’ every time rather than varying the English, so the repetition stays visible exactly as the Greek keeps repeating it.

⚠ ‘The son of destruction’ (v12) names Judas without naming him — a Semitic idiom (‘son of X’ for ‘one characterized by X’) this book uses only here, and the ONLY other New Testament use of the identical phrase is of the man of lawlessness at the end of days (2 Thessalonians 2:3, not yet on these pages). ‘So that the Scripture would be fulfilled’ uses the exact fulfillment formula this Gospel already used once before, of Judas specifically: ‘the one who eats my bread has lifted up his heel against me’ (John 13:18, quoting Psalm 41:9, already on these pages) — the same betrayal, named with the same formula, from two different points in the same long night.

‘Keep them from the evil one’ (v15) renders tou ponērou with the identical phrase this translation already uses in the Lord's Prayer — ‘rescue us from the evil one’ (Matthew 6:13, already on these pages) — the masculine reading over the equally possible neuter ‘evil’ in general, kept consistent across both prayers on the same grounds in both places: a request to be kept FROM someone reads more naturally than a request to be kept from an abstraction. The request itself is deliberately narrow: not removal from the world (stated and then immediately denied in the same verse) but protection within it.

Verses 17–19 · ‘sanctify’ said three times in three verses, the Lord's Prayer's own verb, and a sending handed on with the identical word used of Jesus's own sending

Hagiazō, ‘sanctify,’ is said THREE TIMES across these three verses (17, 19 twice) — of the disciples, by means of the truth; then of Jesus himself, consecrating himself; then of the disciples again, as the result of that self-consecration. It is the identical verb behind this translation's own rendering of the Lord's Prayer's opening line, ‘let your name be sanctified’ (Matthew 6:9, already on these pages) — the same verb asking, there, that God's own name be set apart, and here, that God's own people be set apart by the identical means, the truth.

‘Just as you sent me into the world, I also have sent them into the world’ (v18) uses one verb, apostellō, of both sendings, with no distinction of rank marked in the grammar — the mission handed on in the same terms it was received.

⚠ ‘On their behalf I sanctify myself’ (v19) switches prepositions from the rest of the prayer, and the switch is easy to lose in English: everywhere else Jesus prays peri autōn, ‘concerning’ or ‘about’ them (vv9, 20, kept as ‘concerning’ in this translation to keep the word visible), but here, once, it is hyper autōn, ‘on behalf of,’ ‘for the sake of’ them — the preposition Paul elsewhere uses of Christ dying hyper the ungodly (Romans 5:6, not yet on these pages, same preposition, same substitutionary weight). The shift lands on the one verb in this chapter that is not a request to the Father at all but something Jesus does to himself, and the stronger preposition arrives at the exact moment the stronger claim does.

Verses 20–23 · the prayer widens to everyone who will ever believe through a word not yet spoken, and ‘one’ becomes a claim the world itself can check

⚠ ‘Those who will believe in me through their word’ (v20) is the moment this prayer stops being only about the eleven men in the room and starts being about every reader of this page — a future tense reaching forward past the resurrection, past the book's own composition, to anyone whose faith depends on the disciples' testimony rather than on having seen Jesus directly. This Gospel will later state the same point as a direct blessing on exactly this condition: ‘blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed’ (John 20:29, already on these pages).

The unity prayer's full statement, reached at v23 — ‘I in them, and you in me, so that they may be perfected into one’ — uses the identical verb, teleioō, Jesus already used of his OWN finished work at the prayer's opening (‘having finished the work you gave me to do,’ v4): one task completed, one people completed, the same verb measuring both. And each of the three times ‘one’ is reached in this stretch (vv21, 22, 23), the stated PURPOSE is the same single thing stated three different ways — that the world may believe, that the world may believe, that the world may know — unity offered not as an end in itself but as evidence a watching world can check.

Verses 24–26 · a glory asked to be SEEN rather than merely believed, ‘before the founding of the world’ echoed from two already-shipped books, and a prayer that ends mid-argument

⚠ ‘Before the founding of the world’ (v24) renders the identical Greek noun, katabolē, this translation has already rendered the same way at the king's words to the sheep (‘prepared for you from the founding of the world,’ Matthew 25:34) and at Paul's own opening blessing (‘he chose us in him before the founding of the world,’ Ephesians 1:4, both already on these pages) — three different writers, one fixed phrase, each time marking something decided before the first day of Genesis 1 rather than at any point within history. ⚠ ASV and KJV both print ‘foundation’ here; NWT (1984 and 2013 alike) prints ‘founding,’ matching this translation; and NIV prints ‘creation’ at this verse — a different English noun from its own ‘began’ four verses earlier at v5, for the identical Greek word. This chapter now carries the phrase a second way: v5 placed Jesus's OWN glory there; v24 places the Father's LOVE for him there — the same point in time named twice from two different angles in one prayer.

‘So that they may SEE my glory’ (v24) asks for sight, not merely belief — a request this chapter has not made of anything else it discusses, and a request Jesus does not extend to the disciples until he first establishes, in the same breath, that it rests on a love that predates them entirely.

The prayer does not conclude; it simply stops, on an unfinished clause about love being ‘in them’ the way Jesus is already ‘in them’ — no closing amen, no narrator's summary, nothing marking an ending at all. The next words in this book are ‘when Jesus had spoken these things, he went out,’ straight into the garden and the arrest — the prayer's own last sentence is still open when the narrative simply moves on from it.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Hagiazō (vv17, 19) is kept as ‘sanctify,’ matching the Lord's Prayer's own ‘let your name be sanctified’ (Matthew 6:9). Tou ponērou (v15) is kept as ‘the evil one,’ the masculine reading, matching the identical choice already made at Matthew 6:13. Katabolē (v24) is kept as ‘the founding of the world,’ matching Matthew 25:34 and Ephesians 1:4. Hen (vv11, 21, 22 twice, 23) is kept as the plain ‘one’ every time, with no variation, to keep the repetition visible.

Echoes paid. ‘The hour has come’ (v1) closes the ring begun at 13:1, answered in between at 12:23 and split across 16:32's own ‘is coming, in fact it has come’ (all already on these pages). ‘The only true God’ (v3) extends alēthinos to its fifth cited use, after the true light (1:9), the true worshippers (4:23), the true bread (6:32) and the true vine (15:1, all already on these pages). The fulfillment formula at v12 repeats John 13:18's own identical words about the same man (already on these pages). ‘The evil one’ (v15) repeats the identical rendering already chosen at Matthew 6:13 (already on these pages). And teleioō (v23) answers the same verb Jesus used of his own finished work four verses into this same chapter (v4).

Echoes planted. ‘Those who will believe through their word’ (v20) is answered by this Gospel's own direct blessing on that condition, ‘blessed are those who have not seen and yet have believed’ (John 20:29, already on these pages). And the prayer's own unfinished ending runs straight into the arrest in the garden across the Kidron (John 18:1, already on these pages), where the discourse that began three chapters ago finally meets the hour it has been naming since John 13.

Already up ahead of sequence, now fully folded in: this chapter closes out the three-chapter Farewell Discourse (John 14, John 15, John 16) with the prayer all three were building toward. This book's own sequence now runs unbroken from chapter 1 through this chapter.
