📖 1 Thessalonians

Πρὸς Θεσσαλονικεῖς Α (Pros Thessalonikeis A) — 'To the Thessalonians, 1' — the congregation in Thessalonica, capital of the Roman province of Macedonia, a free city and a working port standing on the Via Egnatia, the highway from the Adriatic to Byzantium.

Probably the oldest Christian writing that survives — earlier than any Gospel. A letter of relief to a congregation only months old, written by a man who had been run out of their city by night and had spent the interval unable to find out whether they were still standing.

Chapter by chapter

1 Thessalonians 1Very probably the oldest Christian writing that survives — older than any Gospel, written about AD 50, perhaps twenty years after the crucifixion. Paul had been in Thessalonica only a few weeks: he argued in the synagogue on three sabbaths, a mob was raised in the marketplace, the house of his host Jason was stormed, and the missionaries were smuggled out of the city by night, leaving behind a congregation of brand-new converts to deal with the consequences. He had not managed to get back. This letter is what he wrote when Timothy finally returned with the news that they were still standing. ⚠ Almost everything about it is early: the greeting is the shortest he ever wrote — four words, “grace to you and peace,” and it stops, where every other letter extends it; there is no apostolic title, not even “slave,” just three names; and the conventions of Christian letter-writing have plainly not hardened yet. In verse 3 the triad of faith, love and hope appears for the first time in literature, in passing, as though the readers already knew it — and the words attached to it are working words: the LABOUR of love is exhausting toil, and “endurance” is literally a remaining-under. ⚠ The chapter closes with two verses widely thought to be older than the letter carrying them, a portable summary of what Gentile converts were taught — turn, serve, wait — ending in the earliest datable sentence in which Christians say what they are waiting for: “to wait for his Son from the heavens.” There is no timetable in it at all.

About the book

Where it sits
The THIRTEENTH book of the New Testament, and the earliest-written of the twenty-seven. ⚠ The canonical order of Paul's letters is by LENGTH, longest first, not by date — which is why the collection opens with Romans and buries its oldest item two-thirds of the way down.
Genre
A LETTER, and an unusually warm and unpolemical one: no opponents are refuted, no doctrine is corrected, and roughly the first three chapters are thanksgiving and reminiscence. The practical instruction comes late, and the two questions it exists to answer — what happens to believers who have already died, and when the Lord will come — are handled in a single stretch of chapters 4 and 5.
Author
PAUL, uncontested — one of the seven letters no one seriously disputes, and the benchmark against which the disputed ones are measured. SILVANUS (the Silas of Acts) and TIMOTHY are named as co-senders, and the letter says 'we' far more consistently than Paul's later ones, dropping into the singular only three times.
Date written
c. AD 50–51, from Corinth, after Timothy came back from Thessalonica with a report (3:6). ⚠ The date is anchored by the firmest fixed point in New Testament chronology: an inscription found at Delphi dates the proconsulship of GALLIO, before whom Paul was hauled at Corinth (Acts 18:12-17), to AD 51–52. ⚠ Whether this or GALATIANS is the earliest surviving Christian document depends entirely on the unsettled question of which 'Galatia' that letter addresses — on the South-Galatian reading Galatians may be a couple of years older. The library reports the competition and does not award the prize.
Place
CORINTH, during the eighteen-month stay of Acts 18.
Audience
A congregation a few months old, founded on a visit cut short by a riot (Acts 17:1-9) — the missionaries were smuggled out at night and the converts left to face the consequences. It was largely GENTILE: 1:9 describes them as having 'turned to God from the idols', which is not language for Jewish hearers, and in a Greek city that turn meant withdrawing from the festivals, trade guilds and civic sacrifices that made up ordinary social life. They were under real social pressure, and some of their number had died before the Lord returned — which had unsettled them badly enough to prompt the letter's most famous passage.

How it's laid out

1:1–10Thanksgiving: faith, love and hope; the gospel that came 'not in word only'; and a congregation that became one pattern for two provinces.
2:1–16How the missionaries actually behaved in the city — gentle as a nurse, working night and day, no flattery and no pretext for greed.
2:17–3:13The separation, the anxiety, and Timothy's errand: 'we could bear it no longer'.
4:1–12Practical instruction — holiness, brotherly love, and the striking command to mind your own business and work with your hands.
4:13–18The dead in Christ: 'we do not want you to be uninformed… so that you may not grieve as the rest do, who have no hope.'
5:1–11The day of the Lord comes like a thief in the night — so stay awake and sober.
5:12–28Closing instructions, rapid-fire: rejoice always, pray unceasingly, test everything, hold fast to what is good.

What it's about

  • The RETURN of Christ — mentioned at the end of every chapter, and the horizon the whole letter is written under; 1:10, 'to wait for his Son from the heavens', is the earliest datable Christian statement of it.
  • FAITH, LOVE and HOPE — the triad's first appearance in literature (1:3), and it returns at 5:8 as armour.
  • IMITATION — a chain running from the Lord to the missionaries to the Thessalonians to 'all the believers in Macedonia and Achaia' (1:6-7) and on to the congregations in Judea (2:14).
  • AFFLICTION — thlipsis, pressure; not a metaphor but the concrete cost of leaving the civic gods, and something the letter says they were warned about in advance (3:4).
  • WORK — Paul's own night-and-day labour so as to burden nobody (2:9), and the instruction to 'work with your hands' (4:11), which the sequel has to repeat more sharply.
  • GRIEF — the letter does not forbid it. It forbids grieving 'as the rest do, who have no hope' (4:13), which is a narrower and kinder instruction than it is usually quoted as being.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. The text is calm. Chapter 1's two notable readings are both small: the greeting at 1:1 stops at 'grace to you and peace' in the earliest witnesses, where later copies supply the usual 'from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ' (the KJV prints the longer form); and at 1:7 the earliest text has typos SINGULAR, 'a pattern', where much of the later tradition reads the plural. ⚠ The letter's one genuinely contested passage is 2:14-16, on 'the Jews… and the wrath has come upon them at last', which a minority of scholars argue is a later interpolation — partly on the grounds that it seems to presuppose the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, after Paul's death. The majority accept it as Paul's. Both positions are reported.

Looking forward

Christ appears here almost entirely as the one who is COMING — the letter ends every chapter on it. 1:10 waits for 'his Son from the heavens, whom he raised from the dead… the one rescuing us from the wrath that is coming'; 4:16 gives the most detailed description of the return anywhere in Paul, 'the Lord himself will descend from heaven with a shout, with the archangel's voice and with God's trumpet'; and 5:2 supplies the image everything later borrows, 'the day of the Lord comes like a thief in the night'. ⚠ 4:17's 'caught up' is harpagēsometha, which the Latin rendered rapiemur — the origin of the English word RAPTURE, though the elaborate systems built on the verse are nineteenth-century and later, and the passage itself is a pastoral answer to a bereavement question rather than a timetable. Notably absent, for a letter this early: no argument about the law, no justification vocabulary, no cross-and-resurrection exposition. Those arrive when the controversies do.

Where the debates are

Whether this or GALATIANS is the earliest surviving Christian writing — which turns on the unsettled address of Galatians. Whether 2:14-16 is a later interpolation. What exactly the Thessalonians had misunderstood about the return, and whether Paul himself expected to be alive for it ('we who are alive, who are left', 4:15-17). How 4:13-18 relates to 5:1-11, and how much weight the 'caught up' of 4:17 will bear. And why a letter written this early says so little about the topics that dominate Paul later. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
