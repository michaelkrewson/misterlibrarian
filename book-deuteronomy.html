📖 Deuteronomy

דְּבָרִים Devarim — 'Words' — from the opening line, 'these are the WORDS that Moses spoke.' The Hebrew title names the book by the thing it actually is: speech. Moses does almost nothing in Deuteronomy but talk.
Δευτερονόμιον (Deuteronomion) — 'Second law' — the Greek and Latin (Deuteronomium) title, and a slightly misleading one. It comes from the Septuagint's rendering of a phrase at 17:18 that in Hebrew means 'a COPY of this instruction,' written out for the king. The name has stuck for two thousand years; the book is not a second law but the first one preached.

The last words of Moses — three sermons and a song, delivered on the plains of Moab to a generation that did not see Egypt, by a man who will not cross the river with them.

Chapter by chapter

Deuteronomy 1Moses begins the longest speech of his life, on the far side of the Jordan, to a generation that was not there. "Eleven days from Horeb" — and then "in the fortieth year": the whole chapter is the explanation of that gap. Judges appointed, spies sent, a land refused, and the flat closing line that nothing happened for a very long time.Deuteronomy 2The long way round. Israel is told to leave Edom, Moab and Ammon alone and not to take a foot of their land, because those countries were given to Esau's sons and to Lot's — the same word, "possession," given to four nations before Israel's own — and then the road runs into Sihon king of Heshbon, who refuses passage because Jehovah hardened his heart, and is destroyed.Deuteronomy 3Og of Bashan comes out to battle, and the retelling quotes Numbers almost word for word. His iron bed, nine cubits long, is still on show at Rabbah when this chapter is written — the last of the Rephaim, a giant-clan name older than Israel itself. Transjordan is divided among Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh, and Moses begs to cross the Jordan and is refused: 'Enough from you. Never speak to me again of this matter.' He is sent up Pisgah to look, not to cross, and told to strengthen the man who will.Deuteronomy 4The eyes that watched Baal-peor become the sermon's first proof: obey, and live, as everyone in this valley still standing can testify. Moses lays down the Bible's oldest rule of self-preservation — do not add to this word, do not take from it — then argues that Israel saw no form at Horeb, only heard a voice, and so must carve no image of anything in heaven, earth or sea. A prophecy of exile follows, and inside it a promise: seek God even from exile and he is found, because he is merciful. Then the loudest claim in the chapter — Jehovah alone is God, in the heavens above and the earth below, there is no other — and Moses, before a single refuge law exists, quietly sets apart three cities where an accidental killer can run and live.Deuteronomy 5Moses repeats the Ten Words to the generation that did not stand at Horeb — and the repetition is not word for word. "Remember" the sabbath becomes "Keep" it, grounded now in slavery in Egypt rather than in creation; honoring father and mother gains a second promise Paul will later quote; the tenth Word swaps the neighbor's wife and house and reaches for a second, different verb; and Jehovah himself, once the Ten Words are spoken, "added nothing more" — the same restraint the previous chapter demanded of Israel. The people, too afraid to hear the voice again, ask for a mediator, and are told they asked well: it is how Moses gets his job.Deuteronomy 6The Shema itself — four Hebrew words with no verb in them, and every translation on earth has to insert an “is” and decide where it goes. Moses commands Israel to love God with heart, soul and a third word that is not a body part at all but an adverb pressed into service as a noun, and to sharpen these words into their children — a verb that everywhere else in the Bible sharpens arrows and tongues. Bind them on the hand, write them on the doorpost the Passover blood once went on. Then the danger: cities you did not build, and the warning lands not on hunger but on fullness. Do not test God as you tested him at Testing. And when your son asks tomorrow what all this means, the answer he is given is not a definition but a story.Deuteronomy 7Seven nations named once and only once in the Hebrew Bible, and the command to devote them to destruction — a word that means consecrate, not merely kill, and that Deuteronomy adds to the two Exodus passages it is otherwise rewriting almost clause for clause. The reason Israel is chosen is that there was no reason: not for numbers, because it was the fewest of all peoples, only because he loved you. God is called ‘the faithful God’ here and nowhere else in the Bible; the punishment that ran to the third and fourth generation is cut back to one man in his own lifetime; the blessing on the lambs is spelled with a Canaanite goddess’s name in it; and the chapter that opened by devoting seven nations closes by warning that a piece of silver carried indoors devotes YOU.Deuteronomy 8Manna, and the reason for it: forty years of hunger built to teach one lesson, quoted back word for word by Jesus in the wilderness. The clothes did not wear out; the good land ahead has wheat and barley, vines and figs and pomegranates, iron in its hills and copper to mine. Then the warning, built like a trap: eat, be satisfied, build good houses, watch the silver multiply — and only then does the heart grow high and forget the God who gave it, almost in the very words the chapter itself used fourteen verses earlier for the wilderness that taught the lesson in the first place.Deuteronomy 9Moses stops warning and starts correcting: the land is not being given for Israel’s righteousness — said three times in three verses — but for the wickedness of the nations and an oath sworn to three dead men. The terrifying spy report that broke the first generation is quoted back word for word and CONCEDED rather than refuted; the ‘devouring fire’ that was a warning to Israel four chapters ago now goes out ahead of it. Then the golden calf, retold by the man who was there — forty days on a mountain without bread or water, the tablets shattered before their eyes rather than beneath a mountain, Moses’ own anger gone from the story altogether, and two things about Aaron that Exodus never says. The chapter brackets itself with three Hebrew words that occur nowhere else in the Bible, and changes grammatical number at the same seam.Deuteronomy 10The second pair of tablets, cut by Moses himself this time and carried up in his own hand to a God who writes the same words again — and an ark of plain wood, made before the climb, that Exodus builds four chapters later in gold and by another man’s hands. Then Aaron dies at Moserah, where Numbers twice buries him on a mountain four stops away, and the Geneva Bible of 1599 answers the contradiction in a marginal line. The tribe of Levi is set apart for three verbs — to carry, to stand, to bless — and paid in a person instead of land. Then the sermon turns from the neck to the heart: circumcise its foreskin, and stiffen the neck no more. The reason given is not power but partiality reversed — the God of gods lifts no faces and takes no bribe, and loves the stranger, giving him bread and a coat.Deuteronomy 11Watered with the foot — Egypt’s irrigation, and the land that drinks rain instead. Moses argues from what one generation watched and the next did not: a sea flooded over Egypt’s horses with a verb that occurs exactly once in the Bible, and a ground that GAPES under Dathan and Abiram — retold without Korah in it, and with the word Moses had predicted rather than the one the narrator used, while what goes down the hole is the noun Genesis reserves for what the Flood destroyed. Then the early rain and the late rain, named here for the first time in the Bible, and the second paragraph of the Shema — which finally explains why the ‘you’ of this book keeps changing between singular and plural mid-sentence: twelve alternating blocks in thirty-two verses, and the seam is a quotation mark. What Deuteronomy rewrote from chapter 6 it pluralised; what it copied word for word it left singular, and 11:20 is 6:9 letter for letter. Then the borders Joshua will quote back in a different tense, and a blessing and a curse set on two named mountains before either has been explained.Deuteronomy 12The law itself begins, and it begins by demolishing everything: pull down their altars, smash their standing stones, burn their Asherahs, and destroy their NAME out of that place — then go to the place Jehovah chooses to put HIS name. Six times this chapter sends Israel to that place and not once, in the whole book, is it named; Jerusalem and Zion do not occur in Deuteronomy at all. Because one altar a week’s walk away would end meat entirely, the chapter then concedes what Leviticus 17 forbade — slaughter and eat at home, unclean and clean alike, as you would a gazelle — and gives its own reason, distance. Only the blood is withheld, and abstaining from it takes a war-word: BE STRONG, the two other occurrences of which are the charge to Joshua. And the sentence ‘each man whatever is right in his own eyes’, which the book of Judges will end on twice, is answered fourteen verses later by moving the eyes: right in the eyes of Jehovah — the formula Kings will use to grade every king in Israel.Deuteronomy 13In the Hebrew this chapter opens with the verse every English Bible prints as the end of chapter 12 — do not add to it, do not take from it — and the Masoretes put their heaviest paragraph break straight after it, attaching it to neither side. Deuteronomy 4:2 gave the same command in the plural; here it is singular, which turns a duplicate into evidence. Then the hardest question the last chapter raised: if worship happens in one place and no other, who decides? Three cases, each marked off by its own scribal break — a prophet whose sign actually COMES TRUE and who then says ‘let us go after other gods’; a brother, a son, a daughter, the wife of your bosom, the friend who is as your own soul; and a whole town. One verb drives all three. The town is put under the ban reserved for Canaanites and sentenced to be a tel forever — two words that stand in exactly one other verse of the Hebrew Bible, of Ai. And the chapter closes on the formula chapter 12 coined, catching the KJV and Geneva getting back the eyes they had just lost.Deuteronomy 14The chapter turns from a town that must be burned to what may be eaten, and does something this project has not seen since Leviticus: it repeats a whole chapter of another book. Set it beside Leviticus 11 and the rewriting is legible line by line — twenty-one bird names against twenty, with the extra one almost certainly a kite copied twice in two spellings, since ד and ר differ by the length of a corner and each of the three names stands in exactly one verse of the Bible. Exactly ONE bird moves, the cormorant, from second in its run to last. Deuteronomy adds ten permitted animals Leviticus never lists — three of the seven game names occur nowhere else, which is why the shelf produces a unicorn and a giraffe — drops the permission to eat locusts, and swaps the operative word from Leviticus 11’s sheqets (eight times there, never here) to to’evah, the word chapter 13 used of a whole town’s apostasy. It opens by forbidding two mourning rites at the one square of skin the Shema says to bind God’s words to, and closes on a tithe you convert to silver and spend on beer.

About the book

Where it sits
The fifth and final book of the TORAH (the Law / Pentateuch, the five books of Moses), and the fifth book of the Christian Old Testament. It is the hinge of the canon: the last book before Israel enters the land, and the one the rest of the Bible quotes most. Jesus answers all three temptations from it.
Genre
Preached law. Formally it is a series of ADDRESSES rather than narrative or statute: a covenant document in the shape of an ancient treaty, with historical prologue, stipulations, blessings and curses, witnesses and a written deposit — and, in the middle of it, some of the most affectionate prose in the Hebrew Bible.
Author
By ancient and traditional reckoning, Jewish and Christian alike, the addresses are the words of MOSES — the book presents them as such throughout, in the first person, and says he wrote them down (31:9, 24). The account of his own death in chapter 34 is traditionally credited to JOSHUA or to a later hand; the Talmud already discusses the question. Critical scholarship since the nineteenth century has widely identified Deuteronomy (or a core of it) with the 'book of the law' found in the temple under Josiah in 622 BC (2 Kings 22), and dated its composition to that period or somewhat earlier. See 'Where the debates are.'
Date written
On the traditional view, c. 1406 BC — the eleventh month of the fortieth year after the exodus (1:3), in the last weeks of Moses' life, immediately before the crossing. On the Josianic view, seventh century BC, with older material behind it.
Place
The plains of MOAB, east of the Jordan, opposite Jericho — the staging ground for the crossing. Israel never moves in this book. The whole of Deuteronomy takes place standing still, at a border.
Audience
The SECOND generation — the children of those who came out of Egypt. Moses says so bluntly: 'not with our fathers did Jehovah make this covenant, but with us, we, these here today, all of us alive' (5:3). It is a book about how to hand faith to people who were not there.

How it's laid out

1–4First address: what happened, and what to make of it — the wilderness retold to a generation that did not live it, ending in a plea to remember Horeb.
5–11Second address, part one: the Ten Words repeated, the SHEMA ('Hear, O Israel'), and the great command to love God with all the heart, soul and strength — with the warning that prosperity, not hardship, is the likelier danger.
12–26Second address, part two: the statutes — worship at one place, kings, prophets, courts, war, land, debt, gleaning, wages, weights. The law given a human face.
27–28Blessings and curses on Ebal and Gerizim — the covenant sanctions, with the curses at four times the length of the blessings.
29–30Third address: covenant renewal, and the offer of 'life and good, death and evil' — 'therefore choose life.'
31–34The handover: Joshua commissioned, the Song of Moses, the blessing of the tribes, and the death of Moses on Nebo, buried by God in a grave no one knows.

What it's about

  • REMEMBER — the book's central verb. A generation that did not see the exodus is told to hold it as its own story; 'remember' and 'do not forget' run through it like a drumbeat.
  • LOVE as a command and a motive. Deuteronomy is where Israel is told to love God with everything (6:5) — and, remarkably, where God's own choice of Israel is explained by nothing but love (7:7-8): not their size, not their merit.
  • The danger of SUCCESS. The warnings are not mainly about suffering but about comfort: when you have eaten and are full, and built good houses, 'beware lest you forget' (8:11-14).
  • The STRANGER, the orphan and the widow — protected again and again, with the same reason each time: you were slaves in Egypt. Ethics grounded in memory rather than in principle.
  • ONE place, one God — the centralisation of worship, and the SHEMA's insistence that Jehovah is one.
  • CHOICE. The book ends by setting life and death in front of the people and telling them to choose. It assumes they can.

The source text

Translated from the Hebrew MASORETIC TEXT — the digital Hebrew of Mechon-Mamre (the Leningrad/Aleppo tradition), consonants with the Masoretes' vowel-points and cantillation, and its scribal peculiarities kept. The scroll's own paragraph breaks are shown as it marks them: petuchah {פ} (open) and setumah {ס} (closed). Deuteronomy is also one of the best-attested books at Qumran — a large number of copies were found in the Dead Sea Scrolls, along with the oldest known texts of the Shema in the tefillin and mezuzah fragments. The seven-version shelf under every chapter compares the NIV, KJV, Douay-Rheims, The Living Bible, the 1599 Geneva, ASV, and NWT.

Looking forward

Deuteronomy is the book Jesus quotes most in the crisis moments. All three answers in the wilderness temptation come from it — 'man does not live by bread alone' (8:3), 'you shall not put Jehovah your God to the test' (6:16), 'him only shall you serve' (6:13). Asked for the greatest commandment, he answers with the SHEMA and its love-command (6:4-5). And Moses' promise of 'a prophet like me from among your brothers, him you shall hear' (18:15) is applied to Jesus in Acts 3:22 and 7:37. This translation marks those echoes as they come; it does not force them.

Where the debates are

The central question is DATE, and it is sharper here than for any other book of the Torah. The book presents itself as the addresses of Moses in the fortieth year, and traditional Jewish and Christian reading takes it that way. Since W. M. L. de Wette in 1805, however, a large body of scholarship has linked Deuteronomy to the scroll discovered in the temple in 622 BC (2 Kings 22-23), whose reading triggered Josiah's reform — a reform that looks strikingly like this book's programme, above all the abolition of local shrines in favour of one sanctuary. On that view Deuteronomy is a seventh-century composition (or an older core given seventh-century form), presented in Moses' voice. Arguments run both ways and deserve to be heard as arguments: the book's treaty FORM closely matches SECOND-MILLENNIUM Hittite vassal treaties rather than the first-millennium Assyrian ones, which cuts toward an early date; its language and concerns match late-monarchic Judah, which cuts toward a late one. Smaller questions attach to the account of Moses' own death (34), to the phrase 'across the Jordan' spoken as if from the west, and to notes like 'to this day.' This library lays the readings out with their pedigrees and does not cast a vote — the same posture it takes on Genesis's composition and the date of Daniel.
