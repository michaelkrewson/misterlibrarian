📖 Titus

Πρὸς Τίτον (Pros Titon) — 'To Titus' — Paul's Gentile delegate, left on Crete to organise its congregations.

The shortest of the three PASTORAL letters, and the most practical: a working commission to organise raw congregations on a hard island — appoint elders, silence the profiteers, and let a sound faith show in good works. It carries the New Testament's clearest single glimpse of the earliest church order, and quotes a pagan Cretan poet against the Cretans.

Chapter by chapter

Titus 1The shortest of the three Pastoral letters, and a working commission: Titus is left on CRETE to “set right what was left undone” and appoint elders town by town. The greeting is the most theological in the Pastorals — a whole sentence of doctrine before it reaches the addressee — and it rests the hope of eternal life on a striking title, “the God who does not LIE,” who promised it “before times eternal.” ⚠ Chapter 1 gives one of the clearest windows in the New Testament onto the earliest church order: it says appoint “elders” (v5) and then, describing the same man, calls him the “overseer” (v7) — two words for one office, the bishop-over-elders still centuries off. The qualifications are almost all matters of character; the one skill asked is to hold the sound teaching and rebuke those who contradict it. ⚠ Then the confrontation, with its famous flourish: Paul quotes a Cretan poet — “Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons” — calls him “a prophet of their own,” and adds “this testimony is true” (the line is Epimenides, and carries the ancient liar’s-paradox knot). It closes on the letter’s whole theology of conduct: “they profess to know God, but by their works they deny him.”

About the book

Where it sits
The SEVENTEENTH book of the New Testament, the third of the Pastorals, placed after the two letters to Timothy. At 46 verses it is among the shortest books in the collection.
Genre
A LETTER, and essentially an administrative one — a field manual for a delegate. It runs church order (ch. 1), household conduct for every group in the congregation (ch. 2), and the believer's bearing toward the world (ch. 3), each anchored to a burst of doctrine. It is close kin to 1 Timothy in vocabulary and aim.
Author
Named as PAUL — and swept up in the same authorship dispute as 1 and 2 Timothy. ⚠ The majority of critical scholars judge the three Pastorals pseudonymous: their vocabulary, style and settled church order set them apart from the undisputed letters, and their movements do not fit Acts. The case for Paul rests on a secretary given latitude, the new administrative subject, an older man's manner, and personal notes (like the errands of 3:12-13) hard to invent — with a mission to Crete after a release from the Roman imprisonment to hold it. The library reports both and does not vote.
Date written
Traditionally c. AD 63–66, from the same post-Acts period as 1 Timothy; on the majority-critical view, c. AD 90–110. The date follows from the authorship question.
Place
Written to TITUS on CRETE (1:5); the writer plans to winter at Nicopolis (3:12), on the west coast of Greece.
Audience
TITUS — a Greek, an uncircumcised Gentile whom Paul had refused to circumcise as a test case (Galatians 2:3), and one of his most trusted troubleshooters — left in charge of the Cretan congregations. Behind him stand young churches on an island of poor reputation, troubled by teachers 'of the circumcision' pushing Jewish myths, purity rules and 'commands of men' for shameful gain, and unsettling whole households. The letter equips Titus to appoint leaders, refute the teachers, and order the common life.

How it's laid out

1:1–4A long, theological greeting — 'the God who does not lie', 'God our Saviour'.
1:5–9Appoint elders in every town: the qualifications of the elder/overseer.
1:10–16The Cretan false teachers, silenced — with the quoted line 'Cretans are always liars'.
2:1–10Sound conduct by group: older men and women, the young, slaves — 'that the word of God may not be maligned'.
2:11–15The letter's centre: 'the grace of God has appeared… training us' to live self-controlled, upright and godly lives.
3:1–8Bearing toward the world, and why: 'he saved us… by the washing of rebirth', 'not by works of righteousness'.
3:9–15Avoid foolish controversies; warn a divisive person twice; closing errands and greetings.

What it's about

  • GOOD WORKS — the refrain of the letter, sounding six times in three chapters: a sound faith is one that shows, against teachers who are all talk (1:16; 2:14, a people 'zealous for good works').
  • SOUND TEACHING — hygiainō, the Pastorals' medical metaphor: doctrine as health, and rebuke as surgery aimed at a cure (1:9, 13; 2:1).
  • CHURCH ORDER — elders appointed town by town, and the elder and overseer shown to be one office (1:5-7); the simplest church structure in the New Testament.
  • GOD OUR SAVIOUR — the title laid on both Father and Son (1:3-4; 2:10, 13; 3:4-6), and the grace that 'has appeared, bringing salvation to all'.
  • GRACE THAT TRAINS — 2:11-14, the letter's most quoted sentence: salvation is not only rescued but schooled, grace teaching a whole way of life while it waits for 'the blessed hope'.
  • GODLINESS and self-control — eusebeia and sōphrosynē, ordered reverence and a sound mind, pressed on every group in the congregation.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. Chapter 1's variants are minor (the greeting at 1:4 divides over whether 'mercy' joins 'grace and peace', as in 1-2 Timothy). ⚠ The letter's large question, as with all three Pastorals, is authorial rather than textual. Its most quoted single line — 'Cretans are always liars, evil beasts, lazy gluttons' (1:12) — is not scripture Paul composed but a hexameter he quotes from the Cretan seer EPIMENIDES and endorses; the same poet is the likeliest source of the 'in him we live and move and have our being' Paul cites at Athens (Acts 17:28).

Looking forward

Christ is 'our Saviour' (1:4; 2:13; 3:6), the title the letter also gives the Father, sometimes so closely that the referent blurs. ⚠ Titus 2:13 is one of the New Testament's strongest single texts for the deity of Christ — 'the appearing of the glory of our great God and Saviour, Jesus Christ' — which on the natural reading of the Greek (a Granville-Sharp construction, one article over both nouns) calls Jesus 'our great God and Saviour' outright. And 3:4-7 sets the whole of salvation as the work of God's kindness, 'not by works of righteousness that we had done, but according to his mercy… through the washing of rebirth and renewal by the Holy Spirit, whom he poured out on us richly through Jesus Christ our Saviour'. Here as in 1 Timothy the cross is assumed rather than expounded; what the letter presses is the grace that saves and then trains.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP, shared with 1 and 2 Timothy — the central question. The 'husband of one wife' of 1:6, read as married-once, monogamous, or simply faithful. Whether 'elder' and 'overseer' were truly one office (1:5-7). The propriety of Paul quoting and endorsing a pagan 'prophet' (1:12), and the liar's-paradox knot in the line itself. And the force of the great Christological reading of 2:13. The library reports the positions; it does not vote.
