Paul comes to Ephesus while Apollos is at Corinth, finds twelve disciples who know only John’s baptism, has them baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus and lays hands on them, and speaks boldly in the synagogue for three months; he then separates the disciples and reasons daily in the lecture hall of Tyrannus for two years, and God does extraordinary acts of power through him. The seven sons of Sceva fail to cast out a spirit by the name of Jesus, the name is magnified, and a considerable number who had practiced the curious arts burn their books. Paul resolves to go to Jerusalem by Macedonia and Achaia and then to Rome, sends Timothy and Erastus ahead, and the silversmith Demetrius raises the city against him; the crowd fills the theater crying “Great is Artemis of the Ephesians,” and the town clerk dismisses the assembly.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are ‘three months’ in the synagogue (v8), ‘two years’ in the lecture hall (v10) and ‘a time’ more in Asia (v22), which Luke does not add together; Paul will speak of ‘three years’ at Acts 20:31. It follows Acts 18 at Corinth and Ephesus. This library dates the events no more closely than the book does.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–7 · Ἀπολλῶ … ἀνωτερικὰ μέρη … Εἰ πνεῦμα ἅγιον ἐλάβετε πιστεύσαντες … Εἰς τί … βάπτισμα μετανοίας … εἰς τὸ ὄνομα τοῦ κυρίου Ἰησοῦ … ἐπιθέντος … χεῖρας … ἐλάλουν τε γλώσσαις καὶ ἐπροφήτευον … ὡσεὶ δώδεκα Apollō … anōterika merē … Ei pneuma hagion elabete pisteusantes … Eis ti … baptisma metanoias … eis to onoma tou kyriou Iēsou … epithentos … cheiras … elaloun te glōssais kai eprophēteuon … hōsei dōdeka — Ephesus: twelve disciples who had not heard of the Holy Spirit, and the order of water, hands and Spirit

The upper regions, and Apollos at Corinth (Acts 19:1). Anōterika stands in this verse alone in the New Testament; it is the comparative of anō, “up,” and Luke has it say ta anōterika merē, “the higher parts.” He names no road and no country. KJV, Geneva and Douay “the upper coasts” put a coast into a phrase that has merē, “parts,” and no word for shore; ASV “the upper country,” NIV “the road through the interior” and NWT 1984 “the inland parts” each say what the higher parts were taken to be, and TLB “through Turkey” puts a modern country into Luke’s sentence. The verse dates Paul’s arrival by where Apollos is: en tō ton Apollō einai en Korinthō, “while Apollos was in Corinth,” the city to which he had crossed over at 18:27. Paul’s coming to Ephesus is also the return he promised there, “I will return again to you, God willing” (18:21); Luke does not say that he is keeping the promise, and he does not mention Priscilla and Aquila, whom Paul left in Ephesus at 18:19. The apparatus gives elthein, “came,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont and katelthein, “came down,” to Nestle–Aland 28; NWT 1984 “came down” to Ephesus has the second. It gives heurein, “to find,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the participle heurōn, “finding,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which is what KJV “and finding certain disciples” prints.

“Did you receive Holy Spirit when you believed?” (Acts 19:2). Ei pneuma hagion elabete pisteusantes? The verb is an aorist, “did you receive,” and the participle is an aorist too, “having believed.” The Greek puts no “when” and no “since” between them and says nothing of how long there was between believing and receiving; each English word is a choice. ASV “Did ye receive the Holy Spirit when ye believed?”, NIV “Did you receive the Holy Spirit when you believed?”, NWT 1984 “Did you receive holy spirit when YOU became believers?” and TLB “Did you receive the Holy Spirit when you believed?” say “when”; KJV “Have ye received the Holy Ghost since ye believed?”, Geneva “Have ye received the holy Ghost since ye believed?” and Douay “Have you received the Holy Ghost since ye believed?” say “since,” and the three also turn the aorist into “have received.” This translation prints “when,” and this page does not decide what either word implies about whether the gift and the belief come together. Pneuma hagion has no article in the question, as at 8:15 and 8:17, where this translation also prints “Holy Spirit”; at v6 below Luke writes to pneuma to hagion, with both articles. NWT 1984 prints “holy spirit” in lower case here and at v6; that is a doctrinal rendering, which treats the phrase as God’s active force, and the Greek, with no article at v2 and both at v6, does not decide it. The answer is all’ oud’ ei pneuma hagion estin ēkousamen, “but we did not even hear whether there is a Holy Spirit.” KJV “We have not so much as heard whether there be any Holy Ghost” and NWT 1984 “we have never heard whether there is a holy spirit” keep the question of whether there is one; NIV “we have not even heard that there is a Holy Spirit” turns the question into a statement; ASV “Nay, we did not so much as hear whether the Holy Spirit was given” supplies a different question, whether the Spirit was given, as it also supplies “given” at John 7:39, where the Greek is the same bare oupō gar ēn pneuma, “for the Spirit was not yet”; and TLB “What is the Holy Spirit?” makes the answer a question. Luke does not say how men who knew John’s baptism had not heard of the Spirit; John’s own words that the one coming after him would baptize “with the Holy Spirit and fire” are at Luke 3:16, and the Lord’s at 1:5 and 11:16.

“Into what, then?” and “a baptism of repentance” (Acts 19:3–4). Eis ti oun ebaptisthēte? keeps eis, “into,” the preposition of the baptism at v5 below. ASV “Into what then were ye baptized?” and KJV “Unto what then were ye baptized?” keep the question, as do Douay “In what then were you baptized?” and NWT 1984 “In what, then, were YOU baptized?”; the NIV asks “Then what baptism did you receive?” and the TLB “what beliefs did you acknowledge at your baptism?” Paul’s definition is Iōannēs ebaptisen baptisma metanoias, “John baptized a baptism of repentance,” a verb with its own noun in the accusative and no word for “with”; KJV “John verily baptized with the baptism of repentance” and Geneva “John verily baptized with the baptism of repentance” add the “with” and, in “verily,” the particle men, which the apparatus gives to Robinson–Pierpont alone; the Douay has “the baptism of penance.” The words he gives John, eis ton erchomenon met’ auton, “the one coming after him,” are the words he spoke himself at Pisidian Antioch, “a baptism of repentance to all the people of Israel” (13:24) and “one is coming after me” (13:25); John’s “one coming after” stands in Acts only at 13:25 and here. At the end of the verse the apparatus gives ton Iēsoun, “Jesus,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and ton Christon Iēsoun, “Christ Jesus,” to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “on Christ Jesus” and Geneva “in Christ Jesus” print the longer reading. The two stories are set side by side: Apollos was “knowing only the baptism of John” (18:25) and Luke does not say he was baptized again, and here men who knew only John’s baptism are baptized into the name of Jesus; Luke does not say that these men knew Apollos or were taught by him, and this page does not join them.

Water, hands and the Spirit (Acts 19:5–6). Ebaptisthēsan eis to onoma tou kyriou Iēsou, “they were baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus,” is in the passive with no one named as baptizing, and the exact phrase eis to onoma tou kyriou Iēsou stands in two verses, here and 8:16. Luke uses other prepositions elsewhere: epi tō onomati, “on the name,” at 2:38, and en tō onomati, “in the name,” at 10:48. Then epithentos autois tou Paulou cheiras, “Paul having laid hands on them,” with the verb epitithēmi that is used of laying on hands at 8:17, 9:12, 9:17 and 13:3, and again at 28:8. The apparatus gives Paulou alone to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles and Paulou tas cheiras, with the article, to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. The Spirit then ēlthe, “came,” upon them, where Luke says it “fell” at 8:16, 10:44 and 11:15. Four scenes in Acts join water, hands and the Spirit, and they come in more than one order: at Samaria they are baptized, then the apostles lay hands, then the Spirit is received (8:12–17); at Damascus Ananias lays hands on Saul and speaks of his being filled with Holy Spirit, and then Saul is baptized (9:17–18); at Caesarea the Spirit falls while Peter is still speaking, and the water comes after, with no hands (10:44–48); at Ephesus the order is the Samaritan one, water, hands, Spirit. Luke does not draw a rule from the four, and this page offers none. Elaloun te glōssais kai eprophēteuon, “they were speaking in tongues and prophesying,” has two imperfects. Glōssais with lalein stands in three scenes, 2:4, 10:46 and here, and only at 2:4 does Luke write heterais, “other”; TLB “they spoke in other languages” carries the “other” into this verse, Geneva “they spake the tongues” has no preposition, and KJV “they spake with tongues, and prophesied,” ASV “they spake with tongues, and prophesied” and Douay “they spoke with tongues and prophesied” do not. Prophēteuō stands in four verses of Acts: 2:17 and 2:18, the verb of Joel’s prophecy, here, and 21:9, the four daughters of Philip.

“About twelve” (Acts 19:7). Ēsan de hoi pantes andres hōsei dōdeka, “there were about twelve men in all.” Hōsei is Luke’s “about,” the word of the “about three thousand” at 2:41, and the word is andres, “men,” not anthrōpoi, “people.” The apparatus gives dōdeka to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the form dekadyo to Robinson–Pierpont, the same number. KJV “And all the men were about twelve,” NWT 1984 “All together, there were about twelve men” and NIV “There were about twelve men in all” keep the number as Luke gives it. Luke gives no reason for the number and does not liken it to another twelve; this page offers none.

Verses 8–12 · ἐπαρρησιάζετο … διαλεγόμενος καὶ πείθων … ἐσκληρύνοντο καὶ ἠπείθουν κακολογοῦντες τὴν ὁδόν … ἀφώρισεν … ἐν τῇ σχολῇ Τυράννου … δυνάμεις … οὐ τὰς τυχούσας … χρωτός … σουδάρια ἢ σιμικίνθια eparrhēsiazeto … dialegomenos kai peithōn … esklērynonto kai ēpeithoun kakologountes tēn hodon … aphōrisen … en tē scholē Tyrannou … dynameis … ou tas tychousas … chrōtos … soudaria ē simikinthia — Ephesus: three months in the synagogue, two years in the lecture hall of Tyrannus, and cloths carried from Paul’s skin

“Spoke boldly,” “reasoning” and “persuading” (Acts 19:8). Eparrhēsiazeto, “he spoke boldly,” is the verb Luke gave Apollos in the same city’s synagogue at 18:26; it stands in nine verses of the New Testament, seven of them in Acts (9:27, 9:28, 13:46, 14:3, 18:26, here, and 26:26). Dialegomenos, “reasoning,” is the verb of 18:4 and 18:19; it stands in ten verses of Acts, 17:2, 17:17, 18:4, 18:19, here, v9 below, 20:7, 20:9, 24:12 and 24:25. With it Luke writes peithōn, “persuading,” the verb of 18:4 (epeithen), and the Jews’ own word for what they say Paul does at 18:13 (anapeithei). The subject is peri tēs basileias tou theou, “about the kingdom of God,” a phrase that stands in six verses of Acts: 1:3, 8:12, 14:22, here, and 28:23 and 28:31. The apparatus gives peithōn alone to Westcott–Hort and Tregelles and peithōn ta, “persuading the things,” to Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “disputing and persuading the things concerning the kingdom of God” and ASV “reasoning and persuading as to the things concerning the kingdom of God” print the article; Geneva “disputing and exhorting to the things that appertain to the kingdom of God” and Douay “disputing and exhorting concerning the kingdom of God” change the second verb to “exhorting”; NIV “arguing persuasively about the kingdom of God” and NWT 1984 “giving talks and using persuasion concerning the kingdom of God” keep it. TLB “each Sabbath day” adds a day that the verse does not have, and “persuading many to believe in Jesus” puts Jesus where Luke has the kingdom of God. The three months are epi mēnas treis; with the two years of v10, that is two years and three months, and Paul will say “three years” at 20:31; Luke gives no sum, and this page does not reconcile them.

“Disobeying,” “the Way” and the lecture hall of Tyrannus (Acts 19:9–10). Esklērynonto kai ēpeithoun, “were hardening themselves and disobeying,” sets the verb of v8 against its opposite: apeitheō is peithō with a negative in front, “to refuse to be persuaded.” It stands in Acts at 14:2, where this translation has “the Jews who disobeyed,” and here, and its adjective is at 26:19. ASV “were hardened and disobedient” and Geneva “were hardened, and disobeyed” keep the obedience; KJV “were hardened, and believed not” and Douay “were hardened, and believed not” do not; NIV “they refused to believe” and NWT 1984 “not believing” do not either. Kakologountes tēn hodon, “speaking evil of the Way,” uses a verb that stands in four verses of the New Testament: Matthew 15:4 and Mark 7:10, where the law says “the one who speaks evil of father or mother,” Mark 9:39, and here. “The Way” with the article and no genitive stands in Acts at 9:2, here, v23 below, and 22:4, 24:14 and 24:22; at 18:25 and 18:26 Luke wrote “the way of the Lord” and “the way of God.” Geneva “speaking evil of the way of God” and Douay “speaking evil of the way of the Lord” carry the genitive of 18:25 and 18:26 into this verse, where the Greek has none; NIV “publicly maligned the Way” and NWT 1984 “speaking injuriously about The Way” keep it, and TLB “publicly spoke against Christ” puts a name where Luke has none. Aphōrisen tous mathētas, “separated the disciples,” is the verb of 13:2, where the Holy Spirit says “set apart for me Barnabas and Saul”; it stands in Acts at 13:2 and here, and Paul uses it of himself at Romans 1:1 and Galatians 1:15. Scholē, “lecture hall,” and the name Tyrannos each stand in this verse alone in the New Testament. Scholē began as the word for leisure, and so for what is done with leisure, a talk and then the place for one (reported here, not checked); the verse does not say whether Tyrannus owned the hall or taught in it, or what it was used for at other hours. ASV “the school of Tyrannus” and NWT 1984 “the school” keep the old word, NIV “the lecture hall of Tyrannus” and TLB “the lecture hall of Tyrannus” name the room; KJV “the school of one Tyrannus,” Geneva “the school of one Tyrannus” and Douay “the school of one Tyrannus” print the tinos, “of one,” that the apparatus gives to Robinson–Pierpont alone. A Western reading is reported to add that he reasoned there “from the fifth hour to the tenth”; the apparatus here does not carry it, and this page has not read it. At v10 Luke gives epi etē dyo, “for two years,” and pantas tous katoikountas tēn Asian, “all those living in Asia.” At 16:6 the Holy Spirit had forbidden Paul to speak the word in Asia, and the ban is lifted here; NIV “the province of Asia” and TLB “the Turkish province of Asia Minor” put a province and a modern country into a word that has neither. Ioudaious te kai Hellēnas, “both Jews and Greeks,” stands in four verses of Acts: 14:1, here, v17 below and 20:21. The apparatus gives tou kyriou, “of the Lord,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and tou kyriou Iēsou, “of the Lord Jesus,” to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “the word of the Lord Jesus” and Geneva “the word of the Lord Jesus” have the longer phrase.

“Extraordinary” acts of power, and cloths carried from his skin (Acts 19:11–12). Dynameis te ou tas tychousas is a negative, “not the ordinary acts of power,” and the negated participle of tynchanō stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and at 28:2, where the islanders show the party “extraordinary kindness” (ou tēn tychousan, “not the ordinary”). NIV “extraordinary miracles” and NWT 1984 “extraordinary works of power” say it with one word; KJV “special miracles” and ASV “special miracles” say it another way; TLB “unusual miracles” and Douay “more than common miracles” keep the comparison. The agent is God (ho theos epoiei) and the instrument “the hands of Paul,” as at 5:12 of the apostles. Then chrōtos, “skin,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and the cloths are soudaria, which stands in four verses: Luke 19:20, John 11:44, John 20:7 and here, the word of Lazarus’s face-cloth and of the cloth in Jesus’s tomb; this translation prints “cloth” in all four. Simikinthia stands in this verse alone and is reported to be Latin, semicinctium, “half-girdle” (reported here, not checked); the Greek says “aprons” only in the sense of what is worn, and does not say who wore them. KJV “handkerchiefs or aprons,” ASV “handkerchiefs or aprons,” Douay “handkerchiefs and aprons” and NIV “handkerchiefs and aprons that had touched him” print “handkerchiefs,” NWT 1984 “cloths and aprons” keeps the cloth, Geneva “kerchiefs, or handkerchiefs” gives the one word two names, and TLB “handkerchiefs or parts of his clothing” makes the second a garment. Luke does not say that Paul knew of them or sent them. The verse has the shape of 5:15: both open with hōste kai, “so that even,” and have the sick and a carrying, but at 5:15 it is the sick who are carried out to where Peter’s shadow may fall, and here it is cloths that are carried to the sick. The diseases apallassesthai, “to be released,” a verb that stands in three verses of the New Testament, Luke 12:58, here and Hebrews 2:15 (the last not yet on these pages), and the evil spirits ekporeuesthai, “to go out,” each with a verb of its own. The apparatus gives apopheresthai, “to be carried away,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and epipheresthai, “to be brought upon,” to Robinson–Pierpont; ASV “carried away from his body” has the first, and KJV “were brought unto the sick” has no “away.” At the end the apparatus gives ekporeuesthai to the three editions and exerchesthai ap’ autōn, “to go out from them,” to Robinson–Pierpont; KJV “the evil spirits went out of them” and Douay “the wicked spirits went out of them” print the “of them.”

Verses 13–20 · ἐξορκιστῶν … ἐπεχείρησαν … ὀνομάζειν … Ὁρκίζω ὑμᾶς τὸν Ἰησοῦν … Σκευᾶ … Τὸν Ἰησοῦν γινώσκω καὶ τὸν Παῦλον ἐπίσταμαι … ἀμφοτέρων … ἐμεγαλύνετο … ἐξομολογούμενοι … τὰ περίεργα … μυριάδας πέντε … κατὰ κράτος … ηὔξανεν καὶ ἴσχυεν exorkistōn … epecheirēsan … onomazein … Horkizō hymas ton Iēsoun … Skeua … Ton Iēsoun ginōskō kai ton Paulon epistamai … amphoterōn … emegalyneto … exomologoumenoi … ta perierga … myriadas pente … kata kratos … ēuxanen kai ischyen — the exorcists, the sons of Sceva, the books that were burned, and a word that grew and prevailed

“Exorcists,” “undertook” and “I adjure you” (Acts 19:13). Exorkistōn, “of exorcists,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. They are perierchomenōn, “going around”: KJV “vagabond Jews,” Geneva “vagabond Jews,” ASV “strolling Jews,” NWT 1984 “roving Jews,” Douay “who went about,” NIV “Some Jews who went around” and TLB “itinerant Jews.” Epecheirēsan, “undertook,” is the verb of Luke’s own preface, “many have undertaken to compile an account” (Luke 1:1); it stands in three verses of the New Testament, Luke 1:1, Acts 9:29 and here. KJV “took upon them” and ASV “took upon them” keep it, NWT 1984 “undertook” has the word itself, Geneva “took in hand,” Douay “attempted” and NIV “tried” give the sense, and TLB “planned to experiment” adds an experiment that the verse does not mention. What they try is onomazein to onoma tou kyriou Iēsou, “to name the name of the Lord Jesus” over those who had the evil spirits, a verb and its own noun together, as in 2 Timothy 2:19, “everyone who names the name of the Lord” (not yet on these pages). Plutarch, a Greek writer of the next generation (reported here, not checked), says that “the magi tell the demon-possessed to recite and name the Ephesian letters to themselves” (hoi magoi tous daimonizomenous keleuousi ta Ephesia grammata pros hautous katalegein kai onomazein, Quaestiones convivales 7.5, 706E, read in the Greek); Luke names no letters and no formula but the one in the next sentence, and this page does not tie Plutarch’s words to these exorcists. The formula is Horkizō hymas ton Iēsoun hon Paulos kēryssei, “I adjure you by the Jesus whom Paul proclaims.” Horkizō with the person adjured and the one sworn by, both in the accusative, is the construction of Mark 5:7, where the unclean spirit says to Jesus horkizō se ton theon, “I adjure you by God”: there it is the spirit who adjures, and here it is those who address spirits. The simple verb stands in Mark 5:7 and here; its compounds are at Matthew 26:63 and 1 Thessalonians 5:27. The apparatus gives Horkizō, “I adjure,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and Horkizomen, “we adjure,” to Robinson–Pierpont; ASV “I adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” and TLB “I adjure you by Jesus, whom Paul preaches” have the singular, KJV “We adjure you by Jesus whom Paul preacheth” and Geneva “We adjure you by Jesus, whom Paul preacheth” the plural, Douay “I conjure you by Jesus, whom Paul preacheth” the singular with another verb, NWT 1984 “I solemnly charge YOU by Jesus whom Paul preaches” the singular with a plainer one, and NIV “In the name of the Jesus whom Paul preaches, I command you to come out” turns the oath into a command.

Sceva, a Jewish high priest, and his seven sons (Acts 19:14). Skeua, “Sceva,” is named in this verse alone in the New Testament, and Luke gives no other fact about him. He is Ioudaiou archiereōs, “a Jewish high priest”; this translation prints “high priest” for archiereus at 4:6 and 5:17, and the New Testament names no other high priest called Sceva. NIV “a Jewish chief priest” and NWT 1984 “a Jewish chief priest” keep the “Jewish”; KJV “a Jew, and chief of the priests,” ASV “a Jew, a chief priest” and Douay “a Jew, a chief priest” split it into two; Geneva “a Jew, the Priest” drops the “chief” and TLB “a Jewish priest” drops it too. The apparatus gives tinos Skeua … hyioi, “of a certain Sceva … sons,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and tines hyioi Skeua, “certain sons of Sceva,” to Robinson–Pierpont, who also read hoi touto poiountes, “who were doing this,” with the article; Geneva “certain sons of Sceva” has the second division, and its “about seven” adds an “about” that the apparatus does not give. The number is hepta, “seven,” and v16 will say “both.”

“Jesus I know, and Paul I know,” and “both” (Acts 19:15–16). Ton Iēsoun ginōskō kai ton Paulon epistamai uses two verbs for knowing. Epistamai is the verb of Apollos at 18:25, epistamenos, “knowing only the baptism of John,” and of v25 below, epistasthe, “you know”; Luke does not mark a difference between the two verbs here, and this translation prints “know” for both. KJV “Jesus I know, and Paul I know” and ASV “Jesus I know, and Paul I know” and Douay “Jesus I know, and Paul I know” and TLB “I know Jesus and I know Paul” do the same; Geneva “Jesus I acknowledge, and Paul I know,” NWT 1984 “I know Jesus and I am acquainted with Paul” and NIV “Jesus I know, and Paul I know about” mark the second verb. The apparatus gives men, “indeed,” after Ton to Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28, and the printed text does not. Spirits that know Jesus are in the Gospels (Luke 4:34, “I know who you are”) and in Acts at 16:17, where the spirit in the girl says that these men are slaves of the Most High God. Then the question, hymeis de tines este?, “but who are you?” At v16 the man “in whom the evil spirit was” acts, ephalomenos, “leaping on,” a participle that stands in this verse alone, and katakyrieusas amphoterōn, “having mastered both.” Katakyrieuō stands in four verses of the New Testament, Matthew 20:25 and Mark 10:42, where the rulers of the nations “lord it over” them, 1 Peter 5:3, and here; amphoteroi, “both,” stands in Acts at 8:38, here and 23:8. The sons are seven (v14) and the Greek says “both”; the apparatus gives katakyrieusas amphoterōn to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and kai katakyrieusan autōn, “and having mastered them,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which has no number. ASV “mastered both of them” and Douay “mastering them both” keep the “both”; KJV “overcame them” and Geneva “overcame them” have the reading without it; NIV “overpowered them all” and NWT 1984 “got the mastery of one after the other” agree with neither, and TLB “leaped on two of them” makes the pair a stated number. Luke does not reconcile seven with both, and this page does not. Ischysen kat’ autōn, “prevailed against them,” is a verb to be heard again at v20. They flee gymnous kai tetraumatismenous, “naked and wounded,” and traumatizō stands in two verses, Luke 20:12 and here: KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NWT 1984 print “naked and wounded,” NIV “naked and bleeding” and TLB “naked and badly injured.” Luke does not say whose house it was.

Fear, “magnified,” “confessing” and the practices (Acts 19:17–18). Epepesen phobos epi pantas autous, “fear fell upon them all,” has matches at Luke 1:12, where phobos epepesen ep’ auton falls on Zechariah, and at Revelation 11:11, where “great fear fell on those who were watching” the two witnesses stand up, and these are the only three verses of the New Testament in which phobos is the subject of the compound verb epipiptō (searched); the apparatus gives epepesen to Westcott–Hort, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont and epesen to Tregelles. KJV “fear fell on them all” keeps the falling, NIV “they were all seized with fear” does not, and TLB “a solemn fear descended on the city” names the city and adds the solemnity. Emegalyneto to onoma tou kyriou Iēsou, “the name of the Lord Jesus was being magnified,” uses a verb that stands in eight verses of the New Testament and three of Acts: 5:13, 10:46, where the hearers of the tongues are heard “magnifying God,” and here. KJV “the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified,” ASV “the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified,” Geneva “the Name of the Lord Jesus was magnified” and Douay “the name of the Lord Jesus was magnified” keep it, NWT 1984 “the name of the Lord Jesus went on being magnified” keeps the imperfect, and NIV “the name of the Lord Jesus was held in high honor” and TLB “the name of the Lord Jesus was greatly honored” say it another way. The word will be heard again in the other direction at vv27–35. Exomologoumenoi, “confessing,” stands in nine verses of the New Testament, among them Matthew 3:6 and Mark 1:5, where the people are baptized by John “confessing their sins,” and here; what these men confess is tas praxeis autōn, “their practices,” a noun that stands in six verses, Matthew 16:27, Luke 23:51, here and Romans 8:13 (Romans 12:4 is now on these pages; Colossians 3:9 is not yet). KJV “came, and confessed, and shewed their deeds,” ASV “came, confessing, and declaring their deeds” and Douay “came confessing and declaring their deeds” print “deeds,” NWT 1984 “confess and report their practices openly” prints “practices,” and NIV “openly confessed what they had done” makes it a clause. This translation prints “practices” so that the noun here and the participle praxantōn, “who had practiced,” in the next verse sound as one root.

“The curious arts,” the books and the sum (Acts 19:19). Ta perierga is made of peri, “around,” and ergon, “work”; its relatives are periergos and periergazomai, “busybody,” at 1 Timothy 5:13 and 2 Thessalonians 3:11 (neither yet on these pages), and this neuter plural, “the over-busy things,” stands in this verse alone. The Greek has not the word for a magician or for magic, which Luke used at 8:9, 8:11 and 13:6 (dictionary); that these were magical practices is what the burning of books suggests, and the versions say it differently. KJV “used curious arts,” Geneva “used curious arts” and Douay “followed curious arts” keep the old word; ASV “practised magical arts” and NWT 1984 “practiced magical arts” say magic; NIV “practiced sorcery” and TLB “practicing black magic” say more. This translation prints “the curious arts” because it is strange enough to send the reader here. The verse does not say that these were the same people as the believers of v18, only that “a considerable number” (hikanoi) of them brought their biblous, “books,” and burned them enōpion pantōn, “in front of everyone”; TLB “Many of the believers who had been practicing black magic” makes them one group. NIV “scrolls” where the others print “books.” Synepsēphisan tas timas autōn, “they reckoned up their prices,” has a compound that stands in this verse alone; the root psēphizō stands in four verses, Luke 14:28, Acts 1:26, here and Revelation 13:18. The sum is argyriou myriadas pente, “five ten-thousands of silver”; myrias stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Luke 12:1, here, Acts 21:20, Hebrews 12:22, Jude 14, Revelation 5:11 and 9:16. Five times ten thousand is fifty thousand, and the Greek names no coin: KJV “fifty thousand pieces of silver,” ASV “fifty thousand pieces of silver,” Geneva “fifty thousand pieces of silver,” Douay “fifty thousand pieces of silver” and NWT 1984 “fifty thousand pieces of silver” supply “pieces,” NIV “fifty thousand drachmas” supplies a named coin, and TLB “$10,000” turns it into a modern sum. This page gives no modern equivalent.

“Mightily,” “growing” and “prevailing” (Acts 19:20). Kata kratos, “according to might,” stands in this verse alone. The clause ēuxanen, “was growing,” is the third of Luke’s reports that the word grows: ēuxanen kai eplēthyneto, “was growing and being multiplied,” at 6:7 and 12:24, and here ēuxanen kai ischyen, “was growing and prevailing”; the form ēuxanen stands in these three verses of Acts. Ischyō stands in six verses of Acts: 6:10, 15:10, v16 above, here, 25:7 and 27:16. In four of the six it is negated or qualified, “they could not” (6:10), “not able” (25:7), “neither we nor our fathers were able” (15:10), “we were scarcely able” (27:16), and the only two verses where it is simply affirmed are v16, of the man with the spirit, and here, of the word. The apparatus gives tou kyriou ho logos, “of the Lord the word,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and ho logos tou kyriou to Robinson–Pierpont, a change of order only; all four have kyrios. KJV “So mightily grew the word of God and prevailed,” Geneva “So the word of God grew mightily, and prevailed” and Douay “So mightily grew the word of God, and was confirmed” print “God,” and the last changes the second verb; ASV “So mightily grew the word of the Lord and prevailed” and NIV “the word of the Lord spread widely and grew in power” print “the Lord”; NWT 1984 “Thus in a mighty way the word of Jehovah kept growing and prevailing” prints “Jehovah” for kyrios, and TLB “God’s message” paraphrases. This translation prints “the word of the Lord,” as at 13:48, 13:49, 15:35, 15:36 and 16:32, where Luke’s phrase leaves open whether the Lord is Jesus or Jehovah; the same phrase is at v10 above.

Verses 21–22 · ἐπληρώθη ταῦτα … ἔθετο … ἐν τῷ πνεύματι … δεῖ με καὶ Ῥώμην ἰδεῖν … Τιμόθεον καὶ Ἔραστον … ἐπέσχεν χρόνον eplērōthē tauta … etheto … en tō pneumati … dei me kai Rhōmēn idein … Timotheon kai Erraston … epesken chronon — Paul’s plan: Macedonia, Achaia, Jerusalem, and “I must also see Rome”

“Resolved in the spirit” (Acts 19:21). Etheto ho Paulos en tō pneumati is the verb tithēmi, “to put,” with the place where the thing is put, as Luke writes it of the heart: ethento … en tē kardia at Luke 1:66, thete en tais kardiais at Luke 21:14 and ethou en tē kardia at Acts 5:4; only here is the place the spirit. The Greek has neither “his” nor “holy,” as at 18:25, zeōn tō pneumati, and this page does not decide whether it is Paul’s own spirit or the Spirit of God. ASV “purposed in the spirit,” KJV “purposed in the spirit” and Douay “purposed in the spirit” leave it open; Geneva “purposed by the Spirit” and TLB “felt impelled by the Holy Spirit” choose the Spirit of God, NWT 1984 “purposed in his spirit” chooses Paul’s own, and NIV “decided” drops the phrase. The plan has three stops, dielthōn tēn Makedonian kai Achaian, “having passed through Macedonia and Achaia,” eis Hierosolyma, “to Jerusalem,” and then dei me kai Rhōmēn idein, “I must also see Rome.” Rhōmē as a city stands in Acts at 18:2, where the Jews are told to leave it, here, 23:11, 28:14 and 28:16. Dei, “it is necessary,” is Luke’s verb of what has to be: of the Lord’s servant at 9:16, “how much he must suffer for the sake of my name,” and here of Paul and Rome; it is said to him again at 23:11 and 27:24. The infinitive idein, “to see,” is Paul’s own in his letter, “I long to see you” (Romans 1:11). KJV “I must also see Rome,” ASV “I must also see Rome,” Geneva “I must also see Rome” and NWT 1984 “I must also see Rome” keep the verb of seeing; Douay “I must see Rome also” changes the order; NIV “I must visit Rome also” and TLB “I must go on to Rome” change the verb.

Timothy and Erastus, and “a time” in Asia (Acts 19:22). The two he sends are duo tōn diakonountōn autō, “two of those who served him” (dictionary): KJV “two of them that ministered unto him,” ASV “two of them that ministered unto him,” Geneva “two of them that ministered unto him,” Douay “two of them that ministered to him” and NWT 1984 “two of those who ministered to him” keep the verb, NIV “two of his helpers” and TLB “his two assistants” make it a noun. Timothy is named in Acts at 16:1, 17:14, 17:15, 18:5, here and 20:4. Erastos is named in three verses of the New Testament: here, Romans 16:23, where “Erastus, the city’s steward” (ho oikonomos tēs poleōs) greets the Romans, and 2 Timothy 4:20, “Erastus stayed in Corinth” (the last two not yet on these pages). Whether the man of Acts and the man of Romans are one, and which city Romans means, Luke does not say, and this page reports the question and does not decide it. He himself epesken chronon eis tēn Asian, “stayed on for a time in Asia.” KJV “he himself stayed in Asia for a season” and ASV “he himself stayed in Asia for a while” keep it, NIV “he stayed in the province of Asia a little longer” adds a province and a comparison, and TLB “he stayed awhile longer in Asia Minor” adds a modern name. The verse gives no length, and the “time” and the two years and three months of vv8–10 are not added together.

Verses 23–27 · τάραχος οὐκ ὀλίγος περὶ τῆς ὁδοῦ … Δημήτριος … ἀργυροκόπος … ναοὺς ἀργυροῦς Ἀρτέμιδος … οὐκ ὀλίγην ἐργασίαν … ἐκ ταύτης τῆς ἐργασίας ἡ εὐπορία … οἱ διὰ χειρῶν γινόμενοι … ἀπελεγμόν … τῆς μεγαλειότητος … ἡ οἰκουμένη tarachos ouk oligos peri tēs hodou … Dēmētrios … argyrokopos … naous argyrous Artemidos … ouk oligēn ergasian … ek tautēs tēs ergasias hē euporia … hoi dia cheirōn ginomenoi … apelegmon … tēs megaleiotētos … hē oikoumenē — Demetrius the silversmith: profit, temples of silver, and the goddess whose magnificence is in danger

“No small disturbance concerning the Way” (Acts 19:23). Tarachos, the noun, stands in two verses of the New Testament, 12:18, where there is “no small disturbance among the soldiers” over Peter, and here, and in both Luke writes the same words, tarachos ouk oligos. The negative with oligos is a habit of Luke’s, and it stands in eight verses of Acts: 12:18, 14:28, 15:2, 17:4, 17:12, here, v24 below and 27:20. Hodos, “the Way,” is the name Luke gave the movement at 9:2 and v9 above, and it comes again at 22:4, and at 24:14 and 24:22. KJV “no small stir about that way” prints the word in lower case with “that”; ASV “no small stir concerning the Way,” NWT 1984 “no little disturbance concerning The Way” and NIV “a great disturbance about the Way” capitalize it; Geneva “no small trouble about that way” follows the KJV, Douay “no small disturbance about the way of the Lord” adds a genitive the Greek does not have here, and TLB “a big blowup developed in Ephesus concerning the Christians” names a city and a people where Luke names neither.

Demetrius, the silversmith, and “silver temples” (Acts 19:24). Dēmētrios is named in three verses of the New Testament: here, v38 below and 3 John 12; Luke does not say whether the man of 3 John is this one, and this page does not decide. Argyrokopos, “silversmith,” stands in this verse alone; technitēs, “craftsman,” stands in four verses, here, v38, Hebrews 11:10 and Revelation 18:22 (the last two not yet on these pages). What he makes is naous argyrous Artemidos, “silver temples of Artemis.” Naos, the sanctuary-house, stands in Acts at 17:24, where Paul says that God “does not dwell in handmade temples,” and here, and this translation prints “temples” in both so that the word is heard to return; the temple of Artemis in v27 is hieron, the whole precinct, a different word. Geneva “silver temples of Diana” and Douay “silver temples for Diana” keep the word; KJV “silver shrines for Diana,” ASV “silver shrines of Diana,” NIV “silver shrines of Artemis,” NWT 1984 “silver shrines of” Artemis and TLB “silver shrines of the Greek goddess Diana” say “shrines.” The Greek says only that the temples were of silver: it does not say how large they were, or what they were used for. “Diana” is the Roman name of the goddess, the one the Greek text does not use (reported here, not checked); five versions on the shelf print it, KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and TLB, and two print the Greek name, NIV and NWT 1984. The profit is parecheto tois technitais ouk oligēn ergasian, “was providing the craftsmen no small profit”; the verb is the one of 16:16, pareichen, where the girl with the spirit “was bringing her masters much profit” (ergasian pollēn), and ergasia stands in Acts in four verses, 16:16, 16:19, here and v25. The apparatus gives ouk oligēn ergasian to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and ergasian ouk oligēn to Robinson–Pierpont, a change of order. KJV “brought no small gain unto the craftsmen,” Douay “brought no small gain to the craftsmen” and NWT 1984 “furnished the craftsmen no little gain” say “gain”; ASV “brought no little business unto the craftsmen” and NIV “brought in a lot of business for the craftsmen there” say “business”; Geneva “brought great gains unto the craftsmen” drops the negative; and TLB “employed many craftsmen” makes the silversmith an employer.

“From this business we have our prosperity” (Acts 19:25). Hous synathroisas, “whom, having gathered,” is a verb that stands in three verses of Luke–Acts: Luke 24:33 (in the form ēthroismenous), Acts 12:12 and here. The workmen are tous peri ta toiauta ergatas, “the workmen of such trades,” and the speech opens Andres, epistasthe hoti ek tautēs tēs ergasias hē euporia hēmin estin. Epistasthe, “you know,” is a form of the verb of 18:25, epistamenos, said of Apollos. Euporia, “prosperity,” stands in this verse alone; the verb of the same root, euporeō, “to have means,” stands in Acts at 11:29 alone. This translation prints “profit” for ergasia at 16:16, 16:19 and v24, where what is named is what the work brings in, and “business” here, where it is the trade that the prosperity comes from. The apparatus gives hēmin, “for us,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and hēmōn, “our,” to Robinson–Pierpont. NWT 1984 “from this business we have our prosperity” and ASV “by this business we have our wealth” say “business”; KJV “by this craft we have our wealth” and Geneva “by this craft we have our goods” say “craft”; Douay “our gain is by this trade” says “trade”; NIV “we receive a good income from this business” and TLB “this business is our income” say “income.”

“This Paul” and “gods made through hands” (Acts 19:26). Theōreite kai akouete, “you see and hear,” and ho Paulos houtos, “this Paul,” with houtos placed after the name. What Paul has done is peisas metestēsen hikanon ochlon, “persuaded and turned away a considerable crowd”: the narrator’s verb of 18:4 and v8 above, peithō, which the Jews at Corinth used as their accusation at 18:13 (anapeithei) and which Demetrius uses here, so that Paul’s own preaching word and both sets of accusers’ word are one root. Hikanos is the adjective of v19, hikanoi, “a considerable number.” The substance is ouk eisin theoi hoi dia cheirōn ginomenoi, “the ones made through hands are not gods,” and the preposition and noun, dia cheirōn, are those of v11, where God does acts of power dia tōn cheirōn Paulou, “through the hands of Paul.” Luke gives Paul no such sentence at Ephesus; it is Demetrius’s report. Its nearest matches in Acts are Paul’s own words at Athens, God “does not dwell in handmade temples” (17:24) and “we ought not to suppose that the divine is like gold or silver or stone, an engraving of craft and human thought” (17:29), and at Lystra “turn from these futile things to a living God” (14:15). KJV “they be no gods, which are made with hands,” ASV “they are no gods, that are made with hands,” Geneva “they be not Gods which are made with hands” and Douay “They are not gods which are made by hands” and NWT 1984 “the ones that are made by hands are not gods” say “by” or “with” hands, NIV “gods made by human hands are no gods at all” adds “human,” and TLB “handmade gods aren’t gods at all” makes the phrase an adjective.

“Disrepute,” the temple and the “magnificence” (Acts 19:27). Kindyneuei hēmin to meros eis apelegmon elthein, “this share of ours is in danger of coming into disrepute.” Apelegmos stands in this verse alone and is made from elegchō, “to expose, to refute”; kindyneuō, “to be in danger,” stands in four verses of the New Testament, Luke 8:23, here, v40 below and 1 Corinthians 15:30 (the last not yet on these pages). ASV “come into disrepute” and NWT 1984 “come into disrepute” say it with one phrase; KJV “set at nought” and Douay “set at nought” say it another way; NIV “lose its good name” says a third. Then the argument turns from the trade to the goddess: to tēs megalēs theas Artemidos hieron eis outhen logisthēnai, “the temple of the great goddess Artemis to be counted as nothing.” Thea, “goddess,” stands in this verse in the printed text; the apparatus gives it to Robinson–Pierpont alone at v35, and at v37 Luke writes tēn theon, the masculine noun with a feminine article, for “our goddess.” The last clause is mellein te kai kathaireisthai tēs megaleiotētos autēs: the verb “to be about” has no stated subject, this translation reads it as the goddess, and the noun is in the genitive, “brought down from her magnificence.” The apparatus gives the genitive tēs megaleiotētos to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the accusative tēn megaleiotēta, so that the magnificence itself is what is destroyed, to Robinson–Pierpont. ASV “deposed from her magnificence” and NIV “robbed of her divine majesty” have the genitive; KJV “her magnificence should be destroyed” and Geneva “her magnificence” with “destroyed” have the accusative, as does NWT 1984 “her magnificence which the whole” world “is about to be brought down to nothing”; Douay “her majesty shall begin to be destroyed” has the accusative and a different noun; TLB “will be forgotten” says neither. The relative hēn is feminine and can refer to the goddess or to her magnificence: ASV “whom all Asia and the world worshippeth,” KJV “whom all Asia and the world worshippeth,” Douay “whom all Asia and the world worshippeth” and NIV “who is worshiped throughout the province of Asia and the world” read it as the goddess, Geneva “which all Asia and the world worshippeth” and NWT 1984 “which the whole” of Asia and the inhabited earth worships read it as the magnificence. Megaleiotēs stands in three verses of the New Testament, Luke 9:43, where all are astonished “at the majesty of God,” here, and 2 Peter 1:16. The last word is hē oikoumenē, “the inhabited world,” the word of 17:6, “turned the inhabited world upside down,” and 11:28 and 17:31; NWT 1984 “the inhabited earth” keeps it, and KJV “the world,” ASV “the world,” NIV “the world” and TLB “all around the world” do not.

Verses 28–34 · πλήρεις θυμοῦ … Μεγάλη ἡ Ἄρτεμις Ἐφεσίων … τῆς συγχύσεως … ὥρμησάν τε ὁμοθυμαδὸν εἰς τὸ θέατρον … Γάϊον καὶ Ἀρίσταρχον … συνεκδήμους … τὸν δῆμον … οὐκ εἴων … τῶν Ἀσιαρχῶν … ἡ ἐκκλησία συγκεχυμένη … συνεβίβασαν Ἀλέξανδρον … ὡς ἐπὶ ὥρας δύο plēreis thymou … Megalē hē Artemis Ephesiōn … tēs sygchyseōs … hōrmēsan te homothymadon eis to theatron … Gaïon kai Aristarchon … synekdēmous … ton dēmon … ouk eiōn … tōn Asiarchōn … hē ekklēsia sygkechymenē … synebibasan Alexandron … hōs epi hōras dyo — the riot: the cry, the theater, Paul held back, and Alexander who is not heard

“Full of rage” and “Great is Artemis” (Acts 19:28). Plēreis thymou, “full of rage,” has a match in Luke only at Luke 4:28, eplēsthēsan pantes thymou, where the synagogue at Nazareth is “filled with rage”; the noun thymos stands in Luke–Acts in those two verses and no others. KJV “full of wrath,” Geneva “full of wrath” and ASV “filled with wrath” say “wrath,” NWT 1984 “full of anger” and Douay “full of anger” say “anger,” NIV “they were furious” makes it an adjective, and TLB “their anger boiled” a verb. The cry is Megalē hē Artemis Ephesiōn, with the predicate first, “Great is Artemis of the Ephesians,” and it comes again at v34. The root megal- runs through the chapter, in five of its verses: emegalyneto, “was being magnified,” of the name of the Lord Jesus at v17; megalēs theas and megaleiotētos at v27; the cry at vv28 and 34; and megalēs Artemidos at v35. The name that is “magnified” and the goddess who is “great” are in one chapter, and Luke draws no line between them. KJV “Great is Diana of the Ephesians,” ASV “Great is Diana of the Ephesus,” Geneva “Great is Diana of the Ephesians” and Douay “Great is Diana of the Ephesians” print the Latin name; NIV “Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!” and NWT 1984 “Great is” Artemis of the Ephesians keep the Greek.

“The confusion,” “with one accord,” the theater, Gaius and Aristarchus (Acts 19:29). Sygchysis, “confusion,” stands in this verse alone; its verb stands in Acts at 2:6, 9:22 (“bewildering” in this translation), v32 below, 21:27 and 21:31. Hōrmēsan te homothymadon, “they rushed with one accord,” is the phrase of 7:57, where the crowd “rushed upon him with one accord” at Stephen; homothymadon stands in ten verses of Acts, with 18:12, where the Jews rise against Paul, the ninth, and this the tenth. Theatron, “theater,” stands in three verses of the New Testament, here, v31 and 1 Corinthians 4:9, “we became a theater to the world” (not yet on these pages). KJV “rushed with one accord into the theatre,” Douay “rushed with one accord into the theatre” and ASV “rushed with one accord into the theatre” keep the word; Geneva “rushed into the common place with one assent” changes it; TLB “Everyone rushed to the amphitheater” names another building. The apparatus gives polis, “the city,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and polis holē, “the whole city,” to Robinson–Pierpont; KJV “the whole city was filled with confusion” and NIV “Soon the whole city was in an uproar” have “whole,” ASV “the city was filled with the confusion” and NWT 1984 “the city became filled with confusion” do not. Synarpasantes, “seizing,” stands in three verses of the New Testament, 6:12, here and 27:15; the men seized are Gaius and Aristarchus, Makedonas, synekdēmous Paulou, “Macedonians, Paul’s traveling companions.” Synekdēmos stands in two verses, here and 2 Corinthians 8:19 (not yet on these pages). Aristarchus is named in five verses, here, 20:4, and 27:2, Colossians 4:10 and Philemon 24 (not yet on these pages); at 20:4 he is “of the Thessalonians” and Gaius is “of Derbe,” and Luke does not say whether the Gaius of 20:4 is the Macedonian of this verse. Gaius is named in five verses, here, 20:4, Romans 16:23, 1 Corinthians 1:14 and 3 John 1, and this page does not decide how many men they name. NIV “Paul’s traveling companions from Macedonia,” NWT 1984 “traveling companions of Paul” and KJV “Paul’s companions in travel” keep the “traveling”; TLB “for trial” adds a purpose the verse does not give.

“The people,” the disciples and the Asiarchs (Acts 19:30–31). Ton dēmon, “the people,” is the word of Herod’s speech (12:22) and of the crowd in front of whom Jason is to be brought (17:5); dēmos stands in four verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 12:22, 17:5, here and v33. Ouk eiōn auton hoi mathētai, “the disciples were not allowing him,” has the verb of 16:7, where “the Spirit of Jesus did not allow them” to enter Bithynia. Asiarchōn, “of the Asiarchs,” stands in this verse alone. Asiarchs are reported to have been leading men of the province of Asia with a part in its festivals and games and in the worship of the emperors (reported here, not checked), and the word says only that they were ontes autō philoi, “being friends of his.” ASV “certain also of the Asiarchs, being his friends” keeps the Greek word; KJV “certain of the chief of Asia, which were his friends,” Geneva “Certain also of the chief of Asia, which were his friends” and Douay “some also of the rulers of Asia, who were his friends” translate the title; NIV “some of the officials of the province, friends of Paul” adds “province”; NWT 1984 “commissioners of festivals and games” says what the office was taken to be; and TLB “Roman officers of the province” says it was Roman, which the Greek does not. What they plead is mē dounai heauton eis to theatron, “not to give himself to the theater”: KJV “he would not adventure himself into the theatre,” ASV “besought him not to adventure himself into the theatre” and Douay “he would not venture himself into the theatre” use the old word for risk; NWT 1984 “not to risk himself in the theater” and TLB “not to risk his life by entering” use the new one, the second with a life; Geneva “he would not present himself in the Common place” changes the verb.

“The assembly” was in confusion (Acts 19:32). Ēn gar hē ekklēsia sygkechymenē, “for the assembly was in confusion,” is the participle of the verb of v29. Ekklēsia stands in 23 verses of Acts as the archive numbers them; in three of them, here, v39 and v41 (v40 in the archive), it is the civil assembly of a city, in one, 7:38, it is Israel at Sinai, and in the other nineteen it is the believers. This translation prints “congregation” for the believers and for Israel, and “assembly” for a crowd that has gathered by shouting, because “the congregation was in confusion” would tell a reader something Luke does not say; the word is the same, and the clerk will make it plain at v39 by adding “lawful.” KJV “the assembly was confused,” ASV “the assembly was in confusion,” Douay “the assembly was confused,” NIV “The assembly was in confusion” and NWT 1984 “the assembly was in confusion” print “assembly”; Geneva “the assembly was out of order” does too, and TLB “everything was in confusion” has no word for it. Luke adds that hoi pleious ouk ēdeisan tinos heneka synelēlythesan, “most did not know for what reason they had come together,” and says it without comment.

Alexander, and “about two hours” (Acts 19:33–34). Ek de tou ochlou synebibasan Alexandron: symbibazō stands in seven verses of the New Testament and three of Acts, 9:22, where Saul is “proving,” 16:10, where they are “concluding,” and here, and its root sense is “to put together”; this translation prints “put forward” and has the Jews “pushing him to the front” for proballontōn, a verb that stands in Luke 21:30 and here. The apparatus gives synebibasan to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and proebibasan, “brought forward,” to Robinson–Pierpont, whose verb stands in Matthew 14:8, of the girl “prompted” by her mother, and here. KJV “they drew Alexander out of the multitude, the Jews putting him forward,” Douay “they drew forth Alexander out of the multitude, the Jews thrusting him forward” and Geneva “drew forth Alexander, the Jews thrusting him forwards” say “drew”; ASV “they brought Alexander out of the multitude, the Jews putting him forward” and NWT 1984 “they brought Alexander out of the crowd, the Jews thrusting him up front” say “brought”; NIV “The Jews in the crowd pushed Alexander to the front, and they shouted instructions to him” reads the verb as “instruct”; TLB “dragged forward” says it with force. Alexandros is named in five verses of the New Testament: Mark 15:21, where he is a son of Simon of Cyrene, Acts 4:6, where he is of the high priest’s family, here, 1 Timothy 1:20 and 2 Timothy 4:14; whether the man here is one of the others, Luke does not say, and this page does not decide. He kataseisas tēn cheira, “motioning with his hand,” as Peter does at 12:17, Paul at 13:16 and Paul again at 21:40, and then ēthelen apologeisthai tō dēmō, “wished to make a defense to the people”; the verse does not say whose defense it was, and the next verse gives no word of it. KJV “would have made his defence unto the people,” ASV “would have made a defense unto the people,” NIV “to make a defense before the people” and NWT 1984 “was wanting to make his defense to the people” keep it, Geneva “would have excused the matter to the people” and Douay “would have given the people satisfaction” change it, and TLB “tried to speak” drops the defense. At v34 epignontes hoti Ioudaios estin, “recognizing that he is a Jew,” the crowd gives phōnē egeneto mia ek pantōn, “one voice from all,” hōs epi hōras dyo, “for about two hours.” NIV “they all shouted in unison for about two hours” and NWT 1984 “one cry arose from them all as they shouted for about two hours” keep the number, as does KJV “all with one voice about the space of two hours cried out”; TLB “kept it up for two hours” drops the “about.”

Verses 35–41 · ὁ γραμματεύς … καταστείλας … νεωκόρον … τοῦ διοπετοῦς … ἀναντιρρήτων … κατεσταλμένους … προπετές … ἱεροσύλους … βλασφημοῦντας τὴν θεὸν ἡμῶν … ἔχουσιν πρός τινα λόγον … ἀγοραῖοι ἄγονται καὶ ἀνθύπατοί εἰσιν … ἐν τῇ ἐννόμῳ ἐκκλησίᾳ … στάσεως … ἀποδοῦναι λόγον … συστροφῆς … ἀπέλυσεν τὴν ἐκκλησίαν ho grammateus … katasteilas … neōkoron … tou diopetous … anantirrhētōn … katestalmenous … propetes … hierosylous … blasphēmountas tēn theon hēmōn … echousin pros tina logon … agoraioi agontai kai anthypatoi eisin … en tē ennomō ekklēsia … staseōs … apodounai logon … systrophēs … apelysen tēn ekklēsian — the town clerk: the temple keeper, the thing that fell from heaven, proconsuls and a lawful assembly, and the dismissal

The town clerk, the quiet and the temple keeper (Acts 19:35). Ho grammateus is the noun that this translation prints “scribes” at 4:5 and 6:12, of the Jerusalem scribes; it stands in four verses of Acts, those two, here and 23:9, and here it is the city’s own officer, which is why this translation prints “town clerk.” KJV “townclerk,” ASV “townclerk” and Douay “town clerk” say it so; NIV “city clerk” and NWT 1984 “city recorder” say “city”; TLB “mayor” gives him an office the Greek does not name. He does what the next verse asks of the crowd: katasteilas, “having quieted,” and katestalmenous, “quiet,” at v36 are one verb, which stands in these two verses and no others in the New Testament. ASV “had quieted the multitude,” NIV “quieted the crowd” and NWT 1984 “had quieted the crowd” keep it, KJV “had appeased the people” and Douay “had appeased the multitudes” say “appeased,” Geneva “had stayed the people” says “stayed,” and TLB “was able to quiet them down” adds an effort. His question is tis gar estin anthrōpōn hos ou ginōskei, “who is there among human beings who does not know”; the apparatus gives the genitive plural anthrōpōn to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and the singular anthrōpos to Robinson–Pierpont: KJV “what man is there that knoweth not” and ASV “what man is there who knoweth not” have the singular, NWT 1984 “who really is there of mankind that does not know” the plural. The city is neōkoron, “temple keeper” of the great Artemis, a word that stands in this verse alone; its parts are neōs, an older form of naos, “temple,” and the verb koreō, “to sweep” (reported here, not checked). ASV “temple-keeper of the great Diana” and NWT 1984 “the temple keeper” keep the word, NIV “the guardian of the temple of the great Artemis” paraphrases it, and KJV “a worshipper of the great goddess Diana,” Geneva “a worshipper of the great goddess Diana” and Douay “a worshipper of the great Diana” make the city a worshipper; TLB “the center of the religion of the great Diana” makes it a center.

“The thing that fell from heaven” (Acts 19:35). Kai tou diopetous has no noun: it is the neuter of an adjective, “of the fallen-from-Zeus,” and it stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. Its first part is Dios, the genitive of Zeus (Zeus, whom the Lystrans named of Barnabas at 14:12), and its second the verb piptō, “to fall” (the parts are reported here, not checked). In the Greek of Euripides’s Iphigenia among the Taurians the image of Artemis is diopetes (line 977, Phoibos m’ epempse deuro, diopetes labein, “Phoebus sent me here to take the heaven-fallen”), and the same image is said to have fallen “from the sky” into the temples there (lines 87–88, ho phasin enthade es tousde naous ouranou pesein apo), read in the Greek. Euripides is not writing of Ephesus, and the lines show only that the word was used of an image of Artemis that was said to have come down from the sky. Four versions print the name Jupiter, the Latin Zeus, where Luke has only the first part of a compound: KJV “the image which fell down from Jupiter,” ASV “the image which fell down from Jupiter,” Geneva “which came down from Jupiter” and Douay “of Jupiter’s offspring,” the last turning the verb into a birth; three print heaven: NIV “her image, which fell from heaven,” NWT 1984 “the image that fell from heaven” and TLB “whose image fell down to us from heaven.” Six of the seven add the noun “image,” and the Douay does not. This translation prints “the thing that fell from heaven,” with no noun, as the Greek has none.

“Undeniable,” “quiet,” “rash” and “temple-robbers” (Acts 19:36–37). Anantirrhētōn, “undeniable,” is an adjective that stands in this verse alone; its adverb stands at 10:29, where Peter says he came “without objection.” Deon estin, “it is necessary,” is the verb of Paul’s “I must” at v21, dei, in the form of a participle: the same verb is in the mouth of Paul about Rome and of the clerk about quiet. Propetes, “rash,” stands in two verses, here and 2 Timothy 3:4 (not yet on these pages). KJV “cannot be spoken against,” ASV “cannot be gainsaid” and Douay “cannot be contradicted” say it as a negative; NIV “are undeniable,” NWT 1984 “are indisputable” and TLB “an indisputable fact” say it as a positive; and Geneva “no man can speak against these things” makes it a person. KJV “to do nothing rashly,” ASV “to do nothing rash” and NIV “not do anything rash” keep the clerk’s two negatives. The charge he clears is oute hierosylous oute blasphēmountas tēn theon hēmōn, “neither temple-robbers nor blasphemers of our goddess.” Hierosylos stands in this verse alone; its verb, hierosyleō, is Paul’s at Romans 2:22, “do you rob temples?” ASV “robbers of temples” and NWT 1984 “robbers of temples” and NIV “neither robbed temples” keep the word temples; KJV “robbers of churches” prints “churches” for the temples of a goddess, a different Greek word from ekklēsia; Geneva “committed sacrilege” and Douay “guilty of sacrilege” use an abstract word; and TLB “stolen nothing from her temple” narrows it to one temple. The apparatus gives hēmōn, “our,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and hymōn, “your,” to Robinson–Pierpont: ASV “blasphemers of our goddess,” NIV “nor blasphemed our goddess” and NWT 1984 “blasphemers of our goddess” have “our,” KJV “blasphemers of your goddess,” Geneva “do blaspheme your goddess” and Douay “blasphemy against your goddess” have “your,” and TLB “have not defamed her” has neither. The two charges are the two things that Josephus says the Jewish law forbids: “Let none blaspheme gods whom other cities hold as such, nor rob foreign temples” (blasphēmeitō de mēdeis theous hous poleis allai nomizousi, mēde sylan hiera xenika, Antiquities 4.207) and, in another work, that the lawgiver “has expressly forbidden us to mock or blaspheme those held to be gods among others” (mēte chleuazein mēte blasphēmein tous nomizomenous theous par’ heterois, Against Apion 2.237), both read in the Greek. Josephus says nothing of Paul, and Luke does not cite him; the lines are a parallel, not a source. At Athens Luke shows Paul beginning from an altar, not from an insult (17:23).

“A case,” “court days” and “proconsuls” (Acts 19:38–39). Echousin pros tina logon is literally “have a word against anyone,” the noun that Gallio used twice at 18:14 and 18:15, kata logon, “with reason,” and peri logou, “about a word,” and the clerk will use it again at v40 for an “account”; this translation prints “case” here, as the sense, and the Greek is the same noun in all four. KJV “have a matter against any man,” ASV “have a matter against any man,” Geneva “have a matter against any man” and Douay “have a matter against any man” say “matter”; NIV “have a grievance against anybody” says “grievance”; NWT 1984 “do have a case against someone” and TLB “have a case against them” say “case.” Agoraioi agontai has no noun: it is “market-place ones are being held,” and the adjective agoraios stands in two verses, here and 17:5, where it is the “wicked men of the marketplace”; this translation supplies “court days.” NWT 1984 “court days are held” supplies the same noun, ASV “the courts are open,” NIV “the courts are open” and Douay “the courts of justice are open” say “courts,” KJV “the law is open” and Geneva “the law is open” say “law,” and TLB “the courts are currently in session” says “currently.” Anthypatoi is the plural of the title 18:12 gave Gallio, “proconsul”; the Greek does not say whether the plural names the office or more than one man who holds it. ASV “proconsuls,” Douay “proconsuls,” NIV “proconsuls” and NWT 1984 “proconsuls” print the title, KJV “deputies” and Geneva “Deputies” its older English. The clerk sends the quarrel to the courts with enkaleitōsan allēlois, “let them bring charges against one another”; enkaleō stands in seven verses of the New Testament: here, v40, Romans 8:33, and 23:28, 23:29, 26:2 and 26:7. Anything more goes en tē ennomō ekklēsia, “in the lawful assembly”: ennomos stands in two verses, here and 1 Corinthians 9:21 (ennomos Christou, not yet on these pages), and epilyō, “to settle,” in two, here and Mark 4:34, where Jesus “explains” everything to his disciples in private. KJV “a lawful assembly,” Geneva “a lawful assembly” and Douay “a lawful assembly” keep “lawful”; ASV “the regular assembly” and NWT 1984 “a regular assembly” say “regular,” NIV “a legal assembly” says “legal,” and TLB “regular City Council meetings” makes it a council. The apparatus gives peraiterō, “further,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and peri heterōn, “about other matters,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which is what KJV “concerning other matters,” ASV “about other matters” and Geneva “concerning other matters” print; NIV “anything further” and NWT 1984 “anything beyond that” have the first.

“Insurrection,” “account” and the dismissal (Acts 19:40–41). Kindyneuomen enkaleisthai staseōs, “we are in danger of being charged with insurrection,” has the verb of Demetrius’s kindyneuei at v27, now said of the whole city. Stasis stands in nine verses of the New Testament: Mark 15:7, Luke 23:19 and 23:25, where Barabbas is held for it, Acts 15:2, here, 23:7, 23:10 and 24:5, and Hebrews 9:8 (the last, in Hebrews, not yet on these pages); this translation prints “insurrection” at Luke 23 and here, and “dissension” at 15:2. Apodounai logon, “to give an account,” is the fourth appearance of logos in the two chapters, and systrophē, “turbulent crowd,” stands in two verses, here and 23:12. The apparatus gives apodounai to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and dounai to Robinson–Pierpont. KJV “to be called in question for this day’s uproar” and Douay “to be called in question for this day’s uproar” say “uproar,” ASV “to be accused concerning this day’s riot” says “riot,” Geneva “to be accused of this day’s sedition” and NWT 1984 “being charged with sedition over today’s affair” say “sedition,” NIV “being charged with rioting” says “rioting,” and TLB “being called to account by the Roman government for today’s riot” names the government. The last clause is kai tauta eipōn apelysen tēn ekklēsian, “having said this he dismissed the assembly”: KJV “he dismissed the assembly,” ASV “he dismissed the assembly,” NIV “he dismissed the assembly” and NWT 1984 “he dismissed the assembly” keep the word, Geneva “he let the assembly depart” and TLB “he dismissed them” change it. The Greek text printed here, and six of the seven versions on the shelf, number the chapter at forty-one verses, with the dismissal as v41; the Douay-Rheims numbers it at forty, with the dismissal at the end of v40, which matters when a citation is taken from a Douay-based book. Two officials end two scenes in two chapters: Gallio “drove them away from the judgment seat” (18:16) and the clerk dismisses the assembly, and both send the charge to the proper court. Paul speaks in neither scene and, in this chapter’s last, is not there; the next chapter begins, “after the tumult had ceased” (20:1).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “Holy Spirit” for pneuma hagion at vv2 and 6 (as at 8:15), with “when” the translation’s own choice at v2; “into” for eis at vv3 and 5; “baptized a baptism” at v4, with no “with”; “tongues” at v6; “reasoning” for dialegomai at vv8 and 9; “disobeying” for apeitheō at v9, as at 14:2; “the Way” at vv9 and 23; “cloths” for soudaria at v12, as at both tombs of John; “high priest” for archiereus at v14; “know” for both verbs at v15; “prevailed” and “prevailing” for ischyō at vv16 and 20; “magnified” at v17; “practices” and “practiced” at vv18 and 19; “the word of the Lord” at vv10 and 20; “no small” for ouk oligos at vv23 and 24; “temples” for naos at v24, as at 17:24; “profit” and “business” for ergasia; “with one accord” at v29; “assembly” for the civil ekklesia at vv32, 39 and 41, with “congregation” kept for believers; “quieted” and “quiet” at vv35 and 36; “proconsuls” at v38, as at 18:12; and “insurrection” for stasis at v40, as at Luke 23:19.

Tongues — 2:4 and 10:46, at v6. “Spoke boldly” — 18:26, at v8. “Reasoned” — 17:2, 18:4 and 18:19, at vv8–9. “The Way” — 9:2 and 18:25–26, at vv9 and 23. A demon’s “I know” — Luke 4:34 and 16:17, at v15. “Magnifying God” — 10:46, at v17. “The word grew” — 6:7 and 12:24, at v20. “No small disturbance” — 12:18, at v23. “Handmade temples” — 17:24 and 17:29, at vv24 and 26. The profit that starts a riot — 16:16 and 16:19, at vv24–25. “Prosperity” — 11:29, at v25. “The inhabited world” — 11:28 and 17:6, at v27. “With one accord,” and the rush — 7:57 and 18:12, at v29. ⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether “when” or “since” at v2 says anything about the Spirit and belief. How men who knew John’s baptism had not heard of the Spirit, and whether they knew Apollos. What the order of water, hands and Spirit shows, if anything. Whether Tyrannus owned the hall or taught in it, and what the Western addition about the fifth hour to the tenth is. How the seven sons of v14 became “both” at v16. Whether the confessing believers of v18 are the book-burners of v19. Whether “in the spirit” at v21 is Paul’s own or the Spirit of God. Whether Erastus of v22 is the city steward of Romans 16:23. What the silver temples of v24 were, and what “Diana” is. Whether the relative of v27 is the goddess or her magnificence. Whether the Gaius and Aristarchus of v29 are the men of 20:4 and the Colossian letter. What the Asiarchs were. Whose defense Alexander meant to make, and which Alexander he is. Whether the plural “proconsuls” names the office or several men. What the verse numbering of the last verses is in Douay-based books.
