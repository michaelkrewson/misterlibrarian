“Bless Jehovah, O my soul”: a psalm of David that counts out God’s dealings — forgiveness, healing, redemption from the pit — quotes the name proclaimed to Moses, “merciful and gracious, slow to anger”, sets mortal days like grass against steadfast love from everlasting to everlasting, and calls angels, armies and all his works to bless him.
Undated: the heading says only “Of David”, and the psalm names no event.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–2 · “Bless Jehovah, O my soul” — a psalm that speaks to itself, “all that is within me”, and “forget none of his dealings”

A voice talking to itself. “Of David. Bless Jehovah, O my soul, and all that is within me, his holy name”: barakhi nafshi, an imperative in the feminine, because nefesh is a feminine noun, and every “you” and “your” through verse 5 is feminine singular too: the speaker is addressing his own self. “Bless Jehovah, O my soul” stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, the first, second and last verses of this psalm and the first and last of Psalm 104. Nefesh is the whole living self rather than a part inside it, so that Psalm 23 has “He brings my life back” (Psalm 23:3, already on these pages); “soul” stays here because a self is speaking to itself, as in “my soul knows it well” (Psalm 139:14, already on these pages). Kol-qeravai, “all that is within me”, is literally “all my inward parts”, and the Hebrew does not repeat the verb: “and all that is within me, his holy name”. The NWT 1984 keeps that, “Even everything within me, his holy name”; the ASV fills it in, “And all that is within me, bless his holy name”; the NIV has “all my inmost being, praise his holy name”.

To bless God. Barakh is the verb of the berakhah, the blessing; said of God it runs downward, a gift, and said to God it runs upward, praise spoken well of him. The Greek has eulogei, “speak well of”. The psalm uses the verb six times, twice at the start and four times at the end. The NIV turns every one into “Praise”; the Geneva has “My soul, praise thou”; the ASV, KJV and NWT 1984 keep “Bless”.

Forget none of his dealings. “Bless Jehovah, O my soul, and forget none of his dealings”: al-tishkechi kol-gemulav, word for word “do not forget all his dealings”, which in English sounds as if forgetting some would do. Gemul is a dealing that comes back on someone, the word of Obadiah’s “your dealing will return upon your own head” (Obadiah 1:15, already on these pages); here what comes back is good that was not earned, and verse 10 turns the same root around: he has not “dealt with us according to our iniquities”. The Greek reads antapodoseis, “repayments”. The ASV and KJV have “forget not all his benefits”; the NWT 1984 “do not forget all his doings”; the Douay “never forget all he hath done for thee”.

Verses 3–5 · “Who forgives all your iniquity, who heals all your diseases” — redeemed from the pit, crowned, and youth renewed like the eagle

Five who-clauses. “Who forgives all your iniquity, who heals all your diseases”: the dealings are counted out in five participles, each with the article, “the one forgiving … the one healing … the one redeeming … the one crowning … the one satisfying”. The first is salach, the verb whose only subject in the Hebrew Bible is God, and with avon, “iniquity”, it is what Moses asked on the mountain, “pardon our iniquity and our sin” (Exodus 34:9, already on these pages). The second, rofe, “healer”, is the name Jehovah gives himself at Marah, “for I am Jehovah, your healer” (Exodus 15:26, already on these pages). The ASV has “Who forgiveth all thine iniquities; Who healeth all thy diseases”; the NWT 1984 “Him who is forgiving all your error, Who is healing all your maladies”.

Redeemed from the pit. “Who redeems your life from the pit, who crowns you with steadfast love and compassion”: the redeemer is the go’el, the next of kin whose duty is to buy a relative back. Shachat, “the pit”, is the grave; the word can be taken from a verb of sinking, or from the verb shachat, to ruin, and the Greek took it the second way, phthora, “decay”, which is why one line of the shelf reads “destruction”. The crown is woven of chesed and rachamim, the compassion named from the womb. The NIV reads “from the pit” and the NWT 1984 “from the very pit”; the KJV and ASV “Who redeemeth thy life from destruction”; the Geneva “from the grave”; the KJV goes on “who crowneth thee with lovingkindness and tender mercies”.

Your years, and the eagle. “Who satisfies your years with good, so that your youth renews itself like the eagle”: “your years” is a guess, and the word, edyekh, is the hardest in the psalm. Elsewhere the noun adi means an ornament, and the same letters, “your ornaments”, are what Israel is told to take off at Horeb (Exodus 33:5, already on these pages); but “who satisfies your ornament with good” gives no clear sense. The NWT 1984 reads “your lifetime”, as this translation does, and the 1917 Jewish Publication Society version “thine old age”; the Greek has epithymian, “desire”, followed by the ASV and Douay with “thy desire”; the KJV and Geneva read “thy mouth”. The eagle is the nesher, the great soaring bird; Isaiah makes a like promise with a different verb, “those who wait for Jehovah shall renew their strength; they shall mount up with wings like eagles” (Isaiah 40:31, already on these pages). The NWT 1984 has “Your youth keeps renewing itself just like that of an eagle”; the ASV “So that thy youth is renewed like the eagle”.

Verses 6–10 · “Merciful and gracious, slow to anger” — the ways shown to Moses, the words of Exodus 34, and no grudge forever

Righteous acts. “Jehovah performs righteous acts, and judgments for all the oppressed”: tsedaqot, the plural of tzedaqah, righteousness done rather than possessed, and mishpatim, rulings given in someone’s favor. The oppressed are the ashuqim, from ashaq, to defraud, to keep back what is owed. The Greek turns the righteous acts into eleēmosynas, “acts of mercy”, the word that later meant alms (Matthew 6:2, already on these pages). The NWT 1984 has “Jehovah is executing acts of righteousness And judicial decisions for all those being defrauded”; the ASV “Jehovah executeth righteous acts, And judgments for all that are oppressed”; the Douay, from the Greek through the Latin, “doth mercies, and judgment for all that suffer wrong”.

The ways shown to Moses. “He made known his ways to Moses, his deeds to the children of Israel”: yodia derakhav, the verb of Moses’ own prayer, “show me now your ways”, whose Hebrew is “make known to me now your ways” (Exodus 33:13, already on these pages). The psalm answers that the prayer was granted. The deeds are alilot, exploits; the Greek reads “his wills”, and so the Douay has “his wills to the children of Israel”. The ASV has “He made known his ways unto Moses, His doings unto the children of Israel”; the NWT 1984 “His dealings even to the sons of Israel”.

The words from the mountain. “Jehovah is merciful and gracious, slow to anger and abundant in steadfast love” is what Jehovah proclaimed when he passed before Moses, “a God merciful and gracious, slow to anger, and abundant in steadfast love and truth” (Exodus 34:6, already on these pages), and the psalm stops before “and truth”. “Merciful and gracious” in that order stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, Exodus 34:6, Psalm 86:15 and this one. The reverse order, “gracious and merciful”, stands in eight verses of the Hebrew Bible, among them Joel 2:13 and Jonah 4:2. In Greek the pair is oiktirmōn kai eleēmōn, and James’s “Jehovah is full of compassion and merciful” recalls it (James 5:11, already on these pages). The ASV has “Jehovah is merciful and gracious, Slow to anger, and abundant in lovingkindness”; the NWT 1984 “Slow to anger and abundant in loving-kindness”; the NIV “compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, abounding in love”.

No grudge forever. “He will not contend perpetually, nor will he bear a grudge forever”: yariv, from riv, a quarrel or a lawsuit, and yittor, from natar, to keep, the verb of the law “You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against your own people” (Leviticus 19:18, already on these pages). The grudge Israel is forbidden, God declines to keep. The Hebrew names no object; the ASV supplies one, “Neither will he keep his anger for ever”, and the NWT 1984 reads “Neither will he to time indefinite keep resentful”. “He has not done to us according to our sins, nor dealt with us according to our iniquities”: gamal, the root of verse 2’s dealings. The ASV has “Nor rewarded us after our iniquities”.

Verses 11–14 · “As far as the east is from the west” — love as high as heaven, a father’s compassion, and “he remembers that we are dust”

High and far. “For as high as the heavens are above the earth, so mighty is his steadfast love toward those who fear him”: gavar, to be strong, to prevail, the root of the gibbor, the mighty man, which comes back at verse 20 in the angels “mighty in strength”. “As far as the east is from the west, so far has he removed our transgressions from us”: mizrach is where the sun rises and maarav where it sets, two points that never meet. The transgressions are pesha, the word for rebellion. The NWT 1984 has “As far off as the sunrise is from the sunset”; the ASV “So far hath he removed our transgressions from us”; the Douay “our iniquities”; the Geneva “our sins”.

A father’s compassion. “As a father has compassion on his children, Jehovah has compassion on those who fear him”: richam, the verb of rachamim, compassion named from the womb, said here of a father. “Those who fear him” comes three times in seven verses (11, 13, 17); the fear is yir’ah, reverence and fear in one word. The ASV has “Like as a father pitieth his children, So Jehovah pitieth them that fear him”; the NWT 1984 “As a father shows mercy to his sons”.

Formed, and dust. “For he knows how we are formed; he remembers that we are dust”: yitsrenu, from yatsar, the potter’s verb of “Jehovah God formed the human from the dust of the ground” (Genesis 2:7, already on these pages); the noun yetzer is elsewhere the inclination of the heart, and here the shape a person was given. The dust is afar, “for dust you are, and to dust you will return” (Genesis 3:19, already on these pages): what is said there as a sentence is said here as a reason to be gentle. The Greek reads “remember that we are dust”, a prayer. The NIV has “for he knows how we are formed”; the ASV “For he knoweth our frame; He remembereth that we are dust”; the NWT 1984 “the formation of us, Remembering that we are dust”.

Verses 15–18 · “As for man, his days are as grass” — the flower of the field, a passing wind, and steadfast love from everlasting to everlasting

Grass and flower. “As for mortal man, his days are like grass; like the flower of the field, so he blossoms”: enosh, man in his frailty, and ketsits ha-sadeh, “like the flower of the field”, Isaiah’s phrase, “All flesh is grass, and all its loveliness like the flower of the field” (Isaiah 40:6, already on these pages). The tzitz is the blossom that does not last, and the verb beside it is its own root, “so he blossoms”. The ASV has “As for man, his days are as grass; As a flower of the field, so he flourisheth”; the NWT 1984 “his days are like those of green grass”.

Its place knows it no more. “For a wind passes over it, and it is gone, and its place knows it no more”: ruach, wind or breath, and Isaiah has the grass wither “when the breath of Jehovah blows upon it” (Isaiah 40:7, already on these pages). “Its place knows it no more” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and Job 7:10. The Greek turns the line around, so that the man no longer knows his place, and the Douay follows: “For the spirit shall pass in him, and he shall not be: and he shall know his place no more”. The ASV has “For the wind passeth over it, and it is gone; And the place thereof shall know it no more”; the NWT 1984 “And its place will acknowledge it no further”.

From everlasting to everlasting. “But the steadfast love of Jehovah is from everlasting to everlasting upon those who fear him, and his righteousness to children’s children”: me-olam ve-ad-olam, from olam to olam, against the days of grass. “To children’s children” is the reach of Exodus 34:7, where iniquity is visited “upon the children and upon the children’s children” (Exodus 34:7, already on these pages); the psalm sends righteousness the same distance. Mary’s song says it again in Greek, “and his mercy is for generations and generations on those who fear him” (Luke 1:50, already on these pages). “To those who keep his covenant, and to those who remember his precepts, to do them”: the precepts are piqqudim, a word of the Psalms (Psalms 19:9 and 111:7, and many times in Psalm 119, now on these pages); in Numbers the same letters are the counts of a census. The ASV has “To such as keep his covenant, And to those that remember his precepts to do them”; the KJV “his commandments”; the NWT 1984 “his orders”, and before that “from time indefinite even to time indefinite”.

Verses 19–22 · “Bless Jehovah, you his angels” — a throne in the heavens, angels mighty in strength, armies and attendants, and the soul at the end

A throne and a kingship. “Jehovah has established his throne in the heavens, and his kingship rules over all”: the kisse is set firm, hekhin, and malkhuto, his kingship, mashalah, rules, the verb of mashal, which comes back in verse 22 as “his dominion”. The ASV has “Jehovah hath established his throne in the heavens; And his kingdom ruleth over all”; the NWT 1984 “And over everything his own kingship has held domination”.

Doers of his word. “Bless Jehovah, you his angels, mighty in strength, who carry out his word, listening to the voice of his word”: the mal’akhim are messengers, and gibborei koach, “mighty in strength”, brings back the root of verse 11. Doing comes before hearing, as in Israel’s answer at the covenant, “All that Jehovah has spoken, we will do, and we will hear” (Exodus 24:7, already on these pages). “Bless Jehovah, all his armies, his attendants who do his will”: tsava, the army of “Jehovah of armies”, and mesharetav, the word Joshua carries as “Moses’ attendant” (Joshua 1:1, already on these pages). The Greek has “all his angels”, and so the Douay has “all ye his angels”. The ASV has “Bless Jehovah, ye his angels, That are mighty in strength, that fulfil his word, Hearkening unto the voice of his word”; the KJV “that excel in strength”; the NWT 1984 “Bless Jehovah, all YOU armies of his, You ministers of his, doing his will”.

Back to the one voice. “Bless Jehovah, all his works, in all places of his dominion. Bless Jehovah, O my soul”: the call has widened from one self to the angels, the armies and everything he made, everywhere he rules, and then it comes back to where it began. The last line is the first line, and Psalm 104 opens with it again. The ASV has “Bless Jehovah, all ye his works, In all places of his dominion: Bless Jehovah, O my soul”; the NWT 1984 “In all places of his domination. Bless Jehovah, O my soul”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “Bless Jehovah, O my soul” for barakhi nafshi, with “bless” kept in all six places; “dealings” and “dealt” for the root gamal in verses 2 and 10; “steadfast love” for chesed, four times, as in Exodus 34:6; “merciful and gracious, slow to anger” in the words of Exodus 34:6; “bear a grudge” for natar, as in Leviticus 19:18; “formed” for yatsar, as in Genesis 2:7; “the flower of the field”, as in Isaiah 40:6; and “attendants” for mesharetim, as in Joshua 1:1.

Echoes paid. The ornaments taken off at Horeb and the ways Moses asked to know (Exodus 33); the name proclaimed on the mountain and the pardon Moses asked (Exodus 34); “your healer” at Marah (Exodus 15); the grudge forbidden in Leviticus 19; the dust of Genesis 2 and 3; the grass, the flower and the eagles of Isaiah 40; “we will do, and we will hear” (Exodus 24); Joshua the attendant; and the Greek of this psalm heard in James 5:11 and Luke 1:50.

Echoes planted. “Bless Jehovah, O my soul” again, opening and closing Psalm 104; “merciful and gracious” in Psalm 86:15, and turned around in Joel 2:13 and Jonah 4:2; “its place knows it no more” in Job 7:10. None is translated here yet; the precepts of Psalm 119 now are.
