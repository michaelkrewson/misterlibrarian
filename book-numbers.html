📖 Numbers

בְּמִדְבַּר Bemidbar — 'In the wilderness' — from the fifth word of the opening verse, 'in the wilderness of Sinai.' The Hebrew title names the true subject of the book: the forty wilderness years.
Ἀριθμοί (Arithmoi) — 'Numbers' — the Greek and Latin (Numeri) title, taken from the two great censuses that frame the book, of the first wilderness generation (ch. 1) and the second (ch. 26).

The book of the wilderness years — a redeemed people counted and set in order, who then refuse the land at its border and are left to die in the desert, while God keeps his promise for their children.

Chapter by chapter

Numbers 1'In the wilderness of Sinai' the redeemed people are counted and arrayed as an army for the march — twelve tribes, twelve chieftains, 603,550 fighting men; and one tribe, Levi, left off the war-roll to carry and guard the tent at the camp's center.Numbers 2The same 603,550 men from last chapter's census, given an actual floor plan. Twelve tribes, sorted into four groups of three, one group facing each direction of the compass, each under its own banner planted before a single tent goes up. East gets Judah, Issachar, and Zebulun, and they move out first whenever the camp breaks. South gets Reuben, Simeon, and Gad, moving second — Reuben still gets the honor of leading a group despite losing his birthright standing two books ago, because a census list apparently doesn't forget an accident of birth order just because a father's deathbed blessing did. West gets Ephraim and Manasseh — Joseph's two sons, camped side by side exactly as they were blessed side by side, with Ephraim's name still listed first even though he was the younger of the two. North gets Dan, Asher, and Naphtali, moving last, and Dan's group turns out to be the single largest of the four despite Dan being a fairly minor son in the family story. And directly in the middle of this whole square, not at the front, not off to one side, but dead center: the tent itself, with the Levites camped in a ring around it, so that no matter which direction the column is facing or how far it has to march, the same architecture holds — the tribes on the outside, the priesthood in the middle, and the presence of God at the exact point everything else is built around. The chapter ends the way these things always seem to end here: they did it exactly as they were told, camping and marching both, right down to the last family.Numbers 3A family tree that gets cut short in the second sentence. Aaron has four sons, but two of them are already dead — offered the wrong fire on the altar and paid for it on the spot, no children left behind — so the whole priestly line has to run through just the two who survived. Then the chapter turns to a trade that's been owed since the night of the Exodus itself: every firstborn son in Israel was marked as belonging to God on the night the plague passed over, and nobody ever followed up on that claim — until now. One entire tribe, the Levites, gets handed over as the payment, a substitute standing in for every firstborn son in every other family in the nation. To make the trade official they get counted the same way the fighting men were counted last chapter, except starting from one month old instead of twenty years, because this isn't about who can hold a weapon — it's about who belongs to whom from birth. Three sons of Levi, three jobs: one clan hauls the tent's fabric and curtains and camps behind it to the west; one clan carries the single most dangerous cargo in the whole camp — the ark, the table, the lampstand, the altars — camped to the south under the direct watch of Aaron's own surviving son; and one clan hauls the heavy stuff nobody thinks about, the frames and bars and posts that hold the whole structure up, camped to the north. Moses and Aaron's family plant themselves directly in front of the entrance, east, so nothing gets near that door without going through them first. And then the chapter ends on a piece of arithmetic it makes you watch happen twice: count every firstborn son in the nation, count every Levite, and the numbers don't quite match — 273 firstborn sons too many, no Levite left to stand in for them. So they get bought back instead, five silver coins each, cash paid straight to the priests, down to the last coin.Numbers 4A second census of the same Levites, and this time the age bracket is narrower on both ends: thirty to fifty, not one month and up — the years of a man's actual working strength, because this chapter isn't about who belongs to God from birth, it's about who's strong enough to carry God's furniture across a desert. And before a single Kohathite is allowed anywhere near that furniture, Aaron and his sons have to do something first: wrap it. The ark disappears under a veil, then a covering of the same mystery-material leather this project already flagged back in Exodus, then a cloth of solid blue on top. The table gets the same treatment, and the lampstand, and the incense altar, and the bronze altar outside — each one wrapped in its assigned color before anyone's allowed to touch it, and the warning that goes with the wrapping isn't subtle: look at the holy things uncovered, even for a second, and you die. Only after everything's invisible under cloth are the Kohathites finally allowed to come pick it up — carrying poles already threaded through the rings, so no hand ever actually makes contact with the thing itself. Aaron's son Eleazar gets one more job on top of supervising all this: the lamp oil, the incense, the daily grain offering, and the anointing oil — the ordinary supplies that keep the place running day to day, separate from the furniture. The other two Levite clans get no such warning, because nothing they're hauling can kill anyone — Gershon's got the curtains and the coverings, Merari's got the frames and the bars, and both of them answer to Aaron's other son, Ithamar, not Eleazar. Then the actual headcount: 2,750 men from Kohath, 2,630 from Gershon, 3,200 from Merari. Add them up and you get exactly 8,580 — no gap this time, no leftover 300 nobody can account for, unlike the arithmetic problem the last chapter left sitting on the table. Just a number that adds up, for a job description that's mostly about staying alive long enough to do it.Numbers 5Three laws that have nothing to do with each other except one thing: what it takes to keep a camp fit for God to actually live in the middle of. First, a housekeeping order — anyone with a skin disease, a bodily discharge, or contact with a corpse gets sent outside the camp, not as punishment exactly, just because those things can't share space with where God has said he'll be. Then a debt law: if you wrong someone, you don't get to make it right with God until you've made it right with the person first — pay back what you took, add twenty percent on top, and only then does anyone talk about a sacrifice. And if the person you wronged has died and left nobody behind to collect on their behalf? The debt doesn't just vanish. It goes to the priest instead, because apparently a wrong doesn't stop being owed just because there's no one left to hand the payment to. Then the chapter turns to something stranger and harder to sit with: a husband gets suspicious of his wife, no proof either way, no witness, nothing anyone could take to court. So instead of a trial with no evidence to run on, there's a ritual — dust from the tabernacle floor mixed into water, her hair unbound, an oath she has to speak out loud agreeing in advance to a curse that only lands if she's actually guilty. She drinks it. If she's lying, something happens to her body. If she's telling the truth, nothing happens at all, and she goes home cleared. It's the one place in the whole law code built specifically for a case where there's genuinely no way to know the truth by ordinary means — and whichever way it goes, the husband himself never answers for having raised the suspicion in the first place.Numbers 6A vow anyone could take, for as long as they wanted to take it. Skip wine, skip everything that comes off a grapevine — the juice, the raisins, the skins, all of it. Don't cut your hair. Don't go near a dead body, not even your own mother or father's, for as long as the vow runs. That's it — no temple duty, no special robes, just three restrictions anyone in Israel could commit to for a season and then walk away from once the time was up. Except three people in this book never do walk away from it — Samson, marked for it before he's born; Samuel, promised to it by a mother who hadn't even conceived him yet; and, centuries later, a baby who'll grow up to baptize people in a river, marked the same way from the womb. The vow was built to be temporary. Nobody told the mothers that. There's a strange contingency clause tucked into the middle: what happens if someone just drops dead next to you, no warning, and ruins the vow through absolutely no fault of your own? You don't get punished for it — but you don't get credit for the time already served either. Shave your head, wait a week, bring two birds on the eighth day, and start over from zero, whatever you'd already gotten through simply erased. And finishing the vow properly, when nothing interrupts it, costs way more than keeping it ever did — three different animals, bread, grain, wine, the whole production, for a commitment whose daily requirements were just three things you didn't do. Then, with no transition at all, the chapter hands Aaron and his sons the exact words for blessing the entire nation — three lines, each one a little longer than the last, ending on a claim that undercuts the whole ceremony in the best way: the priests don't actually do the blessing. They just say it out loud. God does the blessing part himself, once his own name has been put on the people standing there listening.Numbers 7The tabernacle is finished, and the very next thing it does is get thrown a twelve-day party. Every day for twelve days running, one tribal chieftain steps forward with an offering — and it's the exact same offering, every single time, item for item, right down to the shekel: one silver dish, one silver basin, a golden spoon of incense, a bull, a ram, a lamb, a goat, and enough oxen and rams and goats and lambs for a peace-offering that could feed half the camp. The text writes it out in full all twelve times anyway, instead of just saying 'repeat eleven more times' — every name gets said, every number gets counted, because apparently no gift here is allowed to look smaller just because eleven identical ones came before it. And the order the chieftains show up in isn't random: it's the exact marching order the camp already used to break camp every morning, tribe by tribe — a calendar built out of last chapter's floor plan. Before any of that, there's a stranger detail buried at the start — the chieftains donate six wagons and twelve oxen to help move the tabernacle, and two of the three Levite clans take them gladly. The third clan, the ones trusted with the ark itself, get nothing — because that cargo doesn't ride in a cart, it rides on a man's own shoulder, no wheel ever allowed near it. Then the chapter does its own math out loud, twice, adding up every dish and every animal to a number that comes out exact. And right at the very end — after fifteen chapters spent building a tent to a set of specifications, a whole book of sacrificial law, and six more chapters besides — one line finally happens: Moses walks in, and for the first time, hears a voice answer him from between the wings of two golden statues, exactly the way he'd been promised it would, chapters and chapters ago, before a single board was ever cut.Numbers 8The golden lampstand changes hands. Moses lit it once himself when the tent was first set up; here the job passes to Aaron for good, and the priesthood takes over tending the light. Then the chapter turns to something stranger: the Levites get purified for duty, and the ritual reads like a checklist for undoing a person's ordinary humanity for a moment — water sprinkled on them, a razor run over every inch of their skin, their clothes washed, then the whole nation of Israel lays hands on them at once, and Aaron waves them — the actual, living men — back and forth like a priest waving a piece of meat at an altar, because that's literally the same gesture used everywhere else in the Torah for the fat and breast of a sacrifice, just performed here on an entire tribe of people instead. Two bulls die to make it official, one for a burnt offering, one to atone for the men being consecrated. Then God explains, in his own voice this time, why any of this is happening: every firstborn son in Israel already belongs to him, marked the night he killed every firstborn in Egypt and let Israel's own firstborn walk out untouched — and the Levites are the one-tribe trade that settles that account for good, so the rest of Israel doesn't have to hand over an actual firstborn son to serve God for it. And the chapter ends with a number that flatly contradicts a number from four chapters ago — Levites start their duty at twenty-five here, not thirty like last time — and the text never once acknowledges the difference.Numbers 9Israel keeps Passover in the desert — only the second one ever held, a year to the night after the first one got them out of Egypt. And then a problem nobody had written a rule for: a group of men had recently handled a dead body, which under the camp's own purity laws makes them unfit to take part, and they are not willing to let it go. They come to Moses and ask, flatly, why they should be shut out of the one thing the whole nation is doing. Moses has no answer. He tells them to wait while he asks — a lawgiver admitting on the record that the law doesn't cover this. What comes back isn't an exemption, it's a whole second Passover: anyone unclean, or simply too far from home, gets to keep it a month later to the day, same rules, same meal, nothing left over, no bone broken. The same ruling widens the door again in a direction nobody asked about — a foreigner living among them can keep it too, on identical terms, one law for both. And it shuts exactly one door: anyone perfectly able to show up who just doesn't bother is cut off from his people. Then the chapter pulls back to the cloud sitting over the tent — fire in it at night — and the arrangement that governs everything from here on: when it lifts they march, when it settles they stop, and nobody is told beforehand whether that means two days, a month, or a year.Numbers 10They finally leave. Israel has been parked at the same mountain since the third month of the previous year — through the covenant, the golden calf, the entire building of the tabernacle, the whole of Leviticus, and nine chapters of this book — and here, on a date the text gives down to the day, the cloud lifts and the column moves out. First God has Moses make two trumpets, hammered out of silver, and attaches a signal code to them precise enough to steer a nation: blow both and everyone assembles, blow one and only the chiefs come, blow a hard broken blast and the eastern camps start walking. The distinction the chapter cares most about isn't how many horns but how you play them — same instrument, same players, and the difference between 'gather' and 'march' rides entirely on the shape of the note. Then the tribes move off in the exact formation drawn up eight chapters earlier, and Moses turns to his Midianite brother-in-law and asks him to come along as a guide, because a nation with a pillar of cloud overhead still wants somebody who knows the terrain — 'you will be our eyes.' The man says no. Moses asks again. The text never says whether he came. And it closes on two short verses that scribes have bracketed, for centuries, with a letter written backwards — a mark found nowhere else in the Torah, over a passage one ancient tradition insists is not a passage at all but a separate book of the Bible hiding in plain sight, all eighty-five letters of it.Numbers 11The mood breaks three days out from Sinai, and the book changes character for good. Everything up to here has gone right — the counting, the camp, the dedication, the departure. From this chapter on, Numbers is mostly a record of things going wrong, and it opens with people grumbling about nothing the text will even specify, and fire burning the edge of the camp. Then comes the complaint that gives the chapter its shape, and it is startlingly human: they want meat, and what they actually miss is a produce list — the fish, the cucumbers, the melons, the leeks, the onions, the garlic — remembered as free, by people who had been slaves. Nobody in the chapter corrects the memory. Moses, meanwhile, breaks down completely, and the words he reaches for are a mother's: did I conceive this people, did I give birth to them, that I have to carry them at my chest like a nurse? Then, plainly: kill me. God's answer is not a rebuke but a reorganization — seventy elders get a share of the same spirit so the weight stops resting on one man. Two of them start prophesying in the wrong place, off the official list, and when Joshua urges Moses to shut them down Moses gives the best line in the book: he wishes everyone could do it. And then the meat arrives, in a quantity meant as a punishment rather than a gift, and the place where they eat it becomes a cemetery whose name is built out of the same word the chapter used for wanting.Numbers 12Moses’ brother and sister turn on him, and the chapter opens with a grammatical detail every English Bible hides: two people are named as speaking, but the Hebrew verb is feminine SINGULAR — she spoke, Miriam and Aaron. Their complaint comes in two pieces that the text never connects. One is about the Cushite woman Moses married. The other is the thing Moses himself had wished for out loud only a chapter earlier: has God really spoken only through him? God summons all three outside, calls two of them forward, and delivers the nearest thing the Bible has to a job description for prophecy — visions and dreams for everyone else, and for Moses mouth to mouth, in plain sight, not in riddles, beholding the very thing the second commandment forbids anyone to make. Then he leaves without passing sentence. The punishment is not announced; it is discovered when the cloud lifts. Only one of the two accusers is struck, in words identical to those used of Moses’ own hand at the burning bush, and the brother who spoke too is untouched and starts pleading. Moses answers with five Hebrew words, the shortest prayer in the Torah, and asks nothing for himself. The verse Aaron pleads with carries two of the eighteen places where Jewish tradition says the scribes softened the text. And a nation with a pillar of cloud over it and its marching orders ready waits a full week for one woman before it moves.Numbers 13Twelve chieftains, one per tribe, are sent from the wilderness of Paran to look at the land — and the chapter opens on a two-letter Hebrew word that has carried centuries of argument. God tells Moses to send men ‘for yourself’, a dative pronoun with no ordinary job in the sentence, and whether it means ‘I am commanding this’ or ‘do it on your own responsibility’ matters enormously, because Deuteronomy tells the same story the other way round and says the spies were the people’s idea. Moses renames one of the twelve Joshua before they go, four chapters after the Bible started calling him Joshua. They cross the whole length of the country in forty days and come back carrying a single cluster of grapes on a pole between two men. And their report is true — every word of it, right up to one word. It flows with milk and honey, and here is the fruit, ONLY the people are fierce and the cities are fortified and the giants are there. Caleb hushes the shouting and says go, disputing not a single fact in the report, only its conclusion. The other ten say the inhabitants are stronger than we are, in a Hebrew phrase that can also be read ‘stronger than HE is’, and then put out a bad report of a land that eats its inhabitants, where they saw the Nephilim and felt like grasshoppers — and then claim to know how they looked through the giants’ eyes.Numbers 14The report lands and the nation breaks. Israel weeps all night and says out loud the wish that will become its sentence — if only we had died in this wilderness — and then proposes, in a phrase found in exactly one other verse in the Bible, to give itself a head and go back to Egypt. Joshua and Caleb tear their clothes and argue that the inhabitants are bread and that their SHADE has turned away from over them, an image not one of the eight versions checked here keeps; the congregation's answer is to reach for stones. God offers to wipe out the people and rebuild the nation from Moses — the identical offer made at the golden calf, with three words added, and mightier than they — and Moses refuses it by quoting God's own self-description from Sinai back at him, in an abbreviation that quietly drops almost every merciful clause and keeps the punishing one entire. The pardon comes back in two Hebrew words, and the next word is BUT. Then the oath: as I live — the first time in the Bible God swears by his own life, twice in this chapter and then not again until Isaiah — and the census that opened the book, 603,550 men from twenty years old and upward, is read back as a list of who will die in the desert, a year for each day the scouts were gone. The ten die of plague that day. The people mourn, and the next morning do the one thing left to do wrong: they go up to fight without the ark, using a verb that appears in only one other verse in the Hebrew Bible — the verse where Habakkuk sets the swollen soul against the righteous who lives by his faith.Numbers 15A law about arriving, addressed to the generation that has just been told it will never arrive: the words immediately following the sentence in the wilderness are ‘when you come into the land.’ Grain and wine become a fixed accompaniment to every animal offering, on a sliding scale — a tenth of flour and a quarter-hin of oil for a lamb, three tenths and half a hin for a bull. Then the formula this project was promised twice in writing, at Exodus 12 and Leviticus 24, and which arrives here piled up five times in fifteen verses: one statute, one law, one ordinance, for the native and for the sojourner alike. A loaf lifted out of the first dough — challah, the only offering in the chapter nobody leaves home to make, and a word that outlived its own law onto every Sabbath table since. ⚠ Then the boundary Leviticus 4 pointed at from three books away: everything the sacrificial system covers is bounded by shegagah, error, nine times in eight verses — and the moment a person acts ‘with a HIGH HAND,’ the identical two Hebrew words used of Israel marching out of Egypt, the offerings stop and no rite is left. ⚠ A man is found gathering sticks on the sabbath, held in custody because it had not been made clear what should be done to him, and stoned outside the camp — the third of the Torah’s four cases where a law is made in answer to a question, and all four carry a scribal paragraph break at exactly the moment the question hangs unanswered. ⚠ And it closes on tassels with a cord of blue: the third paragraph of the Shema, the garment Matthew three times shows Jesus wearing without naming, and a commandment turning on a verb no version on either shelf prints — lo taturu, do not go SCOUTING after your own heart and your own eyes, the exact verb used twelve times in the two preceding chapters of the twelve men who scouted the land and came back with a report that killed a generation.Numbers 16Korah's rebellion — and it opens on a broken sentence. ⚠ And Korah took, says the Hebrew, and never says what he took: the verb wants an object and there isn't one, and the seven versions checked here invent seven different solutions, from ‘took men’ to ‘became insolent’ to dropping the verb entirely. This translation leaves the hole marked, as it did for Cain in Genesis 4. The revolt is really two revolts braided: a Levite cousin of Moses who wants the priesthood, and three Reubenites who never mention priesthood at all — and one of them, On son of Peleth, is named once here and never again in the Bible. ⚠ The argument is built out of sentences said twice in opposite directions: rav lakhem, ‘you have too much’, goes from Korah to Moses and comes straight back four verses later; ha-me'at, ‘is it a small thing’, goes from Moses to Korah and back from Dathan and Abiram. And Korah's premise is a QUOTATION of Leviticus 19:2 — the whole congregation is holy — which makes the rebellion an argument from the law rather than against it. ⚠ Then the most startling line in the chapter, easy to read past: Dathan and Abiram call EGYPT ‘a land flowing with milk and honey’, the Torah's own formula for Canaan, and accuse Moses of bringing them out of it to kill them. Moses stakes his commission on a falsifiable public test and asks God to create a creation — the verb of Genesis 1, which has no other subject in the Bible. ⭐ And the ground opens its mouth: patzetah, a form that occurs in exactly three verses in the whole Hebrew Bible, the first of which is the ground opening to receive Abel's blood from Cain's hand. ⚠ The chapter also ends where English readers do not expect — the Hebrew has 35 verses where every English Bible has 50, because their 16:36-50 is Numbers 17 in the Masoretic text. So it stops on fire consuming two hundred and fifty men, with no atonement and no memorial; and it never actually says what became of Korah himself.Numbers 17⚠ Read the versification note before anything else: the Hebrew chapter has 28 verses and holds TWO episodes, so vv1-15 here are printed in every English Bible as Numbers 16:36-50, and vv16-28 as Numbers 17:1-13. Nothing is added or missing on either side — the same text is divided in two places, and this is where the fifteen verses Numbers 16 planted arrive. First: the censers of the two hundred and fifty dead men are not destroyed but CONSECRATED — ‘for they have become holy’, on the purely procedural ground that they were brought near before Jehovah — and hammered into plating for the altar, so every sacrifice Israel offers afterwards is offered on a surface made of the rebellion. Then, the very next morning, the whole congregation comes back to accuse Moses and Aaron of murder: ‘you have killed the people of Jehovah.’ ⚠ God repeats the offer of Numbers 16:21 almost word for word — and this time nobody argues; Moses simply sends Aaron out with a censer, the same object that killed 250 men, filled with fire from the altar now plated with their censers, and Aaron stands between the dead and the living and the plague is checked at fourteen thousand seven hundred. Then the second sign, and it is the opposite of the first in every way: twelve rods laid in the dark overnight, and the Hebrew word for a rod is also the word for a TRIBE, so each chieftain is holding his tribe with his name written on it. ⭐ Aaron’s buds, blossoms and ripens almonds in four stages in one verse — and the word for the blossom it produces is the same word as the golden PLATE already tied to Aaron’s forehead. ⚠ It does not end in relief. It ends in panic, on the exact verb the whole revolt was about: everyone who draws near, who draws near, dies.Numbers 18⚠ The chapter is addressed to AARON, and that is the finding: searched across the whole Hebrew Bible, God speaks directly to Aaron with Moses not in the sentence in exactly five verses, and THREE of them are here (vv1, 8, 20). The other sustained one is Leviticus 10:8, the wine prohibition given right after two of his sons died for approaching wrongly — so both times God instructs Aaron in his own right, it follows a death at the sanctuary. ⚠ And this chapter is the answer to the question Numbers 17 ended on. The congregation had cried that everyone who draws near dies; the reply is not reassurance but a job description: you shall BEAR the guilt of the sanctuary — nasa avon, the same phrase that in Numbers 14 meant God FORGIVING, turned a third way so that a priest absorbs the liability professionally, on everyone else's behalf. ⚠ V2 sets both Hebrew words for ‘tribe’ side by side — matteh Levi shevet avicha — the only verse in the Bible that does so of one tribe, and one of the two is the rod-word Numbers 17 spent a chapter on. The Douay-Rheims, following the Vulgate, takes the second in its OTHER sense and reads ‘the SCEPTRE of thy father’. ⚠ Then a landless tribe's entire economy: the contributions, the firstfruits, the devoted things (cherem, the Bible's darkest word, appearing here as a line in a household budget), and the firstborn, redeemed at five shekels — which the Living Bible converts to ‘two and a half dollars’, telling its reader a serious price was pocket change. ⭐ Aaron is given no land at all: I am your portion and your inheritance, a consolation built out of the two legal words just taken away from him. And the tithe turns out not to be charity but payroll — wages, in exchange for service — on which the Levites are then themselves taxed a tithe of the tithe.Numbers 19⚠ The chapter everyone knows as the RED HEIFER, which this translation calls a red COW — because parah means a cow, because Hebrew's actual word for a heifer (eglah) is already spent on Genesis 15:9, and because the shelf is not on the famous side: four say heifer (KJV, ASV, NIV, TLB) and six say cow — Geneva, the NWT 1984, a Douay-Rheims that adds ‘of full age’, and the entire Spanish shelf, which says vaca without exception. ⭐ The colour is doing work: adumah, red, is the same three consonants as adam, a human being, and the chapter deals them out in order — a RED cow, her BLOOD burned with her, and then from v11 the HUMAN corpse this all exists to deal with. ⚠ She is burned outside the camp, whole, hide and flesh and blood and dung, with cedar, hyssop and scarlet thrown in — the same three items that CLEANSE a healed skin-disease in Leviticus 14, used here the opposite way. And then the paradox the chapter is famous for and never explains: the ash makes the unclean clean, and every clean man who handles it — the priest, the burner, the gatherer, the sprinkler — is unclean until evening. ⚠ Nefesh does three unrelated jobs here in six occurrences — a corpse, living people, and twice the subject of ‘that SOUL shall be cut off’ — and v13 does two of them in one sentence. ⭐ And the detail almost every version loses: v9 gathers the efer, the ASHES, but v17 takes the afar, the DUST — Genesis 2's word for what a human is made of and Genesis 3's word for what he returns to. Of the ten versions checked here only the NWT 1984 prints the difference. What cures a man defiled by a human body is the substance a human body is.Numbers 20⚠ Thirty-eight years pass in the white space before v1, and the chapter opens by burying Miriam in a single clause — no mourning recorded, where Aaron will get thirty days by v29. ⭐ Then something no translation can carry: at v10 Moses turns on the people and shouts ha-morim, ‘you REBELS’ — which is, letter for letter, the name Miryam with the definite article in front. The vowels differ and the roots are probably unrelated, but an Israelite has just heard her buried nine verses earlier. And the chapter closes the loop at v24, where God uses the same root back at Moses: because you REBELLED. ⭐ The other finding is a noun. Exodus 17's water-from-rock was at a tzur, and God said STRIKE it. This one is at a sela, a crag, and God says SPEAK to it — and Moses strikes it twice. Two different rocks, two different words, opposite instructions, thirty-eight years apart; of ten versions checked on both shelves only the NWT 1984 keeps them distinct in English, which is why this translation prints ‘crag’. ⚠ The rod he swings is the one taken ‘from before Jehovah’ — where Numbers 17 stored Aaron's budded staff as a sign. ⚠ The whole shelf keeps the two things that condemn him: the first-person ‘are WE to bring you water’ and the ‘twice’. The place is named Meribah from rib, to quarrel — the second Meribah, since Exodus 17 already named one — and the Living Bible glosses it ‘Rebel Waters’, which is the wrong root in a chapter that happens to be full of the right one. ⚠ Then Edom refuses passage to ‘your brother Israel’ and Israel turns away without a fight, the one time in Numbers it takes no for an answer. ⭐ And at Mount Hor the high priest is dismantled in front of himself: Moses strips Aaron of his garments and dresses Eleazar in them while Aaron watches, and Aaron dies on the summit. The chapter's last verb is the one the people used in v3 wishing death on themselves.Numbers 21The hinge of the book, and the chapter every Bible reader half-remembers. Israel destroys the Canaanite king of Arad and names the place Hormah — the same place the previous generation was beaten back to, now renamed by a victory instead of a defeat. Then the food and the water run out again, and God sends the seraphim: the plural noun in that sentence stands in exactly three verses of the Hebrew Bible, and the other two are Isaiah's six-winged beings at the throne — a word twelve versions transliterate when it stands over God and all thirteen translate when it bites Israel. Read verse 8 slowly and God does not tell Moses to make a snake at all; he says make a BURNING ONE, and every version on both shelves supplies the missing noun. Moses makes one out of bronze, because nachash, serpent, and nechoshet, bronze, sound almost the same — and the phrase 'the serpent of bronze' occurs in two verses of the Bible, this one and the one seven hundred years later where a king of Judah smashes it because people had started burning incense to it. Then the chapter turns into an itinerary and starts quoting poetry nobody else preserved: four lines from a lost Book of the Wars of Jehovah, a work-song sung to a well by a generation that could have quarrelled over it instead, and a captured Amorite taunt-ballad about Heshbon that Jeremiah will sing again, almost word for word, some eight centuries later against Moab. Two kings refuse Israel passage and lose everything, and the book quietly changes the verb it has used for scouting twelve times running — at the one reconnaissance mission in Numbers that goes right.Numbers 22Israel is camped on the plains of Moab and the story leaves them entirely for three chapters. Balak king of Moab has just watched what happened to Sihon and Og, works out that he cannot win a battle, and goes looking for a weapon an army cannot answer — so he sends four hundred miles for a foreign seer called Balaam son of Beor, a man whose name turns up, centuries later, on a broken plaster inscription dug out of the Jordan Valley in 1967. What follows is one of the strangest passages in the Torah. God forbids the trip, then permits it, then is angry that it is taken. An angel with a drawn sword blocks the road three times, and the only party who can see him is the donkey — who is beaten for it, twice, until God opens her mouth and she asks what she has done. A professional visionary is out-seen by his own animal, and when his eyes are finally uncovered he offers to go home and is told to carry on. Two words carry the chapter. Balak asks Balaam to CURSE Israel using one Hebrew verb; Balaam repeats the request to God using a different one, and God’s refusal goes back to Balak’s original word — a three-move exchange only two of thirteen versions let you see. And the angel stands in the road le-satan, as a SATAN: the same noun every one of those thirteen versions prints as a proper name in Job and Zechariah, and not one of them prints here. The donkey, meanwhile, counts her beatings as ‘three regalim’ — the exact phrase the Torah uses everywhere else for the three annual pilgrimages you are commanded to walk to.Numbers 23Balak has hired a curse and he is going to be billed for two blessings. Seven altars go up, then seven more, then seven more — twenty-one bulls and twenty-one rams across three summits — and each time the answer comes back the wrong way round. ⚠ Read the narrator’s verb carefully: four times he says God did not come to Balaam but CHANCED upon him, the ordinary Hebrew root for something falling out by accident, and the whole shelf smooths it to ‘met’. ⭐ The first oracle uses four different words for cursing in two verses, and the exchange between them is the plot: Balaam quotes Balak’s commission back in Balak’s own verb, answers with a different one, and from then on it is the KING who uses the prophet’s vocabulary. One of those verbs, za’am, enters the Torah in this chapter and nowhere else. ⭐ The second oracle contains the Bible’s strongest statement that God does not change his mind — and the exact form Balaam uses stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, the other two of which say he does; 1 Samuel 15 then makes the same argument in a chapter that twice says Jehovah relented over making Saul king. It also contains a wild ox: the animal that became the King James UNICORN and the Douay’s rhinoceros, a translation chain four languages long that the Spanish shelf preserves in 1909 and quietly repairs in 1960. ⚠ And in the middle of his second day of reading omens for money, standing beside his own altars, the professional diviner says there is no divination in Israel. The chapter has no Masoretic paragraph break at all, and it stops in the middle of a sacrifice.Numbers 24Balak has paid for a curse three times and is about to be told what will happen to him. ⚠ The chapter opens by reporting that Balaam stopped going toward omens — the plural of the very noun he had just denied of Israel in the previous chapter, so the man abandons his technique one sentence after preaching against it — and instead the spirit of God comes on him, with no altars in the sentence at all. ⭐ He then introduces himself twice, in words identical letter for letter, as ‘the man whose eye is shetum’ — two Hebrew words standing in exactly two verses of the Bible, which nobody can construe, and which the shelf reads three ways at once: OPEN (KJV, both Reina-Valeras), SHUT (ASV, Geneva, Douay), or a state that has CHANGED. ⭐ What follows is the line every synagogue recites on entering — how good are your tents, Jacob — said by a foreign diviner about tents he was hired to curse; and one verse later the same consonants come back meaning ALOES, except in the Douay-Rheims, which alone keeps them as tents. Balak claps his hands in fury and makes, almost word for word, the complaint Balaam’s donkey made two chapters ago. ⭐ Then the fourth oracle: a STAR steps out of Jacob and a SCEPTRE rises from Israel — the verse that a Jewish revolt took its leader’s name from in 132 AD, and whose second half is so broken that every version repairs it differently. Three short oracles close it, one of them punning the Kenite’s name against the word for a NEST and then calling his nation Cain. ⚠ And Balaam goes home unharmed, which is a problem, because Numbers 31 says he was killed with the sword and that the ruin of Israel at Peor happened by his advice.Numbers 25The disaster Balaam could not deliver, arriving without him. ⚠ Israel camps at Shittim — the place named after ACACIAS, the wood its own tabernacle is built from — and the people begin sleeping with Moabite women and eating at their gods’ sacrifices. ⭐ The verb for what happens next is built on the word for a YOKE OF PLOUGH OXEN: Israel did not join itself to Baal-peor, it was harnessed to him, and the whole shelf reads a joining instead. God orders the heads of the people hung up facing the sun, in a verb that stands in exactly two verses of the Bible — the other is 2 Samuel 21, where seven of Saul’s sons are left on a hill until the rain comes — and then Moses gives the judges a different order, and neither is reported as carried out. ⭐ What stops the plague is a priest with an infantry spear. Phinehas follows a man into the qubbah, a word nobody can define, and runs both him and the woman through her qevah — two near-homonyms in one sentence, the second found nowhere else in the Bible, and the Douay-Rheims alone reads the room as a brothel. Twenty-four thousand die; 1 Corinthians says twenty-three thousand, and this translation prints the Hebrew and does not reconcile them. ⭐ Then the reward: ‘my covenant, peace’ — two nouns side by side with nothing binding them, and a word the Masoretic scribes marked with a BROKEN LETTER that no printed edition and no translation can show. ⚠ The victims are named six verses after they die, and one of the names reads as the word for a LIE. And the Zealots of the first century took their name from this chapter, which is a fact about its afterlife that the note states plainly. Balaam is not in this chapter at all; the book waits six more chapters to say the advice was his.Numbers 26The book counts its people a second time, and the arithmetic is the argument. ⚠ Numbers 1 counted a generation at Sinai; this counts their children on the plains of Moab, thirty-eight years later, in the same words — and the last three verses explain why: not one man of the first list is on this one, because they were sentenced to die in the wilderness and they did. Only two names are exempted, and they are the two scouts who brought back the minority report. ⭐ Set the two totals side by side and they barely move — 603,550 then, 601,730 now, a net loss of under a third of one per cent across a generation’s worth of dying. The movement is all inside: Simeon collapses from 59,300 to 22,200, losing nearly two-thirds of its men with no explanation offered, one chapter after a Simeonite chief was named as the man Phinehas killed; Manasseh runs the other way and ends ahead of the brother Jacob had deliberately set above him in Genesis 48. ⭐ And the chapter’s best line is three Hebrew words long. After the earth swallows Korah’s company, the list pauses to say the sons of Korah did not die — a sentence the Masoretes gave its own paragraph break, and the reason eleven psalms have an author. ⚠ Four times this roll of the living stops to record who is not on it: two men dead in Canaan, two priests dead bringing strange fire, and five daughters standing where a son should be — Zelophehad’s, whose names include a future royal city and a Noah who is not the Noah of the ark, a distinction English cannot print and Spanish keeps by accident.Numbers 27Five women walk to the front of the camp and change a law. ⭐ Zelophehad’s daughters — named in a census two chapters ago for no stated reason — stand before Moses, the priest, the chieftains and the whole congregation and argue a case: our father was not in Korah’s faction, he died in his own sin, and he had no sons, so why should his name be taken away from his clan? And the hinge of the chapter is that Moses does not rule; he refers the question up, and a Masoretic paragraph break falls exactly there, between the question and the answer. God’s first word on it is that the women are correct, and within six verses a petition has become a precedent and the precedent a statute. ⚠ Then the chapter turns and gives Moses his own sentence as an errand: climb the ridge, look at the land, and be gathered to your people — for a thing he did at a rock thirty-eight years earlier. He does not argue. His next recorded words ask for a successor, in an image that stands in exactly two verses of the Hebrew Bible: sheep that have no shepherd — the other belonging to a prophet telling a doomed king he is not one. ⭐ And the commissioning is written as a demotion. Joshua gets some of Moses’ majesty, a partitive form found in this one verse and nowhere else, and where Moses spoke to God directly Joshua must stand before a priest and inquire by the Urim — a channel the Bible later records failing Saul and then being lost. ⚠ One last thing: God says lay your HAND on him. Moses lays both.Numbers 28Jehovah hands Moses the tabernacle's fixed offering calendar, and the chapter's whole grammar is one word: tamid, continual — every occasion from here on is layered ON the twice-daily lamb, never instead of it. ⭐ The numbers agree exactly with Exodus 29:38-42, unchanged decades later — a rare moment of pure consistency in a book that keeps repeating its own laws, right before the calendar starts giving real reasons to check every parallel rather than assume one. ⚠ V7's drink offering is not wine but shekhar, strong drink — the very word Leviticus 10:9 forbids Aaron's sons to drink before entering the tent, on pain of death; here it is poured, never swallowed, in that same holy place. ⭐⭐ The new moon's own goat carries a phrase neither of the chapter's other two sin-offering goats do — 'to Jehovah' — and the sages read exactly that oddity: tractate Shevuot has Reish Lakish call it an atonement 'for having diminished the moon', reaching back to Genesis 1's own lesser light. The Passover day itself gets no offering at all — that rite was already legislated as a household meal, in Exodus 12 — and the one festival with nothing here to disagree over instead has no DATE: the Feast of Weeks is named only by relation to a count, and its own headcount here genuinely disagrees with Leviticus 23's list for the identical day.Numbers 29Jehovah completes the offering calendar Numbers 28 began, and this half is where the chapter starts naming its own seams instead of leaving them silent. ⭐ The New Year's day of blowing (v1) is never called a new year here or in its parallel at Leviticus 23 — Exodus 12:2 already gave that title to a different month, six months off; ‘Rosh Hashanah’ is a later name for an older day. ⭐⭐ The Day of Atonement's own goat is offered 'besides the sin-offering of atonement' (v11) — naming Leviticus 16's elaborate high-priest ritual by its technical term and stacking on top of it rather than silently disagreeing with it, the way last chapter's Feast-of-Weeks count quietly didn't match Leviticus 23's. ⚠ Then the numbers themselves become the story: the Feast of Tabernacles holds two rams and fourteen lambs constant across seven days and drops exactly one bull each day — thirteen down to seven, seventy in all — and the Talmud (Sukkah 55b) has an answer for that number even the text itself never gives: seventy bulls for the seventy nations of Genesis 10's own Table of Nations, atoning for the whole world rather than Israel alone. ⭐ And the eighth day that closes the feast turns out to match the New Year that opened the month, animal for animal — one bull, one ram, seven lambs, both times. The chapter's own last verse says what the whole two-chapter calendar always was: a floor under the nation's worship, not a ceiling on anyone's.Numbers 30A single law of vows, and the shelf cannot even agree where it begins — only Douay-Rheims and Geneva 1599 side with the Masoretic text in opening the chapter with the sentence Numbers 29's own closing note promised; the other eleven witnesses on this project's shelf attach it to the chapter before. ⭐ The father's law (vv3-6) uses one verb of override, spoken twice in a single verse; the husband's law (vv7-16) uses two, and the second — 'annul' — is one the father's law never uses at all: seven times across four of the ten verses, three of them in one verse alone. ⭐⭐ Three times an on-time override closes with 'and Jehovah will forgive her' — but the one time a husband waits too long, the sentence is not forgiveness for her but 'he shall bear her guilt' (v16): the unresolved weight of an overridden vow does not vanish, it moves. ⚠ And the one woman no man can override is the one who answers to neither — a widow or a divorced woman, whose own vow carries the identical unconditional force this chapter gives a man's.Numbers 31Moses sends an army to avenge Israel on Midian, and the campaign that follows refuses to stay simple. Every fighting man is killed and every city burned — and Balaam, the gentile seer who filled three earlier chapters with his own voice, dies in six words, the same clause as five kings whose names appear nowhere else in the Bible. Then Moses, furious that women and children were spared, gives the hardest order in this book so far. The purification law that follows treats even a war Jehovah commanded as something that leaves the men who fought it unclean. And the arithmetic, for once, is flawless: every number in the war-tax and the census checks out exactly, twice — including a war-tribute word, mekhes, that appears nowhere else in the Hebrew Bible outside this one chapter.Numbers 32Reuben and Gad, rich in cattle, ask to settle east of the Jordan instead of crossing into Canaan — and Moses answers not with a ruling but with a history lesson, retelling Numbers 13-14 almost word for word to men who were children when the scouts' report cost their parents forty years in the wilderness. The covenant he strikes has no divine speech in it at all — the first extended negotiation in this book settled entirely by a human being, in Jehovah's name but not his voice — and it turns on one word repeated seven times: every man must be armed before he crosses, or 'know that your sin will find you out,' a line the shelf translates two different ways depending on which language, and sometimes which decade, you read it in. Gad, blessed on his father's deathbed as a raiding band that raids at last, becomes exactly that: Israel's own armed vanguard. And while Moses is still negotiating with the two tribes who actually asked, a third — Manasseh's own clans — is already taking Gilead by conquest, without asking anyone at all.Numbers 33Moses writes the whole forty-year itinerary himself, stage by stage, forty-two camps from Rameses to the plains of Moab, and its opening line collects a promise made forty years earlier: Exodus 12:12 has Jehovah say he WILL execute judgments on Egypt's gods; here, in the perfect tense, he HAS. Most of the camp names in between are attested nowhere else in the whole Bible — whole years of wandering compressed into words this list is the only record of — until the march stops twice: once for Aaron's death, dated by year, month, and day — one of only a handful of such precise dates in the whole wilderness narrative — at a station and on an order Deuteronomy 10:6 will later tell differently; once for an enemy king who is only, ominously, listening. Then Jehovah commands the conquest itself — drive out every inhabitant, destroy every carved stone and molten image and high place, divide the land by lot — and closes on a warning built from two words found nowhere else in Hebrew, which the whole shelf agrees are thorns and disagrees, right down to which eye they belong in.Numbers 34The borders of Canaan, drawn corner to corner — and the chapter is a DEED, not a description: gvul, border, stands in it sixteen times in twenty-nine verses, more than in any other chapter of the Torah and nearly three times the runner-up. ⭐ The land is not GIVEN here, it FALLS to you (v2) — the vocabulary of the lot, which the chapter says outright eleven verses later, and which only the Douay-Rheims explains, by putting ‘by lot’ into v2 itself. ⚠ In Hebrew the west IS the sea: v6 says sea-border, sea, sea-border, and every version on both shelves prints ‘west’ and the collision disappears. ⚠⚠ The mount Hor of v7 is NOT the mountain Aaron died on one chapter ago — same letters, opposite end of the country — and the Douay-Rheims alone never creates the problem, because it declines the name and reads ‘the most high mountain’. ⭐ Three sets of letters here carry two unrelated meanings apiece: a riverbed and an inheritance; marking out a border and COVETING (that verb exists nowhere else in the Bible, and is spelled like the one in Deuteronomy’s tenth commandment); and reaching a lakeshore and BLOTTING OUT. ⭐ The border strikes the SHOULDER of the Sea of Galilee — which no version on either shelf says — in a land that by then has hands, a shoulder and eyes. ⚠ Then the accounting: nine tribes are GIVEN their land and two and a half TOOK theirs, a distinction the NWT, the TNM 1987 and both Reina-Valeras keep and the whole KJV tradition flattens. And a roster of ten chieftains listed in map order, three of whom are never called chieftains at all.

About the book

Where it sits
The fourth book of the TORAH (the Law / Pentateuch, the five books of Moses), and the fourth book of the Christian Old Testament.
Genre
Narrative and law interwoven — a march-journal braided with censuses, camp-arrangements, priestly and purity law, and vivid set-pieces of rebellion and mercy, with a few very ancient poems (the Song of the Ark, Balaam's oracles).
Author
By ancient and traditional reckoning, Jewish and Christian alike, Numbers is the work of MOSES — the man at the center of nearly every scene, and the book records that 'Moses wrote down their starting-places, stage by stage' (33:2). The events run from Mount Sinai in the second year after the Exodus to the plains of Moab in the fortieth (15th century BC on the early date this translation follows). Since Wellhausen the critical tradition has read Numbers, like the rest of the Pentateuch, as a weave of sources — much of it assigned to a late 'Priestly' hand around the Babylonian exile, around an older narrative core. This translation leads with the traditional Mosaic authorship — see 'Where the debates are' below.
Date written
On the traditional view, essentially Mosaic: composed across the wilderness period, c. 1445–1406 BC. Its events span from the first day of the second month of the second year after the Exodus (1:1) to the fortieth year on the plains of Moab, just before Moses's death. (The critical view dates the book's Priestly material's composition far later, around the 6th–5th century BC.)
Place
The wilderness — the book moves from the wilderness of SINAI (chs. 1–10), through the desert wanderings by way of Kadesh, to the plains of MOAB across the Jordan from Jericho (chs. 22–36), where Israel encamps at the threshold of the promised land.
Audience
The SECOND generation — the children of those who came out of Egypt, mustered anew on the plains of Moab and about to enter the land their parents refused. Numbers is written to orient and to warn them: here is how the presence of God is to be ordered in the camp, and here is what unbelief cost the generation before you.

How it's laid out

1–10At Sinai — the census and the camp arrayed around the tent, the Levites set apart, the Passover and the silver trumpets; then the cloud lifts and Israel marches.
11–12The march and the first grumblings — the graves of craving, and Miriam and Aaron speaking against Moses, 'the meekest man on the face of the earth.'
13–14KADESH — the twelve spies, the majority's bad report, the people's refusal to enter, and the sentence: forty years in the wilderness, and this whole generation to die there.
15–19Laws and rebellion — the revolt of Korah, the plague stayed by incense, and the budding of Aaron's staff that settles the priesthood.
20–25The second generation moves — the deaths of Miriam and Aaron, Moses striking the rock, the bronze serpent, victories in Transjordan, Balaam's oracles, and the sin at Peor.
26–36On the plains of Moab — the second census, Joshua appointed, the daughters of Zelophehad, the festival calendar, the boundaries of the land, and the cities of refuge.

What it's about

  • The ORDER of the camp around the presence — God at the center, the tribes arrayed in their armies, the Levites a ring between: holiness given a shape you could walk through.
  • UNBELIEF and its cost — Kadesh is the hinge of the book: the refusal of the land, the forty years, 'your corpses shall fall in this wilderness' (14:29).
  • GRUMBLING against God, and his patience — a repeated cycle of complaint, judgment, Moses's intercession, and mercy.
  • The FAITHFULNESS of God through the faithlessness of the people — a whole generation is lost, yet the promise passes to their children intact.
  • PROVISION in the wilderness — the manna, the water from the rock, the guiding cloud and the silver trumpets.
  • INTERCESSION — Moses standing again and again in the breach, pleading for the people who have just risen against him (14:13–19).

The source text

Translated from the Hebrew MASORETIC TEXT — the digital Hebrew of Mechon-Mamre (the Leningrad/Aleppo tradition), consonants with the Masoretes' vowel-points and cantillation, and its scribal peculiarities kept. The scroll's own paragraph breaks are shown as it marks them: petuchah {פ} (open) and setumah {ס} (closed). Numbers carries one of the most striking scribal marks in the whole Torah — the two INVERTED NUNS (נ) that bracket the little Song of the Ark at 10:35–36, an ancient editorial sign whose meaning the tradition still debates (a passage 'out of place,' or set apart as its own book). The seven-version shelf under every chapter compares the NIV, KJV, Douay-Rheims, The Living Bible, the 1599 Geneva, ASV, and NWT.

Looking forward

The New Testament reads the wilderness generation as a deliberate pattern: 'these things happened to them as examples, and were written down for our instruction' (1 Corinthians 10:11). Paul calls the manna and the water 'spiritual food and drink,' and says of the rock that followed them, 'that rock was Christ' (1 Corinthians 10:4). Two images reach furthest: the BRONZE SERPENT lifted on a pole, that whoever looked to it might live (21:9), which Jesus takes for his own cross — 'as Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up' (John 3:14); and Balaam's oracle of 'a STAR that shall come out of Jacob' (24:17), long read as a promise of the Messiah. This translation marks those echoes as they come; it does not force them.

Where the debates are

The questions are AUTHORSHIP, DATE, and — uniquely sharp in this book — the NUMBERS themselves. On authorship and date the picture matches the rest of the Torah: the traditional view is Mosaic composition in the 15th–13th centuries BC; the dominant academic view since Wellhausen reads a late 'Priestly' edition (6th–5th c. BC) around older material. The distinctive debate is the size of the census: taken plainly, 603,550 fighting men imply a nation of perhaps two million crossing the desert — a miracle sustained by daily manna. Because the Hebrew word eleph means both 'thousand' and 'clan / military unit,' some scholars read the totals as a smaller host (so-many units of so-many men); others hold the plain figures. A third strand of debate concerns the wilderness itinerary and the location of Kadesh and of Mount Sinai itself. This library prints the numbers as the text gives them, flags the eleph ambiguity in the word rather than resolving it, and lays out the readings with their pedigrees without casting a vote — the same posture it takes on Genesis's composition and the date of Daniel.
