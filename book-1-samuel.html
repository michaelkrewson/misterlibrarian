📖 1 Samuel

שְׁמוּאֵל א Shemuel Alef — '1 Samuel.' In the Hebrew Bible, Samuel is a SINGLE book (named for the prophet Samuel — 'God has heard,' or 'name of God'); the split into 1 and 2 Samuel comes from the Greek Septuagint, which divided it (and Kings) for length. It is the book of the great turn from the judges to the monarchy.
Βασιλειῶν Αʹ (Basileion A) — '1 Kingdoms' — The Septuagint groups Samuel and Kings as four 'books of the Kingdoms' (Basileion A-D), so that 1 Samuel is '1 Kingdoms' and 2 Samuel '2 Kingdoms' — a title that names the books by their subject, the rise and history of Israel's kings.

The hinge from the judges to the kings — Samuel the last judge and first great prophet, Saul the first king and his fall, and the rise of David; and it opens on a barren woman's answered prayer.

Chapter by chapter

1 Samuel 1The book that gives Israel its kings opens on a barren woman. Hannah, provoked year after year by her rival, prays silently at Shiloh until the priest Eli takes her for a drunk; she vows her son to God, and when Samuel is born she carries the weaned boy back and gives him away — 'lent to Jehovah' for life. The FIRST 'Jehovah of armies' in the Bible, a wordplay ('asked') that reaches toward Saul, and the seed of the song (ch 2) that Mary's Magnificat will be built on.1 Samuel 2Hannah's song: the God who breaks the bows of the mighty, makes the barren bear seven, puts to death and brings to life, raises the poor from the ash heap, and lifts high the horn of his anointed, before Israel has a king. Against it, Eli's sons, 'sons of worthlessness', take the meat before the fat is burned, while the boy Samuel ministers in a linen ephod. A man of God: 'those who honour me I will honour', both sons will die in one day, and God will raise up a faithful priest.1 Samuel 3The call of Samuel, when 'the word of Jehovah was rare': before the lamp of God goes out, Jehovah calls the boy three times and three times he runs to the old priest; the fourth time Jehovah comes and stands, 'Samuel! Samuel!', and the boy answers, 'Speak, for your servant is listening.' The word is the end of Eli's house; Eli says, 'He is Jehovah.' None of Samuel's words falls to the ground, and all Israel from Dan to Beersheba knows him for a prophet.1 Samuel 4The ark is taken. Beaten at Eben-ezer, the elders fetch the ark of Jehovah of armies, enthroned on the cherubim, from Shiloh: 'that it may come among us and save us.' The camp shouts a great shout; the Philistines cry 'God has come into the camp' and fight. Thirty thousand fall, the ark is captured, Hophni and Phinehas die on one day; Eli, ninety-eight, falls backward at the news, 'old and heavy', and a dying mother names her son Ichabod: 'the glory has gone into exile from Israel.'1 Samuel 5The ark in the house of Dagon. The Philistines set the captured ark beside their god at Ashdod; two mornings running Dagon lies on his face before it, the second time with head and hands broken off on the threshold. The hand of Jehovah is heavy on Ashdod with tumours; the lords move the ark around to Gath, then to Ekron, and a very great panic goes with it, until the cry of the city goes up to the heavens.1 Samuel 6The ark comes home. After seven months the Philistine priests and diviners say it must not go back empty: five golden tumours and five golden mice go with it as a guilt offering, on a new cart drawn by two milk cows whose calves are shut up at home. The cows go straight to Beth-shemesh, lowing; the reapers rejoice and offer them on a great stone, but men of the town are struck for gazing at the ark, seventy men, fifty thousand men, as the Hebrew stands, and the ark is sent on toward Kiriath-jearim.1 Samuel 7Thus far Jehovah has helped us. The ark rests twenty years in the house of Abinadab at Kiriath-jearim while Israel laments after Jehovah. Samuel calls them to put away the Baalim and the Ashtaroth and gathers them at Mizpah, where they pour out water and confess; the Philistines attack during his offering, Jehovah thunders and routs them, and Samuel sets up a stone and names it Eben-ezer. The cities from Ekron to Gath return, and Samuel judges Israel all his life.1 Samuel 8Give us a king. Samuel grows old and his sons, judges at Beersheba, bend toward gain, take bribes and bend judgment. The elders ask for a king to judge them like all the nations; Jehovah tells Samuel they have rejected not him but Jehovah as king, and has him declare the due of the king: he will take their sons, daughters, fields and servants, tithe their harvest, and they will be his servants. The people refuse to listen, and Jehovah says, Make a king reign for them.1 Samuel 9Saul and the lost donkeys. Kish of Benjamin sends his son Saul, a young man taller than all the people, to look for lost she-donkeys; after three days the servant proposes asking the man of God, the seer, in a town in the land of Zuph. Jehovah has told Samuel the day before, I will send you a man from Benjamin; anoint him leader over my people. Samuel meets Saul at the gate, seats him at the head of the guests and gives him the kept portion, and at dawn takes him aside to hear the word of God.1 Samuel 10Is Saul also among the prophets? Samuel anoints Saul with a flask of oil as leader over Jehovah's inheritance and gives him three signs: news of the donkeys at Rachel's tomb, bread from pilgrims at the oak of Tabor, and a band of prophets at Gibeah of God. The spirit rushes on Saul and he prophesies. At Mizpah Samuel tells the people they have rejected God; Saul is taken, found hiding among the baggage, and hailed with Long live the king.1 Samuel 11Saul rescues Jabesh-gilead. Nahash the Ammonite besieges Jabesh and offers terms: every man's right eye gouged out as a reproach on all Israel. The spirit of God rushes on Saul, back from the field behind his oxen; he cuts a yoke of oxen in pieces and sends them through Israel, and the people come out as one man. Three companies strike the Ammonite camp in the morning watch, Saul refuses to put his doubters to death, and at Gilgal the kingship is renewed and Saul is made king before Jehovah.1 Samuel 12Samuel's farewell at Gilgal. Samuel asks Israel to testify whether he has taken anyone's ox or donkey or a ransom to hide his eyes, and the people clear him before Jehovah and his anointed. He retells the story from Egypt through Sisera, the Philistines and Moab to Nahash, sets the king and the people under one condition, and calls down thunder and rain at the wheat harvest. The people beg him to pray for them; he warns them not to turn aside after empty things.1 Samuel 13Saul's sacrifice at Gilgal. The verse that should give Saul's age and reign has lost its number. Jonathan strikes the Philistine garrison at Geba; the Philistines mass at Michmash with chariots like the sand of the sea, and Israel hides in caves. Saul waits seven days at Gilgal, then offers the burnt offering himself; Samuel arrives and tells him his kingdom will not stand, for Jehovah has sought a man after his own heart. Israel has no smith, and pays a pim to sharpen its tools.1 Samuel 14Jonathan at the pass of Michmash. With only his armour-bearer Jonathan climbs between the crags Bozez and Seneh to the Philistine outpost, saying nothing holds Jehovah back from saving by many or by few; the camp panics and Jehovah saves Israel that day. Saul binds the army with an oath against food, Jonathan tastes honey, the people eat with the blood, and when God does not answer the lot falls on Jonathan, until the people ransom him.1 Samuel 15Saul and Amalek. Sent to devote Amalek to destruction, Saul strikes them from Havilah toward Shur but spares King Agag and the best of the flock. Jehovah regrets making him king; at Gilgal Samuel answers his “I have carried out the word of Jehovah” with “to listen is better than sacrifice”, the robe tears as Samuel turns away, and the kingdom is torn from Saul and given to a neighbour better than he.1 Samuel 16David anointed at Bethlehem. Jehovah sends Samuel, still mourning for Saul, to Jesse with a horn of oil and a heifer to sacrifice. Jesse’s sons pass before him, beginning with tall Eliab, and Samuel is told “the human sees what meets the eyes, but Jehovah sees into the heart”. The youngest, ruddy David, is fetched from the flock and anointed among his brothers; the spirit of Jehovah rushes on him and turns away from Saul, and David’s lyre brings Saul relief from an evil spirit.1 Samuel 17David and Goliath. The Philistines camp between Socoh and Azekah and Saul’s army faces them across the valley of Elah. For forty days Goliath of Gath, six cubits tall in the Hebrew and four in the Greek, taunts “the battle lines of Israel”. Sent with food for his brothers, David refuses Saul’s armour and goes out with a sling and five smooth stones “in the name of Jehovah of armies”; the stone sinks into the Philistine’s forehead, and Saul asks “Whose son is this youth?”1 Samuel 18“Saul has struck down his thousands, and David his ten thousands.” Jonathan’s soul is bound up with David’s and he gives him his robe, sword and bow; the women’s song turns Saul against him, the evil spirit rushes on Saul and he hurls the spear; Merab is promised and given to another, and Michal, who loves David, is given to him for a hundred Philistine foreskins. Much of the chapter is missing from the Greek.1 Samuel 19“Is Saul also among the prophets?” Jonathan pleads for David and Saul swears he will not die; the evil spirit comes again and the spear is thrown into the wall; Michal lets David down through the window and puts the teraphim in his bed; David flees to Samuel at Naioth, and three sets of messengers and then Saul himself are seized by the spirit and prophesy.1 Samuel 20“Go in peace”: David flees to Jonathan and asks what he has done; they agree a test at the new moon and a signal of arrows by the stone Ezel, and Jonathan asks for the kindness of Jehovah for his house forever; at the feast Saul misses David two days running, curses his son and hurls the spear at him; next morning the arrow flies beyond the boy, and the two friends weep and part.1 Samuel 21“Do I lack madmen?”: David comes alone to Nob, tells the trembling priest Ahimelech a story of a secret errand for the king, and is given the holy bread of the presence and the sword of Goliath from behind the ephod, while Doeg the Edomite watches; then he flees to Achish of Gath, where the servants remember the song of his ten thousands, and he saves himself by playing the madman at the gate.1 Samuel 22“Stay with me”: David escapes to the cave of Adullam, where his family and four hundred men in distress, in debt and bitter of soul gather to him; he leaves his parents with the king of Moab. Under the tamarisk at Gibeah Saul accuses his servants of conspiracy; Doeg the Edomite tells of the priest at Nob, and when the king’s runners will not touch the priests of Jehovah, Doeg kills eighty-five of them and strikes the city. One son, Abiathar, escapes to David.1 Samuel 23“Will the lords of Keilah hand me over?”: David inquires of Jehovah and saves Keilah from the Philistines; Abiathar brings the ephod, and when Saul marches on the walled town the answer comes that its lords would hand David over, so he leaves with six hundred men for the strongholds of the wilderness of Ziph. Jonathan finds him at Horesh and tells him he will be king, and the two cut a covenant; the Ziphites betray him, Saul closes in round a mountain in the wilderness of Maon, and a Philistine raid calls him away at the Crag of the Divisions.1 Samuel 24“Is this your voice, my son David?”: back from the Philistines, Saul hunts David with three thousand chosen men on the rocks of the wild goats near En-gedi and goes alone into a cave where David’s men are hiding. David cuts off the corner of his robe, is struck at heart, and will not let his men touch Jehovah’s anointed; outside, he shows Saul the corner of the robe and leaves judgment to Jehovah. Saul weeps, says “You are more righteous than I” and “you will surely be king”, and makes David swear not to cut off his seed.1 Samuel 25“As his name is, so is he”: Samuel dies and is buried in his house at Ramah. In Carmel the rich sheep-owner Nabal, harsh and evil in his deeds, answers David’s ten young men with “Who is David?”, and David girds on his sword and swears to leave no male of his house alive by morning. Nabal’s wife Abigail, good of understanding, rides down the mountain with bread, wine, raisins and cakes of figs, falls at David’s feet, takes the guilt on herself and speaks of “a faithful house” and “the bundle of life”. David blesses her good sense; ten days later Jehovah strikes Nabal, and Abigail becomes David’s wife.1 Samuel 26“Is this your voice, my son David?” a second time: the Ziphites betray David again, and Saul goes down to the hill of Hachilah with three thousand chosen men. By night David and Abishai walk into the sleeping camp, where Saul lies within the ring with his spear stuck in the ground at his head. David will not let Abishai strike Jehovah’s anointed and takes the spear and the jug of water instead; from a mountain across the valley he shames Abner, and Saul confesses “I have acted foolishly”, blesses him, and the two part for the last time.1 Samuel 27“Now I shall be swept away one day by the hand of Saul”: David says in his heart that there is nothing better than to escape to the land of the Philistines, and crosses over with his six hundred men and their households to Achish son of Maoch, king of Gath; Saul hears and seeks him no more. Achish gives him Ziklag, “to this day” a town of the kings of Judah, and for a year and four months David raids the Geshurites, the Gizrites and the Amalekites, leaving no man or woman alive to tell, while he tells Achish he has raided the Negev of Judah. Achish believes him: “He has made himself a stench among his people, and he will be my servant forever.”1 Samuel 28“Bring up Samuel for me”: the Philistines gather at Shunem and Achish tells David he will march with him; Samuel is dead and Saul has removed the mediums from the land. Camped on Gilboa, his heart trembling, and answered by no dream, no Urim and no prophet, Saul disguises himself and goes by night to a woman at En-dor who is mistress of a ghost. She sees “gods coming up out of the earth”, an old man wrapped in a robe, and Saul knows it is Samuel: Jehovah has torn the kingdom from his hand and given it to David, and tomorrow he and his sons will be with him. Saul falls his full height to the ground, and the woman kills her stall-fed calf and bakes unleavened bread for the king before he goes out into the night.1 Samuel 29“Is not this David?” The Philistines muster at Aphek and march by hundreds and thousands, David and his men in the rear with Achish; the Philistine commanders refuse to take “these Hebrews” into the battle, afraid David will become an adversary, and quote the women’s song; Achish swears “As Jehovah lives” that David is upright and sends him home, and the Philistines go up to Jezreel.1 Samuel 30Ziklag burned. David and his men come home on the third day to find the town burned by the Amalekites and the women and children carried off, among them Ahinoam and Abigail; the men weep until they have no strength and speak of stoning him, and David strengthens himself in Jehovah, asks for the ephod, and pursues. An Egyptian slave left behind leads him to the raiders; he rescues everything, rules that the share of the baggage equals the share of the battle, and sends gifts to the elders of Judah.

About the book

Where it sits
In the Hebrew Bible, one book ('Samuel') among the Former Prophets; in the Christian Old Testament, 1 and 2 Samuel, following Ruth and preceding 1-2 Kings — the second stretch of the four-book sweep (Samuel-Kings) from the last judge to the fall of Jerusalem.
Genre
HISTORICAL NARRATIVE — part of the 'Former Prophets' (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, Kings) in the Hebrew canon, and of what scholars call the Deuteronomistic History. Superb narrative art: the intertwined stories of Samuel, Saul, and David, told with a psychological depth and moral ambiguity rarely matched in ancient literature.
Author
Anonymous. Jewish tradition (Talmud, Bava Batra 14b) credits Samuel with the early chapters and the prophets Nathan and Gad with the rest (cf 1 Chronicles 29:29) — though Samuel himself dies at 1 Samuel 25. Critical scholarship sees the book as woven from older sources (an 'Ark Narrative,' a 'History of David's Rise,' the 'Court History' of 2 Samuel) into the larger Deuteronomistic History, reaching its final form in or after the exile.
Date written
The events span roughly 1100-1010 BC (from Samuel's birth to Saul's death on Gilboa). The book's sources are old; its final composition is debated, commonly placed within the monarchy and edited into the Deuteronomistic History by the exile (6th century BC).
Place
The central hill country of Israel — Ramah, Shiloh, Gibeah, and the Philistine borderlands — in the generation when Israel, pressed by the Philistines and led by a corrupt priesthood and a last great judge, demanded a king 'like all the nations.'
Audience
Israel under, or remembering, the monarchy — a people wrestling with the institution of kingship itself: was asking for a king a rejection of God's own rule, or the means through which God would raise up David and, through him, the promise of an everlasting throne? The book holds both truths in tension and never quite resolves them.

How it's laid out

1-7Samuel — his birth to Hannah, his call at Shiloh, the loss and return of the ark, and his judgeship over a repentant Israel.
8-15Saul — Israel demands a king; Samuel warns them and anoints Saul; Saul's early victories, his disobedience, and his rejection by God.
16-31David's rise and Saul's fall — David anointed in secret, Goliath, David at court, Saul's jealousy and pursuit, David the fugitive, and Saul's death on Mount Gilboa.

What it's about

  • KINGSHIP, and its ambiguity — Israel asks for a king 'like the nations,' which Samuel calls a rejection of God's own kingship (8:7); yet God grants it and works through it. The book neither simply endorses nor simply condemns the monarchy.
  • GOD LIFTS THE LOWLY — sounded first in Hannah's song (2:1-10): 'he raises the poor from the dust… Jehovah kills and makes alive.' The barren bear children, the shepherd-boy becomes king, the mighty are brought down — the theme Mary's Magnificat takes up (Luke 1).
  • OBEDIENCE OVER SACRIFICE — Saul is rejected not for weakness but for disobedience dressed up as worship; 'to obey is better than sacrifice' (15:22) is the book's verdict on him.
  • THE ANOINTED ONE (MASHIACH) — Saul, then David, is 'Jehovah's anointed'; David, 'a man after God's own heart,' becomes the pattern of the king to come, and 'Jehovah looks on the heart, not the outward appearance' (16:7).
  • THE WORD OF THE PROPHET — from Samuel's night-call ('speak, for your servant hears') onward, the prophet's word directs and judges the king; the throne stands under the word.

The source text

Translated from the pointed Hebrew Masoretic Text (as printed by Mechon-Mamre). ⚠️ Samuel is famous among Old Testament books for the DIFFICULTY of its Hebrew text: the Masoretic text has suffered more than most from scribal slips, and the Greek Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls (especially the Samuel scrolls from Qumran cave 4) frequently differ, at times preserving a better reading. This translation follows the Masoretic Hebrew and notes the major variants where they fall (already at 1:24, 'three bulls' vs 'a three-year-old bull').

Looking forward

The book's gravity all leans toward DAVID — 'Jehovah's anointed' (mashiach, 'messiah'), the shepherd-king chosen not for his height but for his heart, whose throne God will promise to establish forever (2 Samuel 7). The New Testament traces Jesus to David's line and calls him 'Son of David'; the anointing, the shepherd, the rejected-then-exalted king are all patterns the Gospels take up. And the book opens with a note the Gospels echo directly: Hannah's song of the God who lifts the lowly and casts down the proud (2:1-10) is the model on which Mary sings her Magnificat (Luke 1:46-55).

Where the debates are

The main questions are SOURCES and COMPOSITION (how the Ark Narrative, the History of David's Rise, and the Court History were combined, and the book's place in the Deuteronomistic History), and the two attitudes to KINGSHIP the book seems to hold at once (the 'pro-' and 'anti-monarchic' strands of chapters 8-12). The TEXT itself is a standing problem — where the Masoretic Hebrew, the Septuagint, and the Qumran scrolls diverge, which preserves the original. And particular cruxes (the two accounts of how Saul became king, the two of David entering Saul's service, Goliath's height in the Hebrew vs the Greek) are much discussed.
