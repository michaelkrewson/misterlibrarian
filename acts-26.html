Agrippa tells Paul he is permitted to speak for himself, and Paul, stretching out his hand, makes his defense: he counts himself happy to speak before a king who is an expert in all the customs and questions among the Jews, and he says that all the Jews know his manner of life from his youth, that he lived a Pharisee according to the strictest party of their form of worship, and that he now stands being judged for the hope of the promise made by God to the fathers, the hope to which the twelve tribes hope to attain, serving earnestly night and day. ‘Why is it judged unbelievable among you if God raises the dead?’ He tells how he once thought he ought to do many things in opposition to the name of Jesus the Nazarene: he shut up many of the holy ones in prisons with authority from the chief priests, cast his vote against them when they were put to death, punished them often in all the synagogues, tried to force them to blaspheme, and persecuted them even in the cities outside. Traveling to Damascus with authority and commission from the chief priests, he saw at midday on the road a light from heaven beyond the brightness of the sun shine around him and those with him; all fell to the ground, and he heard a voice in the Hebrew dialect: ‘Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me? It is hard for you to kick against the goads.’ ‘Who are you, Lord?’ ‘I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting.’ The voice tells him to rise and stand on his feet and appoints him a servant and a witness, rescuing him from the people and from the nations to whom he is sent, to open their eyes, to turn them from darkness to light and from the authority of Satan to God, so that they may receive forgiveness of sins and a lot among those who have been sanctified by faith in him. Paul says he was not disobedient to the heavenly vision but announced repentance first in Damascus and in Jerusalem, throughout the country of Judea and to the nations, and that for this Jews seized him in the temple and tried to kill him; having obtained help from God he stands testifying, saying nothing outside what the prophets and Moses spoke of, if the Christ was subject to suffering and if, as first from the resurrection of the dead, he was about to proclaim light to the people and to the nations. Festus says in a loud voice that Paul is out of his mind and that his great learning is turning him to madness; Paul answers that he is not, but utters words of truth and of a sound mind, that the king knows these things, and that this has not been done in a corner. He asks Agrippa whether he believes the prophets and says he knows he does; Agrippa answers that in a little time Paul is persuading him to make a Christian; Paul says he would pray to God that all who hear him might become such as he is, except for these bonds. The king, the governor, Berenice and those sitting with them rise and withdraw, saying to one another that the man is doing nothing worthy of death or bonds, and Agrippa tells Festus that the man could have been released if he had not appealed to Caesar.
Undated by the text. The chapter is the hearing announced at 25:22-27, on the day after Agrippa and Berenice entered the audience room (25:23); it names no year and no hour beyond the ‘midday’ of the road to Damascus (v13), told as an earlier event in Paul’s speech. The book gives no year for the road to Damascus in this retelling either, and this library dates the events no more closely than the book does. Acts 27 follows on these pages.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · Ἐπιτρέπεταί … ἐκτείνας τὴν χεῖρα … ἐγκαλοῦμαι … ἥγημαι … μακάριον … γνώστην … ἐθῶν τε καὶ ζητημάτων … μακροθύμως Epitrepetai … ekteinas tēn cheira … enkaloumai … hēgēmai … makarion … gnōstēn … ethōn te kai zētēmatōn … makrothymōs — Acts 26:1–3: Paul’s defense before Agrippa begins; what does “permitted” mean here, why does Paul “count himself happy” to be accused before a king, and what are the “customs and questions” of which Agrippa is an “expert”?

“Permitted” (Acts 26:1). Epitrepetai soi hyper seautou legein: “it is permitted you to speak for yourself.” The verb epitrepō stands in five verses of Acts: 21:39, 21:40, here, 27:3 and 28:16 (not yet on these pages). The nearest pair is at Jerusalem: Paul asks the tribune, epitrepson moi, “allow me to speak to the people” (21:39), and the tribune “had allowed it” (21:40). Here no request from Paul is recorded; the permission comes as a present-tense statement from the king who told Festus that he was “wishing to hear the man” (25:22). It is Agrippa, not Festus, who opens the hearing, though Festus had brought Paul out “especially before you, King Agrippa” (25:26); Luke does not say why the permission is the king’s to give, and the library entry for Agrippa II says what is known of him from outside the book. The word is the verb of leave granted by a superior, and the entry epitrepō follows it through Acts. Five shelf versions keep the passive: ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “Thou art permitted to speak,” and NWT 1984 “You are permitted to speak.” NIV “You have permission” names the permission as a thing held. TLB “Go ahead. Tell us your story.” drops the verb of leave and turns the sentence into a request for a narrative, which the Greek does not say. The apparatus (971) gives the preposition as hyper, “for, on behalf of,” to Westcott–Hort and Robinson–Pierpont, and as peri, “about, concerning,” to Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28; the printed text and this translation follow the first. NWT 1984 “in behalf of yourself” is the nearest English to hyper; the others print “for yourself” or “for thyself.”

“Stretching out his hand” (Acts 26:1). Ekteinas tēn cheira is a different participle from the kataseisas tē cheiri, “motioning with the hand,” that marks four earlier moments in Acts where a speaker gestures (12:17, 13:16, 19:33 and 21:40, where the hand brings a hush). The phrase ekteinas with the hand is in seven verses of the New Testament, six of them in the Gospels, where the stretched hand touches a leper (Matthew 8:3, Mark 1:41, Luke 5:13), takes hold of Peter on the water (14:31), draws a sword (26:51) or points to the disciples while speaking (12:49). Acts 26:1 is the seventh and the only one outside the Gospels. The verse describes a gesture of the speaker and says nothing of a silence to be won: no hush is mentioned, as there is at 21:40. The verb for the speech is apelogeito, the imperfect, “was making his defense,” the same verb that Paul uses of himself in the next verse and that Luke uses again at 26:24, where Festus breaks in. The apparatus (972) gives the order ekteinas tēn cheira apelogeito to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and apelogeito ekteinas tēn cheira to Robinson–Pierpont, “was making his defense, stretching out his hand”; the words are the same and only the order differs. The shelf splits three ways on the gesture. NWT 1984 “stretched his hand out,” ASV “stretched forth his hand” and KJV and Geneva “stretched forth the hand” translate the verb, and Douay “stretching forth his hand” does the same. NIV “motioned with his hand” prints the English of the other verb and so joins this opening to the four earlier ones, which the Greek does not do. TLB “with many gestures” makes one stretched hand into several. For the speech itself the shelf varies between a defense and a reply: NWT 1984 “proceeded to say in his defense,” NIV “began his defense” and TLB “presented his defense” keep the noun of defense, ASV has “made his defence,” while KJV and Geneva “answered for himself” and Douay “began to make his answer” say “answer.”

“Accused … make my defense” (Acts 26:2). Peri pantōn hōn enkaloumai hypo Ioudaiōn: “concerning all the things of which I am accused by Jews.” The verb enkaleō, “to bring a charge,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament: 19:38 and 19:40, where the Ephesian clerk sends the quarrel to the courts; 23:28 and 23:29, where Lysias writes that he found Paul “accused about questions of their law”; here; 26:7, where Paul uses it again, of the hope; and Romans 8:33, “Who will bring a charge against God’s chosen?” The Greek has no article before Ioudaiōn, here or at 26:7. NWT 1984 “accused by Jews” keeps that; ASV and Douay “accused by the Jews” and KJV and Geneva “accused of the Jews” add it, as NIV does with “accusations of the Jews.” Paul says “Jews” and not “the Jews,” and the text does not say whether this is meant to name some Jews or to leave the group open. The second verb is apologeisthai, “to make a defense,” with mellōn, “being about to,” and sēmeron, “today.” It is the verb of the speech at 26:1 and 26:24, and in Acts it stands in six verses: 19:33, where Alexander wished to make a defense to the people; 24:10, Paul to Felix, “I cheerfully make my defense”; 25:8; and 26:1, 26:2 and 26:24. The noun is at 22:1 and 25:16 (see apologia). The English of the shelf for this verb follows the split of verse 1: NWT 1984 “make my defense this day,” ASV “make my defense” and NIV “make my defense” against KJV “I shall answer for myself,” Douay “answer for myself” and Geneva “I shall answer this day.” TLB “present my answer” stays with “answer.” The apparatus (973) gives sēmeron apologeisthai to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and apologeisthai sēmeron to Robinson–Pierpont, an order difference only. “Before you” is epi sou, the words with which Festus said he had brought Paul out “especially before you, King Agrippa” (25:26).

“I count myself happy” (Acts 26:2). Hēgēmai emauton makarion: “I have come to count myself happy.” Hēgēmai is the perfect of hēgeomai, “to lead, to consider,” and the form stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Philippians 3:7 (not yet on these pages), where Paul says he has counted his gains as loss. The adjective makarion stands in two verses in Acts, here and 20:35, where this translation prints “blessed” for the saying of the Lord Jesus, as the Acts 20 note explains. Here it prints “happy,” as the dictionary entry makarios does for the Beatitudes: a man is telling a king how he feels about the occasion, and “blessed” would make him sound as if he were giving thanks for a blessing. Five of the seven shelf versions say “happy”: NWT 1984 “I count myself happy,” which is closest to the perfect and the reflexive, and ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “I think myself happy.” NIV “I consider myself fortunate” and TLB “I am fortunate” use “fortunate,” which makes it a matter of luck; TLB also drops the verb of reckoning. What the Greek gives Paul is a stated judgment, and no more: he does not say that he is glad to be accused, only that he counts himself happy to make his defense before this hearer. How the sentence is meant, as courtesy, as relief or as both, is not settled by the word.

“Expert” (Acts 26:3). Malista gnōstēn onta se pantōn tōn kata Ioudaious ethōn te kai zētēmatōn: “especially because you are an expert in all the customs and questions among the Jews.” The noun gnōstēs, “one who knows,” is built on ginōskō and stands in one verse of the archived Greek New Testament, this one; whether it is rarer or commoner outside the New Testament is reported here, not checked on this page. It is not the verb of knowing used of Felix at 24:22, “knowing more accurately the things concerning the Way,” nor the one said of the king at 26:26, but a noun for a person. The construction echoes Paul’s opening to Felix: at 24:10 he says ek pollōn etōn onta se kritēn tō ethnei toutō, “for many years you have been a judge over this nation,” and at 26:3 gnōstēn onta se. The two words onta se stand together as separate words in two verses, 24:10 and 26:3 (counted by reading the hits: the letters also end other participles at Matthew 12:44 and Mark 5:31). Each time the speaker names what the hearer has been, a judge for years or a knower of the customs, before asking for his hearing. The adverb malista, “especially,” stands in three verses of Acts: 20:38, where the elders grieve “especially” over the word that they will not see his face again; 25:26, where Festus brings Paul out especially before Agrippa; and here, where Paul gives the king’s knowledge as the reason he is glad. The shelf: NWT 1984 “expert on” and ASV “expert in” are the nearest; KJV “I know thee to be expert in” and TLB “I know you are an expert” add a “I know” that is not in the Greek, where the participle onta gives the reason, “since you are”; Geneva “thou hast knowledge of” and NIV “well acquainted with” soften an expert into someone who has knowledge; and Douay “thou knowest all” makes it a verb and moves “all” from the customs onto the knowing.

“Customs and questions” (Acts 26:3). Ethōn te kai zētēmatōn. The noun ethos stands in seven verses of Acts, counted by reading the hits, because the same letters also catch the forms of tithēmi: 6:14, where the witnesses say Jesus “will change the customs that Moses handed down”; 15:1; 16:21, the “customs” of the accusers at Philippi; 21:21, the charge that Paul teaches Jews not to walk by the customs; 25:16, where Festus calls it a custom of the Romans; here; and 28:17 (not yet on these pages). The apparatus (974) is a one-letter question: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print ethōn, from ethos, “custom,” and Robinson–Pierpont prints ēthōn, with an eta, from ēthos, which is reported to mean settled habits or character, and which in the archived text stands in one verse of the New Testament, ēthē at 1 Corinthians 15:33 (not yet on these pages). The second noun zētēma stands in five verses, all in Acts: 15:2, 18:15, 23:29, 25:19 and here. Three of the earlier four are the words of Roman officials: Gallio, “if they are questions about a word and names and your own law, see to it yourselves” (18:15); Lysias, “accused about questions of their law” (23:29); and Festus, “they had certain questions against him about their own religion” (25:19). At 25:20 Festus says that he was “at a loss about the inquiry into these things.” Paul now tells the one man in the room named as expert in such matters that he is expert in the very noun the officials used (see zētēma and ethos). The shelf: ASV and KJV “customs and questions,” Geneva “customs, and questions” and Douay “customs and questions” give four of the seven for “questions”; NWT 1984 “controversies” and NIV “customs and controversies” give a second pair, which names the quarrel and not the question; TLB “Jewish laws and customs” has no second noun, and “laws” is not in the verse. Whether Paul’s compliment is courtesy, strategy or plain description is not said; the Greek gives only the claim that he knows.

“I beg you to hear me patiently” (Acts 26:3). Dio deomai makrothymōs akousai mou: “therefore I beg you to hear me patiently.” The dio, “therefore,” makes the request follow from the expertise: because the king knows the matter, Paul can ask him to hear a long account. The adverb makrothymōs stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; its family is at makrothymia, “long-temperedness.” The verb deomai, “I beg,” stands in three verses of Acts in this first-person form: 8:34, the eunuch to Philip, “I beg you, about whom does the prophet say this”; 21:39, Paul to the tribune; and here. At 21:39 and 8:34 the Greek reads deomai sou, “I beg you,” and the apparatus (975) says that Robinson–Pierpont adds sou here, so that its text has the same two words and the other witnesses leave the “you” to be understood. The request has an earlier counterpart, with the opposite length. Tertullus told Felix, parakalō akousai sou hēmōn syntomōs, “I urge you to hear us briefly” (24:4); syntomōs stands in two verses of the archived text, 24:4 and Mark 16:8, where it falls in the bracketed short ending that the archive prints. Paul asks Agrippa for a patient hearing. Luke does not say that either speaker had the other in mind. The English shelf agrees on the sense: NWT 1984 “hear me patiently,” ASV “hear me patiently,” KJV “hear me patiently,” Geneva “hear me patiently,” Douay “hear me patiently,” NIV “listen to me patiently” and TLB “listen patiently” each print “patiently,” seven of the seven. They split on the verb of asking: NWT 1984 and NIV “I beg you,” and ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “I beseech thee,” the older English for the same act, while TLB “Now please listen” makes it a polite imperative with no verb of begging. The speech that this patience is asked for runs from verse 4 to verse 23 of this chapter, and it is Festus, not Agrippa, who first breaks it off (26:24).

Verses 4–8 · βίωσιν … ἄνωθεν … ἀκριβεστάτην αἵρεσιν … θρησκείας … ἐπαγγελίας … δωδεκάφυλον … ἐκτενείᾳ … ἄπιστον biōsin … anōthen … akribestatēn hairesin … thrēskeias … epangelias … dōdekaphylon … ektenia … apiston — Was Paul a Pharisee? Acts 26:4–8: what is the “strictest party” of “our form of worship,” what is “the hope of the promise,” and why is it called “unbelievable” that God raises the dead?

“My manner of life … from youth … among my nation” (Acts 26:4). Tēn men oun biōsin mou tēn ek neotētos tēn ap’ archēs genomenēn en tō ethnei mou en te Hierosolymois isasi pantes Ioudaioi: the object stands first and the verb last, so the sentence arrives at “know” only after the life it is about. The noun biōsis (see biōsis) stands in one verse of the New Testament, here; the verb of the same stem is at 1 Peter 4:2 (not yet on these pages), and a search for the stem finds no other. A noun used once is a word the reader has nothing to compare with, and the sense is carried by the sentence: the way a life was lived, seen by others. The phrase ek neotētos, “from youth,” stands in three verses of the New Testament, and the other two are the rich man’s answer in Mark 10:20 and Luke 18:21, “All these I have kept from my youth.” There the phrase introduces what a man has kept; here it introduces what a whole people has seen. The apparatus has three small differences. The article before ek neotētos is printed on the authority of Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and Westcott–Hort and Tregelles omit it; the te in en te Hierosolymois, “and in Jerusalem,” is printed by Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and Robinson–Pierpont omits it; and pantes Ioudaioi is printed without an article by Westcott–Hort and Tregelles, while Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont add hoi, which is the form “all the Jews” most directly renders. None of the three changes what Paul claims. “My nation” is the singular ethnos that Paul used at 24:17 (“to bring alms to my nation”); the phrase ethnos mou in its three case forms stands in three verses, 24:17, here and 28:19 (not yet on these pages), and the plural ethnē, which this translation prints “nations,” comes back in this speech at 26:17, 26:20 and 26:23, so the one nation is named before the many are (see ethnos). The shelf: NWT 1984 “manner of life from youth up,” ASV “My manner of life then from my youth up” and KJV “My manner of life from my youth” keep the noun; Geneva “As touching my life from my childhood” and Douay “my life indeed from my youth” shorten it to “life”; NIV “the way I have lived” turns it into a verb. TLB “a thorough Jewish training” changes the subject from how he lived to how he was taught, and adds “in Tarsus,” a city this verse does not name; Tarsus and the schooling are in 22:3. For the nation, five of the seven say “nation”: ASV and KJV “mine own nation,” Geneva “mine own nation,” Douay “my own nation” and NWT 1984 “my nation.” NIV “my own country” names a land, where the Greek names a people.

“Having known me beforehand from the first” (Acts 26:5). Proginōskontes me anōthen, ean thelōsi martyrein: “having known me beforehand from the first, if they are willing to testify.” The verb proginōskō stands in five verses of the New Testament: here, Romans 8:29 (“those he knew beforehand”), Romans 11:2 (not yet on these pages), 1 Peter 1:20 (Christ “foreknown before the foundation of the world”) and 2 Peter 3:17 (not yet on these pages). In three of the five God is the one who knows beforehand (at 1 Peter 1:20 it is Christ who is the one known); here and at 2 Peter 3:17 it is people, and Paul’s witnesses know him beforehand only in the sense of before today. The noun of the same family, prognōsis, is Luke’s word for God’s “foreknowledge” at Acts 2:23. The second adverb carries more. Anōthen stands in thirteen verses of the New Testament and has three senses in them: “from the top” of the temple curtain (Mark 15:38, and Matthew 27:51 and John 19:23); “from above” (John 3:31, 19:11, and James 1:17; James 3:15 and 3:17 are not yet on these pages); and “from the start,” which is Luke’s sense in only two verses, the preface at Luke 1:3 (“having followed all things closely from the start”) and here. At John 3:3 and 3:7 the sense is disputed between “from above” and “again,” and “again” is also the sense reported for Galatians 4:9 (not yet on these pages), where the adverb follows palin; that dispute is reported here, not decided. This translation prints “from the start” at Luke 1:3 and “from the first” here, two English phrases for one Greek adverb in the same author. The shelf: NWT 1984 “previously acquainted with me from the first” and ASV “having knowledge of me from the first” keep “first”; KJV “Which knew me from the beginning” and Douay “Having known me from the beginning” say “beginning”; NIV “known me for a long time” keeps the duration and drops the starting point. Geneva “even from my Elders” has no word behind it in the printed Greek of this clause, and the source of that reading is reported here, not traced. TLB “If they would admit it” keeps the condition and drops the knowing. The condition itself, ean thelōsi, “if they are willing,” leaves the testimony in the hands of the people Paul names: they could give it, and the text does not say that they will.

“The strictest party of our form of worship” (Acts 26:5). Kata tēn akribestatēn hairesin tēs hēmeteras thrēskeias ezēsa Pharisaios. The superlative akribestatēn stands in one verse of the New Testament. Its family is Luke’s word for exactness, and in Acts it stands in seven verses: 18:25 and 18:26 (Apollos taught “accurately,” and Priscilla and Aquila set out the way “more accurately”), 22:3 (Paul “educated according to the strictness of the ancestral law”), 23:15 and 23:20 (“more exactly”), 24:22 (Felix “knowing more accurately” about the Way) and here. This translation prints “strictness” and “strictest” where the exactness is applied to keeping a law, and “accurately” or “exactly” where it is applied to knowing. The noun hairesis stands in nine verses of the New Testament (counted by reading the hits of a search for the stem, not by the raw string, which also turns up other words that contain the same letters). Here it is Paul’s own word for the Pharisees, as it is Luke’s at 15:5 and for the Sadducees at 5:17; at 24:14 Paul puts the word in other mouths (“the Way, which they call a party”), so he accepts it here for the Pharisees and attributes it to others there for the Way (see hairesis). Six of the seven shelf versions print “sect”: NWT 1984 “strictest sect,” NIV “strictest sect,” ASV “straitest sect,” KJV “most straitest sect” (a double superlative), Geneva “most straight sect” and Douay “most sure sect,” which, because that version translates the Latin, is the Latin’s choice for the Greek superlative (reported here, not checked on this page). TLB “the strictest of Pharisees” drops the noun. The next noun is thrēskeia, which with its adjective stands in four verses: here, Colossians 2:18 (not yet on these pages), and James 1:26 and 1:27, where this translation prints “religion.” Here it prints “form of worship,” and the reason is the English, not the Greek: “our religion” can sound like a whole faith, where thrēskeia names the observed practice (see thrēskeia). The site therefore has two English words for one Greek noun and one English word for two Greek nouns, because “religion” at 25:19 renders a different noun, Festus’s deisidaimonia, the fear of divinities, which can be praise or blame (see deisidaimonia). On the shelf NWT 1984 “form of worship” stands alone, and five versions say “religion”: ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “our religion” and NIV “our religion.” Last comes ezēsa Pharisaios, “I lived a Pharisee,” an aorist verb of living with a bare noun: the Greek does not say “I was.” Five of the seven keep the sentence as it is, NWT 1984, ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “I lived a Pharisee”; NIV “living as a Pharisee” adds “as.” The same man said 23:6, “I am a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees,” in the present, and in a letter, “as to law, a Pharisee,” with no verb at all (kata nomon Pharisaios, Philippians 3:5, not yet on these pages). Whether “I lived” means a life that is over or the whole span of a life that still has that shape, the verse does not say. For the party and its school, see the Pharisees and Gamaliel, at whose feet Paul says he was brought up.

“I stand being judged for the hope of the promise” (Acts 26:6). Kai nyn ep’ elpidi tēs eis tous pateras hēmōn epangelias genomenēs hypo tou theou hestēka krinomenos. Paul has already put the charge three ways. At 23:6 it was “concerning hope and resurrection of the dead I am being judged”; at 24:15 Paul spoke of “a hope in God, which these men themselves also accept”; at 24:21 it was “concerning resurrection of the dead I am being judged before you today.” Now it is “the hope of the promise,” and the question of verse 8 is about the dead. The text sets these side by side and does not say that one is the other. The verb is the same krinō as in 23:6 and 24:21, and hestēka krinomenos, “I stand being judged,” is the standing of 25:10, “I am standing at Caesar’s judgment seat, where I must be judged.” The phrase ep’ elpidi is “on the ground of a hope”; it stands in two verses of Acts, here and 2:26, where David’s flesh “will pitch its tent on hope.” The noun epangelia stands in eight verses of Acts: 1:4, 2:33, 2:39, 7:17, 13:23, 13:32, 23:21 and here. In seven of the eight it is God’s promise; at 23:21 it is the promise of a Roman tribune, to give his consent. The nearest parallel is 13:32, tēn pros tous pateras epangelian genomenēn, “the promise made to the fathers.” The apparatus bears on it twice. Here the printed text has eis before “the fathers” (979) and hēmōn, “our” (980); Robinson–Pierpont has pros and no “our,” so that its wording is the wording of 13:32 in the genitive. The printed text puts the promise to “our fathers,” and that is what the translation says. The shelf: NWT 1984 “I stand called to judgment,” ASV “I stand here to be judged,” KJV “I stand and am judged,” Douay “do I stand subject to judgment” and NIV “on trial today” all keep a judging; Geneva “I stand and am accused” uses the English of the verb Paul applies to the accusers in the next verse, enkaloumai. Douay “made by God to the fathers” has no “our.” NIV “what God has promised our ancestors” turns the noun into a verb.

“Our twelve tribes” (Acts 26:7). Eis hēn to dōdekaphylon hēmōn en ektenia nykta kai hēmeran latreuon elpizei katantēsai. Dōdekaphylon (see dōdekaphylon) is one word, a neuter singular compound, and it stands in one verse of the New Testament. The two-word phrase dōdeka phylai stands in four verses: Matthew 19:28 and Luke 22:30, where the twelve will sit on thrones judging the twelve tribes of Israel, James 1:1 (the letter’s address) and Revelation 21:12, the names on the gates. Here the same people is one body, and the grammar follows: “the twelve-tribe [people],” singular, “hopes,” singular. English prints the plural, “tribes … hope,” and the shelf does too: seven of the seven print “twelve tribes,” and TLB “twelve tribes of Israel” names Israel. Paul’s “our” puts him inside this body; he says in Philippians 3:5 (not yet on these pages) that he is of the tribe of Benjamin, and Luke does not say which of the twelve are meant here. The relative eis hēn is feminine singular, “to which,” and it can pick up either “the promise” or “the hope” of verse 6; the next clause then names “this hope” separately (peri hēs elpidos), which leans toward “promise.” The shelf chooses: ASV “unto which promise,” NWT 1984 “this promise” and NIV “This is the promise” say “promise”; TLB “this same hope” says “hope”; KJV “Unto which” and Geneva “Whereunto” leave the Greek’s own openness in place.

“Serving earnestly night and day … hope to attain” (Acts 26:7). En ektenia: the noun ektenia stands in one verse of the New Testament. Its adverb is the one in the church’s prayer for Peter, “prayer was being made earnestly” (12:5), and its comparative is Jesus’ praying “more earnestly” at Luke 22:44. The Greek has two words of doing, latreuon and elpizei, and the first has no object: nothing says “God” or “him.” Four shelf versions supply “God” (ASV “earnestly serving God night and day,” KJV and Geneva “instantly serving God day and night” and NIV “earnestly serve God”), NWT 1984 supplies “him” (“intensely rendering him sacred service night and day”), and Douay “serving night and day” and TLB “strive night and day” add nothing. The Greek order is “night and day,” and the shelf is divided on it: ASV, Douay, NWT 1984 and TLB keep “night and day” (four of the seven), while KJV, Geneva and NIV reverse it to “day and night.” The same participle and the same two words in the same order describe Anna, who “never left the temple, serving night and day with fastings and prayers” (Luke 2:37), and Revelation 7:15 has the verb with the order reversed (“serve him day and night in his temple”). The verb latreuō stands in five verses of Acts: 7:7, 7:42, 24:14, here and 27:23; see latreuō. The sentence ends in katantēsai, “to attain,” and that verb is a verb of arriving: in Acts it stands in nine verses, and in the other eight it is a journey’s end, at Derbe (16:1), Ephesus (18:19, 18:24), off Chios (20:15), Ptolemais (21:7), Caesarea (25:13). The last two stand at 27:12 and 28:13 (the second not yet on these pages). Here a hope is the thing arrived at. At 25:13 the same verb was used of the king now listening: “Agrippa the king and Berenice arrived at Caesarea.” Luke draws no link. The shelf for the verb: ASV “hope to attain”; NWT 1984 “hoping to attain to the fulfillment of this promise” and NIV “hoping to see fulfilled” add a fulfilling that the verb of arriving does not state; KJV, Geneva and Douay “hope to come” stay with the motion.

“I am accused by Jews, O king” (Acts 26:7). Peri hēs elpidos enkaloumai hypo Ioudaiōn, basileu. The verb enkaleō stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Acts 19:38, 19:40, 23:28, 23:29, 26:2, here and Romans 8:33. It is the verb of 26:2, “all the things of which I am accused by Jews,” and verse 7 says what the accusation is about: the hope. In both verses the Greek has hypo Ioudaiōn without an article, “by Jews,” where verse 4 said “all the Jews”; the verse does not say whether the Jews who accuse him are the Jews who know his life, and he is a Jew himself. NWT 1984 “by Jews” keeps the bare noun; ASV “by the Jews,” KJV “of the Jews,” Geneva “of the Jews” and Douay “by the Jews” add “the”; NIV “these Jews” makes them the ones present. The apparatus (981) has a substantive difference as well as an order: the printed text reads hypo Ioudaiōn, basileu, and Robinson–Pierpont reads basileu Agrippa hypo Ioudaiōn, which moves the vocative to the front and adds the name. Three of the seven put the name in this verse: KJV “king Agrippa,” Geneva “O King Agrippa” and NIV “King Agrippa.” NWT 1984, ASV and Douay print “O king,” as the translation does, and TLB “O King” likewise has no name. In the printed Greek the name is spoken at 26:2, 26:19 and 26:27 and not here.

“Why is it judged unbelievable among you if God raises the dead?” (Acts 26:8). Ti apiston krinetai par’ hymin ei ho theos nekrous egeirei. Four things in the verse can be checked. First, the pronoun: par’ hymin is plural, and the speech to the king has turned to the room, the hearing of 25:23. NWT 1984 prints it “among YOU men,” with capitals (its way of marking a plural “you,” reported here, not checked on this page) and the addition “men”; the other six print a “you” that English leaves ambiguous. Second, the adjective apiston: in Acts the adjective apistos stands only here. It is usually said of persons, as of a “generation” in Matthew 17:17 and of Thomas in John 20:27; here it is said of a thing. Six shelf versions print “incredible”: ASV “judged incredible,” KJV, Geneva and Douay “a thing incredible,” NIV “consider it incredible” and TLB “Does it seem incredible”; NWT 1984 “judged unbelievable” is the one that uses the word this translation prints, because in current English “incredible” can also mean “wonderful.” The verb krinetai is the passive of the krinō of verse 6, so Paul stands being judged (krinomenos) over a thing that is itself judged (krinetai) unbelievable. Third, the small word ei: it means “if,” and six of the seven shelf versions say “that.” Only ASV “if God doth raise the dead” keeps the conditional; NWT 1984 “that God raises up the dead” and NIV “that God raises the dead” use the plain present, and KJV, Geneva and Douay “that God should raise” add a “should” that the plain present indicative of the Greek verb does not have (TLB “that God can bring” has a “can” instead). English “that” and Greek ei after a verb of judging are often matched in the grammars (reported here, not checked on this page); this translation prints “if,” which leaves the question of whether God does it where Paul puts it. The same word opens two clauses at 26:23, ei pathētos ho christos, ei prōtos ex anastaseōs nekrōn. Fourth, the verb: egeirei is a present, “raises,” and where Luke speaks of the raising of Jesus the verb is the aorist, “God raised him,” at 3:15, 5:30, 10:40, 13:30 and 13:37. The present with “the dead” as its object is also John 5:21, “the Father raises the dead and gives them life” (John 5:21); Hebrews 11:19 has the infinitive. The object here, nekrous, has no article, the same bare plural as in “resurrection of the dead” at 17:32, 23:6 and 24:21: no article in the Greek, “dead people” as a class. The text gives no answer to Paul’s question; the next verse begins with what he himself once thought.

Verses 9–11 · ἔδοξα ἐμαυτῷ … ἐναντία … Ναζωραίου … ἁγίων … κατέκλεισα … ψῆφον … τιμωρῶν … ἠνάγκαζον βλασφημεῖν … ἐμμαινόμενος edoxa emautō … enantia … Nazōraiou … hagiōn … katekleisa … psēphon … timōrōn … ēnankazon blasphēmein … emmainomenos — Acts 26:9–11: what did Paul mean by “I cast my vote” against the holy ones, and did he really force people to blaspheme?

“I thought to myself” (Acts 26:9). Egō men oun edoxa emautō … dein: “I, then, thought to myself that I ought.” The verb is the aorist of dokeō, “to seem, to think,” with a reflexive dative, so the decision is placed inside Paul’s own reckoning before any outside authority enters (the chief priests come in only at verse 10). The same aorist is the verb of decisions that “seemed good” to a group: Luke 1:3 (“it seemed good to me too”), Acts 15:22 (“it seemed good to the apostles and the elders”) and 15:28 (“it seemed good to the Holy Spirit and to us”). Here it is one man, “to myself,” and the thing that seemed right is the opposite of the verdict of Jerusalem in chapter 15. Whether the opening men oun turns from the “unbelievable” of 26:8 to Paul’s own earlier disbelief is a reading; the Greek only says “I, on my part, then.” On the shelf, four versions keep the reflexive: ASV “thought with myself,” KJV “thought with myself,” Geneva “thought in myself” and NWT 1984 “thought within myself.” Douay “did formerly think” drops it and adds “formerly,” NIV “I too was convinced” turns the verb of seeming into one of conviction, and TLB “I used to believe” does the same.

“Many things in opposition to the name” (Acts 26:9). Polla enantia prāxai is “to do many things opposed,” with enantia the neuter plural of the adjective “opposite, contrary.” The same adjective returns in a negative at 28:17 (not yet on these pages), where Paul says he did ouden enantion against the people or the customs of the fathers. The shelf is close: NWT 1984 “many acts of opposition” is the most noun-like; ASV “things contrary to the name” and Geneva “many contrary things against the Name” follow the Greek; NIV “all that was possible to oppose” changes “many” to a limit, and TLB “many horrible things to the followers of Jesus” supplies an adjective the Greek does not have and swaps “the name” for “the followers.” The Greek aims the opposition at to onoma, “the name,” the thing that Ananias’s fear named at 9:14 (“all who call on your name”) and that Peter used at 3:6 and 4:10 (“in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene”).

“Jesus the Nazarene” (Acts 26:9). The title is Nazōraios (see the dictionary entry). The word Nazōraios stands in thirteen verses of the New Testament, and this verse is the seventh of Acts: 2:22, 3:6, 4:10, 6:14, 22:8, 24:5 and 26:9. At 22:8 Paul quotes Jesus as giving the title to himself (“I am Jesus the Nazarene, whom you are persecuting”); here Paul says it in his own voice, of the name he opposed. In this chapter’s own account of the voice, at 26:15, the Greek has Egō eimi Iēsous hon su diōkeis, “I am Jesus whom you are persecuting,” with no title, as at 9:5. Luke does not say why one telling carries it and two do not. Six of the seven shelf versions print the town where the Greek has the title: ASV “Jesus of Nazareth,” KJV “Jesus of Nazareth,” Geneva “Jesus of Nazareth,” Douay “Jesus of Nazareth,” NIV “Jesus of Nazareth” and TLB “Jesus of Nazareth.” Only NWT 1984 keeps the title as a title, in its syllable-dotted spelling of the name.

“The holy ones” (Acts 26:10). Pollous te tōn hagiōn, “many of the holy ones.” As a name for the believers, hagioi stands in four verses of Acts, hagioi at 9:13, 9:32, 9:41 and here, a count reached by reading the hits (the fifth Acts hit, 3:21, is the “holy prophets”). The first of them, in Ananias’s mouth, is “your holy ones in Jerusalem”; Paul now says what he did to them, in the same city (hagios). On the shelf, five of the seven print “saints”: ASV “saints,” KJV “saints,” Geneva “Saints,” Douay “saints” and TLB “saints.” NWT 1984 “holy ones” is the other family, and NIV “the Lord’s people” is a paraphrase that this version does not use for the same Greek at 9:13. “Saint” reached English through Latin, and in modern English it often means a specially honored person; the Greek here is the ordinary plural of “holy.”

“Shut up in prisons,” “the authority from the chief priests” (Acts 26:10). Katekleisa is the aorist of katakleiō, “to shut up, to confine,” and the verb stands in two verses of the New Testament, katakleiō here and Luke 3:20, where Herod “shut John up in prison.” The other tellings of Paul’s own work use other verbs: “dragging off men and women, he would hand them over to prison” (8:3) and “binding and handing over to prisons both men and women” (22:4). The source of the authority is told three ways. At 9:1–2 Saul goes to the high priest and asks for letters; at 22:5 the high priest “and the whole body of elders” bear witness and he “received letters” (dexamenos); here the participle is labōn, “having received,” the thing received is exousia, “authority” (exousia), and the givers are the plural “chief priests,” as at 26:12 (“authority and commission”) and 9:14 (“authority from the chief priests”). The book does not harmonize the singular and the plural or the letters and the authority, and this note does not either. Two witnesses of the shelf are close: ASV and KJV “having received authority from the chief priests.” NIV “On the authority of the chief priests” turns a thing received into a basis, and TLB “as authorized by the High Priests” makes the noun a verb.

“I cast my vote” (Acts 26:10). Anairoumenōn te autōn katēnegka psēphon: “and when they were being put to death I brought down a pebble.” Psēphos is a small smooth stone, used for counting, for calculating and for casting a vote (the dictionary entry psēphos gives the uses around the white stone of Revelation 2:17). The stem psēph- stands in six verses of the New Testament: the verbs at Luke 14:28 (“calculate the cost”), Acts 1:26 (Matthias “counted in” with the eleven), 19:19 (they “reckoned up” the prices of the books) and Revelation 13:18 (“calculate the number”), and the noun here and at Revelation 2:17. Of those, this verse is the one where the pebble is a verdict, and it is the only use of the noun in Acts. The verb that carries it, katapherō, “to bring down,” stands in three verses, all in Acts: 20:9 (Eutychus “borne down by deep sleep”), 25:7 (the Jews “bringing many heavy accusations”) and here, with psēphon. Two readings are in print and the Greek does not settle them. One is a literal vote: Paul, as a member of a council such as the Sanhedrin, voted for the death sentences, which would make him a voting member of a body that Luke does not say he sat on. The other is an idiom of assent: he gave approval to the killings, as at 8:1 (“Saul was approving of his killing,” with the noun anairesis from the same root as the participle here) and 22:20 (“standing by and approving”). Luke narrates one death with Saul present, that of Stephen (7:58), and says of the persecution “to the death” (22:4); how many died is not stated. The shelf prints a vote four times: NWT 1984 “I cast my vote against them,” NIV “I cast my vote,” TLB “I cast my vote” and ASV “I gave my vote against them.” KJV “I gave my voice against them” is the older sense of “voice” as a vote, and Geneva “I gave my sentence” and Douay “I brought the sentence” name a judge’s act. For the death itself, NWT 1984 “when they were to be executed” reads the present participle as something still ahead, KJV “when they were put to death” as something done, and TLB “when they were condemned to death” as a sentence, which is a different event from a death.

“Punishing … I tried to force them to blaspheme” (Acts 26:11). Kata pasas tas synagōgas pollakis timōrōn autous ēnankazon blasphēmein: “in all the synagogues, often punishing them, I was forcing them to blaspheme.” The punishing verb timōreō and its noun stand in three verses: 22:5 (“so that they might be punished”), here, and Hebrews 10:29 (not yet on these pages). In the telling at 22:19 the Greek is kata tas synagōgas, printed “in one synagogue after another,” with no “all”; here it is “in all the synagogues.” The verb anankazō, “to compel,” stands in three verses of the New Testament, here and at Galatians 2:14 and 6:12 (not yet on these pages). Its form here is the imperfect, which can be a repeated action or an attempt, and the Greek does not say that anyone complied. The shelf divides on exactly that. Four print the attempt: NWT 1984 “tried to force them to make a recantation,” NIV “I tried to force them to blaspheme,” ASV “I strove to make them blaspheme” and TLB “to try to make Christians everywhere curse Christ.” Three print a result: KJV “compelled them to blaspheme,” Geneva “compelled them to blaspheme” and Douay “I compelled them to blaspheme.” Two of these supply what the Greek leaves out: TLB adds “torture” (“I used torture”) for the participle “punishing,” “Christians” for the pronoun “them” and “Christ” as the object of the cursing; and the verb in NWT 1984 is a meaning (the recanting of a faith) where the Greek has blasphēmein, a choice its 2013 revision keeps as “recant.” Within Acts, the stem blasphēm- stands in five verses: 6:11 (“blasphemous words against Moses and God”), 13:45, 18:6, 19:37 (“neither temple-robbers nor blasphemers of our goddess”) and here. The verb means speaking injuriously, and in this verse it has no stated object, so the Greek does not say whose name or what words Paul wanted from them. Paul uses the adjective of himself at 1 Timothy 1:13, “formerly I was a blasphemer and a persecutor and a violent man”; the two statements are about different people doing the speaking, and neither explains the other.

“Exceedingly enraged … the cities outside” (Acts 26:11). Perissōs te emmainomenos autois ediōkon heōs kai eis tas exō poleis: “and being exceedingly enraged at them, I kept persecuting them even to the cities outside.” The participle emmainomenos stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so its range of meaning rests on this one use; its root is mainomai, the one in Rhoda’s “You are out of your mind” (12:15) and, later in this chapter, Festus’s Mainē, Paule (26:24) and Paul’s answer Ou mainomai, “I am not out of my mind” (26:25). The text draws no line between them, and the shared root is the reader’s to weigh. The shelf has “mad” in four: ASV “exceedingly mad against them,” KJV “exceedingly mad against them,” NWT 1984 “extremely mad against them” and Douay “yet more mad against them,” with Geneva “more mad against them” a fifth. NIV “so obsessed with persecuting them” and TLB “so violently opposed to them” describe a stance where the Greek has a state of fury. The persecuting verb is diōkō, the house rendering “persecuting”; it recurs as the voice’s question at 26:14 and 26:15, and the same stretch of years is described in Paul’s own letter as “beyond all measure I was persecuting the congregation of God and trying to destroy it” (Galatians 1:13). The phrase eis tas exō poleis is “to the cities outside,” and the Greek does not say outside of what. NWT 1984 “outside cities” is the plain rendering; ASV, Douay and NIV “foreign cities” and KJV and Geneva “strange cities” read “outside” as “foreign,” and TLB “distant cities in foreign lands” adds distance. The one such city this book names is Damascus, where he went with letters to the synagogues (9:2, 22:5), and his next sentence, 26:12, takes that road. Damascus is in its own entry.

The apparatus (Acts 26:9–11). The archive prints no note on verse 9 or on verse 11. Verse 10 has two, both small: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print the particle te after pollous and the preposition en before phylakais, and Robinson–Pierpont omits each. Neither omission changes what an English reader takes the verse to say.

Verses 12–15 · ἐπιτροπῆς … οὐρανόθεν … λαμπρότητα … τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ … σκληρόν σοι πρὸς κέντρα λακτίζειν … Τίς εἶ, κύριε; epitropēs … ouranothen … lamprotēta … tē Hebraidi dialektō … sklēron soi pros kentra laktizein … Tis ei, kyrie? — Acts 26:12–15: what does “kick against the goads” mean, why do the three tellings of the Damascus road differ, and what is Paul’s “commission” from the chief priests?

“Authority and commission” (Acts 26:12). The verse opens En hois, “in which [things],” a relative that points back at the persecuting of verses 9–11: the road to Damascus is told as the next thing that happened in that campaign, not as a new story. The apparatus marks it; Robinson–Pierpont has en hois kai, “in which also.” Paul travels met’ exousias kai epitropēs. The noun epitropē stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Its relative epitropos, a person, stands in three verses: the “steward” to whom the vineyard owner says “Call the workers” in Matthew 20:8 and Herod’s “steward,” whose wife is Joanna, in Luke 8:3, with a third in Galatians 4:2 (not yet on these pages). Reference works are reported to take epitropē as the thing entrusted, a charge or a commission; that is reported here and not examined on this page. The first noun is the library’s exousia, the right to act. The source of both is the chief priests. At 9:14 Ananias tells the Lord that Saul “has authority from the chief priests to bind” those who call on his name; at 22:5 the high priest and “the whole body of elders” are Paul’s witnesses and the source of the letters; and Paul has just said, at 26:10, that he locked up many of the holy ones “having received the authority from the chief priests,” tēn para tōn archiereōn exousian. The apparatus at verse 12 adds para in Robinson–Pierpont, so that their text there reads tēs para tōn archiereōn, the same preposition as 26:10; the printed text has the bare genitive tēs tōn archiereōn. The printed text has the plural archiereōn, “chief priests,” here and at 26:10, while 22:5 has the singular ho archiereus, “the high priest,” with the body of elders; the library has an entry on the high priest, and the Greek does not say whether the plural and the singular describe one body of authorizing men or two. On the shelf, NWT 1984 “authority and a commission from the chief priests,” ASV “authority and commission of the chief priests,” KJV “authority and commission from the chief priests,” NIV “authority and commission of the chief priests” and TLB “armed with the authority and commission of the chief priests” are five of the seven that print “commission.” Geneva “authority, and commission from the high Priests” makes six, with the plural kept but “high Priests” for archiereis. The seventh is Douay “authority and permission of the chief priest,” which prints “permission” and the singular “priest” where the Greek has the plural; the Douay is made from the Latin Vulgate, and what the Latin has at this verse was not checked on this page. The same singular-or-plural split among the shelf versions comes up for the chief priests at 25:2. Neither here nor at 9:1–2 does the Greek say what legal force a letter from Jerusalem had in Damascus, and this page does not supply one: see Damascus.

“At midday” (Acts 26:13). Hēmeras mesēs is “of the middle of the day.” The form mesēs stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Matthew 25:6, where it is “of the middle of the night” (mesēs nyktos) and the cry goes up for the bridegroom. At 22:6 the hour is peri mesēmbrian, and mesēmbria stands in two verses, 22:6 and 8:26, where it is the quarter of the sky, “toward the south”; Acts 9:3 names no hour. Of the shelf, NWT 1984 “at midday,” ASV “at midday,” KJV “At midday,” Geneva “At midday” and Douay “At midday” are five of the seven that keep the day split in two; NIV “About noon” and TLB “about noon” print a clock hour with an “about” that the Greek of 26:13 does not have (peri, “about,” belongs to the phrase at 22:6, not to this one).

“A light from heaven, beyond the brightness of the sun” (Acts 26:13). Three words in this clause stand alone or nearly so. Ouranothen, “from heaven” with the suffix of source, stands in two verses: here and 14:17, where the rains and fruitful seasons come “from heaven,” as the Acts 14 note says. The noun lamprotēs, “brightness,” stands in one verse, as the Acts 22 note already says of it; the first telling used periēstrapsen (9:3), the second periastrapsai and a light that was hikanon, “considerable” (22:6), and that verb stands in two verses, periastraptō at 9:3 and 22:6. Here the verb is different: perilampsan, the participle of perilampō, which stands in two verses, here and Luke 2:9, where the glory of the Lord “shone around” the shepherds. The Greek is hyper tēn lamprotēta tou hēliou, “above/beyond the brightness of the sun”: hyper with the accusative is a comparison, and the light outshone the sun at its height. The light is the subject of the participle, and it has two objects, both accusative: me, “me,” and tous syn emoi poreuomenous, “those traveling with me.” The shelf is nearly even on the comparison: ASV “above the brightness of the sun,” KJV “above the brightness of the sun” and Douay “above the brightness of the sun” keep the preposition and the noun; Geneva “passing the brightness of the sun” uses an older participle for the same sense; NWT 1984 “beyond the brilliance of the sun” changes the noun; NIV and TLB “brighter than the sun” turn the noun into a comparative adjective. The shipped English prints “beyond the brightness of the sun,” and lamprotēs is not the word the first telling uses for the light’s effect: that was the doxa, “glory,” of 22:11.

“Those traveling with me,” “all of us fallen” (Acts 26:13–14). This is the third time the road is told, and the three tellings do not say the same things about the companions. This page sets them side by side and does not merge them, as the Acts 9 note also declines to. Acts 9:3–7 (Luke as narrator): the light flashes around Saul; Saul alone is said to fall (pesōn); “the men who were traveling with him” (hoi andres hoi synodeuontes autō; the verb synodeuō stands in one verse of the New Testament) “stood speechless,” akouontes men tēs phōnēs mēdena de theōrountes, “hearing the voice but seeing no one.” Acts 22:6–9 (Paul to the crowd in the Hebrew dialect): the light is around Paul, he falls (epesa), and “those who were with me” (hoi syn emoi ontes) “beheld the light but did not hear the voice of the one speaking to me,” with no word on whether they stand or fall. Acts 26:13–14 (Paul to Agrippa): the light shines around “me and those traveling with me,” and the genitive absolute pantōn katapesontōn hēmōn eis tēn gēn says that “all of us” fell; Paul says ēkousa phōnēn, “I heard a voice,” and says nothing about whether the companions heard it or did not. The verb katapiptō stands in three verses: Luke 8:6, where a seed “fell” on the rock, here, and 28:6 (not yet on these pages). The word the first two tellings use for Saul’s fall is the simple piptō (pesōn, epesa), and the preposition-prefixed form here is the one that is said of all of them. The tellings differ in who stands, who falls, what is heard and what is seen; they agree that there was a light, a voice, and a fall of Paul himself. How the three are to be fitted together is a question that readers and commentators have answered in different ways, and it is reported here, not decided on this page; the third telling, in front of a king, has no Ananias and no word for “blind” anywhere in the chapter, so the blindness of 9:8 is not told here at all.

“In the Hebrew dialect” and “Saul, Saul” (Acts 26:14). The voice speaks tē Hebraidi dialektō. Dialektos stands in six verses, all in Acts: 1:19, 2:6, 2:8, 21:40, 22:2 and here; the dative Hebraidi stands in three verses, 21:40, 22:2 and here. Only this telling names the voice’s language: at 9:4 the voice says “Saul, Saul” and no language is given. The Acts 22 note puts the question of Hebrew versus the Aramaic of Judea and Galilee, reports it as argued, and does not decide it; the same stands here. The shelf splits the way it did at 22:2: NWT 1984 “in the Hebrew language,” ASV “in the Hebrew language,” KJV “in the Hebrew tongue,” Geneva “in the Hebrew tongue,” Douay “in the Hebrew tongue” and TLB “in Hebrew” are six of the seven that print Hebrew; NIV “in Aramaic” is the one that prints the other reading. The name is Saoul Saoul, the Hebraic spelling, as against the Greek Saulos of the narrator. The doubled form stands in three verses of the New Testament, 9:4, 22:7 and here (Saoul Saoul), and the spelling Saoul in six verses: those three, and 9:17 and 22:13 (Ananias speaking) and 13:21 (the king). TLB “Paul, Paul” replaces the spelling the voice uses with the name of the man by which the book has called him since 13:9. The apparatus has two marked variants in the speaking: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print phōnēn legousan pros me, “a voice saying to me,” while Robinson–Pierpont has lalousan pros me kai legousan, “speaking to me and saying.” Two shelf versions have the longer wording: KJV “speaking unto me, and saying in the Hebrew tongue” and Geneva “speaking unto me, and saying in the Hebrew tongue,” the same as one another and the same pair of participles as the Robinson–Pierpont text; the others print one verb. (The particle after pantōn, te in the printed text, is de in Robinson–Pierpont, a difference of connective only.)

“Hard for you to kick against the goads” (Acts 26:14). Sklēron soi pros kentra laktizein. The adjective sklēron, in this neuter form, stands in one verse of the New Testament, and laktizō, “to kick,” in one verse, this one. The noun kentron stands in four verses: here, 1 Corinthians 15:55 and 15:56 (not yet on these pages), where it is a property of death, and Revelation 9:10, where the locusts have tails like scorpions’ “and stingers,” kentra. Lexicons are reported to give the sense as a pointed stick for driving oxen and also as a sting; that is reported, not checked on this page. The saying is a proverb with a long history in Greek, and three places where it can be read were fetched for this page from the Perseus texts. In Euripides, Bacchae 794–795, the speaker marked in that text is Dionysus: thyoim’ an autō mallon ē thymoumenos pros kentra laktizoimi thnētos ōn theō, “I would sooner sacrifice to him than, in a rage, kick against the goads, a mortal against a god” (the gloss is this page’s). In Aeschylus, Agamemnon 1624, Aegisthus says to the chorus pros kentra mē laktize, mē paisas mogēs, “do not kick against the goads, lest you strike and suffer.” In Pindar, Pythian 2.94–95, the text has poti kentron de toi laktizemen, with the same verb and noun in the poet’s own dialect forms. So the phrase is a Greek proverb about a beast that hurts only itself by resisting the driver, and nothing in the Greek of Acts says that Luke or Paul took it from any of these. What the text does leave open is where the proverb belongs: the voice speaks tē Hebraidi dialektō, and the sentence is given in Greek. Whether the proverb is part of what Paul heard, or whether Paul, speaking to a king, puts the voice’s meaning into a Greek saying, is a question the verse does not answer, and it is reported here, not decided. It stands in this telling only (9:5 and 22:8 do not have it), and the Acts 9 note records that the KJV, Geneva and Douay carry it into chapter 9 and that reference works are reported to say later manuscripts did the same. The shelf sorts three ways by the noun alone. ASV “it is hard for thee to kick against the goad” and Douay “kick against the goad” print the singular, though the Greek kentra is plural. NWT 1984 “To keep kicking against the goads makes it hard for you” and NIV “kick against the goads” print the plural, and the NWT reverses the clause and adds “keep,” which turns the infinitive into a continuing action that the Greek leaves implicit. KJV “kick against the pricks” and Geneva “kick against pricks” print “pricks,” the older English for the pointed stick. That is six of the seven; the seventh is TLB “You are only hurting yourself,” which drops the proverb and its image and keeps only what it means.

“Who are you, Lord?” (Acts 26:15). Tis ei, kyrie? stands in three verses of the New Testament (tis ei, kyrie), the three tellings: 9:5, 22:8 and here. The answer is Egō eimi Iēsous hon sy diōkeis, “I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting,” and in this telling it has no “the Nazarene”: that title comes only in the second, Iēsous ho Nazōraios (22:8), as the Acts 9 note says; see the library entry nazoraios. The verb diōkō stands in nine verses of Acts: 7:52, 9:4, 9:5, 22:4, 22:7, 22:8, 26:11, 26:14 and 26:15. At 26:14 and 26:15 it is the voice’s word, in the present tense, “you are persecuting”; Paul’s own word for what he did, three verses earlier, is the imperfect ediōkon of 26:11. The question is the one word kyrie: it can address a man (“sir”) or God, and Paul does not say what he took it to be. TLB “Who are you, sir?” picks the first; the other six print “Lord,” and the answer that follows is the one that turns the vocative into a title. The library entry kyrios sets out the range of the word. In this chapter kyrios is the risen Jesus, as the sentence itself says, and nothing is read as Jehovah. The second kyrios of the verse, in ho de kyrios eipen, “and the Lord said,” is marked in the apparatus: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print it, and Robinson–Pierpont omits it, so that the sentence there reads ho de eipen, “and he said.” (At 9:5 the marked difference runs the other way: the archive prints only ho de, and Robinson–Pierpont adds kyrios eipen.) Two shelf versions follow the shorter text here: KJV “And he said, I am Jesus” and Geneva “And he said, I am Jesus” read he, and the other five name the Lord, ASV “the Lord said,” NWT 1984 “the Lord said,” Douay “the Lord answered,” NIV “the Lord replied” and TLB “the Lord replied.” The Lord’s speech is not finished at the end of the verse: the Greek has a raised point after diōkeis, and the words go on into verse 16.

Verses 16–18 · ἀνάστηθι καὶ στῆθι … ὤφθην … προχειρίσασθαι … ὑπηρέτην καὶ μάρτυρα … ἐξαιρούμενος … ἀπὸ σκότους εἰς φῶς … κλῆρον ἐν τοῖς ἡγιασμένοις anastēthi kai stēthi … ōphthēn … procheirisasthai … hypēretēn kai martyra … exairoumenos … apo skotous eis phōs … klēron en tois hēgiasmenois — Acts 26:16–18: what is Paul’s commission on the Damascus road, what makes him “a servant and a witness,” and what is the “lot among those who have been sanctified”?

“Rise and stand on your feet” (Acts 26:16). Alla anastēthi kai stēthi epi tous podas sou: “but rise and stand on your feet.” In the first telling the voice says only anastēthi, with a destination: “rise and enter into the city” (9:6). In the second it is Anastas poreuou eis Damaskon, “Rise, go on into Damascus” (22:10). Here no city comes into the three verses, and a second imperative stands where the destination was. The pair anastēthi kai stēthi stands in one verse of the New Testament. The phrase epi tous podas, “on (or at) the feet,” stands in three verses of Acts, as the Acts 14 note counts: 10:25, where Cornelius falls at Peter’s feet; 14:10, where Paul tells the man at Lystra Anastēthi epi tous podas sou orthos, “Stand up on your feet, upright!”; and this verse. Paul’s words at Lystra are the Lord’s words here with orthos, “upright,” in the place of stēthi, and Luke draws no line between the two. The first command answers the fall of 26:14 (katapesontōn hēmōn, “we having fallen”). It also has a Hebrew counterpart: amod al-raglekha, “stand upon your feet,” as a phrase stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Ezekiel 2:1 (not yet on these pages), where the next verse has the spirit set the prophet “upon my feet” and the verse after it has shole’ach ani otkha, “I am sending you,” to Israel, to “rebellious nations” (goyim). Stand, send and nations are therefore three verses in a row there and three clauses in a row here. Acts gives no citation formula at 26:16–18, unlike 13:47 (“For so Jehovah has commanded us”), so whether Ezekiel’s call is meant to be heard is left to the reader. The shelf keeps two verbs in six of its seven versions: NWT 1984 “Nevertheless, rise and stand on your feet,” ASV “But arise, and stand upon thy feet,” KJV “But rise, and stand upon thy feet,” Geneva “But rise and stand up on thy feet,” Douay “But rise up, and stand upon thy feet” and NIV “Now get up and stand on your feet.” TLB “Now stand up!” has one.

“For to this end I have appeared to you” (Acts 26:16). Eis touto gar ōphthēn soi. The first-person aorist ōphthēn, from horaō, stands in one verse of the New Testament, and the future ophthēsomai, “I will appear,” later in the same verse, in one verse as well. The third-person forms are the usual ones: the God of glory “appeared” to Abraham (ōphthē at 7:2), the risen Jesus “was seen” for many days (13:31), and “a vision appeared to Paul during the night” (16:9). Ananias had said it of Jesus in Damascus, Iēsous ho ophtheis soi, “Jesus, who appeared to you on the road” (9:17), and this verse is the speaker saying it of himself. The reason for the appearing is given in the next words, and it is a purpose, not a rebuke. Six of the seven shelf versions print “appeared”: ASV “for to this end have I appeared unto thee,” Douay “for to this end have I appeared to thee,” KJV “for I have appeared unto thee for this purpose,” Geneva “for I have appeared unto thee for this purpose,” NIV “I have appeared to you” and TLB “For I have appeared to you.” NWT 1984 “I have made myself visible to you” spells out the passive as something the speaker did. The second half of the clause promises more: hōn te eides me hōn te ophthēsomai soi, “both of the things you have seen of me and of the things in which I will appear to you.” Both relatives are in the genitive, the case “witness” governs, as in hōn heōrakas kai ēkousas, “of what you have seen and heard” (22:15). The book then records later occasions, though not in the verb of this verse: the Lord speaks “in the night through a vision” (18:9), Paul “saw him saying to me” in the temple (22:18), and “the Lord stood by him” (23:11). At 27:23 the Greek has an angel of God who “stood by” him in the night, with parestē, not ōphthē. The apparatus marks the little word me after eides: Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 print it, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont omit it. With it the clause reads “the things in which you saw me” or “the things you saw of me”; without it the first clause names no one, and the speaker enters only in “I have appeared.” Three versions carry a “me”: ASV “both of the things wherein thou hast seen me,” NWT 1984 “things I shall make you see respecting me” (which also turns the future passive into something the speaker makes happen) and NIV “what you have seen and will see of me.” KJV “both of these things which thou hast seen,” Geneva “both of the things which thou hast seen” and Douay “of those things which thou hast seen” have none, and TLB turns the clause into “tell the world about this experience.”

“To appoint you a servant and a witness” (Acts 26:16). Procheirisasthai se hypēretēn kai martyra. The verb is the aorist middle infinitive of procheirizō (dictionary), which stands in three verses of the New Testament in three spellings: 3:20, 22:14 and this verse. At 22:14 it is Ananias’s “appointed you beforehand to know his will”; the middle says the appointer chose for himself. This page prints “appoint” here and “appointed you beforehand” there, and the prefix pro- is the one detail of the Greek that the English of 26:16 does not carry. The shelf is split three ways: ASV “to appoint thee a minister and a witness,” Geneva “to appoint thee a minister and a witness,” NIV “to appoint you as a servant and as a witness” and TLB “to appoint you as my servant and my witness” make four of the seven “appoint”; KJV “to make thee a minister and a witness” and Douay “that I may make thee a minister, and a witness” make two “make”; NWT 1984 “to choose you as an attendant and a witness” stands alone with “choose,” the word it also has at 22:14. The noun hypēretēs (dictionary) is literally an “under-rower,” one who works under another’s direction, and this site prints it by setting: “attendant” for the synagogue officer of Luke 4:20 and for John Mark at 13:5, “officers” for those sent to the prison at 5:22 and 5:26, and “servants” for those who “became servants of the word” in Luke 1:2. Here it prints “servant,” which puts Paul with the Luke 1:2 company, where autoptai kai hypēretai, “eyewitnesses and servants,” come in one breath, and hypēretas Christou, “servants of Christ,” is how Paul asks that “us” be reckoned at 1 Corinthians 4:1 (not yet on these pages). The shelf prints “minister” in four versions (ASV, KJV, Geneva, Douay), “servant” in two (NIV, TLB) and “attendant” in one (NWT 1984, the word this site uses at 13:5). The second noun is martys (dictionary), the word of 1:8 (“you will be my witnesses”) and 1:22 (“a witness of his resurrection”). It is not autoptēs, “eyewitness”: a witness is one who gives testimony, and this verse defines the testimony by what Paul has seen and is still to see. Stephen is called “your witness” (martyros sou) in 22:20, and the commission of 22:15 says “seen and heard,” where 26:16 says only “seen” and the hearing is in the voice of 26:14.

“Rescuing you from the people and from the nations” (Acts 26:17). Exairoumenos se ek tou laou kai ek tōn ethnōn, eis hous egō apostellō se. The participle is present, from exaireō, which stands in five verses of Acts: 7:10 (God rescues Joseph), 7:34 (“I came down to rescue them”), 12:11 (Peter: Jehovah “rescued me out of the hand of Herod”), 23:27 (the tribune: “I rescued” this man) and this verse; the Acts 23 note observes that four are the act of God or the Lord. Galatians 1:4 has it again in the middle: Christ gave himself “to rescue us out of the present evil age.” In Jeremiah 1:7–8 the call of a prophet puts the same two ideas in neighboring verses, “to everyone I send you” (Hebrew eshlachekha) and “I am with you to deliver you” (lehatsilekha); whether 26:17 recalls that call the verse does not say. Here ho laos, “the people,” is Israel, as it is in “the people of the Jews” (12:11), “this people Israel” (13:17), and the charge at 21:28 that Paul “teaches everyone everywhere against the people and the law.” Being rescued “from” them is the language of the danger the next verses go on to describe (26:21). The nations are ethnē (dictionary), the house rendering, and the relative that follows is masculine plural, hous, though ethnē is neuter, so it agrees with the persons and not with the noun; whether “to whom” takes in “the people” as well is not settled by its form. The tellings differ in what they list: the Lord tells Ananias that Paul will bear his name “before nations and kings and sons of Israel” (9:15), and in the temple trance he says “I will send you out far away to the nations” (22:21, future, with the longer verb exapostelō); here the sending is the present apostellō, “I am sending,” and the people stands beside the nations. The shelf keeps the participle in four versions: ASV “delivering thee from the people, and from the Gentiles,” KJV “Delivering thee from the people, and from the Gentiles,” Geneva “Delivering thee from this people, and from the Gentiles” and Douay “Delivering thee from the people, and from the nations.” NWT 1984 “while I deliver you from [this] people and from the nations” reads it as simultaneous with the sending (the bracket marks words its translators supplied). NIV “I will rescue you from your own people and from the Gentiles” and TLB “I will protect you from both your own people and the Gentiles” make it a finite promise, and the NIV’s next sentence, “I am sending you to them,” points back at the Gentiles alone, as does TLB’s “send you to the Gentiles.” Five versions print “Gentiles” (ASV, KJV, Geneva, NIV, TLB) and two “nations” (Douay, NWT 1984).

“To open their eyes” (Acts 26:18). Anoixai ophthalmous autōn. The verb is anoigō, the one of 9:8, where Saul, anewgmenōn de tōn ophthalmōn autou, “though his eyes were open,” “saw nothing”; the one who is now sent to open eyes is the man whose own had to be restored. Ananias used anablepō, “recover sight,” of him at 9:17 and 22:13, and the Nazareth scroll of Luke 4:18 promises anablepsin, “recovery of sight to the blind.” Tabitha “opened her eyes” at 9:40 with the same verb and noun. Isaiah 42:6–7 (not yet on these pages) has near words, and in the Hebrew le’or goyim, “for a light of the nations,” and a verse later lifqoach einayim ivrot, “to open blind eyes,” and those who “sit in darkness” brought out of the prison house; Acts 13:47 quotes the same phrase le’or goyim from Isaiah 49:6, and 26:23 will return to the “light.” Whether this verse is drawing on Isaiah 42 is not said, and no citation formula marks it. Then apo skotous eis phōs, “from darkness to light,” and tēs exousias tou Satana, “the authority of Satan.” The phrase exousia tou skotous, “the authority of darkness,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, where Jesus says to those who have come out against him “this is your hour, and the authority of darkness” (Luke 22:53) and where the Father “rescued us out of the authority of the darkness” (Colossians 1:13). Colossians 1:12 has the other half: the Father “qualified you for the share of the lot of the holy ones in the light.” Light, darkness, authority, lot and holy ones therefore stand in two consecutive verses there, and in this verse of Acts. Colossians uses errysato for the rescue, not exairoumenos. Satanas (dictionary, which treats the Hebrew word) stands in Acts in two verses, 5:3 (“why has Satan filled your heart”) and here; and the devil’s claim at Luke 4:6 that “this authority” has been “handed over to me” uses the same noun exousia (dictionary). Only NWT 1984 prints “from the authority of Satan to God” among the seven; ASV, KJV, Geneva and NIV “from the power of Satan,” and Douay “from the power of Satan to God,” make five “power.” TLB “instead of in Satan’s darkness” drops the noun and folds the two powers into one. The infinitive epistrepsai is the next question: the Greek has no object for “turn,” and the verb can be said of oneself or of others. In Luke it is said of John, who “will turn many of the sons of Israel back to Jehovah their God” (Luke 1:16, with the infinitive again at 1:17). In Acts it is usually the people who turn themselves: the people of Lydda and the Sharon “turned to the Lord” (9:35), so did “a great number who believed” (11:21), and the judgment of 15:19 speaks of those from the nations “turning to God”, each with epi and the accusative, as here. Three shelf versions read “turn them”: NWT 1984 “to turn them from darkness to light,” KJV “and to turn them from darkness to light” and NIV “and turn them from darkness to light”; ASV “that they may turn from darkness to light” and Geneva “that they may turn from darkness to light” make them the ones who turn; Douay “that they may be converted from darkness to light” is passive; and TLB “so that they may repent and live in the light of God” makes it repentance. The verse itself leaves the agent open. The next verse of the chapter has the pair that carries the same verb, metanoein kai epistrephein (26:20).

“Forgiveness of sins and a lot among those who have been sanctified” (Acts 26:18). Labein autous aphesin hamartiōn kai klēron en tois hēgiasmenois pistei tēi eis eme. The exact string aphesin hamartiōn stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Matthew 26:28, Mark 1:4, Luke 3:3, Luke 24:47, Acts 5:31, 10:43 and here; at 2:38 it has the article and “your,” and at 13:38 the nominative, which makes five verses of Acts in all (dictionary). Six shelf versions print “forgiveness”: NWT 1984, KJV, Geneva, Douay and NIV “forgiveness of sins,” and TLB “forgiveness for their sins”; ASV alone has “remission of sins.” The second noun is klēros (dictionary), which in Acts stands in four verses: 1:17 (Judas “was allotted his share in this ministry”), 1:26 (“the lot fell on Matthias”), 8:21 (“neither portion nor lot in this word”) and here. It is the pebble cast, then the share it assigns, and Colossians 1:12 uses it of the share of the holy ones. At 20:32 Paul’s farewell to the Ephesian elders ends with a close cousin, tēn klēronomian en tois hēgiasmenois pasin, “the inheritance among all those who have been sanctified”; the dative tois hēgiasmenois, with the article, stands in two verses of the New Testament, 20:32 and this one. Klēronomia is the noun built on klēros, so the two verses say the same thing with the plain word here and the derived one there, and the English “lot” of this verse keeps what “inheritance” at 20:32 turns into a possession. The shelf mostly reads one noun as the other: NWT 1984 “an inheritance among those sanctified by [their] faith in me,” ASV “an inheritance among them that are sanctified by faith in me,” KJV “inheritance among them which are sanctified by faith that is in me,” Geneva “inheritance among them, which are sanctified by faith in me” and TLB “God’s inheritance along with all people everywhere” make five “inheritance”; NIV “a place among those who are sanctified” is the one “place”; Douay “a lot among the saints” is the one that prints “lot,” and it prints “saints” for the perfect participle hēgiasmenois, “having been sanctified,” which is not the noun hagioi, “holy ones,” of 9:13, 9:32 and 9:41. Five versions print “sanctified” (NWT 1984, ASV, KJV, Geneva, NIV); TLB “who are set apart” reads the same verb as setting apart. Last comes pistei tēi eis eme, “by faith in me,” an article-phrase that stands in one verse of the New Testament. The Greek word order allows it to go with “sanctified,” with “receive” or with both; the English can only choose one place, and all seven shelf versions put it last, after “sanctified.” The speaker is the one who named himself “Jesus” at 26:15, and pistis eis is the preposition of 20:21, “faith in our Lord Jesus.”

Three tellings of one commission (Acts 9:15–16, 22:14–15 and 22:21, 26:16–18). In the first telling the Lord speaks to Ananias, not to Saul: Saul is “a chosen vessel, to bear my name before nations and kings and sons of Israel” and will be shown “how much he must suffer” (9:15–16). In the second Ananias speaks to Paul: he is appointed beforehand “to know his will and to see the Righteous One and to hear a voice from his mouth,” and will be “a witness for him to all people” (22:14–15); the sending to the nations comes later, in a trance in the temple (22:17–21). In the third the Lord himself says it on the road, Ananias is not named anywhere in the chapter, there is no temple scene, no suffering and no baptism in these three verses, and the commission adds what neither earlier telling has: the opening of eyes, the turning, the forgiveness and the lot. Paul’s own letter gives the purpose in his voice at Galatians 1:15–16: the one who “set me apart from my mother’s womb” was pleased “to reveal his Son in me, so that I might announce the good news of him among the nations.” The tellings are set side by side here and none is made to correct the others, as the Acts 22 note did for the first two; why the third has no Ananias, no temple scene and no mention of suffering the text does not say.

What the apparatus marks (Acts 26:16–18). Four notes belong to these verses. At 26:16 the word me after eides is printed by Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 and omitted by Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont (above). At 26:17 Robinson–Pierpont omit the second ek, so that the reading is “from the people and the nations,” with one preposition, and reverse the order of apostellō se to se apostellō; the second is a change of word order only and does not alter the sense, and the first changes only whether “the nations” is set off from “the people” by its own preposition. At 26:18 Robinson–Pierpont read hypostrepsai, “to return,” for the printed epistrepsai, “to turn.” That is the one substantive difference in these verses besides me: “to return from darkness to light” would read as a going back, where “turn” does not require one. The seven shelf versions print “turn,” “converted” or “repent,” not “return.”

Verses 19–21 · ἀπειθὴς … οὐρανίῳ ὀπτασίᾳ … ἀπήγγελλον … μετανοεῖν καὶ ἐπιστρέφειν … ἄξια τῆς μετανοίας ἔργα … συλλαβόμενοι … ἐπειρῶντο διαχειρίσασθαι apeithēs … ouraniō optasia … apēngellon … metanoein kai epistrephein … axia tēs metanoias erga … synlabomenoi … epeirōnto diacheirisasthai — “Disobedient to the heavenly vision” (Acts 26:19–21): what is the vision Paul says he obeyed, where does he say he preached first, what are “works worthy of repentance,” and why do “Jews” seize him in the temple?

“Did not become disobedient” (Acts 26:19). Hothen, basileu Agrippa, ouk egenomēn apeithēs tē ouraniō optasia: “Therefore, King Agrippa, I did not become disobedient to the heavenly vision.” The “therefore” hangs on the commission of 26:16–18, and the king is addressed again in the order basileu Agrippa, which stands in four verses of the New Testament: 25:26, where Festus uses it, and 26:2, here and 26:27, where Paul does. The adjective apeithēs stands in six verses of the New Testament (the same search also catches the verb apeitheō, so the six were read, not tallied): Luke 1:17, “the disobedient to the wisdom of the righteous”; here; Romans 1:30, “disobedient to parents”; 2 Timothy 3:2 (not yet on these pages), with the same phrase; Titus 1:16, the people who “by their works … deny him”; and Titus 3:3 (not yet on these pages). This is the one place in the list where the disobedience is denied and not described. The verb behind it is in Acts at 14:2 (“the Jews who disobeyed”) and 19:9 (“disobeying”), and the Acts 19 note promised that “its adjective is at 26:19”; see the entry apeitheō. The sentence is a litotes (“not disobedient” for “obedient”), and the verb is egenomēn, “I became.” NWT 1984 “I did not become disobedient” keeps it; ASV, KJV and Geneva “I was not disobedient unto the heavenly vision” use a plain “was,” as do NIV “I was not disobedient to the vision from heaven” and TLB “I was not disobedient to that vision from heaven.” Douay “I was not incredulous to the heavenly vision” moves from refusing to obey to refusing to believe, the same shift that the notes at 14:2 and 19:9 record there, where the Greek verb is not the one for unbelief.

“The heavenly vision” (Acts 26:19). Optasia stands in four verses of the New Testament (optasia): Luke 1:22, where the people “realised that he had seen a vision in the sanctuary,” that is, Zechariah after the scene whose speech, at 1:17, holds the other adjective apeithēs; Luke 24:23, where the women “had even seen a vision of angels”; here; and 2 Corinthians 12:1 (not yet on these pages). Luke draws no line from the sanctuary to the road, and in Acts the word occurs nowhere else. Acts uses the other noun, horama, in eleven verses (7:31, 9:10, 9:12, 10:3, 10:17, 10:19, 11:5, 12:9, 16:9, 16:10 and 18:9), among them Ananias’s vision in 9:10 and Cornelius’s at 10:3; none of those verses is in the road narratives of 9:3–9, 22:6–11 or 26:12–18, so this is the only place where any of the three tellings gives the encounter on the road a noun of seeing. See horama, ekstasis (Paul’s own “trance” in the temple, 22:17) and optasia. The adjective ouranios, “heavenly,” stands in nine verses of the New Testament (the same search also catches epouranios, which was left out): the “heavenly Father” of seven verses in Matthew, the “heavenly host” of Luke 2:13, and here. It is the adjective for the light that was ouranothen, “from heaven,” at 26:13. On the shelf, NWT 1984 “heavenly sight” is the odd one out: “sight” is also what the eye does and what a spectacle is, and the NWT 2013 revision has “heavenly vision” where its 1984 edition has “sight.” NIV “the vision from heaven” and TLB “that vision from heaven” turn the adjective into a phrase. The Greek does not say what was seen beyond its being heavenly; the chapter has already told it at 26:13–18.

“First in Damascus … and the nations” (Acts 26:20). Alla tois en Damaskō prōton te kai Hierosolymois, pasan te tēn chōran tēs Ioudaias, kai tois ethnesin: “but to those in Damascus first, and in Jerusalem, and throughout all the country of Judea, and to the nations.” The grammar changes as the list runs. The first two members are datives (“to those in Damascus … and Jerusalem”); the third, pasan … tēn chōran, is an accusative with no preposition, a region and no longer a set of people; the fourth goes back to a dative. The apparatus notes it in two places: Robinson–Pierpont has no te after prōton and adds eis before pasan, so that the region is plainly something Paul went “into,” while Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print what the archive prints. English has to flatten it. NWT 1984 “over all the country of” and Douay “unto all the country of Judea” keep “country,” NIV “in all Judea” drops it, and KJV “throughout all the coasts of Judaea” and Geneva “throughout all the coasts of Judea” print “coasts,” which a modern reader takes for a seashore; the Greek is chōra, “country.” The four stops can be set beside the other accounts of these years, without joining them. Acts 9:19–22 has him “with the disciples in Damascus for some days” and proclaiming in the synagogues; 9:26–29 has him in Jerusalem, speaking boldly and disputing with the Hellenists, who “were attempting to kill him”; and 9:30 sends him from there to Tarsus. In his temple trance he is told to “get out of Jerusalem quickly” because “they will not receive your testimony” (22:17–18). In Galatians 1:17–24 he goes to Arabia and back to Damascus, to Jerusalem after three years for fifteen days, then to “the regions of Syria and Cilicia,” and says: “I was unknown by face to the congregations of Judea that are in Christ” (1:22). Acts 26:20 names Judea, Galatians 1:22 says what Judea’s congregations did not know, and neither passage mentions the other. Whether the two describe different periods, different ways of speaking of a “country,” or something else is not decided on this page. The last member is “the nations,” the word Ananias was told of at 9:15 (“to bear my name before nations and kings and sons of Israel”), with a king now in the audience; Luke does not draw that line. NWT 1984 “and to the nations” is the one that keeps the house rendering of ethnē (the other six print “Gentiles”: ASV, KJV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and TLB). See ethnos.

“I kept announcing” (Acts 26:20). Apēngellon is imperfect, a preaching done again and again, with the sense of “kept.” The apparatus has one variant: Robinson–Pierpont reads the participle apangellōn, “announcing,” so that in that edition the whole list hangs on the first clause (“I did not become disobedient … but, announcing …”) and does not stand as a sentence of its own. The shelf renders the verb five ways. NWT 1984 “I went bringing the message” makes it a journey; ASV “declared” and NIV “I preached” (with TLB “I preached first”) are speech; KJV “shewed” and Geneva “showed” turn the announcing into showing, which the Greek does not say; Douay “did I preach” moves the verb to the end of the sentence.

“Repent and turn to God” (Acts 26:20). Metanoein kai epistrephein epi ton theon, both infinitives, the content of what he announced. Acts has a verb of repenting and a verb of turning together in two verses only (metanoeō beside epistrephō, the two lists crossed and read): 3:19, “Repent therefore, and turn back,” and here. Turning to God is a pattern with its own earlier steps: the nations are told “to turn from these futile things to a living God” (14:15), the council decides not to trouble “those from the nations who are turning to God” (15:19), and Paul has just said that the purpose of his sending was “to turn” people “to God” (26:18, apparatus 993: Robinson–Pierpont has hypostrepsai, “to return”). At 20:21 he describes the same message as “repentance toward God and faith in our Lord Jesus”; the dictionary entry metanoia gives the range of the first word, and epistrephō the second. Five of the seven print the same words: NWT 1984, ASV, KJV, Geneva and NIV “should repent and turn to God.” Douay “do penance, and turn to God” is the rendering the dictionary entry traces to the Latin, and TLB “forsake their sins and turn to God” adds a thing to forsake that the Greek verb does not name.

“Works worthy of repentance” (Acts 26:20). Axia tēs metanoias erga prassontas: the participle “doing,” in the accusative, agrees with the “them” who are to repent and turn. The phrase stands in three verses of the New Testament (axios tēs metanoias): John’s demand in Matthew 3:8, “produce fruit worthy of repentance,” and Luke 3:8, “Produce, then, fruits worthy of repentance,” and here. Both Gospels have “fruit”; Acts has “works,” and the verb is prassō, “to do, to practice,” where John’s is poieō, “to make.” Whether Paul’s sentence echoes John’s is not said; the shared adjective and noun are what the Greek gives. The shelf: NWT 1984 “by doing works that befit repentance,” ASV “doing works worthy of repentance” and Douay “doing works worthy of penance” stay with “works”; KJV “do works meet for repentance” uses an older word for “fitting”; Geneva “do works worthy amendment of life” renders the noun metanoia as “amendment of life.” NIV “demonstrate their repentance by their deeds” and TLB “prove their repentance by doing good deeds” change the relation: the Greek says that the works are worthy of repentance, the English that they show it. TLB also adds “good.”

“Seized me in the temple” (Acts 26:21). Heneka toutōn me Ioudaioi synlabomenoi en tō hierō epeirōnto diacheirisasthai: “For these reasons Jews seized me in the temple and tried to kill me.” “These things” is plural. NWT 1984 “On account of these things” and KJV “For these causes” keep the plural (two of the seven); ASV, Geneva and Douay “For this cause” and NIV “That is why” make it one, and TLB “for preaching this” names the cause, which the Greek leaves to the verses before. Luke gives three different reasons for this seizure. Asian Jews cry out that Paul “teaches everyone everywhere against the people and the law and this place” and has “brought Greeks into the temple” (21:27–28); Tertullus says he “tried to profane the temple, whom we seized” (24:6); and here the reason is the preaching of repentance to Jews and to the nations. In 22:21–22 the crowd listened “up to this word,” the nations, and then shouted. The three are set side by side and not joined. The participle is synlabomenoi, from synlambanō, which in Acts stands in four verses: 1:16 (those who arrested Jesus), 12:3 (Peter), 23:27 and here (the same verb in Luke 22:54, Mark 14:48 and Matthew 26:55 of the arrest of Jesus). At 23:27 the tribune’s letter says Paul was “seized by the Jews and about to be killed by them,” so Paul’s retelling and the tribune’s report share a verb that Luke’s own account of the scene (21:30, epilabomenoi, “seizing”) does not use. The apparatus has two variants in this verse: Robinson–Pierpont has hoi Ioudaioi me, “the Jews,” with the article and the other order, and Nestle–Aland 28 adds the participle ontas “being” (apparatus 998; the line does not give its place). The printed text has “Jews” without the article, as at 26:2 and 26:7; on the shelf, NWT 1984 “Jews seized me” is the one that keeps the bare noun; NIV “some Jews seized me” adds a word the Greek lacks and makes the seizers a part of the people; and five others print the article, which agrees with the Robinson–Pierpont reading: ASV “the Jews seized me,” KJV “the Jews caught me,” Geneva “the Jews caught me,” Douay “the Jews, when I was in the temple” and TLB “The Jews arrested me.”

“Tried to kill me” (Acts 26:21). Epeirōnto is the imperfect of peiraomai, “to try,” and the form stands in one verse of the New Testament (epeirōnto). The earlier chapters print “tried” for a different verb, peirazō: 9:26 (he “tried to join himself to the disciples”), 16:7 (“they were trying to go on into Bithynia”) and 24:6 (“tried to profane the temple”), so the English is shared and the Greek verb is not; see peirazō. The Acts 24 note points to this verse as the other “tried” of the temple, and the temple is the shared place: the verb of attempt is said of the profaning there and of the killing here. The infinitive diacheirisasthai is a compound of cheir, “hand,” and stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and 5:30, Peter’s “whom you killed, hanging him on a tree.” See diacheirizomai. The same verb is used of the killing of Jesus and of the attempt on Paul, and Luke does not remark on it. The attempt is imperfect, as in 21:31 (“while they were seeking to kill him”), and the Greek does not say that it came near succeeding. The shelf keeps the attempt in every case: NWT 1984 “attempted to slay,” ASV “assayed to kill,” KJV, Geneva and Douay “went about to kill,” and NIV and TLB “tried to kill.” NIV “temple courts” adds “courts” to the one noun hierō, and Douay “when I was in the temple, having apprehended me” sets Paul’s presence in the temple in a clause of its own. What the hearing does with the sentence is the next note: the words of 26:22, “having obtained help,” follow at once, and the “kill” of this verse is what the audience will weigh at 26:31.

Verses 22–23 · ἐπικουρίας … μαρτυρόμενος … παθητὸς … ἐξ ἀναστάσεως νεκρῶν … φῶς epikourias … martyromenos … pathētos … ex anastaseōs nekrōn … phōs — Acts 26:22–23: what is “the help that is from God,” does ei mean “if” or “that” in “if the Christ was subject to suffering,” and what is the “light” for the people and the nations?

“The help that is from God” (Acts 26:22). Epikourias oun tychōn tēs apo tou theou: “having obtained, then, the help that is from God.” The noun epikouria, “help,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so no other passage shows how Luke or anyone else in the New Testament used it. The “then” (oun) ties the sentence to what has just been told: Jews seized Paul in the temple and “tried to kill” him (26:21), and he is standing in a hall in Caesarea to say so. The help is named as having come apo tou theou, “from God,” and the sentence does not say what form it took. The apparatus has one reading: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print apo, “from,” and Robinson–Pierpont has para, “from beside”; either way the help is God’s. On the shelf, NWT 1984 “the help that is from God” and ASV “the help that is from God” keep the noun and the preposition; KJV “obtained help of God” and Douay “being aided by the help of God” keep the noun with the older “of”; NIV “God has helped me” turns the noun into a verb; and TLB “God protected me so that I am still alive today” supplies a result, “still alive,” that the Greek does not state. The Greek leaves the help unspecified, and this translation leaves it so.

“I stand testifying to small and great” (Acts 26:22). Hestēka martyromenos mikrō te kai megalō. The verb hestēka, “I stand,” is the perfect, a standing that has gone on “to this day.” It is the verb of 26:6, hestēka krinomenos, “I stand being judged,” and the position is the one of 25:10 (“I am standing at Caesar’s judgment seat”). At 26:6 the participle says what the standing is for, a judging; here it says “testifying.” The verb martyromai in this simple form, apart from its compounds with dia- and pro-, stands in five verses of the New Testament, read from the hits: Acts 20:26 (“I testify to you on this very day that I am clean from the blood of all”), here, Galatians 5:3, Ephesians 4:17 and 1 Thessalonians 2:12 (none of the three on these pages). Its compound diamartyromenos is the verb of 20:21, “testifying solemnly” to Jews and Greeks. The verb is related to martys (see martyreō), the “witness” of the commission at 26:16, and the standing is that witness at work. Mikrō te kai megalō is “to small and great”: two datives joined by te kai, the pair meaning everyone from the lowest to the highest. The pair of opposites recurs in other wording at 8:10, where “all paid attention, from small to great” to Simon, and in the plural at Revelation 11:18, “the small and the great.” In this room it is not rhetoric alone: Paul has addressed a king and a governor, and the audience room held tribunes and the leading men of the city (25:23). Shelf: NWT 1984 “I continue to this day bearing witness” and KJV “I continue unto this day, witnessing” render the perfect as a continuing; ASV “I stand unto this day, testifying” keeps the posture; NIV “I stand here and testify” also does, but moves it to “here.” TLB “to tell these facts to everyone, both great and small” turns testimony into telling and reverses the order of small and great.

“Saying nothing outside of what the prophets and Moses spoke” (Acts 26:22). Ouden ektos legōn hōn te hoi prophētai elalēsan mellontōn ginesthai kai Mōÿsēs. Ektos, “outside,” is the plain spatial word, and the phrase says Paul’s speech stays inside the boundary the prophets and Moses drew. The relative hōn (“of what”) is the genitive by attraction to mellontōn, “of things going to happen.” The order is “the prophets … and Moses,” the same two named in 24:14, where Paul tells Felix he believes “the things written in the Prophets” (there beside the law). It is a claim about continuity that Luke has set up before. At 3:18 God “announced beforehand by the mouth of all the prophets, that his Christ would suffer”; at 3:22 “Moses said” a prophet like himself would be raised up; at Luke 24:27 the risen Jesus, “beginning from Moses and from all the prophets,” interprets “the things concerning himself”; and at Luke 24:44 the list widens to “the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms.” In Jesus’ parable, Abraham says “They have Moses and the Prophets” (Luke 16:29). The Greek of 28:23 names “the law of Moses and the prophets” again (not yet on these pages). Whether Paul is reporting what these writers said or claiming a reading of them, the verse states only that he says nothing outside it. Shelf: NWT 1984 “saying nothing except things the Prophets as well as Moses stated” and ASV “saying nothing but what the prophets and Moses did say” turn the “outside” into an exception; KJV “saying none other things than those which the prophets and Moses did say should come” says the same with an older negative; NIV “saying nothing beyond what the prophets and Moses said would happen” keeps the spatial “beyond.” “Going to happen” is mellontōn ginesthai, things still ahead when the prophets and Moses spoke.

“Subject to suffering” (Acts 26:23). Ei pathētos ho christos: “if the Christ was subject to suffering.” The adjective pathētos stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and no verb is printed, so “was” is supplied. Its sense cannot be tested elsewhere in the New Testament; the parallel passages use the verb, never the adjective. At 3:18 it is pathein ton christon, “that his Christ would suffer”; at 17:3 Paul “sets out that the Christ edei pathein,” “had to suffer and rise from the dead”; and at Luke 24:26 and 24:46 the same pair, suffering and rising, comes from Jesus himself. Here the necessity (edei) of 17:3 and Luke 24:26 is absent from the Greek: the adjective on its face says “subject to suffering” (or “able to suffer”), and the English “would suffer” or “must suffer” of the shelf makes it a prediction or an obligation. Shelf: NWT 1984 “the Christ was to suffer,” ASV “the Christ must suffer,” KJV “Christ should suffer,” Geneva “Christ should suffer,” Douay “Christ should suffer” and NIV “the Messiah would suffer” all print a verb of suffering where the Greek has an adjective. The harder question is ei. It is the ordinary word for “if” and for “whether,” and it is the word of the question at 26:8, “Why is it judged unbelievable among you if God raises the dead?” In a verb of inquiry it means “whether”: the Beroeans examined the Scriptures “to see whether these things were so” (17:11, ei echoi tauta houtōs). Read that way, verses 22–23 say that Paul states nothing outside what the prophets and Moses said, whether the Christ is subject to suffering, whether as first from the resurrection of the dead he is to proclaim light: the two questions on which the prophets and Moses are said to speak. This translation prints “if” twice, which keeps the open question and does not decide between “whether” and a conditional. The reading “that” is the shelf’s: NWT 1984 “that the Christ was to suffer,” ASV “how that the Christ must suffer,” Geneva “that Christ should suffer” and KJV “That Christ should suffer” take ei as a bare “that,” so the two clauses become a statement of what the prophets said. The Greek as printed has two ei clauses, and none of the seven shelf versions prints “if” for either one, so the shelf reads the verse one way where the Greek allows more than one.

“As first from the resurrection of the dead” (Acts 26:23). Ei prōtos ex anastaseōs nekrōn. The phrase ex anastaseōs nekrōn, “from resurrection of dead ones,” with no article before either noun, stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Romans 1:4, where this translation prints “by the resurrection of the dead” for the same Greek words after the Son is “appointed … in power.” The preposition ex is “out of” or, with a noun of origin, “as a result of,” and the two verses read it differently in this translation’s own English, “from” here and “by” in Romans. The two readings are real: “first to rise out of the dead” makes a sequence, and “first by means of resurrection” makes an agent. The shelf splits the same way. ASV “he first by the resurrection of the dead should proclaim light” reads “by”; NWT 1984 “as the first to be resurrected from the dead,” KJV “the first that should rise from the dead” and NIV “the first to rise from the dead” read “from.” The idea is also put with the noun “firstborn” in two other books shipped here: prōtotokos ek tōn nekrōn, “firstborn from among the dead,” at Colossians 1:18, and ho prōtotokos tōn nekrōn, “the firstborn of the dead,” at Revelation 1:5 (see prōtotokos). The adjective prōtos here is “first,” and the verse does not say whether it means first in time, first in rank or first for others to follow; those are three readings, and this page reports them without choosing. The article-less “resurrection of the dead” is also the phrase Paul used before the Sanhedrin (23:6) and, as the Acts 17 note records, at 24:21; the “hope” of 26:6 and the question of 26:8 are the same subject. A different phrase stands at Hebrews 11:35: ex anastaseōs tous nekrous autōn, “their dead back by resurrection,” with nekrous in the accusative, so its words are not the same.

“Light … to the people and to the nations” (Acts 26:23). Phōs mellei kataggellein tō te laō kai tois ethnesin: the Christ “was about to proclaim light.” Mellei is the present of mellō, “is about to,” and its subject is ho christos, so this “light” is something the Christ proclaims, not something Paul does. In Acts the light for the nations is also the command of 13:47: Paul and Barnabas say “Jehovah has commanded us: ‘I have set you as a light of nations,’” and the note there sets that use beside Simeon’s “a light for revelation to the nations, and glory for your people Israel” (Luke 2:32) and looks ahead to this verse. The Acts 13 note names Isaiah 49:6 as the quoted verse (Isaiah 49 is not yet on these pages). Here three persons are linked with “light”: the Christ who proclaims it, the “us” of 13:47 who are told to be it, and Paul, whom the risen Jesus sends “to open their eyes, to turn them from darkness to light” (26:18), just after he saw “a light from heaven, beyond the brightness of the sun” (26:13). The verse does not say how those uses relate, and this page does not decide it. Tō laō is “the people,” with the article and no demonstrative: Israel, as in 26:17, where Paul is told he is being rescued “from the people and from the nations.” See tribe, tongue, people and nation and ethnos. The shelf varies. NWT 1984 “publish light both to this people and to the nations” adds “this,” which the Greek does not have; ASV “proclaim light both to the people and to the Gentiles” and Douay “shew light to the people, and to the Gentiles” keep the article; NIV “to his own people and to the Gentiles” supplies “his own,” and TLB “to Jews and Gentiles alike” replaces “the people” with “Jews.” For the second noun, ethnesin, which this translation prints “nations,” six of the seven shelf versions print “Gentiles,” and the exception is NWT 1984 “the nations.” The apparatus has one item for verse 23: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have tō te laō, “both to the people,” and Robinson–Pierpont omits te, “to the people”; the omission changes the connective, not the meaning. The sentence ends there, and what comes next (Festus’s outburst, 26:24) is in the next note.

Verses 24–26 · Μεγάλῃ τῇ φωνῇ … Μαίνῃ … τὰ πολλά σε γράμματα … εἰς μανίαν περιτρέπει … κράτιστε Φῆστε … σωφροσύνης ῥήματα ἀποφθέγγομαι … παρρησιαζόμενος … ἐν γωνίᾳ πεπραγμένον Megalē tē phōnē … Mainē … ta polla se grammata … eis manian peritrepei … kratiste Phēste … sōphrosynēs rhēmata apophthengomai … parrēsiazomenos … en gōnia pepragmenon — “You are out of your mind, Paul” (Acts 26:24–26): why does Festus call Paul mad, what is the “learning” that does it, and what does “not done in a corner” mean?

“In a loud voice” (Acts 26:24). Tauta de autou apologoumenou ho Phēstos megalē tē phōnē phēsin: “And while he was making his defense with these things, Festus, with the loud voice, says.” The genitive absolute with the present participle apologoumenou sets Festus’s cry inside the speech, not after it. The verb is the one that opened the speech at 26:1 and 26:2, and the same genitive absolute, with Paul as the one defending, stands at 25:8; at 24:10 Paul says it of himself (see apologia). The Greek has no word for “interrupted” or “suddenly”: NIV “interrupted Paul’s defense” and TLB “Suddenly Festus shouted” add them. The phrase megalē phōnē, “loud voice,” stands in six verses of Acts: the crowd that stops its ears at 7:57, Stephen’s last cry at 7:60, the unclean spirits at 8:7, Paul to the lame man at 14:10 (“Stand up on your feet”), Paul to the jailer at 16:28, and here. Paul’s two earlier uses are a command to a lame man and a warning to a jailer; here the raised voice is turned on Paul. Only here does the article stand between adjective and noun, megalē tē phōnē, a form found in one verse of the New Testament; the English “in a loud voice” does not carry it, and neither does the “with a loud voice” of KJV, Geneva and Douay “Festus said with a loud voice.” The verb is phēsin, the historical present, “says,” in the printed text (Westcott–Hort, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28); Robinson–Pierpont reads ephē, “said,” so the apparatus (1001) turns on tense alone. Luke gives Festus the same present at 25:5, 25:22 and 25:24. Of the seven versions, ASV “Festus saith with a loud voice” is the one that keeps the present here; NWT 1984 “Festus said in a loud voice” has the past. What prompts the outburst Luke gives only as tauta, “these things.” The nearest candidate is the sentence just before, at 26:23, where the Christ, “as first from the resurrection of the dead,” is about to proclaim light; the same phrase drew a jeer from the Athenians at 17:32. But Festus had already told Agrippa, at 25:19, that the quarrel was about “a certain Jesus who had died, whom Paul asserted to be alive,” so the resurrection was not news to him, and the text does not say which part of the speech he is answering.

“You are out of your mind” (Acts 26:24–25). Mainē, Paule: the verb mainomai stands in five verses of the New Testament, and two of them are on these pages already. At 12:15 those in the house of Mary say the identical word, Mainē, to Rhoda when she reports Peter at the gate, and she “insisted that it was so”; at John 10:20 many say of Jesus, “He has a demon and is out of his mind,” and the next verse answers with “words” (rhēmata, 10:21) that are not those of a man possessed by a demon; the other three are here, at 26:24 and 26:25, and 1 Corinthians 14:23 (not yet on these pages). In each of these the charge is aimed at someone who has said something that the hearers cannot take in; the Greek does not say what Festus means by it, a clinical judgment or a rebuke. Festus’s second clause, eis manian peritrepei, “turns you into madness,” uses two words that stand each in one verse of the New Testament: the noun mania and the verb peritrepō, built from peri-, “around,” and trepō, “to turn”; the New Testament offers no second use of either to compare. The shelf splits on the first word. ASV “thou art mad” and NWT 1984 “going mad” keep the plain English for the Greek, KJV, Geneva and Douay “beside thyself” use an idiom (three of the seven), NIV “out of your mind” matches this page, and TLB “you are insane” is a fifth wording. The later revision, NWT 2013, prints “going out of your mind,” so the two NWT editions differ here. Paul’s denial at 26:25 uses the same verb in the first person, and the shelf stays closer together: ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “I am not mad” (four of seven), NWT 1984 “I am not going mad” TLB “I am not insane” and NIV “I am not insane.” The NIV therefore prints “out of your mind” for Festus’s word and “insane” for Paul’s answer to it, two English words for one Greek verb.

“Your great learning” (Acts 26:24). Ta polla se grammata is, word for word, “the many — you — writings,” with the pronoun se, “you,” wedged between article-and-adjective and noun, and the sentence turns “you” around into madness. The Greek has no word for “learning” and none for “great”: polla is “many,” and grammata is the plural of gramma, “letter, written thing.” The plural grammata stands in nine verses of the New Testament, and it covers a range. In Luke 16:6 and 16:7 it is the debtor’s “bill”; in John 7:15 the Jewish leaders ask, “How does this man know his letters, when he has never studied?”, which is the sense of schooling; in Acts 28:21 (not yet on these pages) the Jews of Rome say they have received no grammata about Paul from Judea, which is correspondence; John 5:47 prints “his writings” of Moses, whom the verse before says “wrote about me”; Galatians 6:11, 2 Corinthians 3:7 and 2 Timothy 3:15 (none yet on these pages) are the remaining three, the last with hiera, “sacred,” before it. Which sense Festus has in mind is the question: Paul’s schooling, as at John 7:15, or the writings he has been quoting, since he has just said that he stands “saying nothing outside of what both the prophets and Moses spoke of” (26:22). “Learning” is the gloss the Acts 25 note already gave this word, and it is the sense that makes a compliment of the insult; the Greek itself says “many writings,” and this page does not decide between schooling and scrolls. Six of the seven print “learning”: NWT 1984 “Great learning,” ASV “thy much learning,” KJV, Geneva and Douay “much learning,” and NIV “Your great learning.” TLB “Your long studying” makes it the activity of reading and not the possession of knowledge, which is nearer to “writings” than the others. The verse’s own “great” is also the word of two of the seven, while the four that print “much” are nearer the plural polla.

“Most excellent Festus” (Acts 26:25). Paul answers the insult with a title: Ou mainomai, phēsin, kratiste Phēste. The word kratistos stands in four verses of the New Testament: the Theophilus of Luke 1:3, the letter of Lysias to Felix at 23:26, Tertullus’s opening to Felix at 24:3, and here. In the second and third it is said of or to Felix; Paul himself, answering Felix at 24:10, used no title at all. Here he gives it to the governor who has just called him mad. Whether it is the formal style for a man of that rank or a courtesy, Luke does not say, and the title’s official standing is reported here, not checked on this page. The shelf: ASV and Douay “most excellent,” NIV “most excellent,” and TLB “Most Excellent” print the same title this page does (four of seven); KJV “most noble,” Geneva “O noble” and NWT 1984 “Your Excellency” print three others. The apparatus (1002) records that Robinson–Pierpont lacks the name Paulos in ho de Paulos, “but Paul,” and so reads “but he”; the shelf follows both readings: KJV “But he said” and Geneva “But he said” print no name, while ASV “But Paul saith” and NWT 1984 “But Paul said” keep it. Which edition stands behind the two that omit it is not checked on this page.

“Words of truth and of a sound mind” (Acts 26:25). Alla alētheias kai sōphrosynēs rhēmata apophthengomai: “but words of truth and of sound-mindedness I utter.” The verb apophthengomai stands in three verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 2:4, where the Spirit “was giving them to utter,” 2:14, where Peter “raised his voice and declared to them,” and here. The pairing with 2:14 is close. At Pentecost others say, “They are filled with sweet new wine” (2:13); Peter “declares” with the same verb, bids them “give ear to my words” (rhēmata), and says that the men “are not drunk” (2:15). Here the accusation is madness, and the answer is the same verb and the same noun. Luke draws no link between the two scenes. The noun sōphrosynē stands in three verses, here and 1 Timothy 2:9 and 2:15 (neither yet on these pages). The verb of the same family, sōphroneō, is what Mark and Luke use of the man who had had the Legion, found sitting “clothed and in his right mind” after the demons had gone out (Mark 5:15; Luke 8:35). In English “right mind” and “out of your mind” are opposites, but the Greek words belong to different families, mainomai and sōphron-, and whether the sound of that scene is meant to be heard here the text does not say. The shelf has four main wordings. ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “soberness” (four of seven) use an English word that leans toward the opposite of drunkenness, which is the charge at 2:13, not the one here; NWT 1984 “soundness of mind” keeps the mind; NIV “true and reasonable” turns the noun into an adjective of the speech; and TLB “words of sober truth” moves the quality onto the truth. For the verb, ASV and KJV “speak forth” and NWT 1984 “uttering” carry the solemn sound of the verb; this page’s “utter” is the word of 2:4.

“The king knows” and “I speak boldly” (Acts 26:26). Epistatai gar peri toutōn ho basileus, pros hon kai parrēsiazomenos lalō: Paul turns from Festus to the king, who is Agrippa and whom he had called an expert in “customs and questions” at 26:3; he is spoken of in the third person here and addressed by name in 26:27. What “these things” are Luke does not say. The participle parrēsiazomenos belongs to a verb that stands in seven verses of Acts, parrēsiazomai at 9:27, 9:28, 13:46, 14:3, 18:26, 19:8 and here (see parrhesiazomai; the noun parrēsia is a separate word and is not counted). Its earlier subjects are Saul at Damascus, Paul and Barnabas, Apollos and Paul at Ephesus; this is the first time the speaker is on trial, and the one he speaks boldly to is a king. The shelf: ASV “unto whom also I speak freely,” KJV “before whom also I speak freely” and NIV “I can speak freely to him” print “freely” (three of seven); Geneva “before whom also I speak boldly” is the only one that prints “boldly” (one of seven); Douay “with confidence,” NWT 1984 “with freeness of speech” and TLB “I speak frankly” are three others. The little word kai, “also,” is printed here with Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and Westcott–Hort omit it (apparatus 1003); four of the seven versions keep “also” in the verse. The sentence continues lanthanein gar auton toutōn ou peithomai outhen, “for that any of these things escapes him I am not persuaded … none.” The verb lanthanein is the verb of Luke 8:47, where the woman who had touched Jesus “had not gone unnoticed” (ouk elathen). The Greek has two negatives, ou before peithomai and outhen at the end of the clause, and the seven versions on the shelf, like this page, read the pair as a positive assurance: NWT 1984 “I am persuaded that not one of these things escapes his notice,” ASV and KJV “I am persuaded that none of these things,” NIV “I am convinced that none of this has escaped his notice.” How the Greek idiom of the raised negative works is reported here, not checked on this page. Westcott–Hort print auton alone after lanthanein, and Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont add the indefinite ti, “any” (apparatus 1004). NWT 1984 “escapes his notice” and NIV “has escaped his notice” keep the idea of escaping; ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “hidden from him” is the other group of four; TLB “familiar to him” turns the double negative into a positive.

“Not done in a corner” (Acts 26:26). Ou gar estin en gōnia pepragmenon touto: “for this is not done in a corner.” The participle pepragmenon is the perfect of prassō, and the perfect of this verb stands in four verses of the New Testament, prassō in its perfect forms at Luke 23:15, 25:11, 25:25 and here. The other three are about what a man has done, in two of them a Roman finds nothing worthy of death in the man before him; at Luke 23:15 the form is the very one used here, the neuter participle, in Pilate’s “nothing deserving of death has been done by him,” and the verse before it has his “I have found nothing in this man” with outhen in the printed spelling (Luke 23:14). Here the participle is said of an event, and touto is singular: “this.” What the singular covers (the suffering and rising of 26:23, the career of the Way, or the whole course of Paul’s case) Luke does not say, and this page reports the question and does not settle it. Two more apparatus lines touch the end of the verse. The printed spelling is outhen, and Robinson–Pierpont print ouden (apparatus 1005); the two are spellings of one word, and the printed text spells the pronoun with th at Luke 23:14 and at 15:9 (“made no distinction”) as well. That the th spelling belongs to the Greek of this period is reported here, not checked on this page. Robinson–Pierpont also omit estin (apparatus 1006), which leaves the clause without its linking verb. The noun gōnia is an “angle” or “corner,” and what makes the English “in a corner” a hiding place is the sentence, not the noun alone: the corners of Matthew 6:5 are those of the main streets, where the hypocrites pray “so that they may appear to men,” and the corner of Acts 4:11 is the “head of the corner” that the stone, once “treated as nothing,” has become (see kephalē gōnias). The shelf keeps the corner and the negative: ASV “this hath not been done in a corner,” NWT 1984 “this thing has not been done in a corner,” NIV “it was not done in a corner” and TLB “they were not done in a corner,” with Douay “neither was any of these things done in a corner” the one that spreads the negative over the “things.” Paul’s claim is about publicity, and the sentence that follows it, at 26:27, turns from what the king knows to what he believes.

Verses 27–29 · πιστεύεις … τοῖς προφήταις … ἐν ὀλίγῳ … Χριστιανὸν ποιῆσαι … Εὐξαίμην ἂν … παρεκτὸς τῶν δεσμῶν pisteueis … tois prophētais … en oligō … Christianon poiēsai … Euxaimēn an … parektos tōn desmōn — Acts 26:27–29: “almost persuaded” to be a Christian, or something else? What does Agrippa’s en oligō mean, and what is Paul’s wish “except for these bonds”?

“Do you believe the prophets, King Agrippa?” (Acts 26:27). Pisteueis, basileu Agrippa, tois prophētais; oida hoti pisteueis. The question comes straight out of 26:26, where Paul has just said that the king “knows about these things”; the verb of knowing now returns as oida, “I know,” and Paul answers his own question before the king can. The address is basileu Agrippa, in the order Festus used at 25:26 and Paul used at 26:2 and 26:19; at 25:24 Festus had said Agrippa basileu. The object is a person-word in the dative, tois prophētais, “the prophets,” the ones one trusts. At 24:14 before Felix the Greek is different: Paul says he is pisteuōn, “believing,” all the things “written in the Prophets,” the same verb with the writings as its object. At 26:22 he has just said he says “nothing outside of what both the prophets and Moses spoke of”; here Moses is not named and the question is whether the king trusts the prophets themselves. What Agrippa is supposed to believe them about is not stated in the verse; the nearest content is the suffering Christ and the light of 26:23. The only ground the chapter gives for “I know that you believe” is Paul’s remark at 26:3 that the king is “an expert in all the customs and questions among the Jews”; the library entry Agrippa II notes that Luke says nothing else about him in this book. Agrippa’s next line answers a different sentence, and he never says yes or no to this one. On the shelf, NWT 1984 “believe the Prophets,” TLB “believe the Prophets” and Geneva “believest thou the Prophets” print a capital, as a title for a body of writings; ASV “believest thou the prophets,” KJV “believest thou the prophets,” Douay “Believest thou the prophets” and NIV “do you believe the prophets” do not, which makes three of the seven and four. The second half repeats the verb, oida hoti pisteueis, and five of the seven keep the repeat: NWT 1984 “I know you believe,” ASV “I know that thou believest,” KJV “I know that thou believest,” Geneva “I know that thou believest” and Douay “I know that thou believest.” NIV “I know you do” and TLB “I know you do” replace it with a stand-in for the verb.

“In a little time” (Acts 26:28). Ho de Agrippas pros ton Paulon: En oligō me peitheis Christianon poiēsai. There is no noun in en oligō, only a neuter singular dative, “in little.” The shipped English reads it as time, “in a little time,” and says so by supplying the noun; the Greek would equally allow “with little” (effort, argument). The shelf splits three ways and a half. As a span of time: NWT 1984 “In a short time,” Douay “In a little” (leaving the noun unsaid, as the Greek does) and NIV “in such a short time.” As a degree, “almost”: KJV “Almost thou persuadest me” and Geneva “Almost thou persuadest me.” As the amount of persuading: ASV “With but little persuasion.” As the weight of Paul’s case: TLB “trivial proofs,” a reading with no counterpart in the Greek words, which never mention proofs. The phrase stands in three verses of the New Testament, en oligō here and at 26:29 and again at Ephesians 3:3 (not yet on these pages), where it follows the verb proegrapsa, “I wrote beforehand”; the senses are reported to differ and are not compared on this page. The verb peitheis is present indicative, “you are persuading,” as shipped: something under way. KJV “persuadest,” Geneva “persuadest” and Douay “persuadest” keep it, three of the seven; NWT 1984 “would persuade” and ASV “wouldest fain make” add a modal that the indicative does not carry; NIV “you can persuade” and TLB “you expect” recast it again. The archive’s Greek ends the verse with no punctuation mark, so what is printed does not mark a statement or a question: NIV “Do you think” and TLB “you expect” print questions, and the other five print statements. Whether Agrippa is conceding, deflecting or mocking is not given by a word of the verse; the readings above are the shelf’s, and none is decided here. Luke’s verb of persuading has Paul as its subject elsewhere, at 18:4 (“was persuading both Jews and Greeks”) and 19:8 (“reasoning and persuading about the kingdom of God”), and it is Paul’s own at 26:26, where peithomai is “I am persuaded” that nothing escapes the king; it stands again at 28:23 (not yet on these pages).

“To make a Christian” (Acts 26:28). Christianon poiēsai, “to make a Christian,” is an infinitive of poieō with Christianon as its second accusative: the Greek has no word for “to be.” The apparatus has the reading poiēsai in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and Robinson–Pierpont has genesthai, “to become,” so that Agrippa would be saying that he is being persuaded to become one himself; Paul’s reply in the next verse uses genesthai, and the apparatus does not say which reading came first. On the shelf the verb is split three ways: ASV “make me a Christian” is the only one that follows the printed poiēsai as “make”; NWT 1984 “to become a Christian,” Geneva “to become a Christian,” Douay “to become a Christian” and TLB “to become a Christian” make four with “become,” and KJV “to be a Christian” and NIV “to be a Christian” make two with “be.” That the four “become” versions agree in sense with the Robinson–Pierpont reading does not show which Greek any of them followed. The noun Christianos stands in three verses of the New Testament: 11:26, where the disciples at Antioch are first called it, this verse, and 1 Peter 4:16 (not yet on these pages); the dictionary entry sets out its Latin ending. At 11:26 the Greek does not say who gave the name, and here it is on a king’s lips, in the singular, as something a person could be made into. Whether Agrippa speaks the word with respect, with distance or with scorn is not marked by the Greek.

“I would pray to God” (Acts 26:29). Euxaimēn an tō theō is the aorist optative of euchomai with an, the form of a wish that stays a wish: “I would pray,” “I could wish.” The verb covers both “pray” and “wish,” and the shelf lands on each side. NWT 1984 “I could wish to God” keeps the potential; ASV “I would to God,” KJV “I would to God,” Geneva “I would to God” and Douay “I would to God” make four, and TLB “Would to God” a fifth, all of them the older idiom of the wish; NIV “I pray to God” is a plain present, one of the seven. The shipped English, “I would pray to God,” keeps the potential and the praying. It is the simple verb, not the compound proseuchomai of the praying at 16:25 (“Paul and Silas, praying”). Forms of euchomai stand elsewhere at 2 Corinthians 13:7 and 13:9, at Romans 9:3 and at James 5:16 (all not yet on these pages), and at 3 John 1:2; at 27:29 a form of it is used in a different scene. Then comes the pair en oligō kai en megalō, “both in a little time and in a long time.” The Greek of the second member is “great,” and the shipped English supplies “long time” so that the pair covers time as the verse before it did; Robinson–Pierpont has pollō, “much,” in the second place, which reads as degree. The shelf follows both: NWT 1984 “in a short time or in a long time” and NIV “Short time or long” take time; ASV “with little or with much” and Douay “in a little and in much” take degree, which agrees in sense with the Robinson–Pierpont reading without showing that either used it; KJV and Geneva “both almost, and altogether” carry Agrippa’s “almost” into Paul’s answer; TLB “trivial or strong” keeps its own reading of the arguments. Robinson–Pierpont also adds a speech verb after “Paul” at both 26:28 and 26:29 (ephē and eipen), an addition with no effect on the sense.

“Such as I also am” (Acts 26:29). Genesthai toioutous hopoios kai egō eimi: where Agrippa spoke of being “made,” Paul’s wish is that they “become,” and it reaches past the king to pantas tous akouontas mou sēmeron, “all who hear me today.” The room is the one 25:23 described: Agrippa and Berenice, tribunes and “the men of distinction in the city,” with Festus, who a few verses ago said Paul was “out of your mind” (26:24). The correlative toioutous … hopoios means “of the kind that …” and Paul does not define the kind in the verse; the content, in the chapter, is the commission and the testimony of 26:16–23, and Luke does not call it “Christian.” Forms of the adjective hopoios stand at 1 Thessalonians 1:9 and James 1:24, and at 1 Corinthians 3:13 (not yet on these pages) and Galatians 2:6 (not yet on these pages). NIV “become what I am” and TLB “the same as I am” shorten the correlative to a plain comparison.

“Except for these bonds” (Acts 26:29). Parektos tōn desmōn toutōn. The preposition parektos stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Matthew 5:32 (“apart from a matter of sexual immorality”) and 2 Corinthians 11:28 (not yet on these pages), in a different construction. Paul’s exception is sharp: everything he is, but not these. The noun desma, “bond,” in its plural forms stands in five verses of Acts: desma at 16:26, 20:23 (the Spirit testifies that “bonds and afflictions” await him), 23:29 (the tribune’s letter: nothing “worthy of death or bonds”), this verse and 26:31, where the audience repeats the pairing. The word is not the halysis of 21:33, where the tribune orders Paul “bound with two chains”; the shipped English prints “chains” for that noun and “bonds” for this one. The shelf prints “bonds” in four: NWT 1984 “these bonds,” ASV “these bonds,” KJV “these bonds” and Geneva “these bonds”; Douay “these bands” is one; NIV “these chains” and TLB “these chains” are two, which gives the 21:33 English to the 26:29 noun. The demonstrative toutōn, “these,” points at something present, and this is the first mention of bonds in the chapter; the Greek does not say in what way Paul is bound as he speaks, or whether the bonds are visible to the room.

Verses 30–32 · Ἀνέστη … ἡγεμὼν … συγκαθήμενοι … ἀναχωρήσαντες … Οὐδὲν θανάτου ἢ δεσμῶν ἄξιον … Ἀπολελύσθαι … ἐπεκέκλητο Καίσαρα Anestē … hēgemōn … synkathēmenoi … anachōrēsantes … Ouden thanatou ē desmōn axion … Apolelysthai … epekeklēto Kaisara — Acts 26:30–32: could Paul have been released, who says “nothing worthy of death or bonds,” and what does the chapter leave unsaid at its close?

“And the king rose” (Acts 26:30). Anestē te ho basileus kai ho hēgemōn hē te Bernikē kai hoi synkathēmenoi autois. The one finite verb, anestē, is singular and belongs to the king; the governor, Berenice and “those sitting with them” follow as nominatives with no verb of their own, so the English has either to supply one or to leave a comma. NWT 1984 “so did the governor” supplies it. ASV “And the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice” leaves the list bare, and so does Douay “And the king rose up, and the governor, and Bernice.” NIV “and with him the governor” turns the rest into companions, and TLB “stood and left” adds a departure that this verse does not state; the leaving comes in the next verse, and only for some. The apparatus has two notes here. Robinson–Pierpont opens with a clause, kai tauta eipontos autou anestē, “and as he said these things, he rose,” where Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 begin at anestē; KJV “And when he had thus spoken, the king rose up” and Geneva “And when he had thus spoken, the king rose up” carry that clause in sense (two of the seven), and the other five begin at the rising. Robinson–Pierpont also omits the particle te after the verb, which has no English of its own. The name Berenice (Greek Bernikē) stands in three verses of the New Testament: 25:13, where she arrives with Agrippa, 25:23, where they enter “with great display,” and here. In all three she is named beside the king and is given no words. On the king, see Agrippa II, and on her, Berenice; the shelf spells the name “Bernice,” and the shipped spelling here follows 25:13.

“The governor” (Acts 26:30). Ho hēgemōn, see hēgemōn, stands in six verses of Acts, hēgemōn in the forms of 23:24, 23:26, 23:33, 24:1, 24:10 and here. In the first five it is Felix, named at 23:24 and 23:26 and the unnamed hearer at 23:33, 24:1 and 24:10. Here the title stands without a name. Festus is named in this chapter at 26:24 (as the speaker), at 26:25 (where Paul addresses him) and in the next verse, 26:32, where Agrippa speaks to him; the reading of “the governor” as Festus rests on that context, together with 25:23, where it is Festus who gives the order that brings Paul in. It is not stated in the verse. The order of the list (king, governor, Berenice) is the order of the Greek, and the Greek does not say it is an order of rank.

“Those sitting with them” (Acts 26:30). Hoi synkathēmenoi autois. The participle synkathēmai stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and Mark 14:54, where Peter is “sitting with the attendants” in the high priest’s courtyard. Luke 22:55 has a related verb (synkathisantōn), which is a different form and is not counted. The seated company is described only at 25:23: tribunes “and the men of distinction in the city,” where Festus addresses the king and “all you men present with us” (25:24). Paul’s “all who hear me today” at 26:29 is spoken to the same room. Whether any of them sat as a council, or whether the room was a hearing and no more, the text does not say; Festus’s own words about the purpose of the occasion are at 25:26, “so that when the examination has taken place I may have something to write.”

“Having withdrawn, they were speaking to one another” (Acts 26:31). Kai anachōrēsantes elaloun pros allēlous. The root anachōreō stands in fourteen verses of the New Testament, twelve of them in Matthew, Mark and John, where it is mostly a retreat from danger or a leaving (see the entry anachōreō); in Acts it is only 23:19 and here. At 23:19 the tribune takes Paul’s nephew by the hand and “withdraws privately” to ask what he has to report, a withdrawal for a private talk before a decision, as here. The imperfect elaloun, “they were speaking,” is an ongoing talk. The plain imperfect with pros allēlous stands in two verses of the New Testament, Luke 2:15, where the shepherds say to one another “Let us go over to Bethlehem,” and here; the compounds synelaloun and dielaloun stand at Luke 4:36 and 6:11 (counted by reading the hits, not by the string). The nearest parallel in Acts is a council’s private conferring at 4:15, where the council sends the two men outside and “conferred with one another” (synebalon pros allēlous), the question there being what to do with them, and the outcome at 4:21 a release, “finding no way to punish them.” That conference is described as a council’s; the talk here is described only as speech among the people in the room, and the text does not say who in it spoke, how many agreed, or whether anyone did not. The shelf has several wordings for the withdrawal: NWT 1984 “as they withdrew” and ASV “when they had withdrawn” stay with the Greek verb; KJV “when they were gone aside” and Geneva “gone apart” make it a stepping-aside; NIV “After they left the room” adds the room, which the Greek does not name. TLB “As they talked it over afterwards” merges the two actions into one and moves them to a time after the leaving.

“Doing nothing worthy of death or bonds” (Acts 26:31). Ouden thanatou ē desmōn axion ti prassei ho anthrōpos houtos. The adjective axion with “of death” stands in five verses of the New Testament, counted by reading the nine verses that contain the adjective: Luke 23:15, where Pilate says of Jesus that “nothing deserving of death has been done by him,” and in Acts 23:29, 25:11, 25:25 and here. At 23:29 the tribune’s letter says he found Paul accused about questions of their law, “but having no accusation worthy of death or bonds,” and that is the pair in the order “death or bonds” that these people repeat; at 25:11 it is Paul’s own conditional, ei men oun adikō kai axion thanatou pepracha ti, with “worthy of death” inside an “if”; at 25:25 Festus says he found nothing worthy of death, without the bonds. Luke gives the same finding to other voices before it: the scribes of the Pharisees’ party say “We find nothing evil in this man” at 23:9, and Pilate says “I have found in him no guilt deserving of death” at Luke 23:22 (there with aition, “cause,” and not axion). Here the sentence comes from people who are not named, in a remark among themselves. The verb is the present prassei, “is doing,” where 25:11 and 25:25 use the perfect (pepracha, pepraxenai, “have done”); the room is not announcing a finding on a past act, it describes what the man is doing now. NWT 1984 “practices,” ASV, KJV and Geneva “doeth” and NIV “is not doing” keep the present; Douay “hath done” and TLB “hasn’t done” move it to the perfect (two of the seven). For desmōn, NWT 1984, ASV, KJV and Geneva print “bonds,” Douay prints “bands,” and NIV and TLB print “imprisonment”; the noun names the bonds themselves, and whether it is read as the chains or as the imprisonment they stand for is the choice the shelf makes differently. It is the noun Paul has just used of himself, “except these bonds,” at 26:29, so the audience’s phrase follows his. The apparatus has two notes. Robinson–Pierpont has axion ē desmōn, with the adjective placed before “or bonds,” where the printed text has ē desmōn axion; the order changes nothing in the sense. And ti, “at all,” before prassei is printed here as the reading of Nestle–Aland 28; Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont omit it. The saying is not a verdict. It is a remark made to one another, and the Greek records no vote, no sentence, no word from Festus and no word to Paul. Paul is not named in these three verses at all; the last verse that names him is 26:29.

“Could have been released” (Acts 26:32). Apolelysthai edynato ho anthrōpos houtos ei mē epekeklēto Kaisara, spoken by Agrippa to Festus. The perfect passive infinitive apolelysthai stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does the pluperfect epekeklēto, “he had appealed”: the form has no second occurrence to compare. The verb apolyō (see the entry apolyō) is the one for letting a person go in four other places where an authority does it: Pilate “had decided to release him” at 3:13; the council “let them go” at 4:21; the magistrates send the rod-bearers with “Release those men” at 16:35; and the tribune “let the young man go” at 23:22. Agrippa’s form is the perfect, the state of having been let go, and the verb of ability is the imperfect edynato, “was able”; the condition is ei mē with the pluperfect, “if he had not appealed.” The shelf has three groups for the release: NWT 1984 “released” alone matches the English printed here; ASV, KJV and Douay “set at liberty” make it a freedom; NIV and TLB “set free” do the same in modern English; and Geneva “loosed” keeps the picture of the loosing in lyō. TLB also writes “He could be set free if he hadn’t appealed,” a present “could be” where the others keep the past “could have been” or “might have been.” The appeal itself is Kaisara epikaloumai at 25:11, answered by Festus’s Kaisara epikekleisai, epi Kaisara poreusē, “You have appealed to Caesar; to Caesar you shall go,” at 25:12, and reported by Festus to Agrippa at 25:21 and 25:25 (there with ton Sebastōn, the Emperor; see sebastos and epikaleō). In the legal sense the verb stands in six verses of Acts, epikaleō in the forms of 25:11, 25:12, 25:21, 25:25 and this verse; the sixth is 28:19 (not yet on these pages). What Agrippa says is therefore the condition he sees as standing between Paul and release: the appeal, which Paul made himself. What the verse does not say is whether Festus could still have released him once the appeal was made, whether Agrippa is describing a rule of Roman procedure, a judgment on the case or a regret, or whether Agrippa would have released him. The Greek is a statement about what was possible, and it does not carry any finding of law. Paul’s own later telling of the appeal is at 28:19 (not yet on these pages).

The close of the chapter (Acts 26:30–32). The chapter opens with Agrippa’s permission, Epitrepetai soi hyper seautou legein, “You are permitted to speak for yourself” (26:1), and ends with the audience speaking among themselves and the king speaking to the governor; Paul, who has the floor through 26:29, is neither named nor quoted in these three. The talk of the room takes up the pairing already written in the tribune’s letter at 23:29, “death or bonds,” but it does not settle what Festus is to write: at 25:26 he has “nothing certain to write to my lord,” and 26:30–32 gives him a remark by the king and the room, not a charge. Paul’s two earlier defenses, 22:1–21 and 24:10–21, are answered in these verses by a remark among the hearers and one from the king, and no sentence is pronounced. What follows is at 27:1, and this note says only what 26:30–32 sets up: a hearing that ends with the audience talking among themselves, and Agrippa’s remark about release and the appeal.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “permitted” for epitrepetai at v1, the verb of 21:39 and 21:40; “accused” for egkaloumai at v2 and v7, the verb of 19:38 and 23:29; “customs” and “questions” for ethōn and zētēmatōn at v3, the nouns of 25:16 and 25:19; “manner of life” for biōsis at v4, a noun the New Testament uses once; “party” for hairesis at v5, the noun of 15:5 and 24:14, beside “form of worship” for thrēskeia, kept apart from the “religion” of 25:19 (deisidaimonia); “holy ones” at v10, the fourth Acts verse after 9:13, 9:32 and 9:41; “cast my vote” for katēnegka psēphon at v10; “commission” for epitropē at v12, a noun used once; “brightness” for lamprotēs at v13, where 22:6 has “considerable light”; “goads” for kentra at v14; “appoint” for procheirisasthai at v16, the verb that 22:14 prints “appointed … beforehand”; “servant” for hypēretēs at v16 against the “attendant” of 13:5; “rescuing” for exairoumenos at v17, the verb of 23:27; “a lot” for klēron at v18 beside the “inheritance” of 20:32; “tried” for epeirōnto at v21, a different Greek verb from the “tried” of 24:6; “subject to suffering” for pathētos at v23; “out of your mind” for mainē at v24, as at 12:15; “a little time” for en oligō at v28 and v29, where the Greek has no word for “time”; “released” for apolelysthai at v32, the verb of 23:22 and 4:21.

Echoes paid. The road to Damascus, told a third time — 9:3–8 and 22:6–11, at v12–15, with who stands, who falls and who hears set side by side. The Hebrew dialect — 21:40 and 22:2, at v14. The prophets and Moses — 24:14 and 3:18, at v22–23. The Christ who suffers — 17:3, at v23. Light for the nations — 13:47, at v23. The persecuting — 8:3 and 22:19, at v9–11. The seizure in the temple — 21:27–31 and 24:6, at v21. The words about boldness — 9:27, 13:46 and 19:8, at v26. “Out of your mind” — 12:15, at v24. “Most excellent” — 23:26 and 24:3, at v25. Christian — 11:26, at v28. Death or bonds — 23:29 and 25:25, at v31. The appeal to Caesar — 25:11, 25:12 and 25:21, at v32. The king, the governor and Berenice — 25:13 and 25:23, at v30.
