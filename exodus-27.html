Notes on this translation

⚠ A bronze altar with horns ‘of one piece,’ and a hollow shell built to keep an older law

Every metal in the tabernacle complex has so far been GOLD — the ark, its cover, the table, the lampstand, all pure gold or gold-overlaid (Exodus 25, already on these pages), with SILVER only at the sockets holding up the walls (26:19–25, already on these pages). This altar, standing OUTSIDE the tent in the open court, is acacia wood overlaid with KJV ‘brass,’ NIV/ESV/NASB ‘bronze,’ NWT ‘copper’ (nechoshet — three genuinely different renderings for one word, reflecting a real uncertainty over the exact alloy meant). This chapter completes a pattern running since chapter 25: gold nearest the ark, silver at the walls holding the sanctuary up, bronze at the boundary facing the camp.

⚠ Its horns (v2) are ‘of one piece with it’ — hammered or cast as part of the altar itself, never bolted on — and they become one of the most consequential architectural details in the whole Hebrew Bible. Fleeing men grasp them for asylum: Adonijah first (1 Kings 1:50–51, already on these pages), then Joab, dragged away from them anyway (1 Kings 2:28–34, not yet on these pages). Their removal is itself a sentence of judgment — ‘the horns of the altar shall be cut off, and fall to the ground’ (Amos 3:14, not yet on these pages). None of that is visible yet in this bare construction order; it is worth knowing it is coming.

‘Hollow, of boards, you shall make it’ (v8) is the detail that lets a built, portable altar keep an older, plainer law. Three chapters back, Israel was told to build God an altar of bare earth, or of unhewn stone never touched by a tool (Exodus 20:24–26, already on these pages). The standard reconciliation offered for the two laws together: a hollow wood-and-bronze shell, filled with earth or stones at each new encampment, satisfies the letter of the older law without a fresh altar built from scratch at every stop. ⚠ And the word for ‘boards’ here is not keresh, the wall-timber word from last chapter (26:15, already on these pages) — it is luchot, the SAME word that just named the two tablets of stone Moses was promised on the mountain (24:12, already on these pages). Stone tablets and wooden altar-planks, three chapters apart, share one Hebrew noun for a flat panel.

A wall made of cloth, and a gate that borrows the tent’s own plainer weave

The tabernacle’s entire outer boundary — a hundred cubits by fifty, roughly a hundred and fifty feet by seventy-five — is a single course of ‘fine twined linen,’ hung from sixty pillars (vv9–18). Nothing about it is defensive; the only thing separating holy ground from the ordinary camp is cloth on a cord, five cubits high, with no gate but the one on the east. A court this size and this construction announces a boundary rather than enforcing one. ⚠ Verse 18’s width, ‘fifty by fifty’ (chamishim ba-chamishim), is an odd repetition in the Hebrew — most likely confirming that the court is a true rectangle, fifty cubits at BOTH its east and west ends (matching vv12–13), rather than tapering to a trapezoid.

⚠ That one gate (v16) is a twenty-cubit ‘work of an embroiderer’ — the SAME one-faced rokem craft, not the two-faced choshev weave, as the tent’s own outer door screen (26:36, already on these pages). The pattern promised when that distinction was first drawn now completes itself: cherubim are reserved for what stands DEEPEST inside — the tabernacle’s own inner curtains (26:1) and the veil before the ark (26:31) — while both outer doorways, the tent’s and now the court’s, get the plainer embroidery and no woven guardians at all. A worshiper entering the gate sees only color.

The metal gradient noted at the altar continues here, and it isn’t new: the tabernacle’s own walls stood in silver sockets (26:19, already on these pages), but even there, the one piece already facing outward — the tent’s own door screen — stood in bronze (26:37, already on these pages). This court simply extends that same outermost-bronze rule around its whole perimeter: every socket here, all sixty of them, is bronze (vv10, 17, 18), even where the hooks and connecting bands above them are silver. Bronze marks the boundary facing the camp; silver, what stands just inside it; gold, only the ark and its own room.

⚠ The people’s turn, and a flame that is tended, not merely left

Every instruction since chapter 25 has told MOSES what to build; this is the first that tells the PEOPLE what to bring, and the object is not gold or fabric but fuel — ‘pure, beaten olive oil,’ the highest grade, for the lamp already specified two chapters back (25:31–37, already on these pages). ‘Continually’ (tamid) is scoped by the very next clause: Aaron and his sons tend it ‘from evening to morning’ (v21) — every single night, without fail, not a flame left to burn unattended around the clock. The synagogue’s modern ‘Ner Tamid,’ an eternal light that genuinely never goes out, takes its name from this phrase but not, originally, its exact nightly practice. (The printed Hebrew source also marks something this English can only note in prose: the traditional synagogue reading cycle opens its next weekly portion, Tetzaveh, exactly at v20 — a real seam in how this chapter is read aloud, invisible in a continuous English translation.)

⚠ ‘An everlasting statute throughout their generations’ (v21) is the identical Hebrew formula — chukkat olam le-dorotam — that first sealed the Passover, three chapters back (12:14, already on these pages). The same binding language that fixed one night’s meal into a permanent yearly feast now fixes one lamp into a permanent nightly duty — and it is the FIRST standing task ever assigned to Aaron and his sons by name, a chapter before either of them is even given the garments that will make them priests (Exodus 28, already on these pages).

‘In the tent of meeting, outside the veil that is before the testimony’ places the lamp precisely: outside the same veil that, one chapter back, was hung to guard ‘the ark of the testimony’ (26:33, already on these pages) — close enough to serve the Most Holy Place, never inside it. KJV calls this space the ‘tabernacle of the CONGREGATION,’ using the same English word, ‘tabernacle,’ that it also uses for the whole sanctuary structure (mishkan) — collapsing two distinct Hebrew nouns into one confusing English one. This translation keeps them apart: ‘tabernacle’ for mishkan, ‘tent of meeting’ for ohel mo’ed, the same distinction ASV, NIV, and NWT already keep.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. ‘Bronze’ kept for nechoshet throughout, the middle course between KJV’s archaic ‘brass’ and NWT’s ‘copper,’ per the note above. ‘Everlasting statute’ kept for chukkat olam (v21), matching this book’s own earlier use of the phrase at 12:14. ‘Tent of meeting’ kept for ohel mo’ed, distinct from ‘tabernacle’ for mishkan, against KJV’s conflating ‘tabernacle of the congregation.’ Verse numbering follows the Hebrew exactly; Hebrew and English Exodus 27 both run 1–21.

A note on order: Exodus stands on these pages at chapters 1–34 — the tabernacle’s furniture (25), its own structure (26), the altar and courtyard enclosing it plus the priesthood’s first standing duty (27), and now Exodus 28’s own priestly garments. Exodus 29, the ordination ceremony, Exodus 30, the incense altar and the half-shekel census, and Exodus 31, the naming of Bezalel and Oholiab and the sabbath command that closes the whole instruction, and Exodus 32, the golden calf and Moses’ intercession, and Exodus 33, Moses’ plea to see Jehovah’s glory and his glimpse of God’s back from the cleft of the rock, and Exodus 34, the covenant remade and Moses’ own face left shining, are now on these pages too. Exodus 35, the people’s own gifts for the tabernacle at last brought forward, remains open, along with the rest of the book.
