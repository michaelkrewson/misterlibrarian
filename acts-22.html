From the steps of the barracks Paul addresses the crowd in the Hebrew dialect as “Men, brothers and fathers,” and they give the more quiet. He says he is a Jewish man born in Tarsus of Cilicia and brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel, a zealot of God, who persecuted this Way to the death, binding men and women, with letters from the high priest and the body of elders. About noon, near Damascus, a considerable light flashes around him; he falls, hears “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?” and “I am Jesus the Nazarene”; those with him behold the light but do not hear the voice of the one speaking, and he is led by the hand into Damascus. There Ananias, a devout man well spoken of by all the Jews who live there, says “Saul, brother, recover your sight,” tells him the God of our fathers has appointed him beforehand to see the Righteous One and to be a witness to all people, and asks why he delays: “be baptized and wash away your sins, calling on his name.” Back in Jerusalem, praying in the temple, Paul falls into a trance and hears “Hurry and get out of Jerusalem,” answers that they know he imprisoned and beat believers and stood by approving at the death of Stephen, the Lord’s witness, and is told “Go, because I will send you out far away to the nations.” The crowd listens up to this word, then cries “Away from the earth with such a man!” and flings garments and dust into the air. The tribune orders him into the barracks to be examined by flogging; when they have stretched him out with the straps Paul asks the centurion whether it is lawful to flog a Roman and uncondemned, the tribune learns that Paul was born a citizen and is afraid, and on the next day, wishing to know the certainty of the charge, he releases Paul and sets him before the chief priests and the whole Sanhedrin.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are the day of the speech, the same day as the riot of Acts 21; ‘about noon’ on the road to Damascus (v6); ‘at that very hour’ for the return of Paul’s sight (v13); and ‘on the next day’ (v30), when the tribune releases him. Luke gives no total for the years between the road to Damascus and this speech, and this library dates the events no more closely than the book does. It follows Acts 21, where the Jews from Asia seize Paul in the temple; Acts 23 is not yet on these pages.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · Ἄνδρες ἀδελφοὶ καὶ πατέρες … ἀπολογίας … τῇ Ἑβραΐδι διαλέκτῳ … μᾶλλον παρέσχον ἡσυχίαν Andres adelphoi kai pateres … apologias … tē Hebraidi dialektō … mallon parescon hēsychian — Acts 22:1–2: what is Paul’s “defense” (apologia) on the temple steps, is the “Hebrew dialect” Hebrew or Aramaic, and why does the crowd go quieter still?

“Men, brothers and fathers” (Acts 22:1). Andres adelphoi kai pateres is the opening of Stephen’s speech to the council at 7:2, word for word: the high priest has asked “Are these things so?” (7:1) and Stephen begins “Men, brothers and fathers, listen.” The three-word address adelphoi kai pateres stands in two verses of the New Testament, 7:2 and here. The shorter address andres adelphoi, “men, brothers,” stands in fourteen verses, all in Acts, though not all are openings of speeches. At 2:37 it is a question put to Peter, and at 7:26 Moses uses it in the sentence “Men, you are brothers.” As an opening it is Peter’s at 1:16 and 2:29 and 15:7, James’s at 15:13, and Paul’s at 13:26 and 13:38, with further openings at 23:1, 23:6 and 28:17. Only the three-word form adds “fathers,” and it has two speakers, Stephen and Paul. Luke does not comment on the repetition. The reader of 22:1 has already met Saul at the feet of the witnesses who stoned Stephen (7:58), and Paul will tell that scene himself at 22:20. Whether the shared address is deliberate, Luke does not say. “Fathers” is not defined in the Greek; the word is the ordinary plural of “father,” and which men in the crowd it honors, the elders or all the older men, is not stated. The shelf divides on whether to print the first word. NWT 1984 “Men, brothers and fathers,” KJV and Douay “Men, brethren, and fathers” and Geneva “Ye men, brethren and fathers” keep andres; ASV “Brethren and fathers,” NIV “Brothers and fathers” and TLB “Brothers and fathers” drop it, so three of the seven print the address without “men.” This translation keeps it, as it does at 7:2 and 2:29. The verb is akousate mou, “listen,” with a genitive; 15:13 has the same two words, “listen to me.”

“Defense” (Acts 22:1). Apologia is a speech made in answer to an accusation, in a court or something like one. The noun apologia stands in eight verses of the New Testament: here; 25:16, where Festus says it is not Roman custom to hand a man over before the accused has met his accusers face to face and been given “a chance to make a defense”; 1 Corinthians 9:3 and 2 Corinthians 7:11 (neither yet on these pages); Philippians 1:7 and 1:16, where Paul writes of the “defense” of the gospel; 2 Timothy 4:16 (not yet on these pages), “my first defense”; and 1 Peter 3:15 (not yet on these pages), where believers are to be ready “for a defense” to anyone who asks for an account of the hope in them. That last verse has the noun with logon, “account.” The verb apologeomai sits beside it in Acts, at 19:33 and at 24:10, and at 25:8, and at 26:2 and 26:24, and in Luke 12:11 and 21:14, where Jesus tells his followers not to be anxious about how they will defend themselves (12:11) and not to prepare their defense beforehand (21:14). The nearest scene to this one is 19:33. There Alexander, “motioning with his hand,” wished “to make a defense” to the people of Ephesus, and the next verse says that when they saw he was a Jew one voice rose from the crowd, crying out for about two hours, “Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!” (19:34). Paul has just motioned with his hand in the same way (21:40), and here, on the Greek of 22:2, the crowd gives him quiet. The word sets this scene as a trial, though no charge has been laid before a court: the people on the steps had been beating him (21:31–32), and the one who might try him, the tribune, is standing by. Pros hymas is literally “toward you.” This translation prints “before you,” the ordinary English for one who answers an accuser, and Geneva “now towards you” keeps the plain preposition. For the word itself, KJV “my defence,” ASV “the defence,” NWT 1984 “my defense,” NIV “my defense” and TLB “my defense” agree with “defense,” and so does Geneva “my defense,” six of the seven; Douay “the account which I now give” is the only one of the seven that names a report in place of a plea. TLB “as I offer my defense” makes the speech an offering, which the noun does not say.

“The Hebrew dialect” (Acts 22:2). Prosephōnei autois tē Hebraidi dialektō, “he was addressing them in the Hebrew dialect.” The verb is the imperfect prosephōnei, “was addressing,” where 21:40 has the aorist prosephōnēsen, “addressed”: there the speech begins, and here the crowd hears it going on. The language is the one the site raised at 21:40, and the facts there stand. Hebrais stands in three verses of the New Testament, 21:40, here and 26:14, where Paul says the voice on the road spoke to him tē Hebraidi dialektō; the voice in the first telling at 9:4 is said to be “saying to him, ‘Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?’” without a language named. Dialektos stands in six verses, all in Acts: 1:19, 2:6, 2:8, 21:40, here and 26:14. Whether the language is Hebrew or the Aramaic of Judea and Galilee, the Greek does not say, and the question is argued; each reading is held by serious scholars, and it is reported here, not decided on this page. The shelf answers in two ways. KJV, Geneva and Douay “the Hebrew tongue,” ASV and NWT 1984 “the Hebrew language” and TLB “in Hebrew” print Hebrew (six of the seven), as they did at 21:40; NIV “in Aramaic” prints Aramaic, the other reading. What the sentence does is plain: a Roman tribune had asked Hellēnisti ginōskeis;, “Do you know Greek?” (21:37), and Paul, who says he is a citizen of Tarsus (21:39), answers the crowd in another. Luke does not say who in the crowd could follow Greek and who needed this one, and the Greek says only that when they heard it they gave more quiet.

“They gave the more quiet” (Acts 22:2). Mallon parescon hēsychian is literally “they furnished more quiet,” and the verb with this noun is, as lexicons give it, an idiom for keeping still; that is reported here, not examined on this page. Mallon is the comparative, “more”: the crowd is already quiet, since “a great hush had come about” before Paul spoke (pollēs sigēs genomenēs, 21:40), and the Hebrew dialect adds to it. That is a different word from the one at 21:40: sigē is a hush, and hēsychia is quiet of a steadier kind. The noun stands in four verses of the New Testament: here, 2 Thessalonians 3:12, where those who “work with quietness” eat their own bread, and 1 Timothy 2:11 and 2:12 (all three not yet on these pages). The verb hēsychazō stands in five verses, among them 11:18 and 21:14, both rendered “quieted down” on these pages: the Jerusalem believers at 11:18, after Peter’s account, and Paul’s companions at 21:14, when he would not be persuaded not to go up (see hēsychazō). The verb and the noun share a root, and Luke uses them of very different groups. KJV, Geneva and Douay “kept the more silence” take the comparative and print “silence,” the English that does not mark the difference between sigē and hēsychia; ASV “were the more quiet” and NWT 1984 “kept all the more silent” and TLB “the silence was even greater” take it too, so six of the seven read mallon as a comparative; NIV “became very quiet” turns it into an intensifier and loses the contrast with the hush already there. The verse ends kai phēsin, “And he says,” a present tense in a past narrative; the Greek has no subject there, where NIV “Then Paul said” supplies one and puts the verb in the past. The apparatus prints no note on either verse (its first note on this chapter is at v3), so every word of vv1–2 stands as printed by Westcott–Hort, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont alike.

Verses 3–5 · ἀνατεθραμμένος … παρὰ τοὺς πόδας Γαμαλιήλ … κατὰ ἀκρίβειαν τοῦ πατρῴου νόμου … ζηλωτὴς ὑπάρχων τοῦ θεοῦ … ταύτην τὴν ὁδὸν ἐδίωξα ἄχρι θανάτου … ἄνδρας τε καὶ γυναῖκας … πᾶν τὸ πρεσβυτέριον … ἵνα τιμωρηθῶσιν anatethrammenos … para tous podas Gamaliēl … kata akribeian tou patrōiou nomou … zēlōtēs hyparchōn tou theou … tautēn tēn hodon ediōxa achri thanatou … andras te kai gynaikas … pan to presbyterion … hina timōrēthōsin — Paul “at the feet of Gamaliel” in Acts 22:3–5: what do “strictness of the ancestral law,” “zealot of God” and “this Way to the death” claim, and who are the “body of elders” who gave him letters?

Born in Tarsus, brought up in this city (Acts 22:3). Gegennēmenos en Tarsō tēs Kilikias, anatethrammenos de en tē polei tautē sets two perfect participles against each other with de: “having been born in Tarsus of Cilicia, but having been brought up in this city.” “This city” is the one Paul is standing in; the verse does not name it. The name Tarsos stands in five verses of the New Testament: 9:11 (“a Tarsian”), 9:30 (the brothers send him off there), 11:25 (Barnabas goes there to search for him), 21:39 and here. At 21:39 Paul told the tribune “a Tarsian of Cilicia, a citizen of a city not without distinction”; here the birthplace is given without the civic boast. Kilikia stands in eight verses: Acts 6:9, 15:23, 15:41, 21:39, this verse and Galatians 1:21 are on these pages, and the other two are 23:34 and 27:5. The first of them is the one to notice: at 6:9 men “from Cilicia and Asia” rise up and dispute with Stephen. Luke does not say that Saul was among them, and the two places are only set side by side here. For the city and the province see Tarsus and Cilicia. The verb anatrephō stands in three verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 7:20 (Moses “brought up three months in his father’s house”), 7:21 (Pharaoh’s daughter “brought him up for herself as a son”) and here. Stephen’s next sentence about Moses uses the other verb of this verse: 7:22 epaideuthē, “was educated in all the wisdom of the Egyptians,” and paideuō stands in two verses of Acts, 7:22 and this one (pepaideumenos, “educated,” later in v3). Luke gives Moses and Paul the same pair, brought up and then educated, and neither passage remarks on it. KJV “brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel,” ASV, Geneva and Douay keep “brought up” as well, and NIV “brought up in this city.” NWT 1984 “educated in this city at the feet of” spends “educated” on this first participle and “instructed” on pepaideumenos, where this translation has “brought up” and “educated”; TLB “educated here in Jerusalem under Gamaliel” also names the city, which the Greek does not. The apparatus has one note in this verse: Egō is printed by Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and Robinson–Pierpont reads Egō men. The added men would give the sentence the “on the one hand” that the later de answers (21:39 prints men in the same formula); the English is the same either way, apart from a possible “indeed.” Verses 4 and 5 carry no apparatus note.

At the feet of Gamaliel (Acts 22:3). Para tous podas Gamaliēl is the promise made at Acts 5:34, where Gamaliel rises in the Sanhedrin as “a Pharisee named Gamaliel, a teacher of the law honored by all the people,” and his name, Gamaliēl, stands in two verses of the New Testament, 5:34 and this one. The phrase para tous podas stands in ten verses of the New Testament, and the feet in each belong to someone: Jesus in five (Matthew 15:30, Luke 7:38, 8:35, 8:41, 17:16), the apostles in three (Acts 4:35, 4:37, 5:2, where the proceeds of sales are laid), Saul in one (7:58), and Gamaliel here. The two Saul verses are the ones to hold together: at 7:58 the witnesses “laid down their garments at the feet of a young man called Saul,” and in this verse he is the one placed at another man’s feet. Luke does not draw the line between them. The Greek has no verb of sitting or learning attached to the feet; the phrase says where, and the sense of a pupil’s place comes from the participles of upbringing and education that surround it. The punctuation also decides what the feet belong to. In the Greek printed here the comma falls after Gamaliēl, so that “brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel” is one stretch and “educated according to the strictness …” begins the next, which is how this translation reads it. KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay print the same “at the feet of Gamaliel” after “brought up,” and NWT 1984 “at the feet of” does the same after “educated.” NIV ends a sentence after “brought up in this city” and starts the next with “I studied under Gamaliel,” losing the feet; TLB “under Gamaliel, at whose feet I learned” keeps the feet but turns them into a relative clause with a verb of learning that the Greek does not have. See Gamaliel.

Strictness and the ancestral law (Acts 22:3). Kata akribeian tou patrōiou nomou is “according to the strictness of the ancestral law.” The noun akribeia stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; the family is wider, from akribōs, “accurately,” in Acts 18:25 (Apollos “teaching accurately the things about Jesus”) to akribesteron, “more accurately,” in 18:26 (Priscilla and Aquila setting out “the way of God more accurately”). The root means exactness, a precision that leaves nothing loose, and “strictness” is the English word that fits when the thing measured is a law kept to the letter. The measure applies to the law, and the verse does not say how Gamaliel himself taught; the speech he gave at 5:38–39 counsels leaving the apostles alone, and Luke does not connect that speech with Paul’s schooling. Paul uses the same family again of the Pharisees, kata tēn akribestatēn hairesin, in 26:5, and says he was a Pharisee in 23:6. The adjective patrōios stands in three verses of the New Testament, all of them Paul’s own words in Acts: here, tō patrōiō theō in 24:14 and tois ethesi tois patrōiois in 28:17. A different adjective from the same noun, patrikos, stands in one verse, Galatians 1:14, where he was “far more zealous for the traditions of my fathers.” “Ancestral” is the English that keeps the root “father” without the weight of “fathers” as a set phrase. NWT 1984 “instructed according to the strictness of the ancestral Law” matches this translation word for word, apart from a capital on “Law.” ASV “the strict manner of the law of our fathers” keeps the exactness; KJV “the perfect manner of the law of the fathers” and Geneva “the perfect manner of the Law of the fathers” read akribeia as “perfect”; Douay “taught according to the truth of the law of the fathers” says “truth,” a divergence that Douay’s Latin base may explain (reported here, not checked on this page); NIV “thoroughly trained in the law of our ancestors” and TLB “very carefully” put the exactness on the learner instead.

Zealot of God (Acts 22:3). Zēlōtēs hyparchōn tou theou kathōs pantes hymeis este sēmeron is “being a zealot of God, just as all of you are today.” The noun zēlōtēs stands in eight verses of the New Testament: Luke 6:15, Acts 1:13, 21:20, this verse, 1 Corinthians 14:12, Galatians 1:14, Titus 2:14 and 1 Peter 3:13. The note at 21:20 set out all eight and said that here Paul would call himself by the word the elders use of the believing Jews, zēlōtai tou nomou. In this verse the object changes, and so does the speaker’s claim: the zeal is for God, not for the law, and the second half of the clause, “as all of you are today,” puts the crowd that had been “seeking to kill him” (21:31) inside the same word. Whether that is a point of common ground, a courtesy or a rebuke, the Greek does not say; it says sēmeron, “today,” and no more. The same participle phrase is in Galatians 1:14, zēlōtēs hyparchōn with the traditions of his fathers as its object; Acts 21:20 has the plural zēlōtai with hyparchousin and the law as its object. Whether “zealot” here already names a party is the same open question as at 21:20: in Luke 6:15 and Acts 1:13 it is Simon’s title, and the Greek of this verse is a plain noun of zeal. This translation keeps the noun; the shelf turns it into an adjective. KJV “zealous toward God” and Geneva “zealous toward God,” ASV “being zealous for God,” NWT 1984 “being zealous for God,” NIV “zealous for God,” and TLB “very anxious to honor God.” Douay “zealous for the law” has the law where the Greek has tou theou; the apparatus has no note on those two words, so the difference lies in the version, not in the printed Greek.

This Way, to the death, men and women (Acts 22:4). Tautēn tēn hodon ediōxa achri thanatou is “I persecuted this Way to the death,” and the Greek has the demonstrative, “this Way,” where 9:2 had the genitive “of the Way.” The name stands at 9:2, 19:9 and 19:23 on these pages, here, and at 24:14 and 24:22; the Greek does not say who named it, or why a road. The verb diōkō stands in nine verses of Acts: 7:52 (the fathers “persecuted” the prophets), 9:4 and 9:5 (the voice: “why are you persecuting me?”), here, 22:7 and 22:8 (the same two sentences, retold), and 26:11, 26:14 and 26:15. Paul’s own use of it at 22:4 is therefore the one the voice answers at 22:7: what he did to “this Way” is what he is asked about, “why are you persecuting me?” Achri thanatou stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Revelation 2:10 (“Be faithful until death”) and 12:11 (“did not love their lives even to death”). Achri is “as far as,” a limit reached; the Greek does not say whether it means that Paul pursued the Way as far as death, so that some died, or that his pursuit had no ceiling. The only death Acts has told by this point is Stephen’s (7:58), and Paul will speak of it at 22:20. NWT 1984 “this Way to the death” matches this translation; ASV “this Way unto the death,” KJV “this way unto the death” and Douay “this way unto death” stay closer to the Greek form; NIV “followers of this Way to their death” adds “followers” and makes the death theirs; TLB “persecuted the Christians, hounding them to death” replaces “this Way” with a name Acts first records at 11:26 and adds “hounding.” “Binding and handing over to prisons both men and women” is desmeuōn kai paradidous eis phylakas andras te kai gynaikas. Andras te kai gynaikas stands in two verses of the New Testament, 9:2 and this one, and 8:3 has the same pair without te. The prisons are plural here (phylakas), where 8:3 has a single prison (phylakēn). KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV, NWT 1984 and TLB all print “both men and women,” seven versions of seven, which is the one phrase of the verse the English shelf shares. See hodos.

The high priest, the body of elders, the letters (Acts 22:5). Hōs kai ho archiereus martyrei moi kai pan to presbyterion is “as the high priest also bears me witness, and the whole body of elders.” Presbyterion stands in three verses of the New Testament: Luke 22:66, where “the council of elders of the people assembled, chief priests and scribes alike,” this verse, and 1 Timothy 4:14 (not yet on these pages). At Luke 22:66 the word names a gathering that has the chief priests and scribes within it and then leads Jesus into “their own Sanhedrin”; the verse here pairs the high priest with the presbyterion and does not say whether he sits inside it. The verb martyrei is present tense: the high priest “bears me witness” as Paul speaks, and the Greek does not say whether he is the high priest of the years of the letters; Luke names no high priest in this verse, as he names none at 9:1. Set beside 9:1–2, the two tellings differ and are not harmonized here. In chapter 9 Saul “went to the high priest” and “asked from him letters … to the synagogues”; in this verse he “received” (dexamenos) letters par’ hōn, “from whom,” a plural that takes in both the high priest and the body of elders, and the letters go pros tous adelphous, “to the brothers.” The brothers in Damascus are not the people to be arrested, who are the next group (tous ekeise ontas, “those who were there”), so they are the recipients; the Greek does not say more about them than that Paul calls them by the word he used of this crowd in 22:1. Chapter 9 says nothing of a purpose clause; here the errand ends hina timōrēthōsin, “so that they might be punished.” See presbyterion, the high priest and Damascus. NWT 1984 “all the assembly of older men” turns presbyterion into the people who make it up (the NWT 2013 revision prints “all the assembly of elders”); KJV “all the estate of the elders” and ASV “all the estate of the elders” keep the elders; Geneva “all the company of the Elders” has “company”; Douay “all the ancients” has the older English for the same men; NIV “all the Council” and TLB “any member of the Council” give the body its court name, a word the Greek of this verse does not use (Luke’s court word is synedrion, which this chapter has at 22:30). For the letters, KJV “letters unto the brethren” and ASV “letters unto the brethren” and NWT 1984 “letters to the brothers in Damascus” keep the family word, while NIV “associates in Damascus” and TLB “the Jewish leaders in Damascus” supply a description the Greek leaves out.

“Punished” (Acts 22:5). Timōrēthōsin is the passive of timōreō, and the Greek does not say who punishes or what the penalty is. The root timōr- stands in three verses of the New Testament: the verb here and in 26:11 (“punishing them often”), and the noun timōria in Hebrews 10:29 (“how much worse punishment,” not yet on these pages). What the Greek fixes is the order of events: letters first, then arrest in Damascus, then Jerusalem, then the punishment. The verse sets the destination and the aim and says nothing of a sentence. KJV “for to be punished,” ASV “in bonds to be punished,” Geneva “that they might be punished,” Douay “to be punished,” NWT 1984 “to be punished,” NIV “to be punished” and TLB “to be punished” make seven of seven.

Three accounts of the same years (Acts 22:3–5, Galatians 1:13–14, 1 Timothy 1:13). Paul looks back on his earlier life in three places that are on these pages, and each gives it a different object and a different aim. In Galatians 1:13 (“beyond all measure I was persecuting the congregation of God and trying to destroy it”) the object is the congregation, the verb is the imperfect ediōkon (“I was persecuting”) and the point is his place in “Judaism”; in v14 he “was advancing in Judaism beyond many of my own age” and was “far more zealous for the traditions of my fathers.” In Acts 22:4 the object is “this Way,” the verb is the aorist ediōxa and the point is his credentials with a Jerusalem crowd: schooling, strictness, zeal, and the testimony of the high priest and the body of elders. In 1 Timothy 1:13 he was “a blasphemer and a persecutor and a violent man,” and the sentence turns on mercy and ignorance: “I acted ignorantly in unbelief.” The noun diōktēs, “persecutor,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, 1 Timothy 1:13. The three passages do not contradict one another, and no one of them is made to speak for the others: Acts 22 says nothing of “ignorance,” 1 Timothy says nothing of Gamaliel or the letters, and Galatians 1 names Damascus (1:17) and Cilicia (1:21) but not Tarsus. What they share is the verb diōkō and a man who counts the persecution as part of the account he gives of himself, each time for a different listener.

Verses 6–11 · περὶ μεσημβρίαν … φῶς ἱκανὸν … περιαστράψαι … εἰς τὸ ἔδαφος … Ἰησοῦς ὁ Ναζωραῖος … τὸ μὲν φῶς ἐθεάσαντο τὴν δὲ φωνὴν οὐκ ἤκουσαν … Τί ποιήσω, κύριε … ὧν τέτακταί σοι ποιῆσαι … ἀπὸ τῆς δόξης τοῦ φωτὸς … χειραγωγούμενος peri mesēmbrian … phōs hikanon … periastrapsai … eis to edaphos … Iēsous ho Nazōraios … to men phōs etheasanto tēn de phōnēn ouk ēkousan … Ti poiēsō, kyrie … hōn tetaktai soi poiēsai … apo tēs doxēs tou phōtos … cheiragōgoumenos — Paul’s Damascus road story told a second time in Acts 22:6–11: did his companions hear the voice, why is Jesus “the Nazarene,” and what is “appointed” for him to do?

About noon, a considerable light (Acts 22:6). Peri mesēmbrian is “about noon.” The noun mesēmbria stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 8:26, where the same noun in kata mesēmbrian is printed “toward the south,” because the word names both the noon hour and the quarter of the sky where the sun stands at noon. Acts 9:3 gives no hour at all; Paul’s later telling has hēmeras mesēs, “of the middle of the day,” and a light “beyond the brightness of the sun” (26:13). The light is phōs hikanon, and hikanos measures an amount before it measures anything else (dictionary): elsewhere in Acts the same stem is attached to days (9:23), a length of time (14:3), lamps (20:8, “many lamps”) and weeping (20:37, “considerable weeping”). “Considerable” keeps that sense of quantity here without saying how bright. The verb periastraptō, “flash around,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 9:3, and the two sentences are built differently. At 9:3 Luke says exaiphnēs te auton periēstrapsen phōs ek tou ouranou, a light “flashed around him” with a finite verb. Here Paul says Egeneto de moi poreuomenō kai eggizonti tē Damaskō, “it happened to me, as I was traveling and drawing near to Damascus,” and the verb is an infinitive, periastrapsai phōs hikanon peri eme, with “around” both in the verb and in the phrase after it: “flashed around … around me.” Both tellings say the light came ek tou ouranou, “out of heaven.” The apparatus lists no variant in vv6, 7, 10 or 11. KJV, ASV and Geneva each print “noon” and “a great light round about me”; Douay “at midday” and “shone round about me a great light”; NWT 1984 “about midday” and “a great light flashed all around me.” NIV “a bright light from heaven flashed around me” and TLB “a very bright light from heaven shone around me” turn a quantity into a brightness, which is what the Greek does not say here. Only the NWT 1984 and NIV keep “flashed,” the verb of lightning; the others have “shone.”

The ground (Acts 22:7). Paul says epesa te eis to edaphos, “and I fell to the ground.” The noun edaphos stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. At 9:4 the Greek is epi tēn gēn and at 9:8 apo tēs gēs, the ordinary word for earth and land, and Paul’s later telling has eis tēn gēn (26:14). This translation prints “ground” for both words, so an English reader cannot see that Paul here chooses a different one; the word gē is the one printed “earth” at 22:22, “Away from the earth with such a man.” Reference works report that edaphos is a base or floor, the surface one stands on, and that it is the noun behind the verb edaphizō at Luke 19:44, “level you to the ground”; that is reported here and not verified. Luke gives no reason for the change of word. What the voice says is the same as at 9:4 down to the last syllable: Saoul Saoul, ti me diōkeis?, “Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?” (see the note on 9:3–6 for the doubled name and the pronoun “me”). Only the participle changes: phōnēn … legousan autō, “a voice saying to him,” there, phōnēs legousēs moi, “a voice saying to me,” here, with “voice” in the genitive (taken up at v9 below). KJV and ASV “unto the ground,” Douay “on the ground,” NWT 1984, NIV and TLB “to the ground” agree with this translation; Geneva “unto the earth” prints the word it would print for gē.

“Jesus the Nazarene” (Acts 22:8). Tis ei, kyrie?, “Who are you, Lord?”, is the question of 9:5 word for word, and the answer is Egō eimi Iēsous ho Nazōraios hon su diōkeis, “I am Jesus the Nazarene, whom you are persecuting.” At 9:5 the name stands bare, as the note on 9:3–6 says; ho Nazōraios is the addition. The word Nazōraios stands in thirteen verses of the New Testament, and in seven verses of Acts (Nazōraios): 2:22, 3:6, 4:10, 6:14, this verse, 24:5 (of the “party of the Nazarenes”) and 26:9. Of the thirteen, this is the one in which the title is in the mouth of Jesus himself; everywhere else it is said by Peter, Paul, a crowd, a servant girl, Pilate or an opponent (a count reached by reading the hits). A second form, Nazarēnos, stands in six verses, all in Mark and Luke and none in Acts: Mark 1:24, 10:47, 14:67, 16:6, Luke 4:34 and 24:19. Matthew ties Nazōraios to the town of Nazareth and to the prophets (2:23, “he shall be called a Nazarene”), and this translation prints “the Nazarene” for the word everywhere (the note at 6:14 says so); see the dictionary. The apparatus at v8 records emé (Westcott–Hort and Tregelles) against me (Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont) after pros, a difference of accent on the pronoun that does not change the English. KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and TLB each print “of Nazareth,” so that six of the seven versions give the town in place of the title. NWT 1984 alone keeps the article and the title, “Jesus the” followed by Nazarene. A reader of the six therefore does not see that Luke’s Greek has a different word here from the one that means “from Nazareth” (apo Nazaret).

“Beheld the light … did not hear the voice” (Acts 22:9). The verse says hoi de syn emoi ontes to men phōs etheasanto tēn de phōnēn ouk ēkousan tou lalountos moi, “and those who were with me beheld the light, but did not hear the voice of the one speaking to me.” Set beside it the verse of the first telling, 9:7: akouontes men tēs phōnēs mēdena de theōrountes, printed on these pages “hearing the voice, yet beholding no one.” The two verses use akouō with phōnē in different cases: the genitive tēs phōnēs at 9:7, the accusative tēn phōnēn here, and the person speaking is in the genitive, tou lalountos. The verb of seeing differs too: theōreō, “beholding,” there, theaomai here, which stands in three verses of Acts: 1:11, 21:27 and this one. The case of “voice” shifts even where the hearing is plainly of speech. Paul himself says ēkousa phōnēs, genitive, at v7 above (akouō with the voice in the genitive); the narrator at 9:4 has ēkousen phōnēn, accusative; Paul’s later telling has ēkousa phōnēn, accusative (26:14); Peter at 11:7 has ēkousa … phōnēs, genitive; and Ananias’s words to Paul at 22:14 are akousai phōnēn ek tou stomatos autou, accusative, “to hear a voice from his mouth.” Greek grammars are reported to distinguish hearing a sound (genitive) from hearing what is said (accusative), and the note at 9:7 says that this is not a rule without exceptions; whether the case marks a difference in these two verses, or the two verses differ in what each telling chooses to say, or differ in what happened, is a question the Greek on the page does not settle, and this translation prints each verse as it stands and does not choose. The apparatus has no note on the case at either verse; its one note at v9 is on the first half: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 end the clause at etheasanto, and Robinson–Pierpont adds kai emphoboi egenonto, “and became terrified.” The stem emphobos stands in five verses in the printed text: Luke 24:5, 24:37, Acts 10:4 (Cornelius, “becoming terrified”), 24:25 and Revelation 11:13; Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 do not print the clause at 22:9. ASV “beheld indeed the light, but they heard not the voice,” NWT 1984 “beheld, indeed, the light but did not hear the voice” and Douay “saw indeed the light, but they heard not the voice” have the shorter text and say “hear.” KJV “saw indeed the light, and were afraid” and Geneva “saw indeed a light and were afraid” print the longer clause that Robinson–Pierpont prints. NIV “did not understand the voice” and TLB “didn’t understand what was said” replace “hear” with “understand”; that is an English reading of ouk ēkousan, and it settles in the translation the question that the cases leave open.

“What shall I do, Lord?” (Acts 22:10). Ti poiēsō, kyrie? is the second question of the telling, and the first one that comes after Paul knows who is speaking. It is the question that the note on 9:3–6 says is not in the printed Greek of ch. 9, where the next words to Saul are an order. The verb is a subjunctive of deliberation, “what am I to do?”, and “shall” keeps that. KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV, TLB and NWT 1984 each print “What shall I do, Lord?”; the English the KJV prints for the added line at 9:6, quoted in the 9:3–6 note, is worded differently from its own rendering here. Both questions in this telling end in kyrie. The first, at v8, is said before the name is given; the second, here, is said after it. This translation keeps “Lord” at both and for ho kyrios in the next clause, since the speaker has named himself Iēsous at v8, and the Greek reads kyrios, not the name of God (dictionary). The answer is Anastas poreuou eis Damaskon, “Rise, go on into Damascus,” where 9:6 says anastēthi kai eiselthe eis tēn polin, “rise and enter into the city.” Here the city is named; there it is not. What Paul is told after that comes to him in Damascus and is told to him, kakei soi lalēthēsetai, “and there it will be told you,” and the telling at 22:12–16 will give Ananias as the one who tells it; the telling in 26:16–18 puts the words of commission on the road itself. The three accounts are set side by side here, and none of them is made to correct the other two.

“Appointed” (Acts 22:10). Peri pantōn hōn tetaktai soi poiēsai is “about all the things that have been appointed for you to do.” The form tetaktai is a perfect, third person singular, of tassō, “to put in order, assign, appoint” (dictionary), and the bare form stands in this verse alone; the only other hit for the letters is the compound hypotetaktai at 1 Corinthians 15:27 (not yet on these pages). It is the same verb as tetagmenoi at Acts 13:48, “as many as were appointed to eternal life,” and that note, which says the verb stands at 22:10 too, is paid here. The two uses differ in what is appointed: there, persons; here, things to do. As at 13:48 the perfect middle and passive are the same in form, and the Greek here does not say who has done the appointing: the sentence has no agent. The parallel at 9:6 uses a different idea, ho ti se dei poiein, “what you must do” (dei, “it is necessary”), with a present infinitive; here the infinitive is the aorist poiēsai and the thing is not a necessity but something already assigned. KJV, ASV and Geneva each print “appointed for thee to do”; NWT 1984 “it is appointed for you to do”; NIV “assigned to do.” Douay “thou must do” drops the appointing for 9:6’s necessity, and TLB “what awaits you in the years ahead” replaces the verb with a prediction the Greek does not make. At 13:48 the KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay print “ordained” (as that note quotes them), so the same English books that say “ordained” there say “appointed” here; and the NWT 1984, which prints “appointed” here, prints “rightly disposed” at 13:48, a doctrinal rendering that note flags. Whether Luke means the one verb to carry the same weight in both places is not said, and this page does not say.

The “glory” of the light (Acts 22:11). Hōs de ouk eneblepon apo tēs doxēs tou phōtos ekeinou is “and as I could not see because of the glory of that light.” The imperfect eneblepon belongs to the compound emblepō, which reference works give as “look at, look into” (reported here, not verified); the form stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and Mark 8:25, where the man healed at Bethsaida “was seeing everything clearly.” The negative here is the opposite of that sight. At 9:8 the Greek says only ouden eblepen, “he saw nothing,” and gives no cause; this verse gives one, apo tēs doxēs, “from the glory.” Doxa (dictionary) is the word the Greek Old Testament is reported to use for the Hebrew kavod, “weight,” the manifest presence of God; the Greek here says that the light has a doxa, and does not say whose. Paul’s later telling uses another word, lamprotēs, “brightness,” of the light (26:13), and that noun stands in one verse of the New Testament (lamprotēs). KJV, ASV and Geneva each print “the glory of that light” and NWT 1984 “for the glory of that light.” Douay “the brightness of that light” and NIV “the brilliance of the light had blinded me” choose the plain word for light-stuff, and the NIV adds the verb “blinded,” which the Greek does not have; TLB “blinded by the intense light” does the same.

“Led by the hand” (Acts 22:11). Cheiragōgoumenos hypo tōn synontōn moi ēlthon eis Damaskon is “led by the hand by those who were with me, I came into Damascus.” The stem cheiragōg- stands in three verses of the New Testament: Acts 9:8, 13:11 and this one (dictionary). The three differ in form. At 9:8 it is an active plural participle, cheiragōgountes, “leading him by the hand,” and Saul is the object: eisēgagon, “they brought him in.” At 13:11 it is the noun cheiragōgous, the blinded Elymas “seeking hand-leaders,” as the note there says, as Saul himself was led. Here it is a passive participle, with Paul as subject and the leaders named in the agent phrase hypo tōn synontōn moi, “by those being with me”; Paul does not say that he was brought but that he came, ēlthon. Those leaders are the same people as in v9, who “beheld the light” and, in the Greek of that verse, ouk ēkousan; the verse names them with a different phrase there (hoi syn emoi ontes) and here (hoi synontes moi). KJV and ASV “being led by the hand of them that were with me”; Geneva “I was led by the hand of them that were with me”; NWT 1984 “being led by the hand of those who were with me”; Douay “led by the hand by my companions” and NIV “My companions led me by the hand” use “companions” where the Greek has only “those with me”; TLB “to be led into Damascus by my companions” drops the hand. This translation keeps “those who were with me,” the Greek’s own words, and “led by the hand,” as at 13:11.

Verses 12–16 · εὐλαβὴς … μαρτυρούμενος … ἀνάβλεψον … προεχειρίσατό … τὸν δίκαιον … μάρτυς … τί μέλλεις … βάπτισαι καὶ ἀπόλουσαι τὰς ἁμαρτίας σου … ἐπικαλεσάμενος τὸ ὄνομα eulabēs … martyroumenos … anablepson … proecheirisato … ton dikaion … martys … ti melleis … baptisai kai apolousai tas hamartias sou … epikalesamenos to onoma — Acts 22:16: what does “be baptized and wash away your sins” say in the Greek, and who was Ananias, the “devout” man who gave Paul his sight and his commission?

A man devout according to the law (Acts 22:12). Ananias here is anēr eulabēs kata ton nomon, “a man devout according to the law.” The adjective eulabēs stands in four verses of the New Testament: Simeon at Luke 2:25, the Jews gathered at Pentecost (Acts 2:5), the men who carried Stephen away (8:2) and this verse. The apparatus gives eulabēs to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and eusebēs to Robinson–Pierpont: a different word, not a different spelling. Eusebēs stands in three verses of the printed text: Cornelius at 10:2, the soldier sent with him at 10:7, and 2 Peter 2:9 (not yet on these pages). So the Robinson–Pierpont reading would give Ananias the adjective Acts uses of Cornelius and his orderly, and the other reading gives him the adjective of Simeon, the Pentecost crowd and Stephen’s mourners. The apparatus names editions, not manuscripts, and this page does not choose between them. English cannot show the difference: this translation prints “devout” at 10:2 for eusebēs and here for eulabēs, and only the Greek and this note carry the two words. Kata ton nomon is “as regards the law,” “by the standard of the law.” The phrase suits this crowd, who have just heard Paul accused of teaching “against the people and the law and this place” (21:28) and whom the Jerusalem elders had described as “all of them zealots for the law” (21:20); Luke does not say whether Paul chose the description for them. Acts 9 introduces the same man as tis mathētēs, “a certain disciple” (9:10), and Acts 22 has no word for “disciple” anywhere in the chapter, so the two tellings give two descriptions of one man and do not contradict each other. The shelf: ASV “a devout man according to the law,” KJV “a devout man according to the law,” NIV “a devout observer of the law,” Geneva “a godly man, as pertaining to the Law,” TLB “as godly a man as you could find for obeying the law,” NWT 1984 “reverent according to the Law.” Douay prints “a man according to the law,” with no word for the adjective at all. See Ananias of Damascus and eulabēs.

Well spoken of by all the Jews who lived there (Acts 22:12). Martyroumenos is the passive participle of martyreō, “to bear witness”: a man to whom witness is borne. The passive of this verb stands in four verses of Acts, and in each Luke uses it of someone whom others vouch for: the seven men to be chosen at 6:3 (“of good testimony”), Cornelius at 10:22, Timothy at 16:2 (in the imperfect) and Ananias here. The earlier notes pointed to this verse by number; the word is a reputation among his neighbors, not a verdict by Luke. The Greek has only hypo pantōn tōn katoikountōn Ioudaiōn, “by all the Jews dwelling”; the “there” is Damascus, named in 22:10. ASV “well reported of by all the Jews,” KJV “having a good report of all the Jews,” Geneva “having good report of all the Jews,” NWT 1984 “well reported on by all the Jews,” NIV “highly respected by all the Jews,” TLB “well thought of by all the Jews” (and it adds “of Damascus”), and Douay “having testimony of all the Jews,” which keeps the witness-root where the others have gone to “report” or “respect.” See martyreō.

Recover your sight (Acts 22:13). The command is anablepson and the answer is kagō autē tē hōra aneblepsa eis auton. The verb anablepō has two ordinary senses, “look up” (Jesus over the loaves at Matthew 14:19; Zacchaeus at Luke 19:5) and “see again” (the blind man at Luke 18:41–43, where the request hina anablepsō, the command Anablepson and the result aneblepsen run in a row). Here the Greek can be read either way, and the second verb carries eis auton, “at him”: the first thing the recovered sight does is look at Ananias. In the earlier telling the verb stands in Ananias’s purpose clause, hopōs anablepsēs kai plēsthēs pneumatos hagiou, “so that you may recover sight and be filled with Holy Spirit” (9:17), with its result at 9:18, aneblepsen, after something like scales fell. Side by side: in Acts 9 Ananias names his sender (“the Lord has sent me”) and mentions the Spirit; in Acts 22 he gives the command in two words after the address, and neither the sender, the Spirit nor the scales stands in these verses. The address Saoul adelphe, “Saul, brother,” is word for word the address of 9:17. The apparatus prints eme (Westcott–Hort, Tregelles) against me (Nestle–Aland 28, Robinson–Pierpont) after “came to”: the same pronoun with and without its own accent, and no change in English. For the command ASV, KJV and Geneva have “receive thy sight,” NIV and TLB “receive your sight,” NWT 1984 “have your sight again,” and Douay “look up,” the one version of the seven that takes only the other sense. For the answer, ASV has “I looked up on him,” KJV “I looked up upon him,” NWT 1984 “I looked up at him,” Geneva “I looked upon him,” NIV “I was able to see him” and TLB “I could see him.” This translation prints “recovered my sight and looked at him” so that both senses stay on the page.

Appointed beforehand (Acts 22:14). Proecheirisato is the aorist middle of procheirizō: the pro- of “before” on a verb built on cheir, “hand,” so “to designate.” The spelling is not uniform because the augment sits inside the compound: proecheirisato here, prokecheirismenon in a perfect passive participle, procheirisasthai in an infinitive. The verb stands in three verses of the New Testament: Acts 3:20, where God is to send “the Christ appointed for you, Jesus” (a passive, God the unstated agent), this verse, and Acts 26:16, where the one who appeared tells Paul he did so procheirisasthai se hypēretēn kai martyra, “to appoint you a servant and a witness.” The middle voice says God chose Paul for himself. A different verb with the same prefix, procheirotoneō, gives the witnesses of 10:41, prokecheirotonēmenoi, “chosen beforehand by God.” The English shelf splits on the prefix. ASV “hath appointed thee” and Geneva “hath appointed thee” leave it out; KJV “hath chosen thee,” NIV “has chosen you,” TLB “has chosen you” and NWT 1984 “has chosen you” say “chosen”; and Douay “hath preordained thee” keeps the pro- as a pre-. See procheirizō.

The Righteous One (Acts 22:14). Ton dikaion, with the article, is a title. The earlier notes promised it here: Peter’s “the Holy and Righteous One” (3:14) and Stephen’s “the coming of the Righteous One” (7:52) use the same adjective of the same person, and in this verse Paul quotes Ananias using it a third time. The sentence lists three things Paul is appointed for, joined by kai: to know God’s will (to thelēma autou), to see the Righteous One, and to hear a voice. Ananias does not name Jesus in these verses; the identification rests on the road scene (22:6–11), where the voice says “I am Jesus the Nazarene.” ASV and NIV print “the Righteous One,” NWT 1984 “the righteous One,” KJV and Geneva “that Just One,” with a demonstrative where the Greek has the article only, and Douay “the Just One.” TLB has “the Messiah,” which names who is meant and replaces the word. The word is in the dictionary.

A voice from his mouth (Acts 22:14). Akousai phōnēn ek tou stomatos autou, “to hear a voice from his mouth.” The apparatus prints no variant in this verse: the “of the Lord” reading belongs to v16 (below), not to this “his.” The Greek has no article, and phōnēn is accusative. The road scene of 22:9 has the same case, tēn phōnēn ouk ēkousan, where the companions did not hear the voice, while 9:7 has the genitive, akouontes tēs phōnēs. This page does not harmonize them; the wordings are set side by side and the note at 22:6–11 takes up 22:9 itself. Whose mouth? The subject of the sentence is “the God of our fathers,” and “his will” is his; the nearest person before the pronoun is the Righteous One. Both readings are grammatically possible and Ananias does not say. ASV “a voice from his mouth” keeps the indefinite “a voice”; KJV, Geneva and NWT 1984 each print “the voice of his mouth,” with an article the Greek lacks; Douay has “the voice from his mouth,” NIV “words from his mouth,” and TLB “hear him speak,” which takes the Messiah as the speaker and drops the mouth.

You will be a witness (Acts 22:15). Esē martys autō pros pantas anthrōpous hōn heōrakas kai ēkousas: “you will be a witness for him to all people of what you have seen and heard.” Martys is the noun of the root that vouched for Ananias in v12: the man of good witness is told that Paul will be a witness. It is the word Jesus gives the apostles at 1:8, esesthe mou martyres, “you will be my witnesses,” and the pair “seen and heard” is the pair of Peter and John’s refusal at 4:20, “what we saw and heard”, with a different form of “see” (heōrakas, perfect, here; eidamen, aorist, there). The witnesses of 10:41 are, like Paul in v14, chosen beforehand. Stephen is “your witness” in 22:20. The two tellings of the commission differ in who speaks and to whom. In Acts 9 the Lord speaks to Ananias about Paul: “a chosen vessel, to bear my name before nations and kings and sons of Israel” (9:15). In Acts 22 Ananias speaks to Paul and says “to all people”; the word for “nations” comes in the vision Paul reports next, eis ethnē makran (22:21). The two accounts are different in scope, and the text does not say they are opposed. ASV “a witness for him” and NWT 1984 “a witness for him” keep the dative as “for him”; KJV “his witness,” Geneva “his witness,” Douay “his witness” and NIV “his witness” read it as a possessive; and TLB “take his message everywhere” has no witness in it. See martys. The same appointment is told again at Acts 26:16, with martyra beside hypēretēn.

Why do you delay? (Acts 22:16). Ti melleis is the verb mellō, “to be about to,” here with no infinitive, so it asks “why are you about to…?” and the sense “why do you wait?” is carried by the question itself. With an infinitive the same two words are the centurion’s Ti melleis poiein, “What are you about to do?”, to the tribune in 22:26, and the plural ti mellete prassein at Acts 5:35. The English shelf has two ways of putting the question. ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay each have “why tarriest thou” (Geneva adds “Now therefore” before it); NWT 1984 has “why are you delaying,” NIV “what are you waiting for” and TLB “why delay.” The three days of blindness and fasting are told at 9:9 and are not in Acts 22.

Be baptized and wash away your sins (Acts 22:16). The clause is anastas baptisai kai apolousai tas hamartias sou epikalesamenos to onoma autou. Anastas, “rising,” is the participle of 9:18, anastas ebaptisthē, “rising, he was baptized”: there the verb is passive and names no agent, and here it is a command. Baptisai is an aorist imperative in the middle voice, and the form stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Apolousai is the same voice of apolouō, “to wash off,” a verb found in two verses: here and 1 Corinthians 6:11 (not yet on these pages), apelousasthe, “you washed yourselves” or “you had yourselves washed,” which goes on in the same verse “you were sanctified, you were justified in the name of the Lord Jesus.” English has no middle voice, and a Greek middle can mean that the subject acts on himself (“baptize yourself”), has the act done for him (“get yourself baptized,” “have your sins washed away”), or acts for his own benefit. The shelf prints passive-sounding English: ASV “be baptized, and wash away thy sins,” KJV “be baptized, and wash away thy sins,” Geneva “be baptized, and wash away thy sins,” Douay “be baptized, and wash away thy sins,” NIV “be baptized and wash your sins away,” NWT 1984 “get baptized and wash your sins away,” and TLB “be baptized and be cleansed from your sins,” which turns the second verb into a passive and loses “wash.” The Greek does not name who baptizes, as at 9:18, and does not say whether the washing is done by Ananias, by God or by Paul. Two more things are in the sentence. The two imperatives are joined by kai, “and,” and the aorist participle epikalesamenos follows them, so “calling on his name” can be heard as the means of the washing, as what accompanies the baptism, or as what goes with both. And the verb of washing is not the verb of baptizing: louō, the base of apolouō, is the verb of washing a body at 16:33, where the jailer “washed them of the blows” and in the same verse was baptized. Traditions read the pair differently, and this page reports them and does not vote. Readings in which baptism is the occasion or instrument of the washing (reported here for Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran and Churches of Christ teaching, not checked against a confessional source on this page) take the two imperatives as one event. Readings in which the washing is the inward cleansing and baptism its sign or public step (reported here for much Baptist and Reformed teaching, also not checked here) take “calling on his name” as the means. NWT 1984 prints “by your calling upon his name,” which makes the participle the means of the washing; the others print “calling” with no “by.” For neighbors in Acts: 2:38 has the passive baptisthētō, “let each of you be baptized,” with eis aphesin tōn hamartiōn, whose preposition the note there reports and does not resolve; and 9:18 has baptism and no washing of sins in its sentence. This translation prints “be baptized and wash away” and leaves the relation between the two to the reader. See baptizō and apolouō.

Calling on his name (Acts 22:16). Epikalesamenos to onoma autou, “having called on his name,” is the aorist middle participle of epikaleō. The phrase is that of Acts 2:21, “everyone who calls on the name” of Jehovah, a verse whose note sets out both the Joel text and the Jesus of the sermon, and of 9:14 and 9:21, where those who “call on your name” are the people Saul came to bind. Ananias himself used the phrase at 9:14, in his protest to the Lord; here he tells the man who came to bind them to do it. The same middle verb is also the verb of appeal to a court, Kaisara epikaloumai, “I appeal to Caesar,” at Acts 25:11, again at 25:21 and 25:25, and at 28:19. The apparatus has one variant: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print to onoma autou, “his name,” and Robinson–Pierpont prints to onoma tou kyriou, “the name of the Lord.” The shelf has both. KJV “calling on the name of the Lord,” Geneva “in calling on the Name of the Lord” and TLB “calling on the name of the Lord” print the longer reading; ASV “calling on his name,” NIV “calling on his name” and Douay “invoking his name” print the shorter, as this translation does. The Douay renders the Latin, and this page has not checked what its Latin read. On the longer reading the Greek would be kyrios; where that word is Jesus or open, as it would be here (the one addressed as “Lord” at 22:8 and 22:10 is Jesus), this translation keeps “Lord” and does not print “Jehovah.” Whether the words “of the Lord” tilt the verse toward Jesus or toward the God of 2:21 is a question this page does not settle. See kyrios and epikaleō.

Verses 17–21 · ἐν ἐκστάσει … Σπεῦσον … ἐν τάχει … οὐ παραδέξονταί … φυλακίζων … δέρων … τοῦ μάρτυρός σου … ἐξεχύννετο … συνευδοκῶν … ἐξαποστελῶ … εἰς ἔθνη μακράν en ekstasei … speuson … en tachei … ou paradexontai … phylakizōn … derōn … tou martyros sou … exechynneto … syneudokōn … exapostelō … eis ethnē makran — Acts 22:17–21: what was Paul’s “trance” in the temple, why would the Jews of Jerusalem “not receive” his testimony, and who is “your witness” Stephen?

A trance while praying in the temple (Acts 22:17). Egeneto de moi hypostrepsanti eis Hierousalēm kai proseuchomenou mou en tō hierō genesthai me en ekstasei is one long sentence: “it happened to me, having returned to Jerusalem and while I was praying in the temple, to come to be in a trance.” The Greek names a state and not an object: there is no noun for a vision in the verse, and what Paul sees comes in the next clause, idein auton, “to see him.” Ekstasis stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Mark 5:42, 16:8, Luke 5:26, Acts 3:10, 10:10, 11:5 and here. In the first four this translation prints “amazement,” that of onlookers (Mark 5:42, Luke 5:26, Acts 3:10) and the “trembling and amazement” of the women at the tomb (Mark 16:8); in the three from Acts 10 onward it prints “trance,” a state a person is in. The phrase en ekstasei stands in two verses, this one and 11:5, and the two are alike in shape: each is a first-person retelling, and in each the praying comes just before the trance (Peter at 11:5, “I was in the city of Joppa praying, and in a trance I saw a vision”; Paul here). At 10:10, the telling in the third person, the trance “came upon him” as he waited for a meal. The noun is the one the dictionary glosses as a “standing outside,” ek plus stasis, and the 10:10 note ties to existēmi, “to be amazed.” The temple is a place Luke has just shown Paul in: he “went into the temple” to give notice of the end of his purification (21:26), and the Jews from Asia seized him there (21:27); here he adds a prayer in that place at an earlier time. Seven versions print “trance”: KJV “trance,” ASV “trance,” Geneva “trance,” Douay “trance,” NIV “trance,” NWT 1984 “trance” and TLB “trance,” the last adding a noun the Greek does not have: “a vision of God.” Paul’s own letter speaks of this kind of experience in other words. 2 Corinthians 12:1–4 (not yet on these pages) has “visions and revelations of the Lord” (optasias kai apokalypseis kyriou), a man “caught up” (harpagenta) to the third heaven “fourteen years” before, and “whether in the body I do not know, or outside the body I do not know”; it does not use ekstasis, it speaks of “a man in Christ” without naming Paul, and nothing in either text says the temple trance and that experience are one event, so this note does not match them.

Whom he saw: “him,” then “Lord” (Acts 22:18–19, 21). The Greek of v18 is idein auton legonta moi, “to see him saying to me”: no name, no noun, only the pronoun. Paul answers at v19 with Kyrie, and the speaker of v21 is again only “he” (kai eipen pros me). The word the Greek reads is kyrios. Here it is the title Paul gives the one in front of him, and the one he addresses is the one in whom the persecuted “believed” (pisteuontas epi se, “those believing on you,” v19). In this same speech the voice from the light says “I am Jesus the Nazarene” (22:8), and “the Lord” speaks to Paul at 22:10 with the same word; so “Lord” stays here: the referent is Jesus or at least left open, and these five verses are neither an Old Testament quotation nor a formula that would call for “Jehovah.” See kyrios. The shelf splits on how much to name. KJV “saw him,” ASV “saw him,” Geneva “saw him,” Douay “saw him” and NWT 1984 “saw him” keep the bare pronoun (five versions); NIV “saw the Lord speaking to me” supplies the title, and TLB “a vision of God” supplies God, repeating it at v21 with “But God said to me.” Either supplied word settles, for the reader, a question the Greek leaves for the verse and its context to answer.

“Hurry … quickly” (Acts 22:18). The command doubles: speuson kai exelthe en tachei ex Hierousalēm, “hurry and go out, in speed, from Jerusalem.” The verb speudō stands in six verses of the New Testament: Luke 2:16 (the shepherds), 19:5 and 19:6 (Zacchaeus: “hurry and come down”), Acts 20:16, this verse, and 2 Peter 3:12 (not yet on these pages). The Acts 20:16 use is Paul’s own: “he was hurrying, if it were possible for him, to be in Jerusalem on the day of Pentecost”; the same verb now tells him to hurry out of it. The phrase en tachei stands in eight verses, among them Acts 12:7, where the angel wakes Peter in the prison with “Rise quickly.” KJV “Make haste, and get thee quickly out of Jerusalem,” ASV and Geneva “Make haste” and Douay “Make haste” keep the two parts; NWT 1984 “Hurry up” does too. NIV “Quick!” and “immediately” and TLB “Hurry!” keep the urgency; the TLB drops the second adverb.

“They will not receive your testimony” (Acts 22:18). Dioti ou paradexontai sou martyrian peri emou: the reason given is a prediction, not a charge. The verb paradechomai stands in four verses of the New Testament, and what they share is the taking of something in as valid. It is the hearer who “accepts” the word in Mark 4:20, the customs “not lawful for us to accept or to practice, being Romans” in Acts 16:21, and two more verses, 1 Timothy 5:19 (an accusation against an elder) and Hebrews 12:6 (the son whom he “receives”), neither yet on these pages. Martyria is the noun of martys, and the verse is a statement about whether a witness will be taken as one: Paul is being sent out because his word will not be accepted. The apparatus has one note here: WH, Tregelles and NA28 print sou martyrian, and RP prints sou tēn martyrian, with the article; the English is “your testimony” either way. The next scene of this speech has the same word coming back: at 23:11 the Lord is said to have “stood by” Paul and to say “you testified to the things about me in Jerusalem” (diemartyrō ta peri emou eis Ierousalēm), so the later verse speaks of testimony given in the city where v18 says it will not be received. KJV and Douay “will not receive thy testimony” keep the verb; Geneva “will not receive thy witness” also does. NWT 1984 “will not agree to your witness” and NIV “will not accept your testimony” give it the sense of consent; TLB “won’t believe you” turns it into belief in the person, where the Greek has the testimony as the thing refused. NWT 2013 prints “accept” where NWT 1984 has “agree to.”

Imprisoning and beating, “in one synagogue after another” (Acts 22:19). Ēmēn phylakizōn kai derōn kata tas synagōgas tous pisteuontas epi se is a periphrastic imperfect with two present participles, “I was imprisoning and beating,” a repeated practice and not a single act. Phylakizō stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; Luke says the same thing in other words in the places he tells it: “binding and handing over to prisons” in this chapter (22:4), “dragging off men and women, he would hand them over to prison” (8:3), and, at 26:10, “shut up in prisons.” The verb derō, “to beat, to flay,” stands in Acts at 5:40 (“they flogged them”), 16:37 (“They beat us publicly, uncondemned”) and here. The “flogging” of 22:24–25 is a different Greek word (mastix, mastizein), which is why this translation prints “beating” here and keeps “flog” for the tribune’s scourge; the 5:40 rendering “flogged” of the same verb derō is the exception. The beating in synagogues is what Jesus had said would happen to his followers: “you will be beaten in synagogues” (Mark 13:9, darēsesthe, a form of the same verb) and “handing you over to the synagogues and to prisons” (Luke 21:12). Kata tas synagōgas is distributive, “synagogue by synagogue,” and it has no “all”; the same speaker in 26:11 says kata pasas tas synagōgas, “in all the synagogues.” The Greek does not say which synagogues or in which city. NWT 1984 “in one synagogue after another” is the wording this translation prints too, and NIV “from one synagogue to another” gives the same distributive sense, though it makes it a journey. KJV and ASV “in every synagogue,” Geneva “in every Synagogue,” Douay “in every synagogue” and TLB “in every synagogue” read it as “all,” a reading the 22:19 Greek leaves unsaid. For the verb, NWT 1984 “flog” is the one version on the shelf that uses it here; KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and TLB all print “beat.” The object has a small point too: pisteuontas epi se is “believing on you,” with epi, and NWT 1984 “upon you,” KJV and ASV “on thee” keep it where NIV prints “in you.”

“Stephen your witness” (Acts 22:20). Stephanou tou martyros sou: the word is martys, and Luke has used it of the apostles from the start (“you will be my witnesses,” 1:8), of the “false witnesses” set up against Stephen (6:13), and of the witnesses who “laid down their garments” at Saul’s feet (7:58); of Paul himself Ananias has just said “you will be a witness for him” (22:15). Here Stephen gets the title, from Paul’s mouth and addressed to the Lord, so that the same word covers the man being sent out and the man who has been killed. Whether the word already carries the later sense of one who dies for his testimony is exactly what the verse does not settle: the Greek says “witness,” and the death is said separately in the same sentence. Revelation 2:13 gives the same pairing without comment, “Antipas, my witness, my faithful one, who was killed among you” (2:13, ho martys mou ho pistos mou, hos apektanthē). KJV “thy martyr Stephen,” Geneva “thy martyr Stephen” and NIV “your martyr Stephen” print the English word that descends from the Greek; ASV “thy witness,” Douay “thy witness,” NWT 1984 “your witness” and TLB “your witness Stephen” print the plain sense. “Martyr” in those three chooses the later sense of the word; “witness” keeps the sense 1:8 gives it, what the believers are to do while alive, and leaves the question open. See martys and Stephen.

His blood “poured out” (Acts 22:20). Hote exechynneto to haima Stephanou is an imperfect, “was being poured out,” the killing seen as under way. The same verb (ekchynnō, in the passive participle) is used of the cup, “my blood of the covenant, which is poured out for many” (Matthew 26:28, Mark 14:24), and of the righteous blood “poured out on the earth” from Abel to Zechariah (Matthew 23:35); Luke 11:50 (11:50) has “the blood of all the prophets, shed” with a perfect participle of the same verb family. Luke does not link Stephen to any of these; the verb says only that blood is spilled. The apparatus prints exechynneto for WH, Tregelles and NA28 and execheito for RP: two forms of the same imperfect, “was being poured out,” with ekchynnō a later by-form of ekcheō (reported here, not checked on this page). KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NIV “was shed” use a completed past; NWT 1984 “was being spilled” keeps the imperfect; TLB “was killed” drops the blood and says the death.

Standing by, approving, guarding the garments (Acts 22:20). Kai autos ēmēn ephestōs kai syneudokōn kai phylassōn ta himatia tōn anairountōn auton: three participles after “I myself was also,” and no act of violence among them. Ephestōs, “standing by,” is a form of the verb behind the angel who “stood by” at 12:7 (epestē) and, at 23:11, the Lord who came and stood by Paul (epistas); that these are forms of one verb is a point of Greek grammar reported here, not checked on this page. The verb syneudokeō stands in six verses of the New Testament, as the 8:1 note says; its account of Saul there, synedokōn tē anairesei autou, “approving of his killing,” is the other half of this sentence, and the noun anairesis stands in one verse of the printed Greek text, 8:1. At 22:20 the same fact is told with the participle of the verb, tōn anairountōn auton, “of those killing him.” The garments: at 7:58 “the witnesses laid down their garments at the feet of a young man called Saul,” and here Paul says he was phylassōn, “guarding,” them. Luke narrates the laying down at Saul’s feet and Paul narrates a keeping; the text says no more about either than each sentence does. Garments come back in this chapter at 22:23, where the crowd is “throwing their garments” (rhiptountōn ta himatia) as it shouts. The second apparatus note of the verse is here: where WH, Tregelles and NA28 print kai (“and guarding”), RP has tē anairesei autou, “his killing,” the words 8:1 prints after synedokōn; the note gives only that swap, not how RP then reads the rest. KJV “consenting unto his death” and Geneva “consented unto his death” print words that match the RP reading; ASV “consenting” and Douay “consented” do not, and neither do NWT 1984 “approving,” NIV “giving my approval” and TLB “agreeing.” For the garments, NWT 1984 “guarding the outer garments” and NIV “guarding the clothes” keep “guarding”; KJV “kept the raiment,” Geneva “kept the clothes” and TLB “keeping the coats they laid aside as they stoned him” use “kept,” and the TLB adds the stoning from ch. 7. See synedokeo.

Three tellings of the leaving of Jerusalem. The scene can be set beside two others, and none is made to fit the rest. Acts 9:26–30 has Saul trying to join the disciples, who are “all afraid of him,” Barnabas bringing him to the apostles, and Saul “speaking and disputing with the Hellenists; but they were attempting to kill him” (9:29, epecheirōn anelein, with the verb behind anairountōn here). Then “the brothers” brought him to Caesarea and “sent him off to Tarsus” (9:30). Acts 9 mentions no temple, no trance and no command; here a vision gives the command and Paul argues. Galatians 1:15–18 has a third emphasis: the purpose of the revelation was “that I might announce the good news of him among the nations” (1:16); he did not go up to Jerusalem to those before him but “went away into Arabia, and again returned to Damascus” (1:17); then “after three years” he went up to get to know Cephas and stayed fifteen days (1:18). Galatians says nothing of a temple or a trance, and Acts 22 says nothing of Arabia; whether the Galatians visit, the Acts 9 visit and the visit of Acts 22:17 are one, and how the three emphases fit, the texts do not say in so many words, and readers have put them together in more than one way. Luke’s own sequence is the point of the speech: Paul in the temple, the Lord who tells him to leave, Paul who objects with his record, and the Lord who answers by sending him farther than he proposed.

“I will send you out far away to the nations” (Acts 22:21). Poreuou, hoti egō eis ethnē makran exapostelō se: the answer to Paul’s plea is poreuou, “go,” with the same shape of command and reason as the Lord’s word to Ananias, “Go, because this man is to me a chosen vessel, to bear my name before nations and kings and sons of Israel” (9:15, Poreuou, hoti). The verb exapostellō stands in fourteen verses of the New Testament, one of them in the bracketed ending printed after Mark 16:8: Luke 1:53, 20:10, 20:11, 24:49, Galatians 4:4 and 4:6 (not yet on these pages), and seven verses of Acts: 7:12, 9:30, 11:22, 12:11, 13:26, 17:14 and here. Three of them are worth setting beside this one. At Acts 9:30 the same verb is what the brothers did to Saul when they “sent him off to Tarsus”; here it is what the Lord promises to do. In Luke 24:49 Jesus says “I am sending out upon you what my Father promised.” At 12:11 Peter says that Jehovah “sent out” his angel (the Greek reads kyrios there). The verb has the same root as apostolos (dictionary), but nothing in this verse says Paul is given that title here; the Lord says “I will send you out.” At 26:17 the verb is the simple apostellō, in the present. Ethnē has no article in the Greek, “to nations,” and makran, “far,” sits beside it without saying whether the distance is a place or a people; the word stands in Acts at 2:39 (eis makran, “all those far off”) and 17:27 (ou makran, “not far”) as well. This translation prints “the nations,” the house rendering of ethnē (see ethnos), and no earlier verse of the speech has used the word: Paul gives no reply to it, and the next sentence of the chapter is the crowd’s (22:22), which says that they listened “up to this word” (achri toutou tou logou). Luke does not name the word; v21 is the sentence just before it. NWT 1984 “nations far off” is the one version that prints “nations”; KJV, ASV and Geneva “far hence unto the Gentiles,” NIV and TLB “far away to the Gentiles” and Douay “unto the Gentiles afar off” print “Gentiles” (six versions). For the first word of the command, KJV, ASV and Geneva “Depart” and NWT 1984 “Get on your way” render poreuou as a departure; NIV “Go” and Douay “Go” keep it a going.

Verses 22–23 · ἄχρι τούτου τοῦ λόγου … Αἶρε ἀπὸ τῆς γῆς τὸν τοιοῦτον … οὐ γὰρ καθῆκεν … κραυγαζόντων … ῥιπτούντων τὰ ἱμάτια καὶ κονιορτὸν βαλλόντων achri toutou tou logou … Aire apo tēs gēs ton toiouton … ou gar kathēken … kraugazontōn … rhiptountōn ta himatia kai koniorton ballontōn — “Away from the earth with such a man” (Acts 22:22): what was the “word” the crowd heard Paul to, what does kathēken (“it is not fit”) say, and why do they throw garments and dust?

“Up to this word” (Acts 22:22). Ēkouon de autou achri toutou tou logou is “and they were listening to him up to this word.” The verb is an imperfect, a hearing that goes on, and the preposition achri toutou gives it an end: the phrase “up to this” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Luke does not name the word. The nearest candidate is the last thing Paul reports the Lord saying, Poreuou, hoti egō eis ethnē makran exapostelō se, “Go, because I will send you out far away to the nations” (22:21), which ends on ethnē, “nations” (see ethnos). That is a reading of the text, not something the verse states, but the charges brought earlier point the same way: the crowd at the temple had cried that Paul “teaches everyone everywhere against the people and the law and this place” and “has brought Greeks into the temple” (21:28), and the elders had told him what the Jerusalem Jews had heard, that he taught “all the Jews among the nations” to abandon Moses (21:21). The word logos itself is wider than a single word and is also “saying” or “statement” (logos), so “this word” may be the whole sentence of 22:21. A hearing that is cut off at a single point has a near parallel at Athens, where the audience heard Paul out until the resurrection of the dead: “some began to jeer, but others said, ‘We will hear you about this again’” (17:32). Here the crowd ends the hearing by shouting, as the hearers of 7:57 did (“crying out with a loud voice, they stopped their ears”). The apparatus lists no variant at v22. The English shelf mostly lets “word” stand. KJV “gave him audience unto this word” and ASV “gave him audience unto this word” use the old sense of “audience” as a hearing; Geneva “heard him unto this word,” Douay “heard him until this word” and NWT 1984 “kept listening to him down to this word” keep it too; NIV “listened to Paul until he said this” turns the word into a saying, and TLB “listened until Paul came to that word” keeps the word and adds Paul as the one who comes to it.

“They raised their voice” and then “Away!” (Acts 22:22). Epēran tēn phōnēn autōn is “they lifted up their voice.” A phrase for lifting the voice, built on airō or its compound epairō with phōnē, stands in six verses of the New Testament: Luke 11:27 and 17:13, Acts 2:14, 4:24, 14:11 and here. The identical form epēran tēn phōnēn autōn stands in two of them: the Lycaonian crowds at 14:11 (“raised their voice, saying in Lycaonian, ‘The gods, made like humans, have come down to us!’”) and this crowd. So in Acts two crowds lift their voice at Paul, and the cries are far apart: at Lystra the cry is that the gods have come down, and here it is that he be taken from the earth. The verb that lifts the voice is the verb of the cry itself. The cry is Aire, the present imperative of airō, “lift, take up, take away,” and the crowd lifts (epēran) its voice to say “lift!” English cannot keep both on one verb. This is what 21:36 promised, and it is now paid: the multitude that followed Paul to the steps cried Aire auton, “Away with him!” (21:36), and the Gospel cries Aire touton, “Away with this man!” (Luke 23:18) and Aron aron (John 19:15) have the same verb. In 21:36 and Luke 23:18 the cry stops at the pronoun; only here does it say where the taking is to go. What differs from the Gospel scenes is stated and not weighed: here there is no Barabbas and no “crucify,” only the removal, and the verb names no death; the clause that follows (“it is not fit that he should live”) does.

“From the earth” (Acts 22:22). Apo tēs gēs, “from the earth,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament. Three of them are about a boat and the shore (Matthew 14:24, Luke 5:3, John 21:8, where “land” is the sense), and one is Revelation 14:3 (not yet on these pages). The other three are in Acts, and all three are about a man: 8:33, where the Ethiopian is reading “his life is taken from the earth” (airetai apo tēs gēs hē zōē autou, the same verb airō and the same prepositional phrase as here); 9:8, where “Saul was raised from the ground” at Damascus; and this verse. The fetched Hebrew of Isaiah behind 8:33 reads nigzar me’erets chayyim, “he was cut off from the land of the living” (Isaiah 53:8); Acts 8:33 gives the sentence with airetai, “is taken,” for “cut off,” and whether the Septuagint already has that verb is reported here, not checked on this page, because the tools here hold the Hebrew and the New Testament Greek only. Luke gives no sign that the crowd means to quote Isaiah, and the fetched Greek shows only that the phrase stands in these three verses of Acts and that in two of them the verb is airō; whether that is arrangement or coincidence, the text does not say. Note also the object: ton toiouton, “such a one,” singular, a man classed by a kind (“such”) and not by a name, as the crowd also did with touton in Luke 23:18. The shelf puts the cry in several ways. KJV, ASV and Geneva all have “Away with such a fellow from the earth”; Douay “Away with such an one from the earth” keeps “such” and drops “fellow,” a word that adds contempt the Greek does not carry; NWT 1984 “Take such a [man] away from the earth” keeps the verb as an act and supplies “man” in brackets; NIV “Rid the earth of him!” changes the verb into a purpose; TLB “Away with such a fellow! Kill him!” adds a second command that the Greek does not print. This translation prints “Away from the earth with such a man!”, as 21:36's note already promised.

“It is not fit” (Acts 22:22). Ou gar kathēken auton zēn is “for it is not fit that he should live,” with zēn, “to live,” the infinitive. The verb kathēkō, “it is fitting, it is proper,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and Romans 1:28, where God gave people over to an unapproved mind “to do the things that are not fitting” (ta mē kathēkonta). A wider search for the letters kathēk- also lands on Luke 5:19 and Acts 9:25, but those are kathēkan, “they let down,” from kathiēmi, a different verb, and they do not count. In both verses that do count the verb is negated, “not fitting,” and both pass a verdict on conduct or on a person; Romans says it of what people do, and the crowd here says it of a man living. In form kathēken is an imperfect, which Greek can use for what should already have been the case (“it was not fitting”); that this is the sense here is reported, not checked on this page, and the English verse has the present, as six of the seven shelf versions do. KJV, ASV and Douay print “it is not fit that he should live”; Geneva “it is not meet that he should live” uses the older word for “suitable”; NIV “not fit to live” and TLB “isn’t fit to live” turn the matter into the man’s fitness; NWT 1984 “he was not fit to live” keeps a past tense, close to the form of the verb. Neither “fit to live” nor “fit that he should live” is the Greek construction exactly: the Greek has the impersonal verb with a pronoun in the accusative, “it is not fitting (as to) him to live.” The statement is the crowd’s and Luke reports it; the narrator gives no verdict on it here, and the Roman tribune’s own reaction follows at 22:24.

Shouting (Acts 22:23). Kraugazontōn te autōn is “and as they were shouting.” The verb kraugazō stands in nine verses of the New Testament, and this is the only one in Acts. Four of the other eight are in John’s Passion scenes (John 18:40, 19:6, 19:12, 19:15), so the verb at v23 is also the verb of the crowd before Pilate in that Gospel; John 11:43 and 12:13 and Matthew 12:19 and Luke 4:41 are the others. Acts usually uses krazō, a related verb: krazō in some form stands in ten verses of Acts, among them 21:28 and 21:36, the two cries of the multitude in the chapter before. That is where the apparatus matters. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print kraugazontōn; Robinson–Pierpont prints Krazontōn, the plainer verb of 21:28 and 21:36, so on that reading the cry of v22 and the cry at the temple use the same verb. A second note at v23 is a small one: all three print te, “and,” where Robinson–Pierpont has de, “and/but,” a link-word only. Neither change alters the English.

Garments and dust (Acts 22:23). Rhiptountōn ta himatia kai koniorton ballontōn eis ton aera is “throwing the garments and casting dust into the air.” The stem rhipt- stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and Luke 17:2, where a millstone has been hung around the neck and the person “has been thrown into the sea.” Other forms of the same verb (aorist rhips- forms, as in Matthew 27:5 and Acts 27:19) are not counted here; the count is of the stem rhipt- only. The Greek says only that they throw the himatia; it does not say “off.” ASV “threw off their garments,” Douay “threw off their garments” and NIV “throwing off their cloaks” add a direction that the Greek verb does not carry, and this translation’s plain “throwing” leaves out; KJV and Geneva “cast off their clothes” do the same; NWT 1984 “throwing their outer garments about” adds a different direction, and TLB “threw their coats in the air” puts the garments into the air as well, where the Greek puts only the dust (eis ton aera follows koniorton ballontōn). The noun himation stands in eight verses of Acts, and most are a handling of garments: laid down for the stoning of Stephen (7:58, “laid down their garments at the feet of a young man called Saul”), torn by the apostles after the cry of the crowds at Lystra (14:14), torn off Paul and Silas by the magistrates (16:22), and shaken out by Paul at Corinth (18:6, “he shook out his garments and said to them, ‘Your blood be on your own heads… From now on I will go to the nations’”). The other four are 22:20, Paul’s own “guarding the garments of those killing him,” and 9:39, 12:8 and 22:23. So the garments Paul once guarded for the men who killed Stephen are now thrown by a crowd that wants him killed: the same noun, two verses apart in this chapter, with the roles turned. Why the crowd throws them is not said. The Greek lists three acts, shouting, throwing garments, throwing dust, and states no reason; what the gestures meant to those who made them is not given on the page, and reference works are reported to read them as signs of rage or of mourning, a claim not checked here.

Dust (Acts 22:23). Koniortos, “dust,” stands in five verses of the New Testament: Matthew 10:14 (“shake the dust off your feet”), Luke 9:5 (“shake off the dust from your feet as a testimony against them”), Luke 10:11 (“the dust from your town that clings to our feet, we wipe off against you”), Acts 13:51 (Paul and Barnabas “shaking off the dust of their feet against them” at Pisidian Antioch) and this verse. In the other four the dust is shaken from the feet of those leaving a place that has refused them; here it is thrown into the air by the people of that place. That is a pairing of gesture with gesture, not a claim that the crowd was copying them; Luke does not link them. Another gesture with dust is Shimei’s at 2 Samuel 16:13 (not yet on these pages): the Hebrew in the archive ends ve’ippar be’afar, a verb and a noun from one root, and the Mechon-Mamre English prints that Shimei “cursed as he went, and threw stones at him, and cast dust.” Which Greek words the Septuagint uses for it is reported here, not checked, because the tools do not hold the Septuagint. The shelf is narrow on the dust: KJV “threw dust into the air,” Geneva “threw dust into the air,” ASV “cast dust into the air” and Douay “cast dust into the air” vary only in the verb; NIV “flinging dust into the air” is the choice this translation shares; NWT 1984 “tossing dust into the air” and TLB “tossed up handfuls of dust” add “tossing,” and the TLB adds “handfuls,” a count the Greek does not give. The tribune’s response comes at 22:24: Paul is to be brought into the barracks and examined by flogging.

Verses 24–26 · εἰς τὴν παρεμβολήν … μάστιξιν ἀνετάζεσθαι … ἐπεφώνουν … προέτειναν αὐτὸν τοῖς ἱμᾶσιν … Ῥωμαῖον καὶ ἀκατάκριτον … ἑκατόνταρχον … ἑκατοντάρχης … Τί μέλλεις ποιεῖν eis tēn parembolēn … mastixin anetazesthai … epephōnoun … proeteinan auton tois himasin … Rhōmaion kai akatakriton … hekatontarchon … hekatontarchēs … Ti mellis poiein — Flogging, the straps and “uncondemned” (Acts 22:24–26): what does it mean that the tribune had Paul “examined by flogging,” what are the himantes he was stretched out “with,” and why does the centurion ask, “What are you about to do?”

Into the barracks, and the order finished (Acts 22:24). Ekeleusen ho chiliarchos eisagesthai auton eis tēn parembolēn is “the tribune ordered him to be brought into the barracks.” At 21:34 the same order used another verb, agesthai, “to be led.” The infinitive eisagesthai, “to be brought in,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 21:37, where Paul, “about to be brought into the barracks” (mellōn eisagesthai), asked the tribune whether he might speak. The speech from the steps (21:40 to 22:21) therefore stands between the “about to be” of 21:37 and the order that now carries it out. Paul has been in two chains since the tribune first laid hold of him, and Luke does not say when, or whether, they came off. The officer is still named only by his rank, ho chiliarchos (see chiliarchos, and 21:31, where he is first called the tribune “of the cohort,” speira); the place is parembolē, whose other uses are set out at 21:34. On the shelf, KJV, ASV and Douay “brought into the castle” and Geneva “led into the castle” keep the older English word for a fortress; NIV “taken into the barracks” agrees with this translation; NWT 1984 “soldiers’ quarters” names the place by who lives there; TLB “brought him inside” leaves the barracks out. For the officer, Douay “The tribune commanded him” keeps the rank, where KJV, ASV and Geneva print “chief captain.” The apparatus has a note on the word order of the order (ho chiliarchos eisagesthai auton in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and auton ho chiliarchos eisagesthai in Robinson–Pierpont), which changes nothing in English.

“So that he might find out” (Acts 22:24). Hina epignō di’ hēn aitian houtōs epephōnoun autō gives the purpose of everything that follows: “so that he might find out for what reason they were shouting so against him.” At 21:33 the tribune “kept inquiring who he might be and what he had done,” and at 21:34 he “could not learn the certainty because of the uproar”; the question is the same, now with the lash as the proposed answer. The verb of the crowd is epiphōneō, which stands in four verses of the New Testament: Luke 23:21 (“Crucify, crucify him!”), Acts 12:22 (“A god’s voice, and not a man’s!”), 21:34 and here. The apparatus gives Robinson–Pierpont eboōn at 21:34 and lists no variant for the word here. Luke does not say whether the tribune had followed the speech Paul gave in the Hebrew dialect (21:40), and the verse says only what he wanted to learn. The same wish returns in the same words at 23:28, where the tribune’s letter says he wanted epignōnai tēn aitian di’ hēn enekaloun autō, “to find out the reason for which they were accusing him.” On the shelf: KJV “they cried so against him,” ASV “they so shouted against him,” Douay “they did so cry out against him,” Geneva “they cried so on him,” NWT 1984 “shouting against him this way” and NIV “shouting at him like this” keep the shouting; TLB “had become so furious” names the crowd’s state instead of its act. TLB also has the lash “to make him confess his crime,” which turns a search for the crowd’s reason into a confession from Paul; the Greek purpose clause asks for the reason (aitia) of the shouting.

“Examine him by flogging” (Acts 22:24). Mastixin anetazesthai auton is literally “that he be examined with lashes.” Mastixin is the dative plural of mastix, a whip or lash, so the flogging is named as the means of the examining, and the infinitive anetazesthai is passive: Luke gives the tribune’s order as “that he be examined,” with no named subject, and the English verse’s “telling them to examine him” supplies the soldiers it implies. The verb anetazō stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 22:29, where “those who were about to examine him” withdraw. With only two uses, its sense rests on the context and on the purpose clause Luke attaches (“so that he might find out”); that Roman officers questioned non-citizens under the lash is reported, and is not checked on this page. A neighbouring verb is easy to confuse with it: anakrinō stands in five verses of Acts, 4:9 (“being examined today”), 12:19 (Herod “examined the guards”), 17:11 (the Beroeans “examining the Scriptures”), and 24:8, and 28:18, and it is the verb of Pilate’s “having examined him in your presence” (Luke 23:14). This translation prints “examine” for both verbs, so the English reader cannot see that Luke used a different word here. The apparatus gives the tribune’s participle as eipas in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and eipōn in Robinson–Pierpont, two aorist forms of the same verb, “having said.” On the shelf, KJV and ASV “examined by scourging” and NWT 1984 “examined under scourging” keep the examining and the lash together; Geneva “scourged, and examined” makes them two acts; NIV “flogged and interrogated” does the same; Douay “scourged and tortured” puts “tortured” where the Greek has “examine,” and whether the Greek verb already carries torture is not settled by its two uses; TLB “lashed with whips” names the instrument.

Flogging, in several Greek words (Acts 22:24–25). Paul’s question uses the verb mastizō, which stands in one verse of the New Testament, 22:25. Its relatives are the noun mastix, in six verses: Mark 3:10, 5:29, 5:34 and Luke 7:21, where it is a bodily affliction (this translation prints “afflictions” and “affliction”), Hebrews 11:36 (“flogging,” with “mocking”) and verse 24 here; and the verb mastigoō, in seven verses: Matthew 10:17, 20:19, 23:34, Mark 10:34, Luke 18:33, John 19:1 and Hebrews 12:6 (not yet on these pages). Six of the seven are the flogging of Jesus or of his followers, and the seventh, Hebrews 12:6, says it of “every son” whom he receives. This translation prints “flog” at Matthew 20:19 and Luke 18:33, and “scourged” at John 19:1, for the same stem. Elsewhere in Acts the English “flogged” renders a different verb, derō, at 5:40 (the apostles), and the same verb is “beat” at 16:37 and “beating” at 22:19 in Paul’s own account of what he did to believers; at Philippi the order was rhabdizein, “to be beaten with rods” (16:22; rhabdizō), with “blows” at 16:23. So on these pages the English word “flog” stands over more than one Greek verb, and Luke does not say whether the punishments he names with different verbs were different. Paul’s question names the act (mastizein), two facts about the man, and nothing about its purpose, whether inquiry (v24) or penalty. On the shelf, KJV, ASV and Douay “scourge a man that is a Roman, and uncondemned,” Geneva “scourge one that is a Roman, and not condemned,” and NWT 1984 “scourge a man that is a Roman and uncondemned” use “scourge”, five of the seven versions; NIV “to flog a Roman citizen” and TLB “to whip a Roman citizen” use the plainer English words.

“Stretched him out with the straps” (Acts 22:25). Ōs de proeteinan auton tois himasin. The verb proteinō, “to stretch forward, stretch out,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, so the posture (the body stretched out, to be tied or held for the lash) rests on the verb and the context and is not described by Luke. The noun is himas, “strap, thong,” here in the dative plural himasin with the article, “the straps.” It stands in four verses of the New Testament: Mark 1:7 and Luke 3:16, where John says he is not worthy to untie “the strap” of the coming one’s sandals (this translation prints “strap” at both), John 1:27 (“sandal-strap”), and here. It is not the noun himatia, “garments,” which the crowd threw about at 22:23 two verses earlier; the two look alike on the page and are different words. The dative allows two readings, and the English cannot keep both. As an instrument, tois himasin is “with the straps”: they stretched him out, and bound him, with straps. As a purpose or advantage, it is “for the straps”: they stretched him out for the thongs, meaning for the lash made of them. The verse does not settle which is meant, and no reference work is consulted on this page; this translation’s “with the straps” takes the first. The shelf splits the same way. KJV, Geneva and Douay “bound him with thongs” and ASV “tied him up with the thongs” take the instrument and print “bound” or “tied” where the Greek has a verb of stretching; NIV “stretched him out to flog him” and NWT 1984 “stretched him out for the whipping” keep the stretching and take the purpose, with no straps at all; TLB “tied Paul down to lash him” mixes both. The apparatus gives proeteinan, “they stretched out,” to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and proeteinen, “he stretched out,” to Robinson–Pierpont. With the singular the nearest subject is the tribune of v24, who ordered the examining; with the plural it is the soldiers who carry it out. The seven versions print a plural subject, as the quotations show (“they bound,” “they had tied,” “they stretched”).

“A Roman, and uncondemned” (Acts 22:25). Ei anthrōpon Rhōmaion kai akatakriton exestin hymin mastizein? is “Is it lawful for you to flog a man who is a Roman and uncondemned?” The adjective akatakritos stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 16:37, where Paul says of himself and Silas that the magistrates had beaten them “publicly, uncondemned, men who are Romans”; see akatakritos. At Philippi Paul said it after the beating, and here he says it as the straps are in place. The one Greek kai joins two descriptions of the man, Roman and uncondemned, and the sentence does not say whether either alone would answer the question. The verb exestin, “it is lawful,” is the verb of Paul’s own question to the tribune at 21:37, Ei exestin moi eipein ti pros se?, “May I say something to you?”: both are questions beginning with ei that ask an officer what is permitted. The pronoun here is plural, hymin, “for you,” so Paul speaks to the centurion about what he and the men with him may do; NWT 1984 “lawful for YOU men” marks the plural, and the other six versions print a bare “you.” Luke names no statute. Roman writers are the nearest witnesses, and two can be read in Perseus’s Latin texts. Cicero, prosecuting Verres, tells of a man, Gavius, beaten with rods in the forum at Messana, who cried out nothing but civis Romanus sum, “I am a Roman citizen,” and who in Cicero’s telling was then put on a cross (Verrine 2.5.162); in the next section Cicero calls on lex Porcia legesque Semproniae, “the Porcian law and the Sempronian laws” (2.5.163), and in 2.5.170 he says facinus est vincire civem Romanum, scelus verberare, prope parricidium necare, in this page’s rendering “to bind a Roman citizen is an outrage, to flog him a crime, to kill him all but parricide.” Livy, at 10.9.4, says that the Porcian law alone seems to have been carried to protect the backs of citizens, imposing a heavy penalty on anyone who flogged or killed a Roman citizen, and that the Valerian law, which forbade rods and execution for a man who had appealed, added only the words inprobe factum, “wickedly done.” Both are a Roman advocate’s and a historian’s statements about what the laws were meant to do; neither is a statute, and neither says what rule governed a prisoner held in a Jerusalem barracks, or whether the laws they name are the ones behind Paul’s words. Later imperial legislation on flogging citizens is reported in handbooks and is not checked on this page. On the shelf, NIV “hasn’t even been found guilty” and TLB “hasn’t even been tried” give two different facts, no verdict and no trial; KJV, ASV and Douay “uncondemned” and NWT 1984 “uncondemned” keep the single word, and Geneva “not condemned” spells out the negative. NIV and TLB also print “Roman citizen” here; the Greek has Rhōmaion, “a Roman,” and no word for citizen (the word politeia comes at 22:28).

The centurion, and “What are you about to do?” (Acts 22:25–26). Paul speaks pros ton hestōta hekatontarchon, “to the centurion standing by,” and the report is made by ho hekatontarchēs, “the centurion,” at v26. The two forms are not spelled alike. Hekatontarchon at v25 is from the second-declension noun hekatontarchos, printed also at Matthew 8:5, 8:8 and 27:54; hekatontarchēs at v26 is the first-declension form, as at Acts 10:1 (Cornelius) and Luke 23:47. The apparatus lists no variant at either word. The stem stands in twenty verses of the New Testament, four in Matthew, three in Luke and thirteen in Acts, where a plural band of centurions first appears with the tribune’s run down at 21:32; Mark has none, and uses the Latin loanword kentyriōn in three verses (15:39, 44 and 45). The word is built like chiliarchos: hekaton, “a hundred,” and archos, “ruler,” and this centurion, unlike Cornelius, is not named. KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NIV print “centurion,” NWT 1984 “army officer” and TLB “an officer.” What the centurion reports is shorter than what Paul said: ho gar anthrōpos houtos Rhōmaios estin, “for this man is a Roman,” with “uncondemned” left behind. Douay “this man is a Roman citizen” and NIV and TLB “Roman citizen” again print “citizen.” His question is Ti mellis poiein?, “What are you about to do?”, with mellō and an infinitive, “be about to.” The two words ti mellis stand in two verses of the New Testament: here and 22:16, where Ananias says to Paul “why do you delay?” with the verb alone. The verb is also the “about to” of 21:37 and of “those who were about to examine him” at 22:29. The apparatus gives Ti to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and Hora ti, “See what,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which makes it a warning, “Take care what you are about to do.” The shelf splits on it. KJV and Geneva “Take heed what thou doest” carry the warning and print a plain present for mellis; ASV and Douay “What art thou about to do?” match this translation; NIV “What are you going to do?” and NWT 1984 “What are you intending to do?” keep the future; TLB “What are you doing?” makes it present. A second variant at v26 is word order only (apēngeilen tō chiliarchō in Robinson–Pierpont, tō chiliarchō apēngeilen in the other three).

Verses 27–30 · σὺ Ῥωμαῖος εἶ … πολλοῦ κεφαλαίου τὴν πολιτείαν … γεγέννημαι … ἐφοβήθη ἐπιγνοὺς … ἦν δεδεκώς … τὸ ἀσφαλές … πᾶν τὸ συνέδριον … καταγαγὼν … ἔστησεν εἰς αὐτούς sy Rhōmaios ei … pollou kephalaiou tēn politeian … gegennēmai … ephobēthē epignous … ēn dedekōs … to asphales … pan to synedrion … katagagōn … estēsen eis autous — Roman citizenship in Acts 22:28: what did the tribune buy for “a large sum,” what does Paul have by birth, and why does the tribune put Paul before the council?

“Tell me, are you a Roman?” (Acts 22:27). Lege moi, sy Rhōmaios ei? is the tribune’s own question, asked in person. The centurion had gone to him with the news (22:26, “this man is a Roman”), and the verb of approach, proselthōn, is printed of both officers, the centurion going to the tribune at v26 and the tribune coming to Paul at v27. He asks Paul directly, though Paul had already said as much to the centurion (22:25); Luke does not say whether the tribune doubted the centurion. Paul’s whole answer is one word, Nai, “Yes.” The apparatus gives sy Rhōmaios ei to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and ei sy Rhōmaios ei to Robinson–Pierpont; the added ei is the particle “if” or “whether,” and English “are you a Roman?” serves for both. This is the second question the tribune has put to Paul about who he is: at 21:37–39 he asked whether Paul knew Greek and took him for the Egyptian, and Paul answered that he was a Jew, a Tarsian of Cilicia, “a citizen of a city not without distinction.” On the shelf: NWT 1984 “Are you a Roman?” has the Greek’s own noun; NIV “Roman citizen” and TLB “Roman citizen” add a word the Greek of v27 does not have (the Greek has only Rhōmaios). For the one-word answer, KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay print “Yea”; NWT 1984 prints “Yes”; NIV “Yes, I am” and TLB “Yes, I certainly am” lengthen what Luke wrote as a single word.

“For a large sum” (Acts 22:28). Egō pollou kephalaiou tēn politeian tautēn ektēsamēn: the tribune puts the price first, “for much capital I acquired this citizenship.” Kephalaion is a noun built on kephalē, “head,” and it stands in two verses of the New Testament, counted by reading the hits: here, where it is the principal sum, the amount, and Hebrews 8:1 (not yet on these pages), where it is the “main point” of what has been said. (The search for the stem also finds compounds such as anakephalaioō and perikephalaia, which are other words.) That kephalaion means a principal sum of money is the sense the lexicons give; it is reported here, not checked on this page. The genitive pollou, “of much,” is what makes the verb a purchase: ektēsamēn is the aorist of ktaomai, “to acquire,” and by itself it does not say “buy.” The verb has money in view elsewhere in Acts: Judas “acquired a field with the wages of his wickedness” (1:18), and Peter tells Simon, “you thought to acquire the gift of God through money” (8:20, dia chrēmatōn ktasthai). The noun is the one the note at 21:39 pointed ahead to: Paul calls himself a politēs of Tarsus there, and for what the tribune bought the word is a different noun, politeia, which stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Ephesians 2:12, where the readers are “alienated from the citizenship of Israel.” Both nouns rest on polis, “city”: politēs is the person who is a citizen, and politeia is the standing he holds, which is what the tribune says he paid for. The shelf divides over it. ASV “citizenship,” NIV “citizenship” and NWT 1984 “rights as a citizen” name it; KJV and Geneva “freedom” render the noun as something else (the Greek of v28 has no word for “free”); Douay “the being free of this city” is a third way of saying it; and TLB “it cost me plenty” does not translate the noun at all, and the “I am too” before it has no counterpart in the Greek of v28. NWT 1984 reads the price as “a large sum [of money],” with the bracket marking its own addition.

What the tribune says he paid, and what Dio Cassius reports (Acts 22:28). Luke gives the tribune’s sentence and nothing around it: no seller, no date, no figure. A Roman historian reports a trade of this kind under the emperor Claudius. Dio Cassius 60.17.5–6, read in the Loeb translation of Cary on the LacusCurtius site (the English was fetched; the Greek was not), says that Claudius granted citizenship “quite indiscriminately,” that “many bought it from Messalina and the imperial freedmen,” and that “though the privilege was at first sold only for large sums,” it later became so cheap that people said a man could become a citizen by giving the right person “some bits of broken glass.” Three things can be said of the two texts together, and no more. Both speak of citizenship being bought for money. Dio’s remark about “large sums” is about the early years of the practice, and Luke dates no part of the tribune’s purchase, so the two cannot be set against each other by date. And Dio, a later historian (his date is reported here, not checked on this page), is describing the reign of Claudius, and the passage names no tribune. The tribune’s own name is a further point, and Luke saves it: he is Claudius Lysias, named in the letter at 23:26. Dio, at 60.17.7, says information was laid against new citizens “that they were not adopting Claudius’ name”; whether the tribune’s name has anything to do with that is a connection a reader may draw, and neither text draws it.

“But I was born so” (Acts 22:28). Egō de kai gegennēmai, “but I have even been born.” The verb is the perfect passive of gennaō: the birth is past, and the state that results from it stands. The Greek has no object; “so” (“born so,” the English of the verse) fills the gap from the tribune’s sentence, and what the birth gave Paul is left for the reader to supply as the politeia that has just been named. The first-person perfect gegennēmai stands in two verses of the New Testament: here, and John 18:37, where Jesus answers Pilate, “for this I have been born.” Both are answers to a Roman official’s question about the speaker’s standing. It is also the verb of Paul’s own account of himself at 22:3, gegennēmenos en Tarsō tēs Kilikias, “born in Tarsus of Cilicia,” so the birthplace of v3 and the birth-right of v28 are said with one verb. What Luke does not say is how Paul came to hold the status by birth: the verse does not name a father, a grandfather, a grant or an earlier purchase, and the text of Acts 21 and 22 gives Tarsus and a Roman birth side by side without connecting them. Any account of the route to the citizenship is therefore an addition to Luke. On the shelf: Douay “born so” is this translation’s own wording, and Geneva “so born” keeps it with the order changed; ASV “a Roman born” supplies the noun; KJV “free born” follows its “freedom” of the clause before; NIV “born a citizen” and TLB “a citizen by birth” name the status; and NWT 1984 “even born [in them]” supplies it by a bracket, as at the price.

“Was afraid, having learned” (Acts 22:29). Eutheōs oun apestēsan ap’ autou hoi mellontes auton anetazein: “immediately, then, those about to examine him withdrew from him.” Anetazō is the verb of the examination ordered at 22:24, where the tribune told them to “examine him by flogging”; it stands in two verses of the New Testament, 22:24 and 22:29, and in this verse it stands without the flogging. Then the tribune’s fear: ephobēthē epignous hoti Rhōmaios estin kai hoti auton ēn dedekōs, “was afraid, having learned that he is a Roman and that he had bound him.” The earlier note at 16:37–38 quoted the parallel, and it is a close one: there the magistrates at Philippi ephobēthēsan akousantes hoti Rhōmaioi eisin, “were afraid when they heard that they were Romans”; here the tribune ephobēthē epignous hoti Rhōmaios estin. The verb of fear and the ground of it are the same; the participle changes from “hearing” to “coming to know,” epiginōskō, a compound of ginōskō, “to know.” Luke does not say what the tribune feared would follow; the verse names two things he learned and one fear. The second thing he learned is ēn dedekōs, a periphrastic pluperfect of deō, “to bind”: “he had bound him.” The active perfect participle dedekōs stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. At 21:33 the tribune “ordered him to be bound with two chains,” the passive dethēnai; here Luke makes him the one who bound. The verse does not say which of the two the tribune finds fearful, the binding or the examination that was only about to be done, and which law stands behind Paul’s protest is, as the note at 16:37 said, “not in Luke.” The apparatus gives auton ēn to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and ēn auton to Robinson–Pierpont, a difference of word order only. On the shelf: KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “afraid” match this translation; NIV “alarmed” and TLB “frightened” vary the word. For the binding, NIV “put Paul, a Roman citizen, in chains” adds “chains” where the verb is only “bound,” and TLB “ordered him bound and whipped” adds a whipping that Luke does not report as done. For the examination, NWT 1984 “examine him with torture” takes the flogging of v24 into v29, where the Greek has only the verb; Douay “torture him” does the same.

“The certainty” (Acts 22:30). Tē de epaurion boulomenos gnōnai to asphales to ti katēgoreitai hypo tōn Ioudaiōn: “on the next day, wishing to know the certainty, the ‘what is he accused of by the Jews.’” The phrase gnōnai to asphales is the one Luke wrote at 21:34, where the tribune “could not learn the certainty because of the uproar”; the tribune who failed to learn it that day now tries again the next, and the Greek names the same thing twice. The neuter adjective asphales stands in four verses of the New Testament: 21:34, this verse, Acts 25:26, where Festus says he has nothing “certain” to write to the lord, and Philippians 3:1 (not yet on these pages), where it is a different thing, “safe” for the readers. The noun asphaleia, the same stem, stands in three verses: Luke 1:4, where Luke writes to Theophilus hina epignōs peri hōn katēchēthēs logōn tēn asphaleian, “that you may know the certainty of the words you were taught”; Acts 5:23; and 1 Thessalonians 5:3 (not yet on these pages). The stem asphal- in all its forms stands in thirteen verses, seven of them in Luke’s two books (Luke 1:4; Acts 2:36, 5:23, 16:23, 21:34, 22:30 and 25:26). The tribune’s sentences and Luke’s own preface therefore share the stem asphal- and a verb of knowing (epiginōskō at Luke 1:4 and 22:29, ginōskō at 21:34 and 22:30). Luke does not say what a reader should make of that. This translation prints “certainty” at Luke 1:4 and at both verses of Acts. On the shelf: ASV “the certainty,” KJV “the certainty” and Geneva “the certainty” match; NWT 1984 “to know for sure” and NIV “find out exactly” turn it into an adverb; Douay “to know more diligently” reads it as a degree of care; TLB “what the trouble was all about” paraphrases it. The question inside the clause is ti, “what,” with the verb in the present, “is accused”; the verse above reads it as “why,” as the ASV, KJV and Geneva “wherefore” do.

“He released him” (Acts 22:30). Elusen auton, “he loosed him,” is the opposite verb to the dedekōs of v29, bound at one verse and loosed at the next. The apparatus gives elusen auton to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and elusen auton apo tōn desmōn, “loosed him from the bonds,” to Robinson–Pierpont; the added words are the ones that tell the reader which release is meant. Luke’s own sentence goes straight on to katagagōn ton Paulon, “bringing Paul down,” so the Greek of the printed text does not say that Paul went free; the tribune has him still in hand. KJV “from his bands,” Geneva “from his bonds” and TLB “from his chains” carry the sense of the longer reading; ASV “he loosed him,” Douay “he loosed him” and NWT 1984 “let him loose” keep the shorter; NIV “released” leaves it open whether the release is from the chains or from custody. The apparatus also gives ekeleusen synelthein, “ordered to assemble,” for Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and ekeleusen elthein, “ordered to come,” for Robinson–Pierpont, and hypo tōn Ioudaiōn for the first three editions against para tōn Ioudaiōn for Robinson–Pierpont; both prepositions give “by the Jews” in English. The verb ekeleusen is the tribune’s verb again: he “ordered him to be bound” at 21:33 and ordered the barracks and the examination at 22:24; here the ones he orders are the chief priests and the council.

“The chief priests and the whole Sanhedrin” (Acts 22:30). Ekeleusen synelthein tous archiereis kai pan to synedrion. Synedrion is a “sitting together,” and its stem stands in twenty-two verses of the New Testament: eight in the Gospels (Matthew 5:22, 10:17 and 26:59, Mark 13:9, 14:55 and 15:1, Luke 22:66 and John 11:47) and fourteen in Acts. Of the fourteen, seven are on these pages before this one (4:15, 5:21, 5:27, 5:34, 5:41, 6:12 and 6:15), this is the eighth, and six come later (23:1, 23:6, 23:15, 23:20, 23:28 and 24:20). This translation prints “Sanhedrin” for the word here as at 4:15, 5:21, 5:27 and 6:12: it is the same Greek word and the same Jerusalem body, as the entry at Sanhedrin gives it. The apparatus gives pan to synedrion to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and holon to synedrion autōn, “the whole council of theirs,” to Robinson–Pierpont. The Robinson–Pierpont holon is the word that stands with synedrion in the Passion narratives of the printed text: holon to synedrion at Mark 14:55 and 15:1 and to synedrion holon at Matthew 26:59. The added autōn, “their,” shows in KJV “all their council” and Geneva “all their Council”; ASV “all the council” and Douay “all the council” do not carry it. NIV “all the members of the Sanhedrin” and TLB “the Jewish Council” add a descriptive word; NWT 1984 prints the Greek word as a name, as this translation did in the earlier chapters. For the other half of the clause, KJV, ASV, NIV, NWT 1984 and TLB print “chief priests” for tous archiereis; Geneva “high Priests” and Douay “the priests” differ. The one who gives the order is the tribune, a Roman military officer, and Luke does not say on what authority he convenes the Jerusalem body.

“Bringing Paul down, he set him before them” (Acts 22:30). Katagagōn ton Paulon estēsen eis autous. Katagō, “to lead down,” runs the other way from the steps (anabathmoi) of 21:35, and the same verb, in the aorist katēgagon, is the one the tribune uses of this step in his letter at 23:28: boulomenos epignōnai tēn aitian … katēgagon eis to synedrion autōn, “wishing to know the cause … I brought him down to their council.” The letter and the narrative describe the same step in nearly the same words, with “the cause” there where this verse has “the certainty.” The verb estēsen is the one this translation printed as “stood them” at 5:27, where the apostles are put in the Sanhedrin, and at 6:13 it is what the Sanhedrin’s side does with the false witnesses against Stephen. The phrase eis autous is literally “into them,” and it can mean “before” or “among.” KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay print “set him before them”; NIV “had him stand before them” and TLB “had Paul brought in before them” read it as “before” and put the causing into the verb; NWT 1984 “stood him among them” takes the other sense.

What the chapter leaves open (Acts 22:30). The chapter stops with Paul standing in front of the Sanhedrin and no word spoken. Two things are not said. The first is what the tribune expects to learn: Luke gives his wish, gnōnai to asphales, to know the certainty of the charge, and does not say how the Sanhedrin is to supply it, by stating the charge, by hearing Paul, or by some decision; the Sanhedrin is given no speech, and the tribune is given none either. The second is whether a Roman is brought into the room. The verse names the man set before the Sanhedrin as “Paul,” and the word Rhōmaios, which Paul, the centurion and the tribune have been using of him in vv25–29, is not in v30. Luke does not say whether the Sanhedrin is told, by the tribune or by Paul, that the man in front of it is a Roman, and he does not say whether the tribune or any soldier stays in the room. Paul’s own first words to the Sanhedrin are at 23:1: Andres adelphoi, egō pasē syneidēsei agathē pepoliteumai tō theō. The verb pepoliteumai is built on the same polit- stem as politeia; what it says there belongs to the note at 23:1.
