🕰
Moses’ own last words: a tribal blessing spoken before he climbs the mountain the previous chapter named, opening with a theophany from Sinai and closing with Jeshurun’s God riding the heavens on Israel’s behalf. Reuben and Judah both get one bare verse; Levi’s curse in Genesis becomes a priesthood here; Benjamin the predator becomes Benjamin the beloved; Joseph’s blessing borrows its own closing words from Jacob’s, forty years on. Eleven tribes are named. Simeon, cursed alongside Levi on the same page of Genesis, is not among them.
Still the plains of Moab in the fortieth year, and the whole of the weekly portion Vezot Haberakhah, the last in the annual cycle. Hebrew and English verse numbers align throughout. Masoretic paragraph breaks fall after vv6, 7 (a stronger petuchah), 11, 12, 17, 19, 21, 23 and 29 — one per tribe, except Dan and Naphtali sharing a unit and Asher running straight into the closing hymn with no break at all.

Verses 1–5 · The only time the whole Torah calls Moses ‘the man of God,’ a fiery law no two shelf versions read alike, and peoples the shelf quietly makes singular

★★★ V1 gives Moses a title the Torah has never once used of him before — at the very moment he stops needing one. Searched across the whole archived Hebrew Bible, ish ha-Elohim, ‘the man of God,’ stands in 65 verses, almost all of them later prophets — Elijah, Elisha, the anonymous figures who confront Eli and Jeroboam. Read against Genesis through Deuteronomy alone, this is the ONLY one: five books, and the phrase is saved for the superscription of Moses’s own last words. Every other title this book has given him — servant, prophet, the man Moses — is retired in the same verse that opens his final speech. All thirteen shelf witnesses keep the title, from KJV/ASV ‘the man of God’ to NWT 1984 ‘the man of the true God’ and the RV60 «varón de Dios».

★★★ V2’s ‘fiery law’ is a written/read split as real as any ketiv/qere on these pages, and the shelf divides on it as sharply as anywhere in this project. Mechon-Mamre’s own printed text shows both forms side by side, exactly as it does for a marked ketiv/qere: the consonants run together as ONE word, אשדת, and the traditional vowelling reads them as TWO, esh dat, ‘fire, a decree’ — the reading this translation follows, with KJV (‘a fiery law’), ASV (the same), Douay-Rheims (‘a fiery law’) and Geneva 1599 (‘a fiery Law’) all in agreement. But dat, ‘decree, law,’ is elsewhere in the Hebrew Bible a LATE word, at home in Esther and Ezra’s Persian-court vocabulary — which is exactly why some readers, ancient and modern, have proposed the consonants hide a different, older word for a mountainside or a company of warriors instead. NWT 1984 reads the whole clause completely differently, ‘at his right hand warriors belonging to them’ — no fire, no law — and the NIV quietly rewrites the verse around the problem rather than choosing a side, dropping the clause and reading instead ‘he came with myriads of holy ones… from his mountain slopes,’ with the fire and the law both gone. Four renderings of one Hebrew word, and this translation states which reading it follows rather than pretending the choice was obvious.

★★ V3’s ‘peoples’ is plural in the Hebrew, and the shelf quietly makes it one. Chovev amim reads, letter for letter, ‘he loves PEOPLES’ — the ordinary plural, not a singular collective. In context, flanked by v5’s ‘all the tribes of Israel together,’ the most natural sense is Israel’s OWN constituent peoples, the tribes now gathering to hear this speech, rather than a sudden universalist claim about the nations of the earth; this translation keeps the plural and lets the reader draw that connection rather than deciding it for them. KJV reads ‘he loved the people’; ASV ‘he loveth the people’; NWT 1984 ‘he was also cherishing his people’ — three collapses of one plural into a singular ‘people,’ and NIV a fourth, ‘it is you who love the people.’ ⚠ Only one shelf version keeps it: the RV 1909 alone reads «amó los pueblos», PEOPLES plural — and its own 1960 revision quietly smooths it away to the singular «su pueblo», the same collapse the whole English shelf makes.

⚠ V4 briefly changes who is speaking, and two shelf versions (in both their editions) mark it, though not by name. Verses 2–3 and 5 are third-person report ABOUT Jehovah; v4 alone shifts to first person — ‘Moses commanded US a law’ — as if the congregation itself breaks in to answer, a single responsive line inside a poem otherwise narrated about them. NWT 1984 sets the whole verse off in parentheses, ‘(Moses laid as a command upon us a law…)’, its 2013 revision keeping the same parenthesis; the Spanish New World Translation does the same in both its editions, «(Moisés nos impuso como mandato una ley…)». The other nine witnesses checked mark the shift not at all; this translation reports it in prose rather than borrowing NWT’s own punctuation.

Verses 6–7 · The two firstborn brothers get the two thinnest blessings in the chapter, and the Douay-Rheims is the one version that will not soften either

★★★ V6’s blessing on Reuben supplies no verb of growth at all, and half the shelf quietly adds one. The bare Hebrew is yechi Re’uven ve-al yamot, vihi metav mispar — ‘let Reuben live and not die, and let his men be a NUMBER’ — mispar alone being the ordinary Biblical idiom for ‘few’ (Genesis 34:30, Deuteronomy 4:27). Read plainly, the whole blessing is one clause of bare survival followed by an unsoftened admission that the tribe will stay small — which is exactly the sentence Genesis 49:3-4 (already on these pages) already passed on Reuben, stripped of his firstborn’s excellence for defiling his father’s bed. KJV and ASV both insert a ‘not’ the Hebrew does not have (‘let NOT his men be few’); the NWT 1984 brackets its own addition outright, ‘[not] become few’, showing its own work; the Living Bible paraphrases the whole clause into a wish, ‘may his tribe increase.’ ⚠ The Douay-Rheims, usually the version most likely to be translating Latin rather than Hebrew, is here the most literal version on either shelf: ‘Let Ruben live, and not die, AND BE HE SMALL IN NUMBER’ — no inserted negative, the bleak reading intact. The Spanish shelf splits the same way: the RV 1909 keeps it bare, «y sean sus varones en número», while its own 1960 revision inserts the negative its parent lacked, «y no sean pocos», and the NVI paraphrases past it entirely, «innumerables», the opposite sense. This translation follows the unsoftened reading, with Geneva’s own concessive ‘though’ carrying the tension rather than erasing it.

★★ V7’s blessing on Judah is a single prayer for military help, with no scepter, no Shiloh, and no kingship anywhere in it. Genesis 49:8-10 (already on these pages) gave Judah the longest, most celebrated blessing of the twelve — a lion, a scepter that will not depart, a ruler’s staff, obedience owed to him by peoples. Forty years later, on the eve of the conquest under a leader from a DIFFERENT tribe, Judah gets one verse asking only that Jehovah hear his voice, bring him home, and help him against his adversaries — no longer than Reuben’s, Benjamin’s, Dan’s or Naphtali’s, and shorter than Levi’s, Joseph’s or Gad’s. Every shelf version keeps the plea intact; none supplies the royal language Genesis 49 already spent. The Masoretic Text marks a petuchah, an open paragraph break, after this verse — the strongest break in the whole poem, closing off Israel’s two firstborn contenders, Reuben by birth and Judah by destiny, before the priestly tribe gets the chapter’s longest word.

Verses 8–11 · Levi’s curse in Genesis becomes Levi’s blessing here, and the hinge is a massacre this project has already shown the reader

★★★ Levi and Simeon were cursed together for the identical crime, and only one of them is standing here to collect a different verdict. Genesis 49:5-7 (already on these pages) condemned Levi in the same breath as Simeon — ‘weapons of violence are their swords… cursed be their anger, for it is fierce… I will divide them in Jacob and scatter them in Israel’ — a punishment for slaughtering the men of Shechem. Deuteronomy 33 does not revoke that verdict; it fulfills it on different terms. Levi IS scattered — into forty-eight towns inside every other tribe’s territory, a landless priesthood by design — but the scattering that was a curse in Genesis is the very thing that lets Levi ‘teach Jacob your judgments, and Israel your law’ in v10. The mechanism God announced as punishment becomes, one book later, the shape of the priesthood.

★★★ V9’s ‘he did not know his own sons’ is not a general description of loyalty — it is this chapter’s own account of a specific massacre this project has already shown in full. At Exodus 32:26-29 (already on these pages), after the golden calf, Moses called ‘whoever is on Jehovah’s side’ and the sons of Levi answered; he sent them through the camp to ‘kill every man his brother, and every man his companion, and every man his neighbor,’ about three thousand fell, and Moses told them they had earned a blessing that day because ‘every man has been against his son and against his brother.’ V9 is that verse’s own vocabulary, decades later, turned into praise: the tribe that did not spare father, brother or son for Jehovah’s sake is the tribe now trusted with incense and the altar. The SAME violent zeal that damns Levi in Genesis 49 (against a foreign town, unauthorized) sanctifies Levi here (against Israel’s own idolatry, commanded) — the chapter does not soften the parallel, and neither does this translation.

⚠ V8’s Urim and Thummim are written with a possessive suffix this project’s own dictionary entry has not yet tracked. The existing Urim entry counts the bare plural across six verses of the Hebrew Bible, from its institution at Exodus 28:30 to its absence at the return from exile. Ureicha, ‘YOUR Urim,’ here in v8, is a different consonantal form — a suffixed possessive rather than the bare noun — so it falls outside that count on a technicality worth stating rather than silently folding in. In the story’s own chronology this is the object’s FIRST appearance, spoken of Levi as a tribe before either the object or Aaron’s breastpiece has been described in Exodus; the poem is not bound by the book order this translation otherwise follows it in.

★ ‘Massah’ and ‘Meribah’ name the SAME event here, the one this project’s own Massah/Meribah entry already built around Exodus 17’s double place-name at Rephidim, near Horeb. That entry already distinguishes this earlier site from the LATER, different Meribah of Kadesh in the wilderness of Zin (Numbers 20, already on these pages), where Moses himself lost the land for striking a rock he was told to speak to. V8’s pairing of the two names together, exactly as Exodus 17 pairs them, anchors this verse to the earlier site rather than the one that cost Moses his own entry into Canaan — a distinction worth keeping clear, since the two episodes are opposites in what they demand (strike, then speak) and this verse names only the first.

Verse 12 · Benjamin the wolf becomes Benjamin the beloved, and a later tradition reads ‘between his shoulders’ as a mountain range

★★ Benjamin’s second blessing reverses his first as completely as Levi’s does. Genesis 49:27 (already on these pages) gave Benjamin one predatory image: ‘a wolf that tears; in the morning he eats prey, and at evening he divides spoil.’ Here the tribe is ‘the beloved of Jehovah,’ sheltered rather than devouring, dwelling ‘in safety BY him’ rather than hunting. Nothing in the two verses’ vocabulary overlaps; only the tribe does.

⚠ ‘Between his shoulders’ is a real geographical reading this project reports without adopting. Later Jewish tradition connects Benjamin’s territory to the hill on which the Jerusalem temple was later built, reading ‘he dwells between his shoulders’ as the two ridges flanking the Temple Mount, with Jehovah himself ‘shouldering’ his sanctuary’s tribe. The Hebrew supports the image without requiring it — a shepherd or a father carrying a beloved child between his shoulders is the plainer sense — and this translation keeps the reading open rather than building a specific hill into the verse.

Verses 13–17 · A phrase that stands in exactly two verses of the whole Hebrew Bible, both of them Joseph, forty years apart

★★★ V16’s closing words are not an echo of Genesis 49’s blessing on Joseph — they are close to a quotation of it. Searched across the whole archived Hebrew Bible, nezir echav, ‘the one set apart from his brothers,’ stands in exactly TWO verses: Genesis 49:26 (already on these pages) — ‘let them be on the head of Joseph, and on the crown of the one set apart from his brothers’ — and this one. The two verses do not merely share a theme; they share their LAST FOUR WORDS in the Hebrew, letter for letter, changing only the verb that brings the blessing ‘in’ (Genesis’s tihyeinah, ‘let them be’) against the one that brings it in this chapter (tavo’atah, ‘let it come’). Whoever composed this poem had Jacob’s blessing of Joseph in front of them, or in memory, closely enough to reproduce its own ending. The wild ox of v17 completes a promise this project already made: Numbers 24:8 (already on these pages) flagged that re’em stands in exactly three verses of Torah poetry, all of them describing this same animal’s horns, and named this verse as the third, not yet shipped. It is now: Balaam gave the image to Israel corporately twice, and this chapter gives it to Joseph by name a third and final time.

★★ The cosmic-blessing vocabulary of vv13-16 — heaven above, the deep below, the ancient mountains, the everlasting hills — is also Genesis 49’s, not this poem’s own invention. Genesis 49:25-26 blesses Joseph with ‘blessings of heaven above, blessings of the deep crouching below… to the bound of the everlasting hills’; this chapter answers with ‘the choice things of heaven… the deep that lies beneath… the everlasting hills.’ Of every tribe in this chapter, only Joseph gets a blessing built this explicitly out of his own earlier one — four generations’ distance collapsed into the same handful of images, twice.

⚠ V17’s closing numbers settle an argument Genesis 48 opened and Numbers 26 already measured. Genesis 48:14-20 (already on these pages) has Jacob deliberately cross his hands to bless the younger Ephraim ahead of the firstborn Manasseh, over Joseph’s own objection — ‘he set Ephraim before Manasseh.’ Numbers 26 (already on these pages) then measured the reversal in real numbers across the wilderness years: Manasseh's fighting men nearly doubled while a neighboring tribe fell, closing 20,200 ahead of where the crossed blessing predicted a younger brother would end up smaller. This verse states the outcome as settled fact, in the same ‘ten thousands’-against-‘thousands’ proportion Jacob's own blessing implied: Ephraim first, Manasseh second, the order the whole Torah now agrees on three times over.

Verses 18–21 · The younger brother named first twice over, and the lawgiver’s portion a later tradition reads as Moses’s own grave

★ Zebulun is named before Issachar in both of Israel’s tribal blessings, and Issachar was born first. Genesis 30 (already on these pages) has Leah bear Issachar as her fifth son and Zebulun as her sixth, two verses apart; yet Genesis 49:13-15 blesses Zebulun before Issachar, and this chapter does the same — ‘Rejoice, Zebulun… and Issachar, in your tents.’ Birth order governs the census lists and the camp layout elsewhere in this project's own pages; here, in BOTH tribal blessings, it is set aside the same way, and neither poem explains why.

★★ V21’s ‘lawgiver’ is the same rare word this project has already tracked to Judah’s own scepter, and this is only its third appearance. The existing mechoqeq entry counts eight occurrences of the word in the Hebrew Bible and names two already on these pages: Genesis 49:10, where the mechoqeq’s staff will not depart from between Judah’s feet, and Numbers 21:18, where Israel’s nobles dig a well with one. Here it names something ‘reserved’ in Gad’s own allotment across the Jordan — the third of the word’s eight verses, and the only one applied to Gad rather than Judah or a literal digging tool. ⚠ A later tradition, reported here rather than verified, reads the reserved ‘portion of the lawgiver’ as Moses’s own grave — unmarked, in the territory Gad received east of the Jordan, the same region this chapter's own next verse (v49, of the previous chapter) sent him to die in sight of, never entered.

★ Gad’s lion image is the fiercest picture in this chapter, and all thirteen shelf versions keep it. ‘He dwells as a LIONESS, and tears the arm, even the crown of the head’ keeps Genesis 49’s predatory register (Judah’s lion, Dan’s serpent, Benjamin’s wolf) alive in a chapter that has otherwise been retiring it tribe by tribe. All thirteen witnesses checked on both shelves keep a lion image here; none reaches for Benjamin’s softening move.

Verses 22–25 · A lion where Genesis had a serpent, a leap toward territory Dan would not hold for generations, and a gate-bolt whose word never recurs

★★ Dan changes animals between the two blessings, and the change is total. Genesis 49:16-17 (already on these pages) made Dan ‘a serpent on the way, a viper on the path, biting the horse’s heels’ — an ambush image, a judge who strikes from concealment. Here Dan is simply ‘a lion’s cub, that leaps forth from Bashan’ — open, fast, unambiguous strength, the same cub-imagery this chapter has already given Gad. Every shelf version keeps both pictures as given; none tries to reconcile a serpent with a lion cub across the two poems.

⚠ ‘From Bashan’ is a genuine geographical puzzle, honestly stated rather than resolved. Dan’s allotted territory, on every account this project has access to, lay on the western coastal plain near Philistia, nowhere near Bashan in the far north beyond the Jordan — the kingdom this project has already shown Israel conquering under Numbers 21 (already on these pages). Later biblical narrative (Judges 18, not yet on these pages) describes the tribe of Dan migrating north in search of new territory, eventually settling near Bashan’s own region; whether this verse anticipates that later move, describes something else entirely, or reflects a poem composed or edited after it happened is a real question this translation states rather than answers.

★ Naphtali’s ‘the west and the south’ keeps a directional idiom already established on these pages. Yam, literally ‘sea,’ is Hebrew’s standing word for WEST — the direction the Mediterranean actually lies from a speaker standing in Canaan — and this translation keeps that established convention here rather than printing ‘sea’ and leaving the reader to work out the geography themselves.

★★ V25’s ‘bolts’ is a hapax four shelf versions read as footwear instead, and the KJV is one of them. Searched across the whole archived Hebrew Bible, min’alekha stands in exactly ONE verse — this one — built on the root na’al, which covers both a SANDAL and a door-BOLT or lock, and nothing elsewhere in the Bible settles which sense this form carries. Paired with ‘iron and bronze,’ hardware for a gate reads more naturally than hardware for a foot, and the shelf splits closer to evenly than any other crux in this chapter, four to nine. KJV reads ‘thy shoes’, Geneva 1599 the same, ‘thy shoes’, and Douay-Rheims, following the Vulgate’s calceamentum, ‘his shoe’ (switching without comment to third person, and then supplying an entirely different clause for what follows: not strength matching days, but ‘as the days of thy youth, so also shall thy old age be’) — three of the four English versions checked, all reading footwear. ASV breaks with its usual closeness to KJV here and reads ‘thy bars’ instead, joined by NWT 1984 (‘your gate locks’), NIV (‘the bolts of your gates’) and TLB (‘strong bolts’). The Spanish shelf keeps the same fault line: the RV 1909 reads «tu calzado», footwear, while its own 1960 revision moves to «tus cerrojos» and the NVI agrees. The word this translation renders ‘strength,’ dov’ekha, is itself a second hapax in the same verse, its meaning inferred from context and a disputed root rather than fixed by any other occurrence — two of the rarest words in the whole chapter, back to back, in its next-to-last verse.

Verses 26–29 · The everlasting arms, a verb that means both ‘cringe’ and ‘lie,’ and the tribe that is never named at all

★★★ Jeshurun completes the four-verse footprint this project already promised, and the closing hymn pays off a word planted two chapters ago. Deuteronomy 32:15 (already on these pages) named Israel Jeshurun for the first time and flagged that the name recurs in exactly four verses of the whole Hebrew Bible: that one, this chapter’s v5 above, this verse (v26), and Isaiah 44:2. This is the name’s SECOND and THIRD appearances inside one poem — v5 crowning Jehovah king ‘in Jeshurun’ at the chapter’s opening, v26 declaring ‘there is none like the God of Jeshurun’ at its close, the whole tribal blessing framed between two uses of a name built on the very root, ‘upright,’ that Deuteronomy 32 used as an accusation against a people who ‘grew fat and kicked.’

★★ V28’s ‘alone’ answers a question Numbers 23 already raised and left open. Numbers 23:9 (now on these pages) flagged that Balaam’s ‘a people that dwells alone,’ le-vadad, is read as isolation by the whole English shelf and as SAFETY by the whole Spanish one, and named this verse’s own badad as the place the ambiguity would return. It does, and here the poem settles it for the reader itself: Israel dwells betach badad, ‘in safety, alone’ — the two words paired in the same clause, so that the isolation and the security are not a translator’s choice but the verse’s own claim, together.

★ V27’s ‘everlasting arms’ is a phrase the whole English shelf keeps intact, syllable for syllable. KJV, ASV and NIV all print ‘underneath are the everlasting arms’; the NWT 1984 varies only the adjective, ‘indefinitely lasting arms.’ It is one of the few lines in this entire chapter where four centuries of English Bible translation have not moved the wording at all.

⚠ V29’s final verb carries two opposite senses, and the shelf divides along them. Ve-yikachashu oyveicha lakh uses the root kachash, which covers both DECEPTION (to lie, to deny) and, by extension, the physical CRINGING of someone caught in one. KJV reads ‘thine enemies shall be found liars unto thee’, and Douay-Rheims the same idea in its own words, ‘thy enemies shall deny thee’; ASV reads the extended sense as submission, ‘shall submit themselves unto thee’; NWT 1984 reads physical fear outright, ‘your enemies will cringe before you’, and NIV a near-synonym of it, ‘your enemies will cower before you’. This translation follows the physical sense, since it pairs more naturally with the next clause’s trampled high places, but states the root’s other half rather than hiding it.

★★★ Eleven tribes are blessed by name in this chapter. The twelfth is never mentioned at all. Reuben opens the list, Asher closes the individually-named tribes, and Joseph is counted twice over, as Ephraim and Manasseh — but Simeon’s name does not appear in these twenty-nine verses, not blessed, not cursed, not mentioned in passing. Genesis 49:5-7 (already on these pages) cursed Simeon and Levi together for the same act of violence and sentenced both to be ‘scattered in Israel’; this chapter shows what that sentence became for each of them separately — Levi’s scattering reworked into a landless priesthood with a blessing of its own, Simeon’s scattering left to run its course with no blessing at all. Numbers 26 (already on these pages) already measured what that looked like in practice: of every tribe in either census, Simeon fell hardest, LOSING NEARLY TWO-THIRDS of its fighting men between Sinai and the plains of Moab, with no explanation offered beyond a chief of that tribe named at the spear-point of Phinehas’s own zeal three chapters earlier. A poem built to bless every tribe on the eve of the conquest passes over the one tribe whose numbers had already told the reader what to expect.

Echoes paid. Yeshurun, promised at Deuteronomy 32:15, reaches its second and third appearances (vv5, 26). Numbers 23:9’s open question about ‘dwelling alone’ is answered at v28. Numbers 24:8’s promised third sighting of the wild ox lands at v17. Genesis 48’s crossed blessing and Numbers 26’s measured reversal both close at v17’s Ephraim-before-Manasseh. And mechoqeq, tracked to Judah’s scepter at Genesis 49:10, reaches its third of eight biblical verses at v21.

Echoes planted. Deuteronomy 34 (not yet on these pages) is where Moses climbs the mountain this chapter’s own predecessor named, and where the ‘portion of the lawgiver’ some later tradition reads into v21 would be fulfilled. Judges 18 (not yet on these pages) is flagged for Dan’s own migration north, the event this chapter’s ‘from Bashan’ may or may not already assume. And Isaiah 44:2 (not yet on these pages) carries the fourth and final biblical use of Jeshurun.

Decisions. Hebrew and English verse numbers align through this whole chapter. Masoretic paragraph breaks fall after vv6, 7 (a stronger petuchah), 11, 12, 17, 19, 21, 23 and 29 — one per tribe, except Dan and Naphtali sharing a unit and Asher running straight into the closing hymn with no break at all, which this translation reports rather than imposes further structure onto. Amim at v3 is kept plural against twelve of the thirteen shelf witnesses (the RV 1909 alone agrees); mispar at v6 is kept without an inserted negative; kachash at v29 follows the physical sense over the legal one, with the alternative stated. ‘Jehovah’ for the tetragrammaton and the established tribal-name spellings continue this project’s own conventions.

Library. Six new dictionary entries in both languages: eshdat, the v2 crux; amim, this verse’s own kept plural; yam as a direction-word; kachash, the closing verb’s two senses; and v25’s own pair of hapax legomena, min’al and dov’ekha. Seven existing entries extended: re’em, urim, massah-meribah, mechoqeq, nazir and yeshurun in both languages (the wild ox’s own promised third citation now a working link; the suffixed v8 Urim form; this chapter’s own citation of the Massah/Meribah pairing; the lawgiver’s third of eight verses; the participial ‘set apart’ sense of nazir, distinct from the vow-law entry already keyed to Samuel; and Jeshurun’s second and third sightings) — and ish ha-Elohim, extended in English with its Torah-wide rarity and given its first SPANISH entry here, closing a parity gap that predates this chapter. One encyclopedia entry: Simeon, extended in English with this chapter’s own absence as a new citation, and likewise given its first Spanish entry here — a second parity gap closed the same evening.

Next installment in this book: Deuteronomy 34 — the last chapter of the Torah, where Moses climbs the mountain this chapter has kept pointing toward, sees the land from a distance, and dies at a hundred and twenty with his eye undimmed and his vigor unspent — a different word for strength than this chapter’s own hapax at v25, and the last one the Torah will supply.
