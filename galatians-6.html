Paul closes his letter to the Galatians: mend the fallen and bear one another’s burdens, sow to the Spirit, and then, in his own large handwriting, boast only in the cross, a new creation, and peace upon the Israel of God.
Undated by the text: Galatians names no year, and the letter does not say where it was written; a later note in some manuscripts says Rome.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–5 · caught in a trespass, mending, one another’s burdens, the law of Christ, and each one’s own load

“Caught in some trespass … mend such a one.” Prolambanō is to be taken by surprise, caught before one sees it coming; the NWT 1984 reads “takes some false step before he is aware of it.” Paraptōma, “trespass,” is the word of Romans 5:15 (dictionary). Katartizō is to mend, to put back in working order, the word for the brothers “mending the nets” at Mark 1:19 and for Paul’s last request to Corinth, “be mended” (2 Corinthians 13:11) (dictionary). “In a spirit of gentleness” is the phrase of 1 Corinthians 4:21, and praütēs is the “gentleness” just named in the fruit of the Spirit (5:23). Then the address turns singular, “watching yourself.” The KJV reads “restore such an one in the spirit of meekness”; the Douay reads “instruct such a one,” from the Vulgate’s instruite.

⚠ “Bear one another’s burdens … each one will carry his own load.” The two verses are often read as a contradiction, but the nouns differ. Barē are weights, heavy things; phortion is a load, the pack a person is expected to carry, the word of “my burden is light” (Matthew 11:30) and of the ship’s “cargo” (Acts 27:10). This translation keeps them apart as “burdens” and “load.” The verb in both is bastazō, “bear,” as in Romans 15:1, “We who are strong ought to bear the weaknesses of the weak.” Whether “will carry” in v5 looks to the judgment or to ordinary life is read both ways. The KJV uses one English word for both, “Bear ye one another’s burdens” and “every man shall bear his own burden”; the NIV and the NWT 1984 read “load” in v5.

“The law of Christ.” “The law of Christ” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; its nearest neighbour is 1 Corinthians 9:21, “not outside God’s law but under Christ’s law.” After a letter against taking on the law, Paul names a law to fulfill. Readings differ on what it is: Jesus’ own teaching, the love command just quoted at 5:14, or the pattern of Christ himself, who “handed himself over for me” (2:20). Tregelles and the Nestle–Aland 28th read the future, “you will fulfill”; Westcott–Hort and the Byzantine text read the command, “fulfill,” and so the KJV: “and so fulfil the law of Christ”; the NIV reads “in this way you will fulfill the law of Christ.”

“Deceiving his own mind … his ground for boasting.” Phrenapataō, from phrēn, “mind,” and apataō, “deceive,” stands as a verb in one verse of the New Testament, this one; its noun is at Titus 1:10, “deceivers of the mind, especially those of the circumcision.” Dokimazō is the “test” of 2 Corinthians 13:5, “Test yourselves.” Kauchēma, “ground for boasting” (dictionary), is to be found in one’s own work, “and not in the other”: not by comparison. The NWT 1984 reads “and not in comparison with the other person”; the KJV reads “then shall he have rejoicing in himself alone, and not in another.”

Verses 6–10 · the one instructed, God not mocked, sowing and reaping, not losing heart, and the household of the faith

“The one who is instructed in the word.” Katēcheō, “instruct,” the verb behind English “catechism,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament, and this one has it twice (dictionary). Theophilus was “instructed” at Luke 1:4, Apollos at Acts 18:25. “Share in all good things” is usually read as support for the teacher, as Paul argues at 1 Corinthians 9:14, “those who proclaim the good news should live from the good news,” though the sharing could be wider. The KJV reads “communicate unto him that teacheth in all good things”; the TLB reads “should help their teachers by paying them.”

“Do not be misled: God is not mocked.” “Do not be misled” is the warning of 1 Corinthians 6:9. Myktērizō is built on myktēr, the nostril: to turn up one’s nose, to sneer. The simple verb stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; its compound is the Pharisees “sneering at him” (Luke 16:14) and the rulers who “sneered at him” at the cross (Luke 23:35). The KJV reads “Be not deceived; God is not mocked”; the TLB reads “you can’t ignore God and get away with it.”

“Whatever a person sows, that he will also reap.” The proverb is the one of 2 Corinthians 9:6, “whoever sows sparingly will also reap sparingly.” Sowing “to the flesh” and “to the Spirit” carries on the contest of 5:16–25. Phthora, “corruption,” is decay, the word of 1 Corinthians 15:42, “It is sown in corruption, it is raised in incorruption.” The Vulgate divides the verses differently, and so the Douay ends v7 at “God is not mocked” and begins v8 “For what things a man shall sow, those also shall he reap.” The NIV reads “from the flesh will reap destruction”; the ASV reads “shall of the flesh reap corruption.”

“Let us not lose heart … in its own season.” Enkakeō, to lose heart, stands in six verses of the New Testament: Luke 18:1, “always to pray and not lose heart”; 2 Corinthians 4:1 and 4:16, “we do not lose heart”; this one; Ephesians 3:13; and 2 Thessalonians 3:13 (dictionary). Kairos, the right time, comes twice: “in its own season we will reap,” and in v10, “as we have opportunity.” The KJV reads “let us not be weary in well doing: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not”; the NIV reads “at the proper time we will reap a harvest if we do not give up.”

“The members of the household of the faith.” Oikeioi, the members of a household, stands in three verses of the New Testament: here; Ephesians 2:19, “members of the household of God”; and 1 Timothy 5:8. The good is to be done “toward all,” and the household comes first, not alone. The KJV reads “especially unto them who are of the household of faith”; the NIV reads “to those who belong to the family of believers”; the NWT 1984 reads “those related to [us] in the faith.”

Verses 11–13 · large letters in Paul’s own hand, a fine showing in the flesh, and boasting in your flesh

⚠ “See with what large letters I have written to you with my own hand.” Paul’s letters were usually written by a scribe, as Romans 16:22 shows, “I, Tertius, who wrote this letter”; here he takes the pen. “With my own hand” stands in five verses of the New Testament: 1 Corinthians 16:21, “The greeting is in my own hand, Paul’s”; this one; Colossians 4:18; 2 Thessalonians 3:17; and Philemon 1:19, “I, Paul, write this with my own hand.” Why the letters were large the verse does not say: for emphasis, as a reader underlines; because his hand was not a trained scribe’s; or, as some connect with the eyes of 4:15, because he could not see well. “I have written” can cover these closing lines or the whole letter. The KJV reads “Ye see how large a letter I have written unto you,” as if of length; the Douay reads “See what a letter I have written to you”; the ASV reads “See with how large letters I write unto you”; the TLB reads “See how large I have to make the letters!”

“To make a fine showing in the flesh.” Euprosōpeō, from prosōpon, “face,” to present a good face, stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. Paul gives his own account of the agitators’ motive: to avoid the persecution that 5:11 says comes with the cross. “Compelling” is anankazō, the verb of Titus, who was not “compelled to be circumcised” (2:3), and of Paul’s question to Cephas (2:14). The KJV reads “As many as desire to make a fair shew in the flesh, they constrain you to be circumcised”; the NIV reads “Those who want to impress people by means of the flesh.”

“Those who are being circumcised” — and boasting in your flesh. The critical text has a present participle, “those who are being circumcised,” which may mean Gentile converts who have just taken it on; the Byzantine has the perfect, “those who have been circumcised,” the agitators themselves. The ASV reads “For not even they who receive circumcision do themselves keep the law”; the KJV reads “For neither they themselves who are circumcised keep the law.” “Boast in your flesh” is the opposite of the boasting in v14.

Verses 14–16 · boasting in the cross, a new creation, walking in line with this rule, and the Israel of God

“May it never be that I boast except in the cross.” Mē genoito is the “May it never be!” of 2:17. Di’ hou can be “through which,” the cross, or “through whom,” Christ: the KJV reads “by whom the world is crucified unto me, and I unto the world”; the ASV and the NIV read “through which.” “Crucified” is the word of 2:19 and 5:24. The NWT 1984 reads “the torture stake of our Lord Jesus Christ, through whom the world has been impaled to me,” its rendering of the cross.

“A new creation.” “A new creation” stands in two verses of the New Testament: 2 Corinthians 5:17, “if anyone is in Christ, he is a new creation,” and this one. The sentence repeats 5:6 and 1 Corinthians 7:19 with a third ending: not faith working through love, not keeping commandments, but creation itself made new. The Byzantine text borrows the opening of 5:6, “in Christ Jesus … avails,” and so the KJV reads “For in Christ Jesus neither circumcision availeth any thing, nor uncircumcision, but a new creature”; the NWT 1984 reads “but a new creation [is something].”

“Walk in line with this rule.” Stoicheō is the “walk in line” of 5:25 (dictionary). Kanōn, a measuring rod or rule, stands in four verses of the New Testament: 2 Corinthians 10:13, 10:15 and 10:16, where this translation prints “sphere,” the measured field God assigned to Paul; and this one. English “canon” comes from it (dictionary). The KJV reads “as many as walk according to this rule”; the NWT 1984 reads “all those who will walk orderly by this rule of conduct.”

⚠ “And upon the Israel of God.” “The Israel of God” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and readers have long divided over it. The Greek has kai twice, “peace upon them, and mercy, and upon the Israel of God,” and kai can mean “and” or “even.” One reading takes the Israel of God to be the same people as “as many as will walk in line with this rule,” the believers, Jews and Gentiles together; that reading has been used to argue that the church has taken Israel’s place. A second takes it to be Jewish believers. A third takes it to be Israel as a people, for whom Paul asks mercy, as at Romans 11:26, “and thus all Israel will be saved”; its readers note that everywhere else in Paul “Israel” means the Jewish people, as at Romans 9:6. This page sets out the readings and does not choose. The words echo the psalms’ “Peace upon Israel,” shalom al-Yisrael, which stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, Psalms 125:5 and 128:6; the Greek Old Testament has eirēnē epi ton Israēl. The KJV reads “and upon the Israel of God”; the NWT 1984 reads “even upon the Israel of God”; the NIV reads “Peace and mercy to all who follow this rule—to the Israel of God”; the TLB leaves the name out, “upon those everywhere who are really God’s own.”

Verses 17–18 · the brand-marks of Jesus, and grace with your spirit, brothers

⚠ “The brand-marks of Jesus.” Stigma, a mark pricked or burned into the skin, stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one (dictionary). In Paul’s world such marks branded slaves and, by report, soldiers and devotees of a god as belonging to someone. Paul’s are most often read as the scars of what he lists at 2 Corinthians 11:24 and 11:25, lashes, rods and a stoning; at 2 Corinthians 4:10 he speaks of “carrying about in the body the dying of Jesus.” English “stigmata” comes from this verse, the word later used for the wounds reported on Francis of Assisi. “Bear” is bastazō, the verb of the burdens in v2 and the load in v5. The Byzantine text reads “the Lord Jesus.” The KJV reads “I bear in my body the marks of the Lord Jesus”; the ASV reads “I bear branded on my body the marks of Jesus”; the NWT 1984 reads “the brand marks [of a slave] of Jesus.”

“With your spirit, brothers. Amen.” The same wish closes Philippians 4:23 and Philemon 1:25, “The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit.” A letter that opened with no thanksgiving, “I am astonished” (1:6), puts “brothers” as its last word before the Amen; the first Amen was at 1:5. The letter does not say where it was written. Some manuscripts add a note, “written from Rome,” and the Geneva prints it, “Unto the Galatians written from Rome”; such notes are later additions and are not in the critical text. The KJV reads “Brethren, the grace of our Lord Jesus Christ be with your spirit. Amen.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “mend” for katartizō, as at Mark 1:19 and 2 Corinthians 13:11; “trespass,” as at Romans 5:15; “a spirit of gentleness,” as at 1 Corinthians 4:21; “burdens” for barē and “load” for phortion, kept apart; “bear” for bastazō at 6:2 and 6:17, and “carry” for it at 6:5; “Do not be misled,” as at 1 Corinthians 6:9; “corruption,” as at 1 Corinthians 15:42; “lose heart,” as at 2 Corinthians 4:1; “members of the household,” as at Ephesians 2:19; “with my own hand,” as at 1 Corinthians 16:21; “compel,” as at 2:3 and 2:14; “May it never be,” as at 2:17; “a new creation,” as at 2 Corinthians 5:17; “walk in line,” as at 5:25; and “with your spirit,” as at Philippians 4:23.

Echoes paid. Everything planted at the end of chapter 5: bear one another’s burdens and the law of Christ (6:2), sowing to the flesh and to the Spirit (6:8), circumcision as nothing and a new creation (6:15), and walking in line once more (6:16). Also the gentleness of 5:23; compel from 2:3 and 2:14; the persecution for the cross of 5:11; crucified from 2:19 and 5:24; the eyes of 4:15; Paul’s own hand from 1 Corinthians 16:21; his scars from 2 Corinthians 11:24; and the Amen of 1:5.

Echoes planted. Paul’s own hand again at Colossians 4:18 and 2 Thessalonians 3:17; and Peace upon Israel in Psalms 125:5 and 128:6.

This is the last chapter of Galatians. The next letter is Ephesians 1, already on these pages.
