📖 Matthew

Κατὰ Μαθθαῖον (Kata Maththaion) — 'According to Matthew' — the traditional title; the book itself names no author. The ascription to Matthew the tax-collector (also called Levi) is early and external, first recorded by Papias around AD 130.

The Gospel the early church placed FIRST — the bridge from the Old Testament to the New, written to show a Jewish readership that Jesus is the promised Messiah, the son of David and son of Abraham, in whom the whole story of Israel comes to its point. Built around five great blocks of Jesus' teaching (the Sermon on the Mount the first of them), and framed, beginning to end, by one promise: 'God with us.'

Chapter by chapter

Matthew 1The New Testament opens on a genealogy — 'the book of the genesis of Jesus Christ, son of David, son of Abraham' — the word that opens the Bible (Genesis) chosen to open the Gospel. Forty-two generations in three panels of fourteen, from Abraham to David, David to the exile, the exile to the Christ — and ⚠ four women break into the list of fathers, three of them Gentile and every one touched by scandal: Tamar, Rahab, Ruth, and 'the wife of Uriah'. The count is a designed thing (fourteen is the number of DAVID's name in Hebrew), not a registry: it drops three kings, runs the royal line through the cursed Jechoniah, and breaks its 'fathered… fathered' drumbeat at the last link, where Jesus is not fathered by Joseph but 'BORN of' Mary. ⚠ Then the birth itself: Mary found with child 'of the Holy Spirit'; Joseph, a righteous man, resolving to divorce her quietly until an angel stops him in a dream; the name JESUS unfolded as 'YHWH saves' ('he will save his people from their sins'); and Isaiah's virgin-sign landed as EMMANUEL, 'God with us' — the name that brackets the whole Gospel, which will close on 'I am with you always'.Matthew 2The nativity's dark half. Magi from the east — astrologer-priests of Persia, not kings, and Matthew never counts them — arrive in Jerusalem asking for a king who was BORN one, which is the one thing Herod, voted his title by the Roman Senate, was not; and the city that should have rejoiced is 'shaken' along with him. ⚠ The chief priests answer correctly out of Micah and then nobody walks the six miles to look, while the men with a star and no Scripture cross half the east and fall down in a HOUSE (there is no stable here and no shepherds — those are Luke's, and the two accounts never overlap). Then the chapter turns: Herod's 'secretly' is the same word as Joseph's 'quietly' one chapter earlier, and the time he ascertains 'exactly' becomes the width of the killing. ⚠ Four dreams, four withdrawals, and four quotations — Bethlehem, Egypt, Ramah, Nazareth — three of them famously difficult: 'out of Egypt I called my son' is a verse about ISRAEL, the angel's 'those who were seeking the child's life are dead' is Moses' own marching orders quoted without a footnote, and 'he shall be called a Nazarene' is a prophecy that exists in no book. Rachel, buried at Bethlehem, weeps over its children and will not be comforted — the same two words Jacob used at the pit.Matthew 5The Sermon on the Mount opens in red letters — eight Happy-sayings, salt and light, not one iota, and six rounds of 'You have heard… but I say': anger, lust, oaths, the other cheek, love for enemies.Matthew 6The Sermon's middle chapter — alms, prayer, and fasting in secret; the Lord's Prayer and its famous missing doxology; treasure, the undivided eye, Mammon — and the birds and the lilies.Matthew 7The Sermon's finale — the splinter and the beam, ask-seek-knock, the Golden Rule signed, the narrow gate, wolves and fruit, 'I never knew you' — and the house on the rock.

About the book

Where it sits
The FIRST book of the New Testament in every canonical list — its position a judgement about its bridging role, not its date. One of the four canonical Gospels, and one of the three SYNOPTICS (with Mark and Luke), which share so much material they can be set in parallel columns and 'seen together'.
Genre
A GOSPEL — the theological narrative form Mark had recently pioneered, here expanded with large collections of teaching. Matthew is the most STRUCTURED of the four: a genealogy and infancy narrative, then the ministry organised around five discourses (each closed by 'when Jesus had finished these words'), then the passion and resurrection. It reads like a teaching manual for the church, catechetical and orderly, and became the most-used Gospel in the early centuries.
Author
By strong and early tradition, MATTHEW (Levi), the tax-collector whom Jesus called from his booth (9:9; Mark 2:14 names the same man Levi) — one of the Twelve. ⚠ The majority of critical scholars doubt direct apostolic authorship: the Gospel appears to draw on Mark (an odd dependence for an eyewitness on a non-eyewitness), its Greek is polished rather than translated, and Papias's remark that Matthew 'compiled the sayings in the Hebrew dialect' does not obviously describe this Greek book. The main options: the apostle Matthew, or a later Jewish-Christian author (or school) drawing on apostolic tradition, writing in Matthew's name or under his authority. The library reports the case and casts no vote.
Date written
Most place it c. AD 80–90 — after Mark (which it seems to use) and apparently after the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, which some read as reflected in 22:7 ('he burned their city'). A minority, holding to apostolic authorship and an early Mark, argue for the 60s. The date follows the questions of authorship and sources.
Place
Uncertain; ANTIOCH in Syria is the most common guess — a large, mixed Jewish-and-Gentile church, the first place believers were called 'Christians' (Acts 11:26), and the earliest to quote this Gospel (Ignatius of Antioch, c. AD 110). Other proposals include Palestine and Phoenicia.
Audience
A JEWISH-CHRISTIAN community, at a moment of painful separation from the synagogue. The Gospel assumes readers who know the Scriptures and Jewish custom (it rarely stops to explain them, where Mark does), argues constantly from prophecy ('that it might be fulfilled'), and is sharply engaged with the Pharisees — the rival teachers of a Judaism reorganising itself after the Temple's fall. Yet it ends by sending the message to 'all nations': a book rooted in Israel and opening outward.

How it's laid out

1:1–2:23The genealogy and infancy — the son of David and Abraham, the virgin birth, the magi, the flight to Egypt, 'God with us'.
3:1–7:29The kingdom announced — John, the baptism, the temptation, and the SERMON ON THE MOUNT (the first discourse).
8:1–10:42Authority and mission — nine miracles, the call of the Twelve, and the missionary discourse (the second).
11:1–13:58Growing opposition — questions and conflict, and the parables of the kingdom (the third discourse).
14:1–18:35The church forming — Peter's confession, the Transfiguration, and the discourse on community and forgiveness (the fourth).
19:1–25:46Toward Jerusalem — the entry, controversies in the Temple, the woes on the Pharisees, and the discourse on the end (the fifth).
26:1–28:20The passion and resurrection — the last supper, Gethsemane, the cross, the empty tomb, and the Great Commission: 'I am with you always.'

What it's about

  • FULFILMENT — Matthew's signature: some dozen times 'this took place to fulfil what was spoken', reading Jesus' life as the deliberate completion of Israel's Scriptures, from the virgin of Isaiah 7 to 'out of Egypt I called my son'.
  • JESUS as the NEW MOSES and son of David — a child threatened by a king and called out of Egypt, giving the law from a mountain, arranging his teaching in five books as the Torah has five; and the royal Messiah, 'son of David', the title that runs the length of the Gospel.
  • The KINGDOM OF HEAVEN — Matthew's reverent phrase for the kingdom of God, the announced reign that the Sermon describes, the parables illustrate, and the church is to embody until the King returns.
  • The CHURCH (ekklēsia) — Matthew is the only Gospel to use the word (16:18; 18:17), and the one most concerned with the community's life: discipline, forgiveness, leadership, and the presence of Christ 'where two or three are gathered'.
  • RIGHTEOUSNESS exceeding the Pharisees' — a deeper obedience of the heart, not less law but more ('unless your righteousness exceeds…', 5:20), set against a religion of display; the sharpest conflict in the Gospels with the Pharisees runs here.
  • GOD WITH US — the frame of the whole book: it opens on 'Emmanuel, God with us' (1:23), promises 'where two or three are gathered, there am I' (18:20), and closes on 'I am with you always, to the end of the age' (28:20).

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. Matthew's Greek is clean and orderly, more polished than Mark's, and deeply shaped by the Greek Old Testament, which it quotes constantly (usually in the Septuagint form, as with parthenos at 1:23). ⚠ Chapter 1 carries two textual points worth naming: the genealogy's 'Asaph' and 'Amos' (1:7-8, 10), where the earliest manuscripts read the psalmist's and prophet's names rather than the kings 'Asa' and 'Amon', printed here as the critical text has them; and the ending of 1:25, where a large part of the tradition expands 'a son' to 'her firstborn son' (borrowed from Luke 2:7). The seven-version shelf under every chapter compares the NIV, KJV, Douay-Rheims, The Living Bible, the 1599 Geneva, ASV, and NWT.

Looking forward

Matthew's Christ is the MESSIAH of Israel and the son of David, whose royal line the Gospel's first page is built to establish — and, from that same page, more than a king: 'Emmanuel, God with us'. He is the new Moses who gives the law from a mountain, the fulfilment toward whom the whole Old Testament leans, the teacher whose authority astonishes ('not as their scribes'), and the risen Lord to whom 'all authority in heaven and on earth' is given. ⚠ And he is present: the Gospel that opens on 'God with us' and closes on 'I am with you always' frames everything between as the abiding presence of God in Jesus — the claim the name Emmanuel makes, carried from the first chapter to the last verse.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP and DATE, which stand together — the apostle Matthew, or a later Jewish-Christian author or school in his name and after his tradition; and with them the SYNOPTIC PROBLEM, the question of how Matthew, Mark and Luke are literarily related (the majority view has Matthew using Mark and a sayings-source, 'Q'). The relationship of the two INFANCY narratives, Matthew's and Luke's, which are independent and hard to harmonise in detail. And the Gospel's sharp language toward 'the Pharisees' and 'the Jews' (chapter 23; 27:25), whose history of misuse the library names plainly while setting the words in their first-century intra-Jewish setting. The library reports the positions and casts no vote.
