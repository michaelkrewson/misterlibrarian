Thirty verses framed as a boundary against two named cultures — 'not as they do in Egypt, not as they do in Canaan.' Twelve forbidden degrees of kinship each get their own paragraph break, one prohibition per verse; then, without pause, the list turns to marrying two sisters as rivals, sex during menstrual impurity, adultery, child sacrifice to Molech, male-male intercourse, and bestiality — all named 'abominations' together. It closes on the land itself as an agent that 'vomited out' its own inhabitants for these very practices, and a direct warning that Israel can suffer the identical fate.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, like the rest of Leviticus. This chapter completes the run from Leviticus 1 straight through 19 with no gap — the last piece needed before Leviticus 20 restates the same catalog with prescribed penalties. Still inside the same Sinai window as the rest of this block (Exodus 40:17 / Numbers 1:1, already on these pages).

Leviticus 18 meaning — the law that names its own alternative: Egypt and Canaan

This chapter opens not with an abstract ethic but a boundary: ‘you must not do as they do in the land of Egypt… and you must not do as they do in the land of Canaan’ (v3), naming Israel's own recent past and imminent future as the two cultures this law sets it apart from. Everything that follows is framed as a definition of that boundary, not a general treatise.

V5’s ‘the one who does them will live by them’ becomes, centuries later, one of the single most argued-over sentences in the whole Bible — quoted directly by Paul at both Romans 10:5 and Galatians 3:12 (neither yet on these pages) as the very definition of law-righteousness he sets against faith, and echoed at Ezekiel 20:11 and Nehemiah 9:29 (neither yet on these pages). An administrative-sounding clause, buried in a chapter's own introduction, becomes a load-bearing verse for the entire law/faith argument of the New Testament.

Leviticus 18's incest laws — why the Masoretic text gives each one its own paragraph

⚠ A structural fact worth naming plainly: vv5–16 each carry their OWN one-verse setumah break — twelve consecutive paragraphs, one per prohibition, found nowhere else in this book. The scribal tradition itself refuses to let these blur into a single list; each forbidden relationship stands alone on the page, exactly as each genealogical name in earlier chapters stood alone rather than running together. ‘Uncover the nakedness of’ (gilah ervah) is the chapter's own fixed formula, kept essentially literal here as across the whole shelf — a well-understood idiom needing no softening.

Ervah returns here to its literal home. This project's own Genesis 42 note (already on these pages) already defined ervah as ‘the word for shameful exposure, used of a body,’ met there only in its figurative extension — ‘the nakedness of the LAND.’ This chapter is where the word's literal, primary sense actually lives. And Genesis 9's own note on Ham (already on these pages) already flagged that ‘saw his father's nakedness’ carries, for some readers, ‘the darker idiom that phrase carries in Leviticus 20’ — worth being precise here: Ham's verb was ra'ah, ‘to see’; this chapter's own operative verb is gilah, ‘to uncover.’ Related, not identical — which is exactly why ancient and modern readers have split on how far Ham's own act should be read.

V18's ban on marrying two sisters as rivals is a law Jacob himself (already on these pages) never kept, since it did not yet exist in his lifetime — the same honest gap this project has already flagged for Amram and Jochebed's marriage (Exodus 6, already on these pages). This exact catalog, restated with prescribed penalties rather than bare prohibition, recurs at Leviticus 20 (already on these pages).

Niddah again — the rule Leviticus 15 already gave, folded into this catalog

V19 restates, in one clause, what Leviticus 15:24 (already on these pages) already legislated at length: intercourse during niddah is off-limits. Nothing new is added here except placement — this chapter is a catalog of relationships and unions, and the point of including it is that this restriction belongs on the same list as everything around it, not that it needs re-explaining.

Molech meaning — child sacrifice, and the ordinary sin named right beside it

Adultery (v20) gets one plain clause before the chapter turns, without any change in register, to child sacrifice (v21) — the two sit side by side in the text with no signal that one is a lesser wrong than the other. ⚠ This verse alone never says ‘through the fire’ — that specific detail comes from parallel texts, not this one: Jeremiah 19 (already on these pages) describes Topheth in the Valley of Hinnom in exactly those terms, and 2 Kings 23:10 and Jeremiah 32:35 (neither yet on these pages) will state it explicitly. This project's own Molech encyclopedia entry (already on these pages) covers the god's identity and the Milcom/Malcam naming tangle in full.

Leviticus 18:22 — what the Hebrew says, and the debate this project doesn't adjudicate

The base translation here is not seriously disputed across the shelf: KJV, ASV, NIV, and NWT all render the clause essentially as this translation does — ‘lie with a male as with a woman.’ Unlike Azazel or the sons-of-God passages, the disagreement here is not over what the Hebrew says but over how the verse should be read and applied today, a question this project has already stated its policy on: this project's own arsenokoitēs entry (already on these pages) — the rare Greek compound Paul very probably minted from this exact clause's own Greek translation — states plainly that ‘the library reports the philology and the spread of renderings and does not adjudicate the modern application, which turns on questions well beyond the lexicon.’ The same policy applies here, at the verse that compound was built from.

To'evah, ‘abomination,’ is not a word reserved uniquely for this one act: this same chapter uses it as its own umbrella term for the entire catalog — incest, adultery, child sacrifice, and the bestiality of the verse immediately following are all called to'evah together at vv26, 27, 29, and 30. Whatever the word signals, this chapter does not single this act out with a harsher vocabulary than it uses for the rest of its own list.

⚠ The whole chapter frames itself (v3) as a boundary against named neighboring cultures' ‘doings,’ not a general treatise on love, desire, or orientation — categories genuinely foreign to the text's own ancient conceptual world. Naming that anachronism is not an argument for any modern position; it is simply what honesty about the text requires.

The land vomited out its inhabitants — a warning aimed at Israel itself

The chapter closes on a violent, physical image — the land itself as an agent that ‘vomited out’ (vataki) its own inhabitants (v25, v28) — and then, deliberately, refuses to let Israel stand outside the warning: ‘so that the land does not vomit YOU out too’ (v28), the identical verb turned back on the very people receiving this law. Native-born and foreigner alike (v26) face the same standard; the text names no exemption by identity, only by conduct. It closes, as so much of this book does, on karet, ‘cut off from among their people’ — and on the same three words that opened it: I am Jehovah.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Leviticus 18 both run 1–30 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Structurally unusual even for this book: vv5–16 each carry their own one-verse setumah break (twelve in a row), then vv17–30 run as one long unbroken unit closed by a single open break at v30. ‘Nakedness’ (ervah) gets its own dictionary entry here, its literal home after Genesis 42's figurative borrowing. ‘Depravity’ (zimah, v17) and ‘perversion’ (tevel, v23) are each entered separately from the chapter's own umbrella term to'evah, ‘abomination’ — three distinct Hebrew words this translation keeps distinct rather than flattening into one English word. V22 is translated according to the shelf's own broad consensus on the Hebrew; the note there states this project's policy on the verse's modern application directly, matching the policy already fixed at this project's arsenokoitēs entry.

Echoes paid. Genesis 42's figurative ‘nakedness of the land’ (already on these pages), now met in its literal home; Genesis 9's own flag that Ham's act echoes ‘the darker idiom’ of this book's incest laws (already on these pages), now precisely distinguished by verb; Jacob's own marriage to two sisters (Genesis 29, already on these pages), a law not yet given in his lifetime; Leviticus 15's niddah rule (already on these pages), restated here in one clause; and this project's own arsenokoitēs dictionary entry (already on these pages), whose stated policy on v22's modern application this chapter's own note now matches.

Echoes planted. Romans 10:5 and Galatians 3:12 (neither yet on these pages), where Paul quotes v5 directly as the definition of law-righteousness; and 2 Kings 23:10 / Jeremiah 32:35 (neither yet on these pages), which will state outright the ‘through the fire’ detail this chapter's own Molech verse leaves unsaid. Leviticus 20 (already on these pages) restates this whole catalog with prescribed penalties, as promised above.
