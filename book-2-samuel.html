📖 2 Samuel

שְׁמוּאֵל ב Shemuel Bet — '2 Samuel.' In the Hebrew Bible there is no such book: Samuel is ONE scroll, named for the prophet whose birth opens it, and it is still counted as one in the Jewish canon. The division into 1 and 2 Samuel comes from the Greek Septuagint, whose translators needed more scroll-length than Hebrew did — so the seam is a matter of stationery, not of subject. ⚠️ It happens to fall well: at the death of Saul, with a runner coming from a lost battle. But the book does not begin here. It continues.
Βασιλειῶν Βʹ (Basileion B) — '2 Kingdoms' — The Septuagint groups Samuel and Kings as four 'books of the Kingdoms,' so 2 Samuel is '2 Kingdoms' and what we call 1 Kings is '3 Kingdoms.' The Greek titles name the books by their institution; the Hebrew names them by their prophet.

The reign of David — his rise to a united throne, the promise of an everlasting dynasty, and then the adultery and murder that break his own house. A national epic that records its hero's worst crime in detail, names the victim, and lets a prophet say to the king's face: you are the man.

Chapter by chapter

2 Samuel 1Saul is dead on Gilboa, and a man runs into Ziklag with the crown in his hand and a story that does not match the one 1 Samuel just told. David — who spent years as Saul's hunted rival — tears his clothes, fasts, executes the messenger for laying a hand on 'Jehovah's anointed', and then chants the Song of the Bow: 'How the mighty have fallen.' A lament that says nothing of the spear thrown at him, the years of pursuit, or the priests of Nob.2 Samuel 2Hebron and Gibeon. David inquires of Jehovah and is told to go up to Hebron, where the men of Judah anoint him king over the house of Judah; he blesses the men of Jabesh-gilead for burying Saul, while Abner makes Saul's son Ish-bosheth king over Israel at Mahanaim. At the pool of Gibeon twelve men a side fall together and Abner is struck down; Asahel, light of foot as a gazelle, will not turn aside from following Abner and is killed with the back of the spear; Abner's plea 'Shall the sword devour for ever?' and Joab's horn end the chase, and three hundred and sixty of Abner's men lie dead against nineteen of David's and Asahel.2 Samuel 3Abner and Joab. The war between the house of Saul and the house of David is long, David grows stronger and six sons are born to him in Hebron. Abner, charged by Ish-bosheth over Saul's concubine Rizpah, offers David all Israel; David asks for Michal and she is taken from Paltiel weeping, and Abner speaks to the elders and to Benjamin, is feasted at Hebron and sent away in peace. Joab brings him back from the cistern of Sirah and kills him in the gate for the blood of Asahel; David disowns the blood, curses the house of Joab, follows the bier, laments, fasts until sundown and says the sons of Zeruiah are too hard for him.2 Samuel 4Ish-bosheth murdered. His hands grow slack at Abner's death, and the two chiefs of his raiding bands, Rechab and Baanah of Beeroth, come at noon as though for wheat, strike him in the belly on his bed and carry his head to David at Hebron, expecting a reward. David answers that he killed the bringer of good news at Ziklag and will require the blood of a righteous man from them; they are put to death, their hands and feet cut off and their bodies hanged by the pool, and the head is buried in Abner's grave. Jonathan's lame son Mephibosheth is introduced, dropped by his nurse at five.2 Samuel 5All Israel comes to David. The tribes and then the elders come to Hebron ('we are your bone and your flesh'), he makes a covenant and is anointed king over Israel, and his thirty years, forty-year reign and seven years and six months at Hebron are counted. He takes the Jebusite stronghold of Zion with its water-channel and calls it the city of David; Hiram of Tyre sends cedar and craftsmen; more sons are born in Jerusalem. The Philistines come up twice into the valley of Rephaim: at Baal-perazim Jehovah breaks through like a breach of waters and they leave their idols, and the second time David is told not to go up but to circle behind them and move when he hears marching in the tops of the bakha trees.2 Samuel 6The ark goes up to Jerusalem. David gathers thirty thousand chosen men and fetches the ark from Baale-judah (Kiriath-jearim) on a new cart; at the threshing floor of Nacon the oxen let it slip, Uzzah puts out his hand to it and God strikes him down, and David, angry and afraid, names the place Perez-uzzah and turns the ark aside to Obed-edom the Gittite. After three months of blessing on that house he brings it up with sacrifice, whirling in a linen ephod, with the shout and the ram's horn; Michal watches from the window and despises him in her heart, mocks him as one of the empty men, and is answered 'before Jehovah' and left without a child to the day of her death.2 Samuel 7A house for the ark, and a house for David. Settled in his house of cedar, David tells Nathan that the ark dwells within the curtain, and Nathan says to do all that is in his heart; that night Jehovah answers that he has walked about in a tent and a tabernacle and never asked for a house, that he took David from the pasture to be leader, and that Jehovah himself will make David a house: a seed from his own body will build a house for the Name, will be a son to Jehovah as to a father, and his house, kingdom and throne will be made sure forever. David goes in, sits before Jehovah and prays.2 Samuel 8David's wars and his officers. He subdues the Philistines and takes Metheg-ha-ammah; strikes Moab and measures them with the line, two lines to death and one full line to life, and the rest become bearers of tribute; strikes Hadadezer of Zobah as he goes to turn back his hand at the Euphrates, hamstrings the chariot horses, and sets garrisons in Aram of Damascus; takes shields of gold and bronze; receives Toi of Hamath's son Joram with gifts, all of which he dedicates to Jehovah; makes a name in the Valley of Salt (Aram in the Hebrew, Edom in other witnesses), and sets garrisons in all Edom. The chapter ends with David doing justice and righteousness and a list of his officers, among them Zadok, Ahimelech, Benaiah over the Cherethites and Pelethites, and sons who were priests.2 Samuel 9Kindness for Jonathan's sake. David asks whether anyone is left of the house of Saul to whom he may do kindness, first for Jonathan's sake and then as "the kindness of God"; Ziba, a servant of that house, names Jonathan's son, lame in his feet, in the house of Machir son of Ammiel at Lo-debar. David has Mephibosheth fetched, tells him not to be afraid, gives back all the field of Saul and has him eat at the king's table continually; the ground is to be worked by Ziba, his fifteen sons and twenty servants. Mephibosheth, who calls himself a dead dog, has a young son named Mica and lives in Jerusalem, lame in both his feet.2 Samuel 10Hanun's insult and the war with Ammon and Aram. David sends servants to comfort Hanun, new king of the sons of Ammon, and does kindness with him as his father Nahash did with David; Ammon's princes call the envoys spies, and Hanun shaves half their beards and cuts their garments to the buttocks. David has them stay at Jericho until their beards grow. Ammon, having become a stench to David, hires Aram of Beth-rehob and Zobah, the king of Maacah and Ish-tob; Joab divides the army with his brother Abishai, with the pact that each will save the other, and says Be strong, and let us be strong. Ammon flees into its city, Hadadezer calls Aram from beyond the River, and David crosses the Jordan to Helam and strikes Aram and its commander Shobach; Hadadezer's servants make peace with Israel and Aram fears to save Ammon any more.2 Samuel 11David and Bathsheba, and the killing of Uriah. At the return of the year David sends Joab and all Israel against Ammon and stays in Jerusalem; from his roof he sees a woman washing, learns she is Bathsheba, daughter of Eliam and wife of Uriah the Hittite, sends and takes her, and she sends word that she is pregnant. David recalls Uriah and tries to send him home, but Uriah will not go down to his house while the ark and the army dwell in booths, even when David makes him drunk; David writes a letter to Joab and sends it by Uriah's own hand, setting him in front of the strong battle. Uriah dies, Joab's messenger brings the news with the story of Abimelech at Thebez, David says Do not let this thing be evil in your eyes, and after the mourning he gathers Bathsheba into his house. The thing that David had done was evil in the eyes of Jehovah.2 Samuel 12Nathan's parable and the child. Jehovah sends Nathan to David with the story of a rich man who takes a poor man's one ewe lamb; David says the man is a son of death and must pay fourfold, and Nathan says You are the man. Jehovah's word recalls the anointing and the house given, names the killing of Uriah, and promises that the sword shall never depart from David's house; David says I have sinned against Jehovah, and Nathan says Jehovah has taken away his sin but the child shall die. The child is struck and dies on the seventh day while David fasts on the ground; David rises, washes and eats, and says I am going to him. David comforts Bathsheba and Solomon is born, and Jehovah sends by Nathan the name Jedidiah. Joab takes the royal city of Rabbah and calls David to take it lest his own name be called over it; David takes the crown of their king, sets the people to the saw and the brickmold, and returns to Jerusalem.2 Samuel 13Amnon and Tamar; Absalom kills Amnon. Amnon son of David loves Tamar, Absalom’s beautiful sister, and on Jonadab’s advice feigns illness and asks that she make two heart-cakes in his sight; David sends her. Amnon has everyone sent out, seizes her, and violates her despite her plea that it is not done in Israel and that he would be one of the fools; then he hates her with a hatred greater than his love, has her put out as “this one” and the door bolted. Tamar puts ashes on her head, tears her long ornamented tunic and goes away crying out; Absalom says to be silent, and she stays desolate in his house. David is very angry, and Absalom says nothing to Amnon for two full years. At the shearing at Baal-hazor he presses David to send Amnon and the king’s sons, orders his servants to strike Amnon down when his heart is merry with wine, and the other sons flee on their mules. The report comes to David that all are dead; he tears his garments and lies on the ground, until Jonadab says that only Amnon is dead. Absalom flees to Talmai, king of Geshur, and David mourns his son and then longs to go out to Absalom.2 Samuel 14Joab and the wise woman of Tekoa; Absalom brought back. Joab sees that the king’s heart is toward Absalom and sends to Tekoa for a wise woman, dressing her as a mourner and putting the words in her mouth. She falls before David with a story of two sons, one of whom killed the other in a field, and of a family that demands the survivor; David swears that not one hair of his head will fall. She then says that in not bringing back his own banished one the king is as one guilty, and that God devises means that no banished one be banished from him; asked whether Joab’s hand is in it, she says that it is. David tells Joab to bring back the young man Absalom, and Joab goes to Geshur and brings him to Jerusalem, where he may not see the king’s face. Absalom is without blemish from sole to crown, his hair weighs two hundred shekels, and he has three sons and a daughter, Tamar. After two full years he sends twice for Joab, burns his barley to bring him, and asks to see the king’s face or be put to death; Absalom bows, and the king kisses him.

About the book

Where it sits
In the Hebrew Bible, the second half of 'Samuel' among the Former Prophets; in the Christian Old Testament, 2 Samuel, following 1 Samuel and preceding 1-2 Kings. 1 Chronicles retells much of the same reign — and pointedly omits the Bathsheba affair and Absalom's revolt, which makes the two accounts a useful lesson in what a given writer is FOR.
Genre
HISTORICAL NARRATIVE — one of the 'Former Prophets' in the Hebrew canon, and part of what scholars call the Deuteronomistic History. Chapters 9-20 (with 1 Kings 1-2) are often set apart as the COURT HISTORY or Succession Narrative, and are widely regarded as some of the finest prose in the Hebrew Bible — close-focus, psychologically acute, and unsparing. The book also carries very old POETRY: the lament here in chapter 1, David's psalm in chapter 22 (a twin of Psalm 18), and the 'last words' of chapter 23.
Author
Anonymous. Jewish tradition (Talmud, Bava Batra 14b) assigns the later material to the prophets NATHAN and GAD, following 1 Chronicles 29:29, which names 'the records of Samuel the seer, of Nathan the prophet, and of Gad the seer' — Samuel himself being dead since 1 Samuel 25. The narrative's intimacy with court affairs has long suggested a writer close to the palace.
Date written
The events span roughly 1010-970 BC — from Saul's death to David's old age. The sources are demonstrably old (the poetry especially, and chapter 1 cites the lost Book of Jashar outright); the final composition is debated and commonly placed within the monarchy, with later editing.
Place
Judah and the central hill country, then JERUSALEM — a Jebusite stronghold David captures and turns into a capital that had belonged to no tribe, which is a shrewd act of unification as much as a military one. The horizon widens through the book to the Philistine coast, Moab, Ammon, Edom and Aram, as a tribal confederation becomes a small empire.
Audience
Israel living under the dynasty this book explains — a people asking why THIS family, and on what terms. The answer given is a covenant (ch. 7) that does not depend on the king's goodness, which is just as well, since the same book then documents at length how bad he could be.

How it's laid out

1-4The end of the house of Saul — David mourns Saul and Jonathan, is made king over Judah at Hebron, and a long civil war grinds on until Abner and Ish-bosheth are murdered (David executing the murderers each time).
5-10The ascent — king over all Israel, Jerusalem taken and made the capital, the ark brought up, the covenant of chapter 7 promising David's throne forever, victories on every border, and kindness shown to Jonathan's crippled son.
11-12The hinge — Bathsheba, Uriah killed by letter, and Nathan's parable: 'YOU are the man.'
13-20The consequences working through the family — Amnon's rape of Tamar, Absalom's revenge and revolt, David fleeing Jerusalem barefoot, Absalom's death and David's cry, and Sheba's rebellion.
21-24An appendix, arranged as a ring — famine and warriors, two great poems at the centre, and the census and plague that ends on the threshing floor which becomes the temple site.

What it's about

  • COVENANT AND DYNASTY — chapter 7 is the theological centre of the book and one of the hinges of the whole Bible: David wants to build God a house, and is told instead that God will build HIM one. The promise of an everlasting throne becomes the root of Israel's messianic hope.
  • THE HONESTY OF THE RECORD — the book's most remarkable feature is what it refuses to hide. Ancient royal literature exists to flatter; this one narrates its king's adultery, his arrangement of a loyal soldier's death, and his catastrophic failures as a father, and it lets the prophet's verdict stand.
  • CONSEQUENCE — Nathan says the sword will not depart from David's house, and the rest of the book is that sentence being served, mostly by David's own children. Forgiveness is granted (12:13) and consequences run their course anyway; the book holds both without resolving the tension.
  • RESTRAINT AND THE ANOINTED — David will not take the throne by force. He refuses to kill Saul, mourns him, executes the men who murder Abner and Ish-bosheth, and here executes a man for merely CLAIMING to have finished the king. The crown must be given, not seized.
  • GRIEF — the book is full of laments, and they are among its finest writing: over Saul and Jonathan here, over Abner, and the terrible repeated cry over Absalom, 'my son, my son.'

The source text

Translated from the pointed Hebrew Masoretic Text (as printed by Mechon-Mamre). ⚠️ Samuel has the most difficult text of any Old Testament book: the Masoretic manuscripts carry a number of damaged or obscure passages, and the Greek Septuagint often differs substantially — sometimes supported by the Samuel scrolls from Qumran, which show that variant Hebrew forms genuinely circulated. This translation follows the Masoretic Text and NOTES significant variants rather than quietly adopting them; where a word is simply uncertain, the note says so.

Looking forward

The covenant of chapter 7 — 'your throne shall be established forever' — is quoted and built on throughout Scripture (Psalm 89; Isaiah 9:7; Luke 1:32-33), and is why the Gospels open by tracing Jesus to David and why 'son of David' functions as a messianic title. ⚠️ The link runs through the word this chapter turns on: MASHIACH, 'anointed,' which becomes Messiah and, in Greek translation, Christ. Here it still means a man with oil on his head — and the book is careful to show that man sinning grievously, which is part of why the hope attached to the promise eventually outgrows him.

Where the debates are

SOURCES AND COMPOSITION — how the History of David's Rise and the Court History were joined, and the book's relation to the Deuteronomistic History. HISTORICITY — how much of the united monarchy's scale is recoverable; the ninth-century TEL DAN inscription's reference to the 'house of David' is widely taken as extra-biblical attestation of the dynasty, while the extent of David's empire is argued over. TEXT — the Masoretic/Septuagint/Qumran differences noted above. And the perennial literary question of how sympathetically the book means us to read David, whose portrait is admiring and damning at once.
