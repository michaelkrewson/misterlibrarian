🏺 Encyclopedia

Baal

The storm god of the Canaanites and Phoenicians, and the rival the Hebrew prophets name more often than any other. Ba’al is an ordinary Hebrew noun before it is a name — ‘owner, master, husband’ — which the prophets exploit: Hosea has God say that Israel will call him ‘my husband’ (ishi) and ‘no longer call me my Baal’ (2:16). As a god he is HADAD, lord of rain, thunder and the fertility of the fields, titled ‘rider of the clouds’; his father is El, his sister-consort Anat, and his enemies are Yam (Sea) and Mot (Death). ⚠ Psalm 68:4 gives ‘rider of the clouds’ to Jehovah instead — the Bible taking the storm god’s own epithet and reassigning it, and the same idiom stands behind ‘one like a son of man coming with the clouds’ (Daniel 7:13).

The archaeology. Almost everything we know about Baal worship from the WORSHIPPERS’ own side comes from one find: in 1928 a farmer’s plough struck a tomb at Minet el-Beida on the Syrian coast, and excavation from 1929 under CLAUDE SCHAEFFER uncovered the city of UGARIT (Ras Shamra) and its archive — clay tablets in a previously unknown alphabetic cuneiform, written c. 1400–1200 BC, including the long poems now called the BAAL CYCLE. Before Ugarit, Baal was known mainly through the polemic of his opponents. ⚠ But the gap must be stated: Ugarit is Late Bronze Age and some 400 km north of Jerusalem, while Jeremiah is Iron Age Judah five centuries later — the cult certainly changed in between, and the tablets describe its Syrian form, not Judah’s. Objects: bronze ‘smiting god’ figurines — a striding male, arm raised to strike — turn up across the Levant, and a celebrated limestone stele from Ugarit (now in the Louvre) shows the god with mace lifted and a spear whose head runs down like a lightning-bolt. ⚠ Identifying any individual figurine as Baal SPECIFICALLY is almost always an inference from iconography, not from an inscription on the object.

What the worship was. High places (bamot), standing stones, altars and incense — Jeremiah’s charge is ‘burning incense to Baal’ (7:9) — and, as the Bible states it, child sacrifice by fire at Topheth in the Valley of Ben-Hinnom (19:5; 32:35 locates ‘the high places of Baal’ there). Elijah’s contest on Carmel is built as a storm-god polemic: the tests are FIRE and then RAIN, Baal’s own portfolio, and he supplies neither. Two finds sit close to Jeremiah’s own doorstep. Thousands of JUDEAN PILLAR FIGURINES — a moulded female head on a pillar body — come from ordinary houses in 8th–7th century Judah, Jerusalem included, which is household religion in exactly Jeremiah’s century; ⚠ what they were FOR (Asherah? a fertility charm? a votive?) is genuinely disputed. And the inscriptions from KUNTILLET AJRUD (c. 800 BC, in the Sinai) read ‘Jehovah of Samaria and his asherah’ — the epigraphic face of the hedging the prophets attack; ⚠ whether ‘his asherah’ means the goddess or a wooden cult object is unresolved. The Jerusalem Topheth itself has never been securely identified in the ground. The Phoenician and Punic tophets — Carthage above all, with Motya, Sulcis and Tharros — do contain thousands of urns holding cremated infant and animal bone, the strongest physical evidence for the practice anywhere in the Phoenician world; ⚠ and their reading is fiercely contested, some specialists taking them for cemeteries of infants who died naturally, with the dental and skeletal age studies argued both ways. This library does not cast a vote.

⚠ Note for Jeremiah 18. Baal is NOT named in the potter’s-house chapter. It says only that the people ‘burn incense to worthlessness’ (18:15), the prophets’ standard shorthand for an idol. The god is named on either side of it — 2:8, 19:5, 23:13, 32:29-35 — so the identification is fair, but it is the surrounding chapters that supply the name, not this one.
