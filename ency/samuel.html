🏺 Encyclopedia

Samuel

'God has heard' (or 'name of God') — the child asked of Jehovah by his barren mother HANNAH and given back to serve at Shiloh (ch 1). He becomes the LAST of the judges and the first of the great prophets, the hinge-figure of Israel's history: he leads a repentant Israel against the Philistines, and then, when the people demand a king 'like all the nations,' he warns them, anoints SAUL, and later anoints DAVID in Saul's place. A lifelong Nazirite, called by God as a boy (‘Speak, for your servant is listening’, 1 Samuel 3:10, now on these pages), he is the prophet under whose word the whole monarchy begins. He dies at 1 Samuel 25, before the book that bears his name is done. ⭐ 1 Samuel 2 (now on these pages) keeps returning to him between the reports on Eli’s sons: the boy “girded with a linen ephod”, the little robe his mother brings each year, and the boy who “went on growing, and was in favour both with Jehovah and with men” (2:18–26), a line Luke uses of Jesus (Luke 2:52). ⭐ After twenty years he gathers Israel at Mizpah, tells them to put away the foreign gods, prays while the Philistines attack, and sets up the stone Eben-ezer; then he “judged Israel all the days of his life”, going round Bethel, Gilgal and Mizpah and returning to his house at Ramah (1 Samuel 7:3–17, now on these pages). ⭐ In old age he makes his sons Joel and Abijah judges at Beersheba; they take bribes, and the elders come to Ramah asking for “a king to judge us, like all the nations”. Jehovah tells him “it is not you they have rejected, but me”, and he warns them of “the due of the king” (1 Samuel 8, now on these pages). ⭐ As “the seer” of an unnamed town in the land of Zuph he receives Saul, seats him at the head of the table and gives him the kept portion, having been told the day before, “you shall anoint him as leader over my people Israel” (1 Samuel 9:15–27, now on these pages). He anoints Saul in private, then gathers the people at Mizpah, tells them “you today have rejected your God”, presents Saul to them, and writes “the due of the kingship” in a book laid up before Jehovah (1 Samuel 10, now on these pages). Saul summons Israel to come out “after Saul and after Samuel”, and after the victory Samuel calls the people to Gilgal to “renew the kingship there” (1 Samuel 11:7, 14, now on these pages); the Greek has Samuel anoint Saul there. ⭐ In his farewell he asks the people to testify against him, “Whose ox have I taken? Or whose donkey have I taken?”, retells the story from Egypt to Nahash, calls down thunder and rain at the wheat harvest, and promises not to cease praying for them (1 Samuel 12, now on these pages). He comes to Gilgal as Saul finishes the burnt offering, asks “What have you done?”, and tells him “You have acted foolishly” and that his kingdom will not stand (1 Samuel 13:11–14, now on these pages). ⭐ 1 Samuel 15 (now on these pages): he sends Saul against Amalek, cries to Jehovah all night when told “I regret that I made Saul king”, answers Saul “to listen is better than sacrifice”, hews Agag in pieces at Gilgal, and goes home to Ramah, mourning for Saul and never seeing him again until the day of his death. ⭐ 1 Samuel 16 (now on these pages): told “How long will you mourn for Saul?”, he goes to Bethlehem afraid that Saul will kill him, takes Eliab for the chosen one and is told “Jehovah sees into the heart”, and anoints David from the horn of oil before going home to Ramah. ⭐ 1 Samuel 19:18–24 (now on these pages): David flees to him at Ramah and they stay at Naioth, where Samuel stands set over a company of prophets; Saul’s messengers, and then Saul, prophesy there, and Saul lies naked before him all day and all night. ⭐ 1 Samuel 25:1 (now on these pages): ‘And Samuel died; and all Israel gathered together and lamented him, and they buried him in his house at Ramah.’ The book tells it again at the opening of the night at En-dor (28:3, now on these pages), where Saul asks a woman who is mistress of a ghost to bring him up, and the woman sees ‘an old man coming up, wrapped in a robe’. What came up has been read as Samuel himself, as a spirit in his likeness, and as the woman’s craft; the narrator calls him Samuel throughout. Later in the same chapter Abigail repeats his sentence to Saul, that Jehovah would command another man ‘to be leader’, to David himself (25:30).
