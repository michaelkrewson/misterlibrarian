“Go in peace”: David flees from Naioth to Jonathan and asks “What have I done?” They agree a test at the new moon and a signal of arrows by the stone Ezel, and Jonathan asks David for the kindness of Jehovah for his house forever. At the new-moon meal Saul notices David’s empty seat two days running, curses Jonathan, calls David “a son of death”, and hurls the spear at his own son. Next morning Jonathan shoots the arrow beyond the boy, and the two friends kiss, weep and part.
Undated: in Saul’s reign, straight after the flight to Naioth of chapter 19. The new-moon meal runs two days (20:5, 27), and David waits until the evening of the third (20:5, 19); the parting is “in the morning” after the second day (20:35).

Y David huyó de Nayot, en Ramá, y vino y dijo delante de Jonatán: «¿Qué he hecho? ¿Cuál es mi iniquidad, y cuál es mi pecado delante de tu padre, que anda buscando mi vida?».
2

And he said to him: ‘Far be it! You shall not die. Look — my father does nothing, great or small, without uncovering my ear; and why would my father hide this thing from me? It is not so.’note
Y él le dijo: «¡Lejos esté! No morirás. Mira, mi padre no hace cosa alguna, grande ni pequeña, sin descubrir mi oído; ¿y por qué me ocultaría mi padre este asunto? No es así».
3

And David swore again, and said: ‘Your father knows well that I have found favour in your eyes; and he has said, “Let not Jonathan know this, lest he be grieved.” But truly, as Jehovah lives and as your soul lives, there is but a step between me and death.’note
Y David volvió a jurar, y dijo: «Tu padre sabe bien que he hallado favor a tus ojos; y ha dicho: “Que no lo sepa Jonatán, no sea que se entristezca”. Pero, de cierto, vive Jehová y vive tu alma, que solo hay un paso entre mí y la muerte».
4

Y Jonatán dijo a David: «Lo que diga tu alma, eso haré por ti».

6

If your father misses me at all, then say, “David earnestly asked leave of me to run to Bethlehem, his city; for the yearly sacrifice is there for all the family.”note
Si tu padre me echa de menos, entonces dile: “David me pidió con insistencia permiso para ir corriendo a Belén, su ciudad; porque allí está el sacrificio anual de toda la familia”.
7

If he says thus, “Good,” it is peace for your servant; but if his anger burns, know that the evil is decided by him.note
Si él dice así: “Bien”, hay paz para tu siervo; pero si se le enciende la ira, sabe que el mal está decidido por él.
8
וְעָשִׂיתָ חֶסֶד, עַל-עַבְדֶּךָ, כִּי בִּבְרִית יְהוָה, הֵבֵאתָ אֶת-עַבְדְּךָ עִמָּךְ; וְאִם-יֶשׁ-בִּי עָוֺן הֲמִיתֵנִי אַתָּה, וְעַד-אָבִיךָ לָמָּה-זֶּה תְבִיאֵנִי.  {פ}
And do kindness with your servant, for into a covenant of Jehovah you have brought your servant with you. But if there is iniquity in me, put me to death yourself; for why should you bring me to your father?’note
Haz, pues, bondad con tu siervo, porque en un pacto de Jehová has hecho entrar contigo a tu siervo. Pero si hay iniquidad en mí, dame muerte tú mismo; pues ¿por qué habrías de llevarme a tu padre?».
9
וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוֹנָתָן, חָלִילָה לָּךְ:  כִּי אִם-יָדֹעַ אֵדַע, כִּי-כָלְתָה הָרָעָה מֵעִם אָבִי לָבוֹא עָלֶיךָ--וְלֹא אֹתָהּ, אַגִּיד לָךְ.  {ס}
And Jonathan said: ‘Far be it from you! For if I do know that the evil is decided by my father to come upon you, would I not tell it to you?’note
Y Jonatán dijo: «¡Lejos esté eso de ti! Porque si yo llego a saber que el mal está decidido por mi padre para venir sobre ti, ¿no te lo diría yo?».
10

And David said to Jonathan: ‘Who will tell me? Or what if your father answers you harshly?’note
Y David dijo a Jonatán: «¿Quién me lo dirá? ¿O qué, si tu padre te responde con dureza?».

Y Jonatán dijo a David: «Jehová, Dios de Israel… Cuando yo tantee a mi padre como a esta hora mañana, al tercer [día], y mira, haya bien para David, ¿no enviaré entonces a ti y descubriré tu oído?
13

Thus may Jehovah do to Jonathan, and more also, if it pleases my father [to bring] the evil upon you and I do not uncover your ear and let you go, so that you go in peace. And may Jehovah be with you, as he was with my father.note
Así haga Jehová a Jonatán, y aun le añada, si a mi padre le parece bien [traer] el mal sobre ti y yo no descubro tu oído y te dejo ir, para que te vayas en paz. Y que Jehová esté contigo, como estuvo con mi padre.
14
וְלֹא, אִם-עוֹדֶנִּי חָי; וְלֹא-תַעֲשֶׂה עִמָּדִי חֶסֶד יְהוָה, וְלֹא אָמוּת.
And will you not — if I am still alive — will you not do with me the kindness of Jehovah, so that I do not die?note
¿Y no harás tú, si todavía estoy vivo, no harás conmigo la bondad de Jehová, para que yo no muera?
15
וְלֹא-תַכְרִית אֶת-חַסְדְּךָ מֵעִם בֵּיתִי, עַד-עוֹלָם; וְלֹא, בְּהַכְרִת יְהוָה אֶת-אֹיְבֵי דָוִד, אִישׁ, מֵעַל פְּנֵי הָאֲדָמָה.
And you shall not cut off your kindness from my house forever; not even when Jehovah cuts off the enemies of David, each one, from the face of the ground.’note
Y no cortarás tu bondad de mi casa para siempre; ni siquiera cuando Jehová corte a los enemigos de David, a cada uno, de sobre la faz del suelo».
16

Y Jonatán cortó [un pacto] con la casa de David: «Y Jehová lo buscará de la mano de los enemigos de David».
17

Y Jonatán volvió a hacer jurar a David, por el amor que le tenía; porque lo amaba con el amor de su alma.
18
וַיֹּאמֶר-לוֹ יְהוֹנָתָן, מָחָר חֹדֶשׁ; וְנִפְקַדְתָּ, כִּי יִפָּקֵד מוֹשָׁבֶךָ.

Y Jonatán le dijo: «Mañana es la luna nueva; y se te echará de menos, porque se echará de menos tu asiento.
19
וְשִׁלַּשְׁתָּ, תֵּרֵד מְאֹד, וּבָאתָ אֶל-הַמָּקוֹם, אֲשֶׁר-נִסְתַּרְתָּ שָּׁם בְּיוֹם הַמַּעֲשֶׂה; וְיָשַׁבְתָּ, אֵצֶל הָאֶבֶן הָאָזֶל.
And on the third day you shall go down quickly, and come to the place where you hid yourself on the day of the deed, and sit beside the stone Ezel.note
Y al tercer día bajarás pronto, y vendrás al lugar donde te escondiste el día del hecho, y te sentarás junto a la piedra de Ezel.
20
וַאֲנִי, שְׁלֹשֶׁת הַחִצִּים צִדָּה אוֹרֶה, לְשַׁלַּח-לִי, לְמַטָּרָה.
And I will shoot three arrows to its side, as though sending [them] for myself at a target.note
Y yo tiraré tres flechas a su lado, como quien las envía para sí a un blanco.
21
וְהִנֵּה אֶשְׁלַח אֶת-הַנַּעַר, לֵךְ מְצָא אֶת-הַחִצִּים:  אִם-אָמֹר אֹמַר לַנַּעַר הִנֵּה הַחִצִּים מִמְּךָ וָהֵנָּה, קָחֶנּוּ וָבֹאָה כִּי-שָׁלוֹם לְךָ וְאֵין דָּבָר--חַי-יְהוָה.
And look — I will send the boy: “Go, find the arrows.” If I say to the boy, “Look — the arrows are on this side of you; take them,” then come; for it is peace for you, and there is nothing [amiss], as Jehovah lives.note
Y mira, enviaré al muchacho: “Ve, busca las flechas”. Si le digo al muchacho: “Mira, las flechas están de este lado de ti; tómalas”, entonces ven; porque hay paz para ti, y no hay nada [malo], vive Jehová.
22
וְאִם-כֹּה אֹמַר לָעֶלֶם, הִנֵּה הַחִצִּים מִמְּךָ וָהָלְאָה--לֵךְ, כִּי שִׁלַּחֲךָ יְהוָה.
But if I say thus to the young man, “Look — the arrows are beyond you,” go; for Jehovah has let you go.note
Pero si así le digo al joven: “Mira, las flechas están más allá de ti”, vete; porque Jehová te ha dejado ir.
23
וְהַדָּבָר--אֲשֶׁר דִּבַּרְנוּ, אֲנִי וָאָתָּה:  הִנֵּה יְהוָה בֵּינִי וּבֵינְךָ, עַד-עוֹלָם.  {ס}
And the word that we have spoken, I and you — look — Jehovah is between me and you forever.’note
Y en cuanto a la palabra que hemos hablado, yo y tú, mira, Jehová está entre mí y ti para siempre».
24
וַיִּסָּתֵר דָּוִד, בַּשָּׂדֶה; וַיְהִי הַחֹדֶשׁ, וַיֵּשֶׁב הַמֶּלֶךְ על- אֶל- הַלֶּחֶם לֶאֱכוֹל.
And David hid in the field. And the new moon came, and the king sat down at the meal to eat.note
Y David se escondió en el campo. Y llegó la luna nueva, y el rey se sentó a la comida para comer.
25
וַיֵּשֶׁב הַמֶּלֶךְ עַל-מוֹשָׁבוֹ כְּפַעַם בְּפַעַם, אֶל-מוֹשַׁב הַקִּיר, וַיָּקָם יְהוֹנָתָן, וַיֵּשֶׁב אַבְנֵר מִצַּד שָׁאוּל; וַיִּפָּקֵד, מְקוֹם דָּוִד.
And the king sat on his seat as at the other times, on the seat by the wall; and Jonathan rose, and Abner sat at Saul’s side; and David’s place was missed.note
Y el rey se sentó en su asiento como las otras veces, en el asiento junto a la pared; y Jonatán se levantó, y Abner se sentó al lado de Saúl; y se echó de menos el lugar de David.
26
וְלֹא-דִבֶּר שָׁאוּל מְאוּמָה, בַּיּוֹם הַהוּא:  כִּי אָמַר מִקְרֶה הוּא, בִּלְתִּי טָהוֹר הוּא כִּי-לֹא טָהוֹר.  {ס}
And Saul did not say anything that day; for he said: ‘It is a chance. He is not clean — surely he is not clean.’note
Y Saúl no dijo nada aquel día; porque se dijo: «Es una casualidad. No está limpio; seguro que no está limpio».
27
וַיְהִי, מִמָּחֳרַת הַחֹדֶשׁ הַשֵּׁנִי, וַיִּפָּקֵד, מְקוֹם דָּוִד;  {פ} וַיֹּאמֶר שָׁאוּל, אֶל-יְהוֹנָתָן בְּנוֹ, מַדּוּעַ לֹא-בָא בֶן-יִשַׁי גַּם-תְּמוֹל גַּם-הַיּוֹם, אֶל-הַלָּחֶם.
And it came about on the day after the new moon, the second [day], that David’s place was missed. And Saul said to Jonathan his son: ‘Why has the son of Jesse not come to the meal, either yesterday or today?’note
Y aconteció al día siguiente de la luna nueva, el segundo [día], que se echó de menos el lugar de David. Y Saúl dijo a Jonatán su hijo: «¿Por qué no ha venido el hijo de Isaí a la comida, ni ayer ni hoy?».
28
וַיַּעַן יְהוֹנָתָן, אֶת-שָׁאוּל:  נִשְׁאֹל נִשְׁאַל דָּוִד מֵעִמָּדִי, עַד-בֵּית לָחֶם.
And Jonathan answered Saul: ‘David earnestly asked leave of me [to go] to Bethlehem.note

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · “What have I done?” — David’s three questions, an uncovered ear, and a step between him and death

Three questions. “What have I done? What is my iniquity, and what is my sin before your father, that he is seeking my life?”: meh asiti, meh-avoni, u-meh-chatta’ti. In the last chapter Jonathan told his father that David “has not sinned against you” (19:4, already on these pages); now David asks what the charge is, and is given none. Jonathan will put the same question to his father in verse 32, “What has he done?”, and the only answer is a spear. “He is seeking my life”: mevaqqesh et-nafshi; the verb biqqesh, seek, comes back in verse 16, where it is Jehovah who will seek. The ASV has “what is mine iniquity? and what is my sin before thy father, that he seeketh my life?”; the NWT 1984 “for he is seeking for my soul?”; the NIV “What is my crime? How have I wronged your father, that he is trying to kill me?”

Far be it, and an uncovered ear. “Far be it! You shall not die”: chalilah, the word the people used for Jonathan himself when Saul had sworn his death, “Far be it! As Jehovah lives, not one hair of his head shall fall to the ground” (14:45, already on these pages) (chalilah). “My father does nothing, great or small, without uncovering my ear”: ve-lo yigleh et-ozni, to uncover someone’s ear, to tell him what was hidden. It is what Jehovah did for Samuel the day before Saul came to him (9:15, already on these pages), and Jonathan uses it three times in this chapter, here and in his promises of verses 12 and 13. The scribes wrote lu asah, “if he had done”, and read lo ya’aseh, “he does not do”; the sense comes out the same. The ASV has “Far from it; thou shalt not die”; the NWT 1984 “It is unthinkable! You will not die” and “not disclose it to my ear”; the TLB “That’s not true!”

An oath, and a step. “And David swore again, and said”: David takes an oath here; in verse 17 Jonathan makes him swear again, and in verse 42 Jonathan says “we have sworn, the two of us”. The Douay, from the Latin, has “And he swore again to David. And David said”, so that the one swearing is Jonathan. “Lest he be grieved”: pen-ye’atsev, the verb that comes true in verse 34, where Jonathan “was grieved for David”. “There is but a step between me and death”: ki khe-fesa beni u-ven ha-mavet, the noun pesa, a single pace, spelled with the same three letters as pesha, rebellion, but read with sin instead of shin. The ASV has “but truly as Jehovah liveth, and as thy soul liveth, there is but a step between me and death”; the NWT 1984 “there is just about a step between me and death!”

Whatever your soul says. “Whatever your soul says, I will do it for you”: mah-tomar nafshekha ve-e’eseh-lakh, literally “what your soul says, and I will do it for you”. The Jonathan who loved David “as his own soul” in 18:1 (already on these pages) now offers to do whatever David’s soul asks (nefesh). The NWT 1984 has “Whatever your soul may say I shall do for you”; the KJV “Whatsoever thy soul desireth, I will even do it for thee”; the NIV “Whatever you want me to do, I’ll do for you.”

Verses 5–7 · “Tomorrow is the new moon” — the king’s monthly meal, a sacrifice in Bethlehem, and David “asked, asked” leave

The new moon. “Look — tomorrow is the new moon”: hinneh-chodesh machar. The chodesh is the day the new moon is sighted and the month begins, and the Law gives it offerings of its own, “at the beginnings of your months” (Numbers 28:11, already on these pages) (chodesh). This chapter shows it at court: a meal with the king that runs at least two days (verses 24–27), with a fixed place for each man, so that an empty one is noticed. “I ought to sit, to sit with the king to eat”: yashov-eshev, the verb doubled for emphasis. The Greek says the opposite, “and I will surely not sit to eat”, and has no third day, only “until evening”. “Until the evening of the third [day]”: ad ha-erev ha-shelishit, “until the evening, the third”, where “third” is feminine and “evening” is not, so “day” is supplied. The ASV has “and I should not fail to sit with the king at meat”; the NWT 1984 “and I myself ought, without fail, to be sitting with the king to eat”; the Geneva “tomorrow is the first day of the month”; the NIV “tomorrow is the New Moon feast”.

Asked, asked — and the name Saul. “David earnestly asked leave of me”: nish’ol nish’al mimmenni David, the verb sha’al, ask, doubled, in the form used for asking something for oneself. The words nish’ol nish’al stand in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, both in this chapter: David scripts them here, and Jonathan says them to Saul in verse 28. The same root gives the king his name, Sha’ul, “asked for” (sha’al), so the excuse set before the king is made of the king’s own name. The NWT 1984 has “David earnestly asked leave of absence of me”; the Douay “David asked me that he might run to Bethlehem his own city”; the TLB “tell him that I asked permission to go home to Bethlehem for an annual family reunion.”

The yearly sacrifice. “For the yearly sacrifice is there for all the family”: zevach ha-yamim, literally “the sacrifice of the days”. The phrase zevach ha-yamim stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible. The other two are Elkanah’s yearly journey to Shiloh, when Hannah brought the boy Samuel his little robe (1:21; 2:19, already on these pages) (zevach). Whether Bethlehem held such a sacrifice that month the story never says; David makes up the excuse, and Jonathan adds to it in verse 29. The ASV has “for it is the yearly sacrifice there for all the family”; the Douay “because there are solemn sacrifices there for all his tribe”; the NIV “because an annual sacrifice is being made there for his whole clan.”

Decided. “But if his anger burns, know that the evil is decided by him”: ki-khaletah ha-ra’ah me-immo, from kalah, to finish, to complete: the evil is finished, settled, on his side (kalah). The same verb comes three times in this chapter: here, in Jonathan’s answer in verse 9, and in verse 33, where Jonathan learns that “it was decided by his father”. “If his anger burns” is charoh yechereh lo, “if it burns, burns to him”, the Hebrew idiom for anger. The ASV has “then know that evil is determined by him”; the Douay “know that his malice is come to its height”; the NWT 1984 “know that what is bad has been determined upon by him.”

Verses 8–11 · “A covenant of Jehovah” — kindness asked for, a life put in Jonathan’s hands, and two men in the field

A covenant of Jehovah. “For into a covenant of Jehovah you have brought your servant with you”: ki bi-verit Yehovah heveta et-avdekha immakh. In 18:3 (already on these pages) “Jonathan and David cut a covenant”; David now calls it Jehovah’s, made in his name and so binding before him, and in verse 42 Jonathan says the same, “we have sworn, the two of us, in the name of Jehovah” (brit). The ASV has “for thou hast brought thy servant into a covenant of Jehovah with thee”; the NWT 2013 “for you have brought your servant into Jehovah’s covenant with you”; the TLB “Do this for me as my sworn brother.”

Do kindness. “And do kindness with your servant”: ve-asita chesed al-avdekha, the covenant word, the loyal kindness one partner owes another (chesed). Saul used it of the Kenites, who “did kindness with all the sons of Israel” (15:6, already on these pages). Here David asks it of Jonathan; six verses later Jonathan asks it back, for himself and for his house forever (verses 14–15). The NWT 1984 has “you must render loving-kindness toward your servant”; the NWT 2013 “Show loyal love to your servant”; the Douay “Deal mercifully then with thy servant”; the ASV “Therefore deal kindly with thy servant”.

Put me to death yourself. “But if there is iniquity in me, put me to death yourself; for why should you bring me to your father?”: hamiteni attah. “Put to death” ran eight times through the last chapter (19:1–17, already on these pages); here David hands the verb to Jonathan, and in verses 32–33 it returns to Saul’s table: “Why should he be put to death?” and “to put David to death”. The ASV has “but if there be in me iniquity, slay me thyself”; the NWT 1984 “put me to death yourself”; the NIV “If I am guilty, then kill me yourself!”

Who will tell me, and the field. “Far be it from you!”: chalilah lakh, the word of verse 2 again, now turned against David’s suspicion. “Who will tell me? Or what if your father answers you harshly?”: mi yaggid li o mah ya’ankha avikha qashah, two questions run together, “who will tell me” and “what your father answers you, harshly”. The KJV keeps them apart, “Who shall tell me? or what if thy father answer thee roughly?”; the ASV joins them, “Who shall tell me if perchance thy father answer thee roughly?” The answer is the plan of verses 18–23, and to give it Jonathan takes David “out into the field”, away from listeners. The Douay runs verse 11 straight on into verse 12, “And when they were both of them gone out into the field”.

Verses 12–13 · “Jehovah, the God of Israel —” — an oath left unfinished, a curse on himself, and “as he was with my father”

An oath without its verb. “Jehovah, the God of Israel — when I search out my father…”: the Hebrew puts the divine name at the head of the sentence and goes straight on with ki, “that, when”, with no verb for the name. Jonathan begins an oath, and the sentence turns into a promise. The versions finish it in different ways: the ASV supplies “Jehovah, the God of Israel, be witness”; the NWT 1984 brackets the same words, “Jehovah the God of Israel [be a witness]”; the Greek adds “knows” after the name, “the God of Israel knows that I will examine my father”. “When I search out my father about this time tomorrow, the third [day]”: ki-echqor et-avi ka-et machar ha-shelishit, the verb chaqar, to search out, to explore. The Hebrew has no “or” between tomorrow and the third day; the ASV supplies one, “about this time to-morrow, or the third day”, and so does the NWT 1984, “about this time tomorrow, or the third day”.

Thus may Jehovah do. “Thus may Jehovah do to Jonathan, and more also”: koh-ya’aseh Yehovah li-Yhonatan ve-khoh yosif, the self-curse that Ruth spoke over herself (Ruth 1:17, already on these pages) and that Saul spoke over this same Jonathan, with “God” in place of the name: “May God do so, and more also; you shall surely die, Jonathan” (14:44, already on these pages). The father swore by it for his son’s death; the son swears by it for David’s life, and names himself in the third person. The Greek has “God” here as well. The ASV has “Jehovah do so to Jonathan, and more also”; the NWT 1984 “and so may he add to it”.

As he was with my father. “And may Jehovah be with you, as he was with my father”: ka’asher hayah im-avi, in the past tense. In 16:14 (already on these pages) “the spirit of Jehovah had turned away from Saul”, and in 18:12 Saul was afraid of David “because Jehovah was with him and had turned away from Saul”. The king’s son prays over David the presence his father has lost, and so speaks of David as the one who will stand where Saul stood. The ASV has “and Jehovah be with thee, as he hath been with my father”; the NWT 1984 “just as he proved to be with my father”.

Verses 14–17 · “The kindness of Jehovah” — Jonathan’s plea for his house, a verb that cuts three times, and the love of his soul

A sentence that keeps breaking. “And will you not — if I am still alive — will you not do with me the kindness of Jehovah, so that I do not die?”: ve-lo im-odenni chai ve-lo ta’aseh immadi chesed Yehovah ve-lo amut, three times lo, “not”, in one line. Read as it stands, it is a question that keeps starting again; many scholars change the first two to lu, “O that”, and read a wish (reported, not decided). The Greek reads two conditions, “if I am still living … and if I surely die”, and so does the Douay, from the Latin, “And if I live” … “but if I die”. The NWT 1984 keeps the question, “And will you not, if I shall be still alive, yes, will you not exercise the loving-kindness of Jehovah toward me, that I may not die?”; the ASV makes it a command, “And thou shalt not only while yet I live show me the lovingkindness of Jehovah”.

The kindness of Jehovah. The phrase chesed Yehovah stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible. The other two are psalms: the earth is full of the chesed of Jehovah (Psalm 33:5), and “the steadfast love of Jehovah is from everlasting to everlasting” (Psalm 103:17, already on these pages). Only here does one man ask another for it. Jonathan asks for the kindness God shows, to be shown by David.

Cut off, cut off, cut. “And you shall not cut off your kindness from my house forever; not even when Jehovah cuts off the enemies of David”: lo-takhrit … be-hakhrit; and then “And Jonathan cut [a covenant] with the house of David”: va-yikhrot. The verb karat comes three times in two verses: cutting off a kindness, cutting off enemies, and cutting a covenant, the ordinary Hebrew for making one (karat). In verse 16 the word “covenant” is not written; the verb carries it, so it stands in brackets here. Saul uses the same bare verb in 22:8 (now on these pages), complaining that none of his servants uncovered his ear “when my son cut with the son of Jesse”: both of this chapter’s idioms, in one accusation. The ASV has “So Jonathan made a covenant with the house of David”; the Geneva “So Jonathan made a bond with the house of David”.

Jehovah will seek it. “And Jehovah will seek [it] from the hand of David’s enemies”: u-viqqesh Yehovah mi-yad oyevei David, the verb of verse 1, where Saul “is seeking” David’s life; here Jehovah is the one who will seek, the phrase for requiring blood or a broken oath. Many read “David’s enemies” as a scribe’s softening of “David” himself, so that the line calls down Jehovah on David if he breaks faith with Jonathan’s house. The same softening is widely seen at 25:22 (now on these pages), where the Hebrew has “thus may God do to the enemies of David” and the Greek simply “to David” (reported, not decided). The Greek of this verse differs again: “that the name of Jonathan be found from the house of David”. The ASV has “And Jehovah will require it at the hand of David’s enemies”; the NWT 1984, which divides verses 15 and 16 in mid-sentence, “be cut off from the house of David” and “And Jehovah must require it at the hand of David’s enemies”; the TLB reads the softening, “and David swore to it with a terrible curse against himself and his descendants, should he be unfaithful to his promise”.

The love of his soul. “And Jonathan again made David swear, by his love for him; for he loved him with the love of his soul”: ki-ahavat nafsho ahevo. In 18:1 and 18:3 (already on these pages) Jonathan loved David “as his own soul”, ke-nafsho; here the measure is the love itself, “the love of his soul” (ahav). The verb is causative, “made David swear”. The Greek reads “and Jonathan swore again to David”, and so do the Douay, “And Jonathan swore again to David, because he loved him”, and the NWT 1984, “swore again to David because of his love for him”; the ASV keeps the Hebrew, “And Jonathan caused David to swear again”.

Verses 18–23 · The stone Ezel and three arrows — a seat that will be missed, a target, and “Jehovah has let you go”

Missed. “You will be missed, for your seat will be missed”: ve-nifqadta ki yippaqed moshavekha, the verb paqad, to visit, to attend to, and so to look for and find wanting (paqad). It comes six times in four verses of this chapter: twice in “if your father misses me at all” (verse 6), the two here, and “David’s place was missed” on each day of the feast (verses 25 and 27). The ASV has “and thou wilt be missed, because thy seat will be empty”; the NWT 1984 “and you will certainly be missed, because your seat will be vacant”; the Greek keeps the doubled verb, “you will be looked for, because your seat will be looked for”.

The third day, and the day of the deed. “And on the third day you shall go down quickly”: ve-shillashta tered me’od, literally “and you shall do [it] a third time, go down greatly”, with a verb made from “three”; David is to wait out the days of the feast and on the third come down to the place. “Where you hid yourself on the day of the deed”: be-yom ha-ma’aseh, which may point back to the hiding of 19:2–3 (already on these pages) or may simply mean a working day. The ASV has “And when thou hast stayed three days, thou shalt go down quickly” and “when the business was in hand”; the NWT 1984 takes the first verb as more missing, “And certainly on the third day you will be missed very much”, and the phrase as “on the working day”; the Douay “on the day when it is lawful to work”.

The stone Ezel. “And sit beside the stone Ezel”: etsel ha-even ha-azel. The name ha-azel stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and no place of the name is known; it may be built from azal, to go away, “the stone of departure”, which would suit what happens beside it (reported, not decided) (Ezel). The Greek has no stone here: David is to sit “beside that ergab”, a heap or mound, and at verse 41 he rises “from the argab”, where the Hebrew has “from beside the south”. The ASV and the KJV have “and shalt remain by the stone Ezel”; the NWT 1984 “and you must dwell near this stone here”, reading a word for “this” instead of a name; the TLB “over by the stone pile”.

Arrows and a target. “And I will shoot three arrows to its side, as though sending [them] for myself at a target”: sheloshet ha-chitstsim tsiddah oreh, le-shallach-li le-mattarah. Chets is an arrow (chets); mattarah is a mark to aim at, and elsewhere a guard (mattarah). The Greek makes the word a name, “sending into the Amattari”. The ASV has “And I will shoot three arrows on the side thereof, as though I shot at a mark”; the NWT 1984 “to send them where I will to a target”; the Douay “as if I were exercising myself at a mark”.

Sent, and let go. The verb shalach, to send, with its intensive shillach, to let go, to send away, carries the whole plan. It stands in eight verses of the chapter: David asks to be let go (verse 5); Jonathan promises to send word and to let him go (verses 12–13); the arrows are “sent” at the target (verse 20); the boy is sent (verse 21); Jonathan puts David’s request into David’s mouth (verse 29); and Saul says “send and take him” (verse 31). At the centre is verse 22: “go; for Jehovah has let you go”, ki shillachakha Yehovah. If the arrows fall beyond the boy, the parting is neither Jonathan’s doing nor David’s flight alone; Jehovah is sending him away. In 19:17 (already on these pages) Saul asked Michal why she had “let my enemy go”, with the same verb. The ASV has “go thy way; for Jehovah hath sent thee away”; the NWT 1984 “go, for Jehovah has sent you away”.

The boy and the young man. In verse 21 Jonathan will send “the boy”, ha-na’ar, the word used of him all through the arrow scene of verses 35–41 (na’ar); in verse 22, in the same breath, he says “the young man”, ha-elem. The word elem, young man, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, and the king’s order over David after Goliath, “You ask whose son the young man is” (17:56, already on these pages) (elem). The KJV keeps the change, “But if I say thus unto the young man”; the ASV has “the lad” in verse 21 and “the boy” in verse 22.

Jehovah between me and you. “Look — Jehovah is between me and you forever”: hinneh Yehovah beni u-veinekha ad-olam, with no verb at all. It is the language of the heap at Mizpah, where Laban said “May Jehovah watch between me and you” (Genesis 31:49, already on these pages), and it returns in verse 42 with the two men’s offspring added. The Greek, here and in verse 42, makes Jehovah a “witness”. The ASV has “behold, Jehovah is between thee and me for ever”; the NWT 1984 “why, may Jehovah be between me and you to time indefinite”; the TLB “for he has witnessed them”.

Verses 24–26 · “David’s place was missed” — the king’s seat by the wall, Jonathan who rose, and Saul’s “It is a chance”

The seat by the wall. “And the king sat on his seat as at the other times, on the seat by the wall”: ke-fa’am be-fa’am, as time after time, the phrase used when Jehovah “called as at the other times” to the boy Samuel (3:10, already on these pages), and when Samson said “I will go out as time after time”, not knowing that Jehovah had turned away from him (Judges 16:20, already on these pages). The wall, qir, is the word of the spear-throws, “I will strike David to the wall” (18:11; 19:10, already on these pages). The ASV has “And the king sat upon his seat, as at other times, even upon the seat by the wall”; the NWT 1984 “in the seat by the wall”.

Jonathan rose. “And Jonathan rose, and Abner sat at Saul’s side”: va-yaqom Yehonatan. Why Jonathan stands is not said. The Greek reads different letters, “and he came before Jonathan”, and several versions follow a reading like it, seating Jonathan opposite the king: the NWT 1984 has “and Jonathan was facing him”; the NIV “He sat in his customary place by the wall, opposite Jonathan”. The ASV keeps the Hebrew, “and Jonathan stood up”, and so does the KJV, “and Jonathan arose”. Abner, the commander of Saul’s army (14:50, already on these pages), sits at the king’s side (Abner).

It is a chance. “For he said: ‘It is a chance. He is not clean — surely he is not clean’”: miqreh hu, bilti tahor hu ki-lo tahor. The words miqreh hu stand in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and in the Philistines’ test of the cows and the ark, “it was a chance that happened to us” (6:9, already on these pages) (miqreh). The Philistines meant that no god’s hand had touched them; Saul means only that something has made David unclean, and so unable to sit at the new-moon meal. The Law kept a man unclean “until evening” after an emission of semen or after lying with his wife (Leviticus 15:16–18, already on these pages), and Deuteronomy uses this very noun for it: the man “not clean, from what happens at night”, miqreh-laylah, goes outside the camp until evening (Deuteronomy 23:11–12, already on these pages). Josephus says that this is what Saul supposed, “that he had not purified himself since he had accompanied with his wife”. The ASV has “Something hath befallen him, he is not clean; surely he is not clean”; the NIV “Something must have happened to David to make him ceremonially unclean—surely he is unclean.”; the NWT 1984 “Something has happened so that he is not clean, for he has not been cleansed.”

Verses 27–29 · “Why has the son of Jesse not come?” — the king will not say the name, and Jonathan improves the excuse

The son of Jesse. “Why has the son of Jesse not come to the meal, either yesterday or today?”: maddua lo-va ven-Yishai. In this chapter Saul never once says David’s name. Three times he says “the son of Jesse” (verses 27, 30 and 31), and once “send and take him”; the narrator, Jonathan and David name him throughout. Saul had used the name in the last chapter, “Where are Samuel and David?” (19:22, already on these pages); from here the father’s name is Saul’s word for him, to his servants at Gibeah and to the priest (22:7–8, 13, now on these pages), and Nabal will throw it back at David’s men: “Who is David, and who is the son of Jesse?” (25:10, now on these pages). The ASV has “Wherefore cometh not the son of Jesse to meat, neither yesterday, nor to-day?”; the Douay, from the Latin, “Why cometh not the son of Isai to meat neither yesterday nor to day?”; the TLB puts the name in Saul’s mouth, “Why hasn’t David been here for dinner either yesterday or today?”

Let me escape. Jonathan does not give the excuse as David worded it. David had said only “run to Bethlehem … for the yearly sacrifice” (verse 6); Jonathan adds a speech for David, with a brother who “has commanded me” and a request that ends “let me escape, please, and see my brothers”: immaletah na, from malat, the verb of the last chapter, where “David fled and escaped that night” (19:10, already on these pages) and the verb returns in four more verses. Jonathan puts the true word into the false story. The ASV has “let me get away, I pray thee, and see my brethren”; the NWT 1984 “let me slip away, please, that I may see my brothers”; the Greek “I will go across, please”. Where the Hebrew has “my brother — he has commanded me”, the Greek has “my brothers commanded me”, and the Douay “one of my brethren hath sent for me”.

The king’s table. “That is why he has not come to the king’s table”: el-shulchan ha-melekh. The phrase shulchan ha-melekh stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible. One of the other two is Jonathan’s own son, Mephibosheth, lame in both feet, who after David becomes king “eats always at the king’s table” (2 Samuel 9:13, now on these pages): the kindness Jonathan asks for in verses 14–15, paid at a king’s table. The third is the table of Solomon (1 Kings 5:7, now on these pages). The ASV has “Therefore he is not come unto the king’s table”; the NWT 1984 “That is why he has not come to the king’s table.”

Verses 30–31 · “Son of a perverse, rebellious woman” — Saul’s insult, a mother’s nakedness, and “a son of death”

The insult. “Son of a perverse, rebellious woman!”: ben-na’avat ha-mardut, literally “son of a twisted woman of the rebellion”. Na’avat is from avah, to bend, to twist, the root of avon, iniquity, the word David used of himself in verse 1. The noun mardut, rebellion, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one. Saul insults his son by insulting the son’s mother, his own wife; the only wife of Saul the book names is Ahinoam (14:50, already on these pages). The versions divide. The ASV has “Thou son of a perverse rebellious woman”; the NWT 1984 “You son of a rebellious maid”, as if reading na’arat, girl, for na’avat, as the Greek also does, “son of deserting girls”; the Douay, from the Latin, “Thou son of a woman that is the ravisher of a man”; the TLB “You fool!”

Shame and nakedness. “Do I not know that you are choosing the son of Jesse, to your own shame and to the shame of your mother’s nakedness?”: ki-vocher attah le-ven-Yishai le-vashtekha u-le-voshet ervat immekha. “Your mother’s nakedness”, ervat immekha, is the phrase of the Law’s first named prohibition, “the nakedness of your mother, you shall not uncover” (Leviticus 18:7, already on these pages) (ervah); the curse reaches back to the body that bore Jonathan. “Choosing”, bocher, is the verb of Jehovah choosing a king, “Do you see him whom Jehovah has chosen” (10:24; 16:8–10, already on these pages) (bachar); the Greek reads “partner”, and Josephus too has Saul call his son “a partner with David”. The ASV has “to thine own shame, and unto the shame of thy mother’s nakedness”; the NWT 1984 “to the shame of the secret parts of your mother”; the NIV “and to the shame of the mother who bore you”.

You shall not be established. “For all the days that the son of Jesse lives on the ground, you shall not be established, you or your kingdom”: lo tikkon attah u-malkhutekha, from kun, the verb Samuel used at Gilgal, “for now Jehovah would have established your kingdom over Israel for ever” (13:13, already on these pages). What Saul now says aloud to Jonathan he had said to himself when the women sang, “and what is left for him but the kingship?” (18:8, already on these pages). The ASV has “thou shalt not be established, nor thy kingdom”; the NWT 1984 “you and your kingship will not be firmly established”.

A son of death. “For he is a son of death”: ki ven-mavet hu, a man marked for death. The phrase ben-mavet stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible. The other is David’s, when Nathan tells him the parable of the poor man’s lamb and David, not knowing he is the man, says “As Jehovah lives, the man who has done this is a son of death” (2 Samuel 12:5, now on these pages). Both speeches begin with a man’s anger burning, va-yichar af (verse 30; 2 Samuel 12:5). Saul says it of David; David says it of himself. The Douay has “for he is the son of death”; the NWT 1984 “for he is destined for death”; the ASV “for he shall surely die”.

Verses 32–34 · “What has he done?” — the spear thrown at Jonathan, the evil decided, and a son grieved and shamed

What has he done? “Why should he be put to death? What has he done?”: lammah yumat, meh asah. Jonathan answers his father with David’s question of verse 1, “What have I done?”, turned into the third person. In the last chapter the same kind of plea won an oath, “As Jehovah lives, he shall not be put to death” (19:6, already on these pages); this time it brings the spear. The ASV has “Wherefore should he be put to death? what hath he done?”; the KJV “Wherefore shall he be slain? what hath he done?”

The spear thrown at a son. “And Saul hurled the spear at him to strike him”: va-yatel Sha’ul et-ha-chanit. The words va-yatel Sha’ul stand in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and the first throw at David, “And Saul hurled the spear” (18:11, already on these pages). The same hand and the same spear; now the target is his own son. The Greek softens it, “and Saul raised the spear against Jonathan”, and Josephus has Saul “hindered by his friends”. Jonathan learns from it what David had asked him to find out, “that it was decided by his father to put David to death”: ki-khalah hi, the verb of verses 7 and 9 for the third time. The ASV has “And Saul cast his spear at him to smite him”; the NWT 1984 “At that Saul went hurling the spear at him to strike him”; the Douay “And Saul caught up a spear to strike him.”

Grieved, and humiliated. “For he was grieved for David, because his father had humiliated him”: ki ne’etsav el-David ki hikhlimo aviv. The verb is atsav, the grief David said Saul meant to spare Jonathan, “lest he be grieved” (verse 3); it has come anyway, and from the father (otzev). “Humiliated him” can be David or Jonathan, and the Hebrew does not say which. The NIV chooses David, “because he was grieved at his father’s shameful treatment of David”; the NWT 1984 leaves it open, “because his own father had humiliated him”. The Greek reads another verb, “because his father had determined [evil] against him”, the kalah of verses 7, 9 and 33 once more. And Jonathan “did not eat food on the second day of the new moon”: at the meal where David’s seat was empty, Jonathan’s seat is now empty too.

Verses 35–40 · The arrow beyond the boy — “Quickly! Hurry! Do not stand still!”, and a signal only two men understood

The appointed time. “Jonathan went out into the field, to the appointed time with David, and a little boy with him”: le-mo’ed David, the word of Saul’s seven days at Gilgal, “to the appointed time that Samuel [had set]” (13:8, already on these pages) (mo’ed). That time Samuel did not come when Saul expected, and Saul could not wait; this time Jonathan keeps the appointment. The ASV has “at the time appointed with David, and a little lad with him”; the NWT 1984 “to the field of David’s appointed place”.

The arrow. “The boy ran, and he shot the arrow to pass beyond him”: ve-hu-yarah ha-chetsi le-ha’aviro. In verses 36–38 the single arrow is chetsi, a rarer form of chets, the form used again of the arrow that came out at King Joram’s heart (2 Kings 9:24); in verse 38 the scribes wrote ha-chetsi and read ha-chitstsim, the arrows (chets). Verse 36 speaks of “the arrows” and then shoots one; the three of verse 20 are never counted out. Jonathan’s call, “Is not the arrow beyond you?”, is the signal agreed in verse 22, “the arrows are beyond you … go”. The ASV has “And as the lad ran, he shot an arrow beyond him”; the NWT 1984 “and he himself shot the arrow to make it pass beyond him”.

Words for the one in hiding. “Quickly! Hurry! Do not stand still!”: meherah chushah al-ta’amod, three commands called to the boy and heard by the man in the field. The boy did as he was told and “knew nothing”, me’umah, the word of verse 26, where “Saul did not say anything”; only “Jonathan and David knew the matter”. The ASV has “Make speed, haste, stay not”; the NWT 1984 “In haste! Act quickly! Do not stand still!”; the TLB “Hurry, hurry, don’t wait.”

His weapons, to the city. “And Jonathan gave his weapons to the boy who was his”: kelav, his gear, here bow and arrows (kelim). Sending the boy back to the city with them leaves the two men alone in the field. The KJV has “And Jonathan gave his artillery unto his lad”, in the old sense of missile weapons; the Geneva “Then Jonathan gave his bow and arrows unto the boy that was with him”.

Verses 41–42 · “Go in peace” — David bows three times, two men weep, and an oath for their offspring forever

From beside the south. “And David rose from beside the south”: me-etsel ha-negev, with etsel, beside, the word of verse 19, “beside the stone Ezel”. As it stands the Hebrew has David rise from beside the Negev, the south; the Greek has “from the argab”, the mound it put in verse 19, and many read with it (reported, not decided). The ASV has “David arose out of a place toward the South”; the NIV “David got up from the south side of the stone”, joining the two readings; the NWT 1984 “he rose up from nearby to the south”.

Three bows, and the weeping. “And fell on his face to the ground, and bowed down three times”: David bows to the king’s son as to his lord. “And they kissed each other, and wept with each other, until David made it great”: ad-David higdil, “until David made [it] great”, a verb with no object. The ASV and the KJV have “until David exceeded”; the NWT 1984 “until David had done it the most”; the NWT 2013 and the NIV “but David wept the most”; the Douay “but David more”; the Greek “until a great completion”. David will weep for Jonathan again, after Gilboa: “Your love to me was more wonderful than the love of women” (2 Samuel 1:26, already on these pages).

Go in peace. “Go in peace, since we have sworn, the two of us, in the name of Jehovah, saying, ‘Jehovah shall be between me and you, and between my offspring and your offspring, forever’”: verse 23’s “Jehovah is between me and you forever”, with the offspring added, u-ven zar’i u-ven zar’akha (zera). This oath is what spares Jonathan’s son when David hands over Saul’s other descendants: “the king spared Mephibosheth, the son of Jonathan the son of Saul, because of the oath of Jehovah that was between them” (2 Samuel 21:7, now on these pages); and it is why David will ask, “Is there still anyone left of the house of Saul, that I may do kindness with him for Jonathan’s sake?” (2 Samuel 9:1, now on these pages). The ASV has “Jehovah shall be between me and thee, and between my seed and thy seed, for ever”; the NWT 1984 “between my offspring and your offspring to time indefinite”.

Where the chapter ends. The Hebrew ends the chapter with the oath. The next words, “And he arose and departed; and Jonathan went into the city”, are verse 1 of chapter 21 in the Hebrew (21:1, now on these pages). Most of the shelf prints them as the end of 20:42: the ASV has “And he arose and departed: and Jonathan went into the city”, and the NWT 1984 “Accordingly David rose up and went his way”; the Douay and the Geneva give them a verse of their own, 20:43. This translation follows the Hebrew numbering, so from chapter 21 its verse numbers will run one higher than the KJV’s.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “iniquity” for avon, as in 3:13, and “sin” for chatta’t; “far be it” for chalilah, as in 14:45; “uncover the ear” for galah ozen in all three places, as in 9:15; “hide” for satar, as in 19:2; “grieved” for atsav in verses 3 and 34 alike; “the new moon” for chodesh throughout; “earnestly asked leave” for nish’ol nish’al in both verses; “the yearly sacrifice”, as in 1:21 and 2:19; “decided” for kalah in verses 7, 9 and 33; “do kindness” and “the kindness of Jehovah” for chesed, as in 15:6; “put to death” for the causative of mut, as in chapter 19; “cut” and “cut off” for karat, with “[a covenant]” in brackets where the noun is not written; “seek” for biqqesh in verses 1 and 16; “as your soul lives”, as in 1:26, and “the love of his soul”, kept apart from the “as his own soul” of 18:1; “missed” for paqad all six times; “the stone Ezel”; “arrow” for chets and chetsi; “a target” for mattarah; “let go” for shillach of a person, as in 19:17; “the boy” for na’ar and “the young man” for elem, as in 17:56; “as at the other times”, as in 3:10; “it is a chance”, as in 6:9; “the son of Jesse” wherever Saul says it; “escape” for malat, as in chapter 19; “a son of death”; “established” for kun, as in 13:13; “hurled” for the spear, as in 18:11; “the appointed time” for mo’ed, as in 13:8; and “offspring” for zera, as in 2:20.

Echoes paid. Jonathan’s plea that David had not sinned (19:4) and the oath it won (19:6); the field and the hiding place of 19:2–3, now with the arrows; Naioth, from which David flees; the people’s “Far be it!” over Jonathan (14:45); Samuel’s uncovered ear (9:15); the love “as his own soul” and the covenant of 18:1–3, now called a covenant of Jehovah; the yearly sacrifice of Elkanah’s house (1:21; 2:19); the new moon’s offerings (Numbers 28:11); the name Saul, “asked for”; the Kenites’ kindness (15:6); “put to death” from chapter 19; the self-curse of Ruth (Ruth 1:17) and Saul’s over Jonathan (14:44); the spirit turned away from Saul (16:14; 18:12); the Mizpah heap, “Jehovah watch between me and you” (Genesis 31:49); Saul’s question over “the young man” (17:56); Michal who “let my enemy go” (19:17); “as at the other times” (3:10; Judges 16:20); the wall of the spear-throws (18:11; 19:10); the Philistines’ “it was a chance” (6:9); the uncleanness of Leviticus 15:16–18; David’s escapes in chapter 19; “your mother’s nakedness” (Leviticus 18:7); the king Jehovah chooses (10:24; 16:8–10); the kingdom that would have been established (13:13) and “what is left for him but the kingship?” (18:8); the spear of 18:11, thrown now at a son; the appointed time at Gilgal (13:8); and David’s lament for Jonathan (2 Samuel 1:26).

Echoes planted. Saul’s complaint that none of his servants uncovered his ear “when my son cut with the son of Jesse” (22:8), and his “son of Jesse” at Gibeah (22:7, 13); Nabal’s “who is the son of Jesse?” (25:10); “the enemies of David” at 25:22; David’s own “son of death” (2 Samuel 12:5, now on these pages); Mephibosheth at the king’s table (2 Samuel 9:1–13, now on these pages), and spared by the oath of Jehovah (2 Samuel 21:7, now on these pages); Joram’s arrow (2 Kings 9:24); and Psalm 33:5’s earth full of the kindness of Jehovah. None of the others is translated here yet; the half-verse that opens chapter 21 now is (21:1), and so are Saul’s complaint and his “son of Jesse” at Gibeah (22:7–8, 13), and Nabal’s question and “the enemies of David” (25:10, 22).

Next in this book: 1 Samuel 21 — David alone at Nob, Ahimelech trembling, the holy bread, Goliath’s sword wrapped in a cloth behind the ephod, and David feigning madness before Achish of Gath.
