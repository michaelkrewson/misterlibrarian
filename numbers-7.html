🕰
The tabernacle's twelve-day dedication. Chieftains donate six wagons and twelve oxen for the Levites to move the structure — Kohath, entrusted with the ark itself, gets none, since that cargo travels only on the shoulder — and then, one chieftain a day for twelve days, an identical dedication-offering reaches the newly anointed altar, written out in full every single time rather than summarized after the first. The chapter totals its own arithmetic twice over — 2,400 shekels of silver, 120 of gold, animals by the dozen — and closes on the first time Moses hears the voice answer him from between the cherubim, exactly as promised back at Exodus 25:22.
⚠ Undated within the chapter itself, but its opening line ties it to the day the tabernacle was 'finished' — the same completion Exodus 40:17 dates to the first day of the FIRST month of the second year — so the twelve-day dedication that follows likely runs across the first half of that month, shortly before Numbers 1's census on the first day of the SECOND month (already on these pages).

Numbers 7 — The Mister Translation

וַיְהִי בְּיוֹם כַּלּוֹת מֹשֶׁה לְהָקִים אֶת-הַמִּשְׁכָּן, וַיִּמְשַׁח אֹתוֹ וַיְקַדֵּשׁ אֹתוֹ וְאֶת-כָּל-כֵּלָיו, וְאֶת-הַמִּזְבֵּחַ, וְאֶת-כָּל-כֵּלָיו; וַיִּמְשָׁחֵם, וַיְקַדֵּשׁ אֹתָם.
And on the day Moses finished setting up the tabernacle — he anointed it and consecrated it, with all its vessels, and the altar and all its vessels, and anointed them and consecrated them —note
Y sucedió que, el día en que Moisés terminó de levantar el tabernáculo —lo ungió y lo consagró, con todos sus utensilios, y el altar y todos sus utensilios, y los ungió y los consagró—
2
וַיַּקְרִיבוּ נְשִׂיאֵי יִשְׂרָאֵל, רָאשֵׁי בֵּית אֲבֹתָם:  הֵם נְשִׂיאֵי הַמַּטֹּת, הֵם הָעֹמְדִים עַל-הַפְּקֻדִים.
the chieftains of Israel, the heads of their fathers’ houses, brought an offering — these were the chieftains of the tribes, who stood over those who were mustered.note
los jefes de Israel, los cabezas de las casas de sus padres, presentaron una ofrenda —estos eran los jefes de las tribus, los que estaban al frente de los contados.
3
וַיָּבִיאוּ אֶת-קָרְבָּנָם לִפְנֵי יְהוָה, שֵׁשׁ-עֶגְלֹת צָב וּשְׁנֵי עָשָׂר בָּקָר--עֲגָלָה עַל-שְׁנֵי הַנְּשִׂאִים, וְשׁוֹר לְאֶחָד; וַיַּקְרִיבוּ אוֹתָם, לִפְנֵי הַמִּשְׁכָּן.
And they brought their offering before Jehovah: six covered wagons and twelve oxen — a wagon for every two chieftains, and an ox for each one; and they presented them before the tabernacle.note
Y trajeron su ofrenda delante de Jehová: seis carros cubiertos y doce bueyes —un carro por cada dos jefes, y un buey por cada uno; y los presentaron delante del tabernáculo.
4
וַיֹּאמֶר יְהוָה, אֶל-מֹשֶׁה לֵּאמֹר.
And Jehovah spoke to Moses, saying:note
Y Jehová habló a Moisés, diciendo:
5
קַח, מֵאִתָּם, וְהָיוּ, לַעֲבֹד אֶת-עֲבֹדַת אֹהֶל מוֹעֵד; וְנָתַתָּה אוֹתָם אֶל-הַלְוִיִּם, אִישׁ כְּפִי עֲבֹדָתוֹ.
Take it from them, and it shall be for doing the work of the tent of meeting; and you shall give it to the Levites, each man according to his work.note
Tómalos de ellos, y serán para hacer el servicio de la tienda de reunión; y los darás a los levitas, a cada uno conforme a su servicio.
6
וַיִּקַּח מֹשֶׁה, אֶת-הָעֲגָלֹת וְאֶת-הַבָּקָר; וַיִּתֵּן אוֹתָם, אֶל-הַלְוִיִּם.
So Moses took the wagons and the oxen, and gave them to the Levites.note
Y Moisés tomó los carros y los bueyes, y los dio a los levitas.

Two wagons and four oxen he gave to the sons of Gershon, according to their work;note
Dos carros y cuatro bueyes dio a los hijos de Gersón, conforme a su servicio;

and four wagons and eight oxen he gave to the sons of Merari, according to their work, under the direction of Ithamar son of Aaron the priest.note
y cuatro carros y ocho bueyes dio a los hijos de Merari, conforme a su servicio, bajo la dirección de Itamar hijo del sacerdote Aarón.

Pero a los hijos de Coat no dio ninguno, porque el servicio de las cosas santas les correspondía a ellos —lo llevaban al hombro.

Numbers 7 meaning — twelve days, twelve gifts, and the wagons that once carried a starving family into Egypt now carrying God's own house out

⚠ V1's opening — ‘on the day Moses finished (kallot) setting up the tabernacle’ — picks up the exact verb that closed the whole construction narrative: Moses ‘finished’ (vayechal) that same work at Exodus 40:33 (already on these pages), the identical root kalah Exodus 39:32 had already used in the passive, with no one named, to report that the work simply ‘was finished.’ Numbers 7 dates the whole twelve-day dedication that follows from that one completed day, and its own ‘he anointed it and consecrated it’ is the event this project already flagged as still pending at Leviticus 8 (already on these pages) — Moses anointing the building itself before he ever pours oil on Aaron.

V3's wagons (agalot) are the identical word, running the opposite direction, as the wagons Pharaoh sent through Joseph to carry a starving Jacob and his whole household down into Egypt — the detail that finally convinced Jacob his son was still alive (Genesis 45:19, 21, 27, already on these pages). Two centuries later, Israelite-owned wagons carry God’s own house through the wilderness instead, on the way OUT. The text draws no attention to the reversal at all, and does not need to.

Kohathites carry the ark by hand — why the holiest cargo got no wagon at all

Six wagons and twelve oxen split unevenly by what each Levite clan actually hauls: two wagons and four oxen to Gershon (curtains and coverings — heavy fabric, no crushing weight), four wagons and eight oxen to Merari (the tabernacle’s own frames, bars, and posts — the load that needed double the transport), and nothing at all to Kohath. V9 states the reason as plainly as this book ever states anything: the holy things ‘belonged to them’ to serve, and ‘they carried it on the shoulder’ — the exact physical detail Numbers 4 (already on these pages) already built an entire wrapping ritual around, ark and table and altars wound in cloth of blue and tachash before a single Kohathite is allowed near them, then borne on poles already fixed through their rings so that no hand and no wheel ever touches the thing itself. A wagon would have been a kindness Kohath’s own cargo could not be permitted to accept.

One chieftain a day — the dedication order follows Numbers 2's own marching order exactly

Jehovah’s own instruction — ‘one chieftain each day, one chieftain each day’ — sets up the chapter’s whole engine: twelve days, twelve chieftains, never two offerings on the same day. And the order the text then follows for all twelve is not a fresh draw: Judah, Issachar, Zebulun; Reuben, Simeon, Gad; Ephraim, Manasseh, Benjamin; Dan, Asher, Naphtali is Numbers 2’s own four-standard marching order (already on these pages), repurposed as a calendar. The tribe that led the column out of camp each morning is the same tribe that steps up to the altar first in its own turn here.

Numbers 7:13 shekel of the sanctuary — the template offering, spelled out once for all twelve days

Every offering that follows for the next eleven days is identical to this one, item for item, down to the shekel — only a name, a tribe, and an ordinal change. One silver dish (130 shekels) and one silver basin (70 shekels), by the shekel of the sanctuary — the fixed weight-standard this chapter finally puts to sustained, repeated use — both heaped with fine flour mixed with oil for a grain-offering; then a golden vessel of incense weighing ten shekels, rendered spoon here to match this project’s own Numbers 4:7 (already on these pages), where the identical Hebrew word (kaph) already named the table’s own incense-vessels. The shelf splits four ways on this one small object: KJV and ASV agree, ‘spoon’; NIV modernizes to ‘dish’; Geneva calls it an ‘incense cup’; Douay-Rheims, following the Vulgate, reads ‘a little mortar.’ Four versions, four different objects, for one small vessel nobody alive has actually seen. Then the four animals that fix every one of the twelve days’ burnt-offering, sin-offering, and peace-offering counts for good — one bull, one ram, one lamb, one goat, two oxen, five rams, five goats, five lambs — sixty-nine numbers about to repeat themselves eleven times running, until the chapter itself does the multiplication in vv84–88.

Eliasaph son of Deuel or Reuel — the eleven remaining chieftains' offerings, and a name the text spells two ways

Eleven more chieftains repeat Nahshon’s exact offering, one per day, in the marching order named above: 2nd, Nethanel son of Zuar, Issachar; 3rd, Eliab son of Helon, Zebulun; 4th, Elizur son of Shedeur, Reuben; 5th, Shelumiel son of Zurishaddai, Simeon; 6th, Eliasaph son of Deuel, Gad; 7th, Elishama son of Ammihud, Ephraim; 8th, Gamaliel son of Pedahzur, Manasseh; 9th, Abidan son of Gideoni, Benjamin; 10th, Ahiezer son of Ammishaddai, Dan; 11th, Pagiel son of Ochran, Asher; 12th, Ahira son of Enan, Naphtali.

⚠ Day six’s chieftain is named ‘Eliasaph son of DEUEL’ here (vv42, 47) — matching Numbers 1:14 (already on these pages) exactly — but the same man was already introduced two chapters earlier as ‘Eliasaph son of REUEL’ at Numbers 2:14 (already on these pages), the Hebrew letters dalet and resh differing by a single stroke. Nothing in the Masoretic text flags either spelling as a correction of the other; two names for one father, and the received text simply carries both without comment (the tally across this book ends four ‘Deuel’ to one ‘Reuel’ — 1:14, 7:42, 7:47 and 10:20 against 2:14, all already on these pages) — the kind of small transmission seam this project reports rather than silently resolves.

2,400 shekels of silver — the tabernacle dedication's own arithmetic, totaled twice

Twelve identical gifts, added up in public: 2,400 shekels of silver (12 × 200), 120 shekels of gold (12 × 10), 12 bulls, 12 rams, 12 lambs, and 12 goats for the burnt- and sin-offerings, and 24 bulls, 60 rams, 60 goats, and 60 lambs for the peace-offerings — the peace-offering total alone (204 animals) outweighs the burnt- and sin-offering total (48) by more than four to one, since the peace-offering was always the largest single item in every one of the twelve daily gifts. This is the same instinct that closed Numbers 3’s own census with a subtraction the text made the reader watch happen twice (already on these pages), and that let Numbers 4’s own tally finally add up clean where Numbers 3 had left a stray remainder — a book that keeps its own books, worked out in front of the reader, rather than asking to be trusted.

Numbers 7:89 meaning — the promise of Exodus 25:22 kept the moment the altar is finished

This is the first time in the whole Torah the sentence actually happens. Jehovah promised, while giving the instructions for the ark’s own gold cover, long before a single board of the tabernacle was cut: ‘there I will meet with you, and I will speak with you from above the cover, from between the two cherubim’ (Exodus 25:22, already on these pages). Fifteen chapters of construction (Exodus 26–40), a full book of sacrificial law, six more chapters of Numbers, and now, the instant the altar’s own consecration is finished, the promise is simply kept, in nearly the same words it was made: the voice, from above the cover, from between the two cherubim, on the ark of the testimony. Nothing in the text marks the moment as unusual — no fire, no cloud, no gathered crowd. The building works exactly as its own blueprint said it would.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Numbers 7 both run 1–89 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift, and by verse count the longest chapter in the Torah (surpassing Genesis 24’s 67). Fourteen Masoretic paragraph breaks divide it: one setumah after v11, twelve petuchah breaks closing each of the twelve daily offerings (vv17, 23, 29, 35, 41, 47, 53, 59, 65, 71, 77, 83), and a final petuchah after v89. Two new dictionary entries: sheqel, anchored at its own first appearance (Abraham weighing silver, Genesis 23:16, already on these pages) and put to its longest sustained use here; and chanukkah, this chapter’s own chanukkat ha-mizbe'ach, ‘the dedication of the altar’ (vv10, 11, 84, 88) — the word’s first biblical appearance, and the direct ancestor of the festival name Hanukkah. nasi is reused from earlier chapters (already on these pages), its entry extended with this chapter’s own count of twelve.

Echoes paid. Genesis 45’s own Egyptian wagons, sent to carry a starving Jacob down into Egypt (already on these pages), now doing the identical word’s opposite errand — carrying God’s own house back out; Numbers 2’s own four-standard marching order (already on these pages), reused here as the literal order of days; Numbers 4’s own shoulder-carried, cloth-wrapped holy things (already on these pages), the reason this chapter’s wagons stop at Kohath; Leviticus 8’s own narrated anointing of the tabernacle before Aaron (already on these pages), which this chapter’s own opening verse assumes as already finished; Numbers 1’s and Numbers 3’s own running habit of arithmetic worked out in front of the reader (already on these pages), reused here at the largest scale yet; and Exodus 25:22’s own promise of a voice from between the cherubim (already on these pages), finally kept the moment this chapter’s dedication is complete.

Echoes planted. This chapter’s own chanukkat ha-mizbe'ach toward Deuteronomy 20:5’s dedication of a new house, 1 Kings 8’s dedication of Solomon’s temple, and Psalm 30’s own superscription, ‘a song at the dedication of the house’ — none yet on these pages — and, further out still, the festival of Hanukkah itself, centuries beyond this book’s own timeline.
