The letter's third movement: 'See what sort of love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God'; 'we will be like him, because we will see him just as he is'; sin as lawlessness and the hard saying that whoever is born of God does not sin; the Son of God made manifest to undo the works of the devil; Cain, 'of the evil one'; 'passed out of death into life'; love 'in deed and in truth'; God greater than our heart; and the Spirit named for the first time.
c. AD 85–95 on the traditional reading; the date is debated (see chapter 1).

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–3 · “Children of God” — “we will be like him, because we will see him just as he is”

“See what sort of love the Father has given us, that we should be called children of God; and we are.” “What sort of” is potapos, the word of “what sort of people ought you to be” at 2 Peter 3:11. “Children” is tekna, the Gospel’s “he gave the right to become children of God” (John 1:12) and “the scattered children of God” (John 11:52). John’s letters keep huios, “son,” for Jesus alone and call believers tekna; the Gospel has “sons of light” once, at John 12:36. “And we are” is not in the Byzantine text. The KJV reads “that we should be called the sons of God: therefore the world knoweth us not”; the ASV reads “that we should be called children of God; and such we are”; the NIV reads “See what great love the Father has lavished on us.”

“For this reason the world does not know us: because it did not know him.” Jesus prays, “the world has not known you” (John 17:25), and warns, “they will do these things because they have not known the Father, or me” (John 16:3). The Byzantine text reads “does not know you.” The NWT 1984 reads “the world does not have a knowledge of us, because it has not come to know him.”

“Beloved, now we are children of God, and it has not yet been made manifest what we will be. We know that when he is made manifest we will be like him, because we will see him just as he is.” “When he is made manifest” repeats the words of 2:28, “when he is made manifest we may have boldness.” ⚠ The Greek has no pronoun, and the subject can be “he” or “it,” the “what we will be” just named; readings differ, and the echo of 2:28 is why this translation prints “he.” Paul has the same hope, “now we see through a mirror, in a riddle; but then, face to face” (1 Corinthians 13:12), and “When Christ, your life, is made visible, then you also will be made visible with him in glory” (Colossians 3:4). The ASV reads “if he shall be manifested, we shall be like him”; the NWT 1984 reads “whenever he is made manifest we shall be like him”; the NIV reads “when Christ appears,” supplying the name.

“And everyone who has this hope set on him purifies himself, just as that one is pure.” Elpis, “hope,” stands only here in John’s Gospel and letters. “Purifies” is hagnizō, the verb of “purify your hearts” at James 4:8 and “Having purified your souls” at 1 Peter 1:22 (dictionary); “pure” is its adjective, hagnos. “That one” is ekeinos, as in 2:6, and in this letter it is Jesus. The NWT 1984 reads “purifies himself just as that one is pure”; the Douay reads “sanctifieth himself, as he also is holy,” from the Latin sanctificat.

Verses 4–6 · ⚠ “Sin is lawlessness” — and “everyone who remains in him does not sin”

“Everyone who does sin also does lawlessness; and sin is lawlessness.” Anomia, “lawlessness,” stands in thirteen verses (dictionary). Among them are “Depart from me, you workers of lawlessness” (Matthew 7:23) and “the man of lawlessness” and “the mystery of lawlessness” (2 Thessalonians 2:3, 2 Thessalonians 2:7). ⚠ Whether John means the breaking of the Law, or the end-time rebellion the antichrists of 2:18 belong to, readings differ; the article, “the lawlessness,” is in the Greek both times. The KJV reads “Whosoever committeth sin transgresseth also the law: for sin is the transgression of the law”; the ASV reads “Every one that doeth sin doeth also lawlessness; and sin is lawlessness”; the NIV reads “Everyone who sins breaks the law; in fact, sin is lawlessness.”

“And you know that that one was made manifest to take away sins, and in him there is no sin.” “Take away” is airō, the verb of “the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world” (John 1:29). The Byzantine text reads “our sins”: the KJV reads “he was manifested to take away our sins”; the ASV reads “to take away sins.”

⚠ “Everyone who remains in him does not sin; everyone who sins has not seen him or known him.” This is the hard saying of the letter, and verse 9 repeats it more strongly. The same writer said, “If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves” (1 John 1:8), and “if anyone does sin, we have an Advocate” (1 John 2:1). Readers have joined the sayings in several ways: the present tense means going on in sin, a settled way of life, and not every single act (the NIV prints “keeps on sinning”; the NWT 1984 “does not practice sin”); the verse states what the new birth is, not what any believer has reached; the sin meant is one sin in particular, the denial and hatred of the brother this letter is fighting (compare “sin leading to death” at 5:16); or the writer is answering two errors, those who claim to have no sin and those who say sin does not matter. Grammarians dispute how much “keeps on” a Greek present can carry. This translation prints the plain present and does not choose. “Has not seen him” is the “has not seen God” of 3 John 1:11. The KJV reads “Whosoever abideth in him sinneth not.”

Verses 7–10 · “Of the devil” — the works undone, “his seed,” and the children of God and of the devil

“Little children … Whoever does righteousness is righteous, just as that one is righteous.” “Lead astray” is planaō, as in “those who are leading you astray” at 1 John 2:26. “Whoever does righteousness” repeats 1 John 2:29, “everyone who does righteousness has been born of him.” The 28th Nestle-Aland edition reads “children” (paidia) for “little children” (teknia). The ASV reads “let no man lead you astray”; the NWT 1984 reads “he who carries on righteousness is righteous, just as that one is righteous.”

“Whoever does sin is of the devil, because the devil has been sinning from the beginning. For this the Son of God was made manifest: to undo the works of the devil.” “Of the devil” is Jesus’s “You are of your father the devil … He was a murderer from the beginning” (John 8:44) (dictionary). “Undo” is lyō, to loosen, untie or break up, the verb of “Destroy this temple” at John 2:19; Hebrews says the same of Jesus’s death, “that through death he might put out of action the one who holds the power of death, that is, the devil” (Hebrews 2:14). The KJV reads “that he might destroy the works of the devil”; the NWT 1984 reads “to break up the works of the Devil.”

⚠ “Everyone who has been born of God does not do sin, because his seed remains in him; and he cannot sin, because he has been born of God.” “Born of God” is the “born of him” of 2:29, now with God named. “His seed” is sperma (dictionary), and “his” is God; what the seed is, readings differ: the Spirit; the word, as “born again, not of perishable seed but imperishable, through the living and abiding word of God” (1 Peter 1:23) and “he gave us birth by the word of truth” (James 1:18); or the new life itself. Some read “his seed” as God’s offspring, the believer, remaining in God (reported, not checked here). “He cannot sin” carries the question of verse 6 at its sharpest, with the same range of readings. The NIV reads “God’s seed remains in them; they cannot go on sinning”; the NWT 1984 reads “His [reproductive] seed remains in such one”; the ASV reads “Whosoever is begotten of God doeth no sin, because his seed abideth in him.”

“By this the children of God and the children of the devil are evident: everyone who does not do righteousness is not of God, nor is the one who does not love his brother.” The last clause turns the letter to its next subject, love of the brother. The KJV reads “In this the children of God are manifest, and the children of the devil”; the NIV reads “nor is anyone who does not love their brother and sister.”

Verses 11–15 · “Not as Cain” — the message from the beginning, the world’s hatred, and “passed out of death into life”

“For this is the message that you heard from the beginning: that we should love one another.” Angelia, “message,” stands in two verses, both in this letter. The first message was “that God is light” (1 John 1:5); this one is the commandment of John 13:34, “that you love one another.” The KJV reads “For this is the message that ye heard from the beginning”; the Douay reads “For this is the declaration.”

“Not as Cain, who was of the evil one and slaughtered his brother. And why did he slaughter him? Because his works were evil, and his brother’s were righteous.” Cain is the only figure of the Old Testament the letter names (encyclopedia). Genesis does not give a reason: “Cain said [something] to his brother Abel. And when they were in the field, Cain rose up against his brother Abel and killed him” (Genesis 4:8). The letter supplies one from the works of the two brothers, as Hebrews does from their offerings, “By faith Abel offered God a better sacrifice than Cain did” (Hebrews 11:4) (encyclopedia); Jude says the false teachers “went the way of Cain” (Jude 1:11). “Of the evil one” is “the evil one” the young men conquered at 1 John 2:13. Sphazō, “slaughter,” stands in nine verses (dictionary): this one, and eight in Revelation, among them “a Lamb standing as though slain” (Revelation 5:6). It is the word for butchering an animal and for killing a sacrifice. The KJV reads “and slew his brother”; the NWT 1984 reads “slaughtered his brother”; the NIV reads “murdered his brother.”

“Do not be surprised, brothers, if the world hates you.” Jesus had said, “If the world hates you, know that it has hated me before you” (John 15:18) (dictionary). The Byzantine text reads “my brothers,” and the 28th Nestle-Aland edition begins “And do not.” The KJV reads “Marvel not, my brethren, if the world hate you.”

“We know that we have passed out of death into life, because we love the brothers. Whoever does not love remains in death.” Metabebēka, “has passed,” in the perfect, stands in two verses. The other is Jesus’s “whoever hears my word and believes him who sent me has eternal life … but has passed out of death into life” (John 5:24). There the passage is by believing; here the sign of it is love. The Byzantine text reads “Whoever does not love his brother”: the KJV reads “He that loveth not his brother abideth in death”; the ASV reads “He that loveth not abideth in death.”

“Everyone who hates his brother is a murderer, and you know that no murderer has eternal life remaining in him.” Anthrōpoktonos, “murderer,” stands in two verses (dictionary): this one, and the devil who “was a murderer from the beginning” at John 8:44. Jesus had put anger beside murder: “You shall not murder … But I say to you that everyone who is angry with his brother shall be liable to the judgment” (Matthew 5:21–22). The NWT 1984 reads “Everyone who hates his brother is a manslayer”; the KJV reads “Whosoever hateth his brother is a murderer.”

Verses 16–18 · “He laid down his life for us” — the world’s possessions, a shut heart, and love “in deed and in truth”

“By this we have come to know love: that one laid down his life for us; and we ought to lay down our lives for the brothers.” “Laid down his life” is the good shepherd’s word, “The good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep” (John 10:11), and the “greater love” of John 15:13. “Life” is psychē (dictionary), which the NWT 1984 prints “surrendered his soul for us.” The KJV reads “Hereby perceive we the love of God,” adding “of God,” as the Douay does from the Latin: “In this we have known the charity of God.” The ASV reads “Hereby know we love.”

“But whoever has the world’s possessions and sees his brother in need and shuts his heart against him — how does the love of God remain in him?” “Possessions” is bios, the word of “the arrogance of possessions” at 1 John 2:16: what one lives on. “Heart” is splanchna, the inward parts, the seat of feeling, as in “the hearts of the holy ones have been refreshed” at Philemon 1:7 (dictionary). James asks the same question: “If a brother or sister is naked and lacking daily food, and one of you says to them, ‘Go in peace, keep warm and eat your fill,’ but you do not give them what the body needs, what good is it?” (James 2:15–16). The KJV reads “whoso hath this world’s good … and shutteth up his bowels of compassion from him”; the NWT 1984 reads “shuts the door of his tender compassions upon him”; the NIV reads “has no pity on them.”

“Little children, let us not love in word or with the tongue, but in deed and in truth.” The Byzantine text reads “My little children.” The KJV reads “My little children, let us not love in word, neither in tongue; but in deed and in truth”; the NIV reads “let us not love with words or speech but with actions and in truth.”

Verses 19–22 · ⚠ “God is greater than our heart” — a condemning heart, boldness toward God, and whatever we ask

“By this we will know that we are of the truth, and we will reassure our heart before him, that if our heart condemns us, God is greater than our heart and knows all things.” “Reassure” is peithō, to persuade; the Douay reads “in his sight shall persuade our hearts.” Kataginōskō, “condemn,” stands in three verses (dictionary): these two, and Paul’s “he stood condemned” at Galatians 2:11. ⚠ The grammar of verses 19–20 is the hardest in the letter. The Greek runs “that if our heart condemns us, that God is greater,” and the two words “that” can be joined in more than one way. Read one way, God is greater than our heart in mercy, and the verse comforts a troubled conscience; read another, God is greater as a judge who knows everything our heart knows and more, and the verse warns. Read with the first word divided, ho ti ean, “in whatever,” it says that before him we will reassure our heart “in whatever” it condemns us: the NWT 1984 reads “as regards whatever our hearts may condemn us in, because God is greater than our hearts.” Readings differ, and this translation does not choose. The editions also differ on small words here: Tregelles, the 28th Nestle-Aland edition and the Byzantine text begin “And by this”; the Byzantine text has “we know”; Tregelles and the Byzantine text read “hearts.” The KJV reads “And hereby we know that we are of the truth, and shall assure our hearts before him. For if our heart condemn us, God is greater than our heart.”

“Beloved, if our heart does not condemn us, we have boldness toward God, and whatever we ask we receive from him, because we keep his commandments and do the things that are pleasing before him.” “Boldness” is parrēsia, as in 2:28, “we may have boldness and not shrink from him in shame” (dictionary); there it is boldness at his coming, here boldness now. “Whatever we ask” is Jesus’s promise, “whatever you ask in my name, I will do it” (John 14:13) and “If you remain in me and my words remain in you, ask whatever you want” (John 15:7). Arestos, “pleasing,” stands in four verses. One is Jesus’s “I always do what pleases him” at John 8:29. The KJV reads “then have we confidence toward God”; the ASV reads “we have boldness toward God”; the NWT 1984 reads “we have freeness of speech toward God.”

Verses 23–24 · “Believe and love” — one commandment, and “by the Spirit he gave us”

“And this is his commandment: that we believe in the name of his Son Jesus Christ and love one another, just as he gave us commandment.” One commandment, in two parts. This is the first time the verb “believe” stands in the letter. The Greek here has the plain dative, “believe the name,” where the Gospel has eis, “into,” as in “to those who believe in his name” (John 1:12); this translation prints the familiar “believe in” for both. “Just as he gave us commandment” points back to John 13:34. Tregelles reads the present, “that we go on believing.” The KJV reads “That we should believe on the name of his Son Jesus Christ”; the NWT 1984 reads “that we have faith in the name of his Son Jesus Christ.”

“And whoever keeps his commandments remains in him, and he in him. And by this we know that he remains in us: by the Spirit he gave us.” “Remains in him, and he in him” is the vine’s “Remain in me, and I in you” (John 15:4) and “abides in me, and I in him” (John 6:56). This is the first time the letter names the Spirit, which returns at 4:2 and 4:13; Jesus had said of “the Spirit of truth” that “he remains beside you, and he will be in you” (John 14:17) (dictionary). The ASV reads “by the Spirit which he gave us”; the NWT 1984 reads “owing to the spirit which he gave us,” printing “spirit” in lower case in keeping with its reading of the holy spirit as God’s active force, a doctrinal rendering; the NIV reads “We know it by the Spirit he gave us.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “what sort of,” as at 2 Peter 3:11; “children of God,” never “sons”; “when he is made manifest,” as at 2:28; “that one”; “lawlessness,” as at 2 Thessalonians 2:3; “does not sin” and “cannot sin,” the plain present kept, with the readings in the notes; “undo”; “his seed”; “slaughtered”; “passed out of death into life,” as at John 5:24; “murderer,” as at John 8:44; “laid down his life,” as at John 10:11; “possessions,” as at 2:16; “heart” for the inward parts; “reassure”; “boldness,” as at 2:28; and “remain.”

Echoes paid. “When he is made manifest” from 1 John 2:28; “born of him” from 1 John 2:29, now “born of God”; boldness from 1 John 2:28, now toward God; “the evil one” of 1 John 2:13, whom Cain was of; the message of 1 John 1:5; “keep his commandments” of 1 John 2:3; the possessions of 1 John 2:16; the new commandment of John 13:34; and the Spirit, named at last.

Echoes planted. “Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits” at 4:1; “God is love” at 4:8 and 4:16; “he has given us of his Spirit” at 4:13; boldness “in the day of judgment” at 4:17; “sin leading to death” at 5:16; and “everyone who has been born of God does not sin” again at 5:18 (all yet to come in this translation).

Next in this book: 1 John 4. “Beloved, do not believe every spirit, but test the spirits”; the spirit that confesses Jesus Christ come in flesh; “God is love”; “There is no fear in love, but complete love throws fear out”; and “We love, because he first loved us.”
