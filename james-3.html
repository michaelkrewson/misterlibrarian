The tongue: not many teachers, for 'we all trip in many ways'; a bit in a horse's mouth, a ship's steering oar, a small fire and a great forest; the tongue set on fire by Gehenna, tamed by no one; blessing and cursing from one mouth; and two wisdoms, the earthly kind with its jealousy and self-seeking, and the wisdom from above, 'first pure, then peaceful.'
Undated in the letter, and one of the most disputed dates in the New Testament (see chapter 1).

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · “Let not many of you become teachers” — “we all trip in many ways,” and the perfect man who bridles his speech

“Let not many of you become teachers, my brothers, knowing that we will receive a greater judgment.” James counts himself among the teachers: “we will receive.” Jesus says “one is your teacher, and you are all brothers” at Matthew 23:8, and Hebrews scolds readers who “ought to be teachers” at Hebrews 5:12. “Judgment” is krima, the verdict, not the process. The KJV reads “be not many masters”; the NWT 1984 reads “knowing that we shall receive heavier judgment”; the NIV reads “we who teach will be judged more strictly.”

“For we all trip in many ways. If anyone does not trip in speech, he is a perfect man, able to bridle the whole body as well.” The verb ptaiō, “trip,” stands in four verses, among them the law kept whole but for one point at James 2:10 (dictionary). “Perfect” is teleios, as at James 1:4. Chalinagōgeō, “bridle,” stands in two verses, here and the man “not bridling his tongue” at James 1:26 (dictionary). “In speech” is en logō; compare “by your words you will be justified” at Matthew 12:37. The KJV reads “For in many things we offend all”; the ASV reads “For in many things we all stumble”; the Douay reads “He is able also with a bridle to lead about the whole body.”

Verses 3–5 · Bits, a steering oar, and a small fire — “how small a fire sets how great a forest ablaze”

“Now if we put the bits into the mouths of horses so that they obey us, we guide their whole body as well. Look at the ships too … they are guided by a very small steering oar wherever the impulse of the helmsman wishes.” The noun chalinos, “bit,” stands in two verses; the other is Revelation 14:20. Robinson–Pierpont reads “Look” for “Now if” (the apparatus). Pēdalion, “steering oar,” stands in two verses; the other is Paul’s ship at Acts 27:40, “loosing the ropes of the steering oars” (dictionary). “Helmsman” is the participle of euthynō, “make straight,” the verb of “Make straight the way of Jehovah” at John 1:23. The KJV reads “Behold, we put bits in the horses’ mouths”; the ASV reads “whither the impulse of the steersman willeth”; the NWT 1984 reads “steered by a very small rudder.”

“So also the tongue is a small member, and it boasts great things. Look how small a fire sets how great a forest ablaze!” Robinson–Pierpont has one word, “boasts greatly,” for “boasts great things” (the apparatus). The Greek uses one word twice, hēlikon … hēlikēn, “how great,” and the sense makes the first small and the second large. Hylē, “forest,” wood or brushwood, stands in one verse (dictionary). The KJV reads “Behold, how great a matter a little fire kindleth!”; the ASV reads “Behold, how much wood is kindled by how small a fire!”; the NWT 1984 reads “How little a fire it takes to set so great a woodland on fire!”

Verses 6–8 · ⚠ “The wheel of birth” and Gehenna — the world of unrighteousness, and the one thing no human can tame

⚠ “And the tongue is a fire; the tongue is set among our members as the world of unrighteousness, staining the whole body, setting on fire the wheel of birth, and set on fire by Gehenna.” The sentence is among the hardest in the letter to divide: “the world of unrighteousness” can end the first clause, as in many versions, or begin the second, as here; kosmos can also mean “adornment” (reported); and Robinson–Pierpont adds “so” (the apparatus). The verb spiloō, “stain,” stands in two verses, here and “the garment stained by the flesh” at Jude 23; its negative is the “unspotted” of James 1:27 (dictionary). Trochos, “wheel,” stands in one verse (dictionary), and “birth” is genesis, the word of “the face of his birth” at James 1:23 (dictionary); “the wheel of birth” is read as the whole course of a life from its beginning, and the phrase is reported from Greek writers on the cycle of existence (reported, not checked here). Geenna, “Gehenna,” stands in twelve verses, every one of the others in the Gospels, as at Mark 9:43 (dictionary). The KJV reads “setteth on fire the course of nature; and it is set on fire of hell”; the ASV reads “setteth on fire the wheel of nature”; the NWT 1984 reads “sets the wheel of natural life aflame and it is set aflame by Ge·henʹna.”

“For every kind of beast and bird, of creeping thing and sea creature, is tamed and has been tamed by humankind; but the tongue no one among humans can tame — an unstable evil, full of death-bringing venom.” The four kinds are those of Genesis 9:2, “every wild animal of the earth and every flying creature of the sky … everything that moves on the ground and … all the fish of the sea.” The verb damazō, “tame,” stands in three verses; the other is the man of the tombs whom “no one had the strength to subdue” at Mark 5:4 (dictionary). Enalios, “sea creature,” stands in one verse. The adjective akatastatos, “unstable,” stands in two verses, here and the double-souled man “unstable in all his ways” at James 1:8 (dictionary); Robinson–Pierpont has “uncontrollable” (the apparatus). “Venom” is ios, “the venom of asps is under their lips” at Romans 3:13; thanatēphoros, “death-bringing,” stands in one verse. The KJV reads “it is an unruly evil, full of deadly poison”; the ASV reads “it is a restless evil”; the NWT 1984 reads “An unruly injurious thing.”

Verses 9–12 · “With it we bless the Lord and Father” — the likeness of God, a spring, and a fig tree

“With it we bless the Lord and Father, and with it we curse human beings, who have been made according to the likeness of God. From the same mouth come blessing and cursing.” “The Lord and Father” is ton kyrion kai patera, one title for God, like “the God and Father” of James 1:27; where the letter has the bare kyrios for God, as at James 1:7, this translation prints “Jehovah,” and here it keeps the paired title as it stands. Robinson–Pierpont reads “God” for “the Lord” (the apparatus). “According to the likeness of God” is the phrase of Genesis 1:26, “according to our likeness,” in the words of its Greek (checked); homoiōsis, “likeness,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). Jesus says “bless those who curse you” at Luke 6:28, and Paul “bless and do not curse” at Romans 12:14. The KJV reads “Therewith bless we God, even the Father”; the ASV reads “Therewith bless we the Lord and Father”; the NWT 1984 reads “With it we bless Jehovah, even [the] Father.”

“Does a spring pour out the sweet and the bitter from the same opening? Can a fig tree, my brothers, produce olives, or a vine figs? Neither can salt water produce sweet.” “Spring” is pēgē (dictionary). Opē, “opening,” stands in two verses, here and the “holes in the ground” of Hebrews 11:38 (dictionary). “Bitter” is the adjective of “bitter jealousy” in verse 14. The tree and its fruit are Jesus’ figure at Luke 6:44, “Figs are not gathered from thornbushes.” Halykos, “salt,” stands in one verse (dictionary). Robinson–Pierpont has the longer “So no spring yields salt and sweet water” (the apparatus), the reading behind the KJV, “so can no fountain both yield salt water and fresh”; the ASV reads “Neither can salt water yield sweet”; the NIV reads “Can both fresh water and salt water flow from the same spring?”

Verses 13–16 · “Who is wise and understanding among you?” — bitter jealousy, self-seeking, and a wisdom that is earthly, natural, demonic

“Who is wise and understanding among you? Let him show by his good conduct his works in the meekness of wisdom.” “Wise and understanding” is the pair of the Greek of Deuteronomy 1:13, where Moses asks for “men who are wise and understanding” to lead the tribes (checked; Deuteronomy 1:13 has “wise and discerning” from the Hebrew). Epistēmōn, “understanding,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). “Show” is the verb of “show me your faith … I will show you faith by my works” at James 2:18. “Conduct” is anastrophē, as at Hebrews 13:7. Prautēs, “meekness,” stands in ten verses, among them “receive with meekness the implanted word” at James 1:21 (dictionary). The KJV reads “Who is a wise man and endued with knowledge among you?”; the ASV reads “Who is wise and understanding among you?”; the NWT 1984 reads “with a mildness that belongs to wisdom.”

“But if you have bitter jealousy and self-seeking in your heart, do not boast and lie against the truth. This is not the wisdom that comes down from above, but is earthly, natural, demonic. For where jealousy and self-seeking are, there is disorder and every worthless deed.” Eritheia, “self-seeking,” stands in seven verses, among them Romans 2:8 and Galatians 5:20 (dictionary). “Boast” is the verb of “mercy boasts over judgment” at James 2:13 (dictionary). “From above” is anōthen, as in “every perfect gift is from above” at James 1:17. Psychikos, “natural,” stands in five verses; Paul’s “natural person” at 1 Corinthians 2:14 is one (dictionary). Daimoniōdēs, “demonic,” stands in one verse (dictionary). Akatastasia, “disorder,” stands in five verses, among them “God is not a God of disorder” at 1 Corinthians 14:33 (dictionary); it is the noun of the “unstable” of verse 8. Phaulos, “worthless,” stands in six verses, as at John 3:20 (dictionary). The KJV reads “glory not, and lie not against the truth”; the NWT 1984 reads “animal, demonic”; the NIV reads “bitter envy and selfish ambition.”

Verses 17–18 · “The wisdom from above” — pure, peaceful, open to persuasion, and “the fruit of righteousness sown in peace”

“But the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceful, reasonable, open to persuasion, full of mercy and good fruits, without making distinctions, without hypocrisy.” “Peaceful” is eirēnikos, the “peaceful fruit of righteousness” at Hebrews 12:11. The adjective epieikēs, “reasonable,” stands in five verses, among them Philippians 4:5 and 1 Timothy 3:3 (dictionary). Eupeithēs, “open to persuasion,” built on peithō, stands in one verse (dictionary). “Full of mercy” answers “mercy boasts over judgment” at James 2:13. Adiakritos, “without making distinctions,” stands in one verse; it is the negative of diakrinō, “made distinctions” at James 2:4 and “doubts” at James 1:6, and so is read as impartial or as undivided (dictionary). Anypokritos, “without hypocrisy,” stands in six verses, as at Romans 12:9 (dictionary). The KJV reads “first pure, then peaceable, gentle, and easy to be intreated”; the NWT 1984 reads “reasonable, ready to obey”; the Douay reads “without judging, without dissimulation.”

“And the fruit of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace.” “The fruit of righteousness” is the phrase of Hebrews 12:11. “By those who make peace” is a dative that can also mean “for those who make peace”; readings differ. Jesus calls them happy at Matthew 5:9, “Happy the peacemakers.” The ASV reads “is sown in peace for them that make peace”; the KJV reads “is sown in peace of them that make peace”; the NIV reads “Peacemakers who sow in peace reap a harvest of righteousness.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “trip,” as at 2:10; “perfect,” as at 1:4; “bridle,” as at 1:26; “steering oar,” as at Acts 27:40; “the world of unrighteousness”; “the wheel of birth,” with the “birth” of 1:23; “Gehenna,” not “hell”; “humankind”; “unstable,” as at 1:8; “the Lord and Father”; “likeness,” as at Genesis 1:26; “wise and understanding,” the pair of Deuteronomy 1:13 in Greek; “conduct”; “meekness,” as at 1:21; “self-seeking,” as at Galatians 5:20; “natural,” as at 1 Corinthians 2:14; “disorder,” as at 1 Corinthians 14:33; “worthless”; “reasonable”; and “without hypocrisy.”

Echoes paid. “We all trip” from James 2:10; the tongue bridled at James 1:26; perfect from James 1:4; the unstable man of James 1:8; the face of his birth at James 1:23; unspotted from James 1:27; from above from James 1:17; meekness from James 1:21; show from James 2:18; boast from James 2:13; made distinctions from James 2:4; and the wisdom asked for at James 1:5, now described.

Echoes planted. “Where do wars come from?” at 4:1, after the “disorder” here; “friendship with the world” at 4:4; “do not speak against one another” at 4:11; and the “rust” at 5:3, the same word ios as the “venom” of verse 8 (all yet to come in this translation).
