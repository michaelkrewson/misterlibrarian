Exodus 38 — The Mister Translation

1
וַיַּעַשׂ אֶת-מִזְבַּח הָעֹלָה, עֲצֵי שִׁטִּים: חָמֵשׁ אַמּוֹת אָרְכּוֹ וְחָמֵשׁ-אַמּוֹת רָחְבּוֹ, רָבוּעַ, וְשָׁלֹשׁ אַמּוֹת, קֹמָתוֹ.
And he made the altar of burnt offering of acacia wood: five cubits its length, and five cubits its breadth, square, and three cubits its height.note
Y hizo el altar del holocausto de madera de acacia: cinco codos su longitud, y cinco codos su anchura, cuadrado, y tres codos su altura.
2

Y hizo todos los utensilios del altar —las ollas, y las palas, y los tazónes, los garfios, y los braseros— todos sus utensilios los hizo de bronce.

The other altar, finally built

The bronze altar of burnt offering repeats Exodus 27:1–8, already on these pages, measurement for measurement — the same hollow construction, the same network grating reaching halfway up, the same horns ‘of one piece with it’ already discussed at length in the dictionary entry for this word. Bronze outside for blood, gold inside for incense (Exodus 37:25–26, already on these pages): the courtyard altar and the inner altar are, at last, both standing.

A basin made from mirrors, a detail the instructions never gave

‘He made the laver of bronze… from the mirrors of the serving women who served at the door of the tent of meeting’ (v8) is entirely new information — the original command for this object (Exodus 30:17–18, already on these pages) says only ‘a laver of bronze,’ with no source given at all. Of every object in the tabernacle, this is the only one whose raw material the text bothers to name, and it names something startling: personal mirrors, handed over by women whose own role at the tent’s entrance the Torah never fully explains. ⚠ The shelf splits on exactly that ambiguity: KJV reads the root’s military sense almost literally, ‘the women ASSEMBLING, which assembled’; ASV softens to ‘the ministering women that ministered’; NIV and NASB both flatten it further to the generic ‘served.’ No version fully carries over what the Hebrew root usually means elsewhere — an army mustered for duty.

⚠ Later rabbinic tradition, most famously Rashi, filled the silence with a story: that these mirrors had once been used by Israelite women in Egypt to draw their exhausted husbands back to them under slavery, and were therefore, in God’s own words to a reluctant Moses, ‘more precious to Me than anything else’ — the very instrument of desire that kept a slave people reproducing now melted into the object that will wash the hands of its priests. Nothing in the Hebrew text of Exodus itself tells this story; it survives as interpretation, not as narration — but it is the oldest and most persistent answer to a question the plain text raises and never closes: why single out, of all the freewill gifts in two chapters of gold and silver, exactly these.

A perimeter of linen, measured and closed

Every hanging, pillar, and socket here repeats Exodus 27:9–19, already on these pages: a hundred cubits north and south, fifty east and west, the gate’s own screen twenty cubits wide and five high, ‘corresponding to the hangings of the courtyard’ (v18) so that the fence and its one opening read as a single continuous wall. Bronze below the ground line, silver at eye level, on every pillar without exception — the same two-metal rule already fixed for the tabernacle’s own boards, now extended to the fence around it.

The gold accounted for, to the shekel

The chapter closes with something the tabernacle account has not done before: a formal audit, ‘the accounts of the tabernacle… by the hand of Ithamar, the son of Aaron the priest’ (v21) — the first time this particular son of Aaron is given any active responsibility on these pages, after being named twice already (Exodus 6:23 and 28:1, both already on these pages). Every talent and shekel of gold, silver, and bronze that passed through the camp is itemized and its destination named — the sockets, the hooks, the altar, the pegs — a level of financial transparency this book has not offered for any other project, sacred or otherwise.

⚠ The arithmetic actually works: a hundred talents and 1,775 shekels of silver, collected at a flat half-shekel per head (v26), divides out to exactly 603,550 men — the very total the verse itself states. The tax total and the census total check each other, in the text’s own numbers, without either one needing to say so directly.

Read against three chapters back, the whole account reads like a quiet correction. The golden calf was gold taken from the people and turned, in secret, into an idol no one could account for. This is gold taken from the people and turned, in public, into a building whose every ounce is written down and signed for.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. ‘Serving women’ kept for the rare root tzava (v8) rather than ‘ministering’ or a flattened ‘served’ — see the note above for the word’s usual military sense and the real fork in the shelf. ‘Laver’ kept for kiyor, matching this translation’s own earlier choice at Exodus 30:18. ‘Bronze’ kept throughout rather than ‘brass,’ matching this translation’s own established convention. The chapter’s four paragraph breaks ({ס} at v7, {ס} at v8, {ס} at v20, {ס} at v23) mark the seam after each finished object and after the credits before the accounting begins. Verse numbering follows the Hebrew exactly; Hebrew and English Exodus 38 both run 1–31.

A note on order: Exodus stands on these pages at chapters 1–39 — the bronze altar and courtyard built at last, the laver made from women’s own mirrors, and the whole project audited to the shekel, under Ithamar’s hand. Exodus 39, the priestly garments finally sewn, is now on these pages too. Exodus 40, the tabernacle raised and the glory of Jehovah filling it, remains open, along with the rest of the book.
