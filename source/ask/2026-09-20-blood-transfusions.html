A reader sent over a video and asked what actually changed, what the position has always rested on, and how a group with almost no central clergy ends up with a single, binding medical policy in the first place

A reader asked

“I just saw that Jehovah's Witnesses changed their position on blood transfusions again — what's the actual current stance, and how did we get here?”

Watch the September 18, 2026 announcement on Instagram (@jw_pressroom)

The short answer. On September 18, 2026, the Governing Body of Jehovah's Witnesses announced that accepting or donating red cells, white cells, plasma, or platelets — the four components whole blood is made of — is now a matter of personal conscience for each Witness.[1] Congregations and elders are not to get involved in that decision either way. What has not changed is the one line that has stood since 1945: a Witness still does not accept a transfusion of whole blood. The reasoning is the same reasoning behind every step this policy has taken for the last forty-five years — the Bible names blood itself as off-limits, but says nothing about a specific medical procedure, so the procedure is left to the individual.

What the announcement actually says

Paul Gillies, the organization's spokesman at its Warwick, New York headquarters, framed the change as continuity, not reversal: “Blood remains sacred to Jehovah's Witnesses, who continue to regard life and blood as precious gifts from God,” he said, “We recognize that medical decisions involving blood components are a matter of conscience between each Witness and their God, Jehovah… Congregations do not involve themselves in personal decisions that rest with the individual conscience.”[1] The organization's Hospital Liaison Committees — volunteers who have coordinated bloodless-care options with physicians since 1988 — were told plainly that they “will not get involved” in an individual member's decision and must preserve patient confidentiality around it.[2] It is the second real revision to this doctrine in 2026 alone: an earlier update on March 20 permitted storing and transfusing a patient's own blood before surgery, not only during it, which had been the rule since 1989.[2]

The two verses the entire doctrine stands on

Both the 1945 original and the 2026 update trace back to the same handful of words, and this project's own translation renders them the way it renders everything else in this library — compared against the Hebrew, cited, and left for the reader to weigh. First, God's instruction to Noah after the flood: “Only flesh with its life still in it — its blood — you must not eat” (Genesis 9:4). Thirteen chapters into the Law given at Sinai, the same rule gets its reason stated outright: “For the life of the flesh is in the blood… for it is the blood, by reason of the life, that makes atonement” (Leviticus 17:11), tightened two verses later into a flat command — “You shall not eat the blood of any flesh, for the life of all flesh is its blood” (Leviticus 17:14). This project's own translator's note on the Genesis passage puts the load-bearing logic in one line: life resides in the blood, “so the blood” — not the meat — “carries the restriction.” Jehovah's Witnesses read a New Testament passage as extending that Levitical food law into a standing rule for Christians: the letter the Jerusalem council sends to non-Jewish converts, instructing them to keep “abstain[ing] from food sacrificed to idols, and from blood, and from what is strangled, and from sexual immorality” (Acts 15:29). That chapter's own translator's note flags something worth sitting with, too: the decree states this twice, nine verses apart, in two different orders and with two different Greek words for “sacrificed to idols” — and the earliest “Western” manuscript tradition (Codex Bezae) drops “what is strangled” from the list entirely and adds a version of the Golden Rule in its place, under which “blood” more plausibly means murder than diet. Every version on the shelf this project checked keeps the four-item list quoted above; the Western alternative is real and old, reported rather than adopted.

Where the doctrine goes further than the plain words is the word eat. Genesis and Leviticus are dietary law, addressed to what enters the mouth. Extending “you must not eat” to cover a transfusion — blood entering the body through a vein rather than a fork — is an interpretive step the text itself doesn't take explicitly, and it's the one every other reading of these verses, Jewish and Christian alike, has historically stopped short of. That's the crux of the disagreement, on both sides of it, and this page doesn't referee it — it just wants you holding the actual verses, not a paraphrase of them, while you decide what you think.

Eighty-one years, mostly moving one direction

The doctrine is much younger than most people assume, and it did not arrive fully formed. Before 1945, Watch Tower publications treated the Acts decree as a minor point for early Gentile converts and said nothing against transfusion.[3] That changed in December 1944, when The Watchtower first applied Genesis 9 and Leviticus 17 to transfusion directly; by mid-1945 the ban on whole blood was total. In January 1961 the stakes rose sharply — accepting a transfusion became grounds for disfellowshipping, the community's most severe discipline, and for a period even blood fractions were included in the ban.[3] Almost everything since then has run the other way. Fraction-based vaccines were left to conscience by late 1961. Dialysis and heart-lung bypass machines were approved in 1978, bone-marrow transplants in 1984, and in 1989 a Witness could accept hemodilution and a cell-saver machine — blood briefly routed outside the body and straight back in — provided the loop was never broken. Individual plasma fractions such as albumin and clotting factors became conscience matters through the 1990s, and by 2000 the position had crystallized into the one that held for a quarter century: no whole blood, no unfractionated red cells, white cells, platelets, or plasma — but any fraction drawn from any of those was each Witness's own call.[3] September 18, 2026 removed the middle of that sentence. The four primary components themselves, not just what can be split out of them, are now conscience matters too. Only whole blood is left standing where the doctrine started.

That history has a genuine human cost attached to it, and this page isn't going to describe forty-five years of a firm medical-refusal rule without naming it once: people have died after refusing a transfusion under the stricter version of this policy, including children whose parents' decisions were, in several countries, overruled by courts.[4] Whatever a reader makes of the doctrine itself, that's part of its real record, not a footnote to skip past.

The part this community is less often given credit for

The same refusal that cost lives also forced a real medical problem to get solved: how do you safely operate on someone who, for whatever reason, cannot or will not receive a transfusion? Cardiac surgeon Denton Cooley took that question seriously earlier than almost anyone — by the late 1970s he and a colleague had published a series of more than 500 open-heart operations performed on Jehovah's Witness patients without transfusion, at a time when that was considered close to reckless.[5] Sustained demand from Witness patients, organized through the Hospital Liaison Committee network since 1988, is a documented driver behind what is now a recognized medical field — Patient Blood Management — and roughly 200 hospitals today run dedicated bloodless-medicine programs covering open-heart surgery, transplants, and total joint replacement.[6] A 2012 study in JAMA Internal Medicine, comparing more than 300 bloodless-surgery patients (most of them Witnesses) against matched controls who received transfusions, found no increased risk of surgical complications and reported five-year survival of 86% in the bloodless group versus 74% among those transfused — a finding about that specific matched comparison, not a general claim that avoiding blood is safer for every patient in every circumstance.[7] A 2004 medical-education journal went further, predicting flatly that techniques refined for Witness patients would become standard surgical practice for everyone.[8] Whatever you make of the theology, the blood-conservation techniques it forced into existence outlived the narrower reason they were first built for.

What actually holds after September 18

  • Unchanged: whole-blood transfusion is still refused, on the same two Old Testament texts this doctrine has always cited.
  • Changed: red cells, white cells, plasma, and platelets — both receiving and donating them — are now each Witness's own decision, not a congregational one.
  • Also changed, March 2026: a patient's own blood may now be stored before surgery, not only cycled through the room during it.
  • Not new: fractions drawn from any blood component (albumin, immunoglobulins, clotting factors, and more) have been conscience matters since 2000.

This page doesn't have a verdict to hand you, on the doctrine or on the medicine, and it wouldn't be this library's place to hand you one if it did. What it can do is put the actual verses, the actual quotes, and the actual history in one place — sourced, not paraphrased — and let you take it from there. This is a record, not medical or legal advice; talk to a doctor about a doctor's decision.
