What Matthew 24 actually lists as signs, how Christians have read the timing for two thousand years, and the one thing Jesus himself keeps saying about trying to answer this

A reader asked

“With everything going on in the world right now, are we already living in the Great Tribulation Jesus talked about — or is it still to come? How would we even know?”

The short answer. You are not the first to ask this. It is, almost word for word, the question his own disciples asked him directly: “Tell us, when will these things be? And what is the sign?” (Matthew 24:3). And his very first word back is not an answer — it is a warning: “See that no one leads you astray” (v4). That sets the shape of everything below. This page lays out what the discourse actually names as signs, the main ways serious readers have understood when they apply, and what Jesus himself says about the project of reading your own moment as the decisive one. It does not tell you the answer is yes, and it does not tell you the answer is no — the same rule as everywhere else in this library.

One question, or two? The seam the whole chapter turns on

The disciples' question already has a fork built into it. In Greek, its second and third parts — “what is the sign of your presence” and “of the completion of the age” — share a single article, which is a real grammatical hint that they took the temple's fall (which he had just predicted, v2) and the end of the age to be one and the same event. Whether Jesus answers on their assumption or corrects it is, in this library's own words, “the difficulty the whole chapter inherits” (note on 24:1–3). The readings on offer: the whole discourse is about Jerusalem's fall in AD 70; it is about a still-future end; it moves from one to the other partway through (candidates: v15, v29, v36); or it holds both together on purpose, a near horizon and a far one described in one prophetic sweep — which is a recognized pattern elsewhere in the prophets, not a translator's invention. This page does not choose between them either; it is the same seam the chapter's own note flags, carried forward.

What the discourse lists as signs — and its own warning about listing them

The first stretch of the answer (vv4–14) is a list almost anyone would recognize: false messiahs, wars and rumours of wars, famines and earthquakes, persecution, betrayal, false prophets, lawlessness multiplying, love growing cold, and the good news reaching “the whole inhabited earth” before “the end” arrives. And this library's own note on that stretch says the load-bearing thing plainly: “Everything in this paragraph is something a reader in any century can point at, which is why it has been pointed at in every century; the passage's own use of it is to forbid the inference” (note on 24:4–14). Twice in this stretch alone the text says not yet — “the end is not yet” (v6), “the beginning of birth pangs” (v8) — labour pain being, on purpose, a pain that is productive and not itself the birth.

What follows gets sharper. “The abomination that desolates” standing in a holy place (v15) quotes Daniel 11:31 and 12:11 — a phrase that had already happened once, in 167 BC, when Antiochus IV set up an altar to Zeus in the temple, which is part of why “let the reader understand” is inserted right there. Then flight instructions so concrete and short-range (do not go back for a cloak; pray it isn't winter or a Sabbath) that they read as advice for an actual siege, not the end of the world. Then, at v21, the phrase this series has already looked at once: great tribulation, “such as has not happened from the beginning of the world until now, nor ever will” — the same phrase, word for word, as Revelation 7:14's “the great tribulation.” More false messiahs (vv23–26), with the same instruction as before — “do not believe it” — and a comparison to lightning (v27): visible everywhere at once, which is the point; nobody has to be told where it happened. Then the sun darkened, the moon without light, the stars falling (v29) — stock Old Testament language for the fall of cities and empires (Isaiah 13:10, 34:4; Joel 2:10), which by this library's own reading “does not by itself require a cosmic end” even though “it was always used of something enormous” (note on 24:29–31) — and finally the Son of Man gathering “his chosen from the four winds” (vv30–31).

The hinge: “this generation”

Whatever else is unresolved, one sentence decides more than any other: “This generation will not pass away until all these things happen” (v34). This library calls it “the hardest sentence in the chapter” and reports four readings without choosing: (1) it means his actual contemporaries, and “all these things” is the temple's fall within about forty years — which fits the word genea and the chapter's Judean detail, at the cost of reading vv29–31 as prophetic language for that event rather than something literally cosmic; (2) genea means “race” or “kind” — possible, but a strained sense Matthew doesn't use elsewhere; (3) it means the generation alive when the final signs actually begin, “this” pointing forward rather than at the crowd in front of him; (4) it is a prediction whose terms were not met as stated, which some readers hold and others regard as impossible for these words to mean (note on 24:32–35). C. S. Lewis called the parallel saying in Mark “the most embarrassing verse in the Bible” — and pointed out that the admission of ignorance in the very next verses (see below) sits right beside it, preserved by copyists who could plainly see the tension and left it alone anyway.

Four ways this has been read across church history

Zoom out from this one chapter and the same seam runs through the whole history of reading it. None of these is a fringe position; all four have been held by serious, careful readers, and this library sets them out with no vote cast.

1. Preterist — mostly or entirely fulfilled already

The discourse (or most of it) describes the Jewish-Roman war and Jerusalem's fall in AD 70 — an actual catastrophe within the lifetime of people Jesus was speaking to, matching “this generation” read as reading (1) above, and matching the flight instructions' concrete, local character. Cost: vv29–31's cosmic imagery has to be read figuratively (which the shelf's own OT background supports, see above), and Revelation 7:14's identical phrase becomes a separate vision, not literally the same event.

2. Futurist — still ahead, a distinct end-times event

The discourse (in whole, or from v15 or v29 on) describes a still-future, unprecedented global tribulation immediately before a visible return — anchored on v21's own claim that nothing before or after will match it, a claim later history arguably tests hard against AD 70 alone. Reads “this generation” as (3) or (4) above, and reads Revelation 7:14 as depicting the very same, still-future event named here. This is the most widely held reading in contemporary evangelical circles, and it's the reading closest to the question this page opened with.

3. Historicist — unfolding across the whole church age

The tribulation, and apocalyptic language generally, describes a pattern playing out progressively through church history — a common Reformation-era reading, when Reformers frequently read persecution under Rome and later powers this way. Less commonly held today, but historically significant, and part of why the Reformation-era shelf (Geneva 1599 among the versions here) sometimes carries interpretive freight modern editions don't.

4. Idealist — a recurring pattern, not one pinpointable event

The passage describes something true of every age — every generation gets its wars, its false messiahs, its persecutions — so “the great tribulation” names a pattern the church is, in some sense, always living inside, rather than a single date on a calendar.

What the text itself says about answering this for yourself

This is the part that's easy to skip past, and it shouldn't be. Set beside every sign in the list above is an instruction not to treat it as decisive:

  • His first sentence, before a single sign is named: “See that no one leads you astray” (v4).
  • Twice more, of specific claims to have located it: “do not believe it” (vv23, 26) — and the lightning comparison right after (v27) makes the point explicit: the true thing needs no one to announce where it happened.
  • The flattest statement in the whole discourse: “About that day and hour nobody knows — not the angels of the heavens, nor the Son — but the Father only” (v36) — on the best manuscript evidence, including “nor the Son,” a clause a scribe would have every reason to remove and none to add (note on 24:36–41).
  • The comparison to Noah that follows (v37) is aimed at obliviousness, not wickedness — people “eating and drinking, marrying and giving in marriage,” ordinary life, with the only fault named being that “they did not know.” The text's own comparison isn't “look how bad things have gotten,” it's “look how ordinary the day looked right up until it wasn't.”
  • And the same answer, given flatly to the same disciples on a different day: “It is not for you to know times or seasons that the Father has set within his own authority” (Acts 1:7).

What history shows about answering it anyway

This isn't a hypothetical risk. Confident, dated claims that a particular generation's crisis was the great tribulation, or that the end had arrived within a specific, named window, have recurred across nearly the whole span of church history — the second-century Montanist movement's expectation of an imminent end; a cluster of apocalyptic movements around the year 1000; the English Civil War's Fifth Monarchy movement, reading current politics as the last days; the Millerite movement's calculation of 1844, remembered today as “the Great Disappointment” when the date passed; and, in the twentieth century, several named-and-missed dates across more than one modern movement. This is a documented pattern, not a judgment on anyone's sincerity, and it cuts in a specific direction: it is evidence that the impulse this page is describing — matching a real, frightening moment against the signs and concluding this is it — has been strong, recurring, and, every time it has attached to a specific date, wrong so far. That doesn't prove the tribulation isn't near; nobody can prove a negative about the future. It is, however, exactly the caution the text itself gives, arriving from an entirely different direction.

So — are we in it?

Here is what the chapter actually hands you, and what it doesn't. It hands you a real pattern — wars, disasters, false messiahs — present in literally every era, including the first-century one Jesus was addressing. It hands you a strong historical candidate for at least part of it, an actual catastrophe within a named generation's lifetime, if you read “this generation” one way; and a still-future, distinct event if you read it another. And it hands you, twice over, in language too plain to soften, an instruction not to treat certainty about the calendar as the goal. What it never hands you is permission to treat a confident yes, now or no, not yet as itself a mark of understanding it correctly. The mark of faithfulness this chapter actually names, in the parables that immediately follow (the faithful and sensible slave, the ten virgins next chapter), is different: stay awake, and be doing what you're supposed to be doing — whether the day turns out to be today or not. That's what the text gives. The weighing of the rest is, as always here, yours.
