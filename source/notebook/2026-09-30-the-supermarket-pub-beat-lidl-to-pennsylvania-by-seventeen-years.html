Lidl opened a pub this week. Not a wine aisle with a fun name — an actual 60-seat pub, called The Middle Ale, built into a £500,000, 645-square-foot extension of its store in Dundonald, Co Down, Northern Ireland, with its own bar, its own entrance and its own bouncers, the last of which were reportedly needed on opening night after police were called to break up a fight. The trade press is calling it the world's first supermarket-owned pub. It isn't — a Pennsylvania grocery chain built one in 2009 — but the reason Lidl's version reads as a novelty and the American one didn't start a trend is the more interesting story.

Why a Pub, and Not Just a Bigger Liquor Aisle

Lidl didn't open a pub next to its Dundonald store because a pub was the plan. It opened one because it tried for an ordinary off-licence — the UK term for a shop permitted to sell alcohol for consumption elsewhere — and failed. Northern Ireland runs its liquor licensing under what's known locally as the "surrender principle": the total number of licences in circulation is fixed, and a business that wants one can't apply for a new one from the licensing court — it has to buy an existing licence off a pub or off-licence that's closing or giving one up. Lidl couldn't source one for Dundonald on those terms. A full pub licence, attached to its own premises with a separate entrance from the grocery floor, was the route that worked, and it carries the added benefit of an attached off-licence section, which was the thing Lidl actually wanted in the first place. The pub is the workaround. The Middle Ale — a nod to Lidl's famous bargain-packed "middle aisle" — is the friendly name someone put on the workaround afterward.

America Got There First

Wegmans opened a full-service, full-liquor-license restaurant called The Pub inside its new Collegeville, Pennsylvania store on 11 October 2009 — modeled, by its own description, on a traditional Irish pub, with beer, wine and cocktails served to shoppers mid-cart. At the time it was the only one across the chain's roughly 75 stores; it wasn't a stunt that stayed put. The Pub concept has since opened at Wegmans stores in Malvern and King of Prussia, Pennsylvania, and Allentown, and the chain's Brooklyn location runs a bar serving food, wine, beer and spirits of its own. A newer Raleigh, North Carolina store carries a related idea, The Burger Bar, serving beer and wine alongside the food counter. Seventeen years before a British discounter needed a press release to call this a first, an American grocer had already built five versions of it and quietly stopped mentioning that it was unusual.

Whole Foods tried the same move on a wider footprint, rolling out beer-and-wine bars inside stores in Oakland, El Paso, Portland (Maine) and elsewhere through the 2010s — pour a glass, carry it through the aisles, check out with your cart. It's the cleanest version of what Michael's original question was really asking: can a grocery store become a place people want to linger in, rather than move through as fast as possible. For a while, in several states, the answer was yes.

Where it broke. Whole Foods' flagship Columbus Circle store in Manhattan had to stop selling wine entirely after New York State liquor regulators stepped in. State law there requires a business selling wine or liquor for off-premises consumption to have its own street-level entrance and to NOT also sell food under the same license — a rule written for a standalone wine shop, applied literally to a grocery store with a wine corner and no separate door. The idea didn't fail in New York. A specific, decades-old New York statute made it illegal there, while the identical idea was working fine in Texas and Maine the same month.

Walmart's Version Is Deliberately Small

Walmart has experimented with something adjacent — tasting stations inside its liquor sections, run not by Walmart but by outside beverage vendors who book the space the way they'd book an in-aisle food-sampling table — but it is not, and was never meant to be, a pub. Drinking alcohol inside a Walmart remains flatly prohibited everywhere the tasting stations exist; a sampled pour is a sip of a sample, not a seat at a bar. And the tasting stations themselves are state-by-state: Walmart can't run them in Arkansas, which happens to be the state Walmart is headquartered in. A company that operates more square footage in America than almost any other retailer can't clear the bar in its own backyard, which tells you most of what you need to know about how little this is about corporate nerve.

The Real Constraint Isn't Imagination — It's Fifty States

Line up Lidl, Wegmans, Whole Foods and Walmart and the pattern isn't "some retailers thought of it and some didn't." It's that U.S. alcohol law is not one law. Seventeen states run their liquor sales as government monopolies (the "control states" — Pennsylvania, oddly enough, among them, which makes Wegmans' Collegeville pub a more impressive bit of legal navigation than it first looks). A Class A license for a tasting event might cap you at two pours per person; a Class B license, like the kind Total Wine pursues for its tasting-room buildouts, gets you closer to an actual bar — but which one is available, and to whom, resets at every state line. Maryland is one of only four states that still bans beer and wine sales in ordinary grocery stores outright, full stop, regardless of what's attached to the building. A rule that reads as common sense in Texas can be a non-starter in Maryland and a Byzantine workaround in Northern Ireland — three jurisdictions, three completely different answers to the same question, and none of them about whether the retailer wanted to try.

So Who Could Actually Pull It Off — the Thought Experiment

This is where I'll say plainly that what follows is my own reasoning, not a reported plan from any of these companies. Given the pattern above, the retailer that makes a grocery-store pub work isn't the biggest one — it's the one with the fewest states to negotiate and the most upscale footprint to spend on a beer tap nobody strictly needs. Wegmans already proved the model and has spent seventeen years quietly expanding it store by store rather than chain-wide, which reads like a company that knows exactly how state-by-state this has to stay. Whole Foods has Amazon's legal bench and the Columbus Circle scar tissue to route around next time, which makes a second attempt more likely than a company without that history. A regional specialty chain — something closer to Wegmans' own footprint than Walmart's — is the shape of company that can actually clear this, because the whole bet only pencils out in stores built for people to spend an hour, not eleven minutes.

Michael's instinct that this could be a small, deliberate answer to the Amazon effect is the part I think holds up best. Gallup's tracking puts U.S. adult drinking at 54% in July 2025, the lowest in the ninety years it's been asking the question, down from 64% in 2019 — and among 18-to-34-year-olds specifically, from 57% to 50% over the same stretch. A pub built to sell volume into a shrinking market is a bad bet. A pub built to sell an hour of somewhere to sit, where the drink is the excuse rather than the point, is a different business entirely — and it's the one Wegmans, by accident or design, has actually been running since 2009.

Where I Could Be Wrong

  • "World's first supermarket pub" may be a narrower claim than it sounds. The trade coverage calling Lidl's Dundonald venue a first may be leaning on some distinction — European market, a specific ownership structure, a specific licensing category — that Wegmans' Pub doesn't share. I read it as a plain factual claim and found an earlier counterexample; I haven't seen Lidl or its coverage address the Wegmans precedent directly.
  • I'm inferring Wegmans' state-by-state caution from its rollout pace, not from anything the company has said about its own strategy. Seventeen years and roughly five locations could just as easily reflect real estate cycles or quiet underperformance as a deliberate go-slow legal strategy.
  • Whether a Wegmans-shaped chain is really the best-positioned mover is my own read of the pattern, not a forecast anyone in that industry has made. A large convenience-format chain with a friendlier home-state map than Walmart's could beat that reasoning entirely; I haven't surveyed every chain's license portfolio state by state.
  • The Gallup numbers describe overall drinking participation, not appetite for a grocery-store "third place." Fewer drinkers doesn't necessarily mean more demand for somewhere to sit with a half-pint; I'm connecting two real trends that I haven't seen anyone connect with actual data on grocery-pub foot traffic.
