I'm skipping ahead. The rule of this series is one record per entry in the order they reached me, and by that rule the next entry should be Frontiers, from 1983. It will get its turn. But I said in the opener that my favorite Journey album of all is Raised on Radio, that this is not the popular answer, and that it would take an entry to defend — and I'd rather make the case while it's fresh than keep a reader waiting for it. So: the record first, then the defense.

The Record

Raised on Radio came out on 21 April 1986, and it is, in nearly every telling of the band's history, the difficult one. Steve Perry produced it himself — the only Journey album he did — and before the sessions started, founding bassist Ross Valory was let go, and drummer Steve Smith ended up playing on very little of it. In their places were session musicians: Randy Jackson on bass, a decade and a half before he became the man behind the desk on American Idol, and Larrie Londin, the Nashville drummer who'd played behind Elvis, on most of the tracks. Journey was, for the length of this record, effectively a trio — Perry, Neal Schon and Jonathan Cain — with hired hands, and the band's own histories treat it that way: Perry has said since that if he could do it over he'd have done it differently.

It was recorded between October and December 1985 at the Record Plant in Sausalito and Fantasy Studios in Berkeley, and it was made under a weight the record doesn't advertise: Perry's mother was dying in Hanford during the sessions, and he was driving back and forth between the studio and her bedside. She died that December. The cover, by Prairie Prince and Michael Cotten, is an illustration of a little radio station standing alone in a flat landscape — it's KNGS, the Hanford station his parents had owned, the radio he was raised on. That's what the title means.

Commercially it did fine and no more: number four on the Billboard album chart, double platinum, four singles in the top twenty — "Be Good to Yourself" (number nine), "I'll Be Alright Without You" (fourteen), "Suzanne" and "Girl Can't Help It" (both seventeen). The tour that followed, with Mike Baird on drums, ran from the summer of 1986 to the winter of 1987 and ended in Alaska in February — and that was the end. Perry went home. The band stopped. There wouldn't be another Journey record for ten years, and by the time there was, this one had settled into its place in the story as the album where it all came apart.

That's the record's reputation. Now here's mine.

The Defense

I was fourteen when it came out, going on fifteen, and I want to be careful to describe what I actually heard then rather than what I've read since, because the two are almost opposites. What the histories hear in Raised on Radio is a band coming apart. What I heard was a band arriving somewhere.

It was mostly Perry. I could tell that even then — it's his voice out front of everything, more than on any record before it — and I was a Perry fan, so that was no complaint. But the thing that made it different, the thing I've never heard anyone else say about it, was the sound. It wasn't just guitar. It had a certain high-tech, extremely beautiful mixing of the human and the synthesizer — of Perry's voice, which is the most human instrument I know, set against the new, clean, modern sounds that had only just become possible. In 1986 those sounds were the future. The digital synthesizers had arrived in 1983 and by the middle of the decade they were on everything on the radio, and most of it was cold. This wasn't. Somebody had figured out how to put that glassy modern texture under a singer who sang like he meant it, and the result, to a fourteen-year-old who'd grown up on the earlier records, sounded like something beyond normal music. It was Journey, but Journey from a few years ahead.

And it was moving. I can't separate that from the sound; the sound is why it moved. "The Eyes of a Woman" is the one that comes to mind first — a slow one, all atmosphere, and Perry holding notes over the top of it in a way that, at fourteen, I didn't know a person could do. That's the other half of the defense: his ability to carry a note, and his range, together with those new-sounding modern sounds. Either one alone would have been a good record. The two together are the reason this is my favorite.

Then there's "Girl Can't Help It," which is the opposite kind of song and the one I love to drive to, and have since the day I had a license, just because of the beat. That's not a critical judgment and I'm not going to pretend it is. Some songs are for thinking about and some are for the interstate, and this is the second kind, and it has never once failed at the job.

Why It's Not the Popular Answer

I understand why it isn't. Escape has the hits. Frontiers has "Faithfully" and "Separate Ways." Raised on Radio has the firings and the session players and the breakup at the end of the tour, and if you come to it through the history first, that's what you hear. But I didn't come to it through the history. I came to it at fourteen, off the radio, the way I'd come to Escape at ten, and what I heard was the most beautiful version of the thing I already loved. I've had a long time to change my mind, and I haven't. It made an impression on this young man, and the young man is fifty-five now and still driving to it.

If Raised on Radio is your favorite too, I'd like to know I'm not alone. If it isn't, I'd like to hear which one is and why. The address is below. Frontiers next, properly, in order.
