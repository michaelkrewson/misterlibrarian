title: The Tree Thief of Breuckelen date: 2026-09-11 part: flags dateline: Gowanus · a day of prayer, March 1662 people: Garret Dircksen Croesen (c.1639–1680), Neeltje Jans (c.1640–c.1690), Jan Pietersen Van Husum (c.1606–c.1675), Henricus Selyns (1636–1701), Femmetje Nyssens summary: In March 1662 a twenty-two-year-old cooper from Winschoten went into the woods on a day of prayer and came home with some fruit trees. Within weeks the village of Breuckelen was calling Garret Dircksen Croesen a tree-thief, his minister had barred him from communion, and Garret had done the one thing nobody did in 1662 — he had the minister summoned to court. The consistory record of what followed runs ten pages, and it is the first time the family's founder speaks. meta_desc: Garret Dircksen Croesen, cooper from Winschoten, and the 1662 tree-thief affair at Breuckelen — he sued his minister; the church wrote it down. Ch. 2. ---

Some time in the middle of March 1662, towards evening, on a day the church had set aside for prayer, a young man walked out of the village of Breuckelen into the woods and came back with trees. Fruit trees, the record is careful to say — "not wild trees but fruit trees" [1]. How many, he never said. Where exactly he had found them, he never said either. He was about twenty-two years old, he had been married five months, and his wife was expecting their first child.

That is the whole of the offence. Everything that follows — the rumours, the pastoral visit, the summons, the letter to the magistrates, ten pages in a church book that had never before spent ten pages on anything — grew out of a few saplings dug up on the wrong day. And it is the first time in four centuries of records that the man this family is named for opens his mouth and says something we can still read.

Gerrit Dircksen Croesen, cooper, from Winschoten

He came from Winschoten, in the province of Groningen, in the far north-east of the Netherlands, close to the German border. That much he said himself, when he transferred his church membership to Breuckelen in 1661: "Gerrit Dirckzen Crousen, from Winschoten" [2]. Nothing else about his life before America survives — not his parents' names, not his ship, not the year he sailed. The town he left was old and had been badly used: walled in 1593 during the war against Spain, taken and burned by the Spaniards in 1624 with its weaving mill and the monastery down the road, and visited by war and plague more than once after that. The church on the market square, built about 1300, is still standing; a cousin photographed it in 1995 [3]. If he was born around 1639, as the family's researchers reckon from his later ages, he grew up in a town still rebuilding from the fire. He left in time. In 1665, a few years after he had gone, "almost the whole population was wiped out by war and pestilence," and "the corpses stayed in the streets for days for lack of coffins" [3].

There is a story about the ship. A family researcher in Pennsylvania, Marie Metzgar, had heard that a passenger on a vessel called the Draetvat — the "Wire-Cask" — kept a journal of the crossing in which he mentioned talking with two other passengers, Garret Dircksen Croesen and his brother. When the Holland Society was asked, it found a Draetvat that sailed on 2 April 1657 under Captain Jan Jansen Bestevaer, carrying, among others, "Marcus De Chousoy, wife, two workmen, and two boys, over twelve years of age." Nobody has ever found the journal.

Cruise (1998), pp. 44–45, who calls it "all conjecture" and says so twice. If Garret was born about 1640 he would have been seventeen in 1657, which fits "two boys over twelve" — and fits nothing else, because there is nothing else. It is recorded here as what it is: a story a researcher heard, with a real manifest underneath it and no proof on top.

What is not conjecture is that by the spring of 1661 he was already in America, already working, and already in the records — because of wine. On 1 May 1661 a notary in New Amsterdam named Salomon Lachaire was trying to collect on eight hogsheads of French wine, some 440 gallons, that a minister named Welius had been selling on the side before he inconveniently died. The wine had been passed to a man named Van Gesel, who had sold it "in part at auction and in part by the anker and by the can at retail," and had not paid for it. The notary went round the witnesses, and one of them told him "that Gerrit Creuse, Cooper, filled up said wines and ever since worked thereat." Then, in the same breath, the notary billed for his afternoon:

I drank with them at the house of Madame Wessels 1 pint of wine on my own account. Comes for my trouble fl. 4. Further at the request of Lieve Ottens, I went with him and the said Van Nas to Breuckelen and there drew up an affidavit of the above named Croesen.

Register of Salomon Lachaire, Notary Public, 1 May 1661 — Scott & Stryker-Rodda (eds.), New York Historical Manuscripts: Dutch, p. 40, as transcribed in Cruise, pp. 48–49 [4].

That is the first sighting. It tells us three things the family's genealogist was glad to have: that Garret was a cooper — a maker of barrels and casks, the containers everything liquid moved in, a real trade in a port town; that he was living at Gowanus, the hamlet on the shore a few miles south-west of Breuckelen village; and that he had been there long enough to have made the casks the wine went into, which puts his arrival some time before the 1660 the family's tradition gives, and possibly well before [4]. A cooper who "filled up" the wine had a customer base. He was not fresh off the boat.

The rest of 1661 is a young man settling in, and it happens fast. On 29 May he joined the Breuckelen church by transfer from Winschoten, with two witnesses to vouch for him, Teunis Nyssen and Jan Martijn [2]. On 2 October he stood witness himself, for a man named Jan Clerck "from Brazil" making his confession of faith — the Dutch had lost their Brazilian colony to the Portuguese a few years earlier and the refugees were still arriving [5]. And on 30 October 1661 he married.

1661, October 30: Gerrit Dirckzen Crousen, to Neeltie Jans, "from Nieuw Nederlant"; witnesses: Jans Pietersz, father of the bride, Jeurie Janszen, on the side of the bridegroom.

Old First Dutch Reformed Church of Brooklyn, First Book of Records, 1660–1752 (van der Linde, ed.), p. 212, as transcribed in Cruise, pp. 50–51 [6].

Neeltje Jans was "from New Netherland" — born in the colony, in Manhattan, around 1640, a full generation before her husband arrived. Her father, Jan Pietersen, stood as her witness. He was not Dutch either; he was from Husum in Schleswig, up near the Danish border, had been in New Amsterdam since about 1638, could not write his name, and had done well enough as a farmer, ship's carpenter and trader to be one of the twenty-five founding members of the Breuckelen church when it was organised in 1660 [7]. Her mother, Elsje, had died when Neeltje was about ten; the children took their mother's family name, Staats, and it is under that name that her brothers appear in the records from here on. She will have her own chapter. What matters for this one is that the cooper from Winschoten had married into the most established family in a village of about two or three hundred people, and that within the year he had a patent for twenty-eight acres at Gowanus, with a house on it, between two neighbours named Volkert Bries and Jacob Brouwer [8]. The family's genealogist thought it "probable" that the father-in-law "had some part in this early rise," and nothing in the record argues with him.

What the twenty-eight acres looked like

Nothing in this section is a document about Garret. It is what the colony's own describers wrote about places like his and men like him, and it is here so that the record has ground under it. Gowanus was not a village so much as a shore: a tidal creek winding up from the bay through salt marsh into low wooded hills, with a scatter of farms along the higher ground and, from about 1661, a tide-mill — Adam Brouwer's — where the creek was dammed [20]. Two Dutch travellers who walked through it in September 1679 were fed oysters roasted on the coals by a farmer there, "the largest and best oysters I have ever seen, some of them a foot long," and found the trees "so laden with peaches that the branches broke under the weight," the fruit lying on the ground for the hogs [21]. That is the fruit country a young man was setting himself up in.

The fruit trees of New Netherland were European stock. Adriaen van der Donck, describing the colony in 1655, lists apples, pears, cherries, plums, quinces and above all peaches, "which grow so plentifully that the hogs are fed on them," all raised from pits and grafts the settlers had carried over, because the country's own trees — the wild plum, the crab, the mulberry — were not what a Dutchman meant by an orchard [22]. So when the consistory pressed Garret on whether the trees were "wild trees" or "fruit trees," and he conceded fruit trees "from the woods," the question was sharper than it sounds. Wild trees belonged to nobody. Fruit trees at the edge of the woods were somebody's young orchard, set out from grafts in the loose ground where the clearing stopped, and everyone in a village of three hundred knew whose. The likeliest reading of his evasions — "not before he was absolutely sure," "did not know how many" — is that he knew exactly, and so did Femmetje.

As for what he wore to dig them: a working Dutchman of 1662 dressed in linen and wool — a long linen shirt, loose woollen knee-breeches, a sleeved jerkin or doublet over the shirt, woollen stockings, and leather shoes with a strap or buckle; wooden shoes were a thing of the old country's wet fields and appear in almost no colonial inventory [23]. Over it, in March, a coat of coarse wool, and on his head a felt hat with a brim — the hat that a Dutchman took off in church, to a magistrate, and to a flag he acknowledged, and that a boy in Breuckelen would refuse to take off to an English one two years later. Neeltje, indoors and expecting, would have been in a linen cap, a laced bodice, a woollen petticoat and an apron, the clothes of every woman in a Dutch genre painting of the decade, which is where most of what we know about them comes from [23]. The house behind them was almost certainly wood — a frame house of a room or two with a steep roof and a chimney at one end, the Dutch stone-and-tile houses of Manhattan and New Utrecht being for fiscals, not coopers [24]. Somewhere near it stood a cooperage: a shed, a fire for heating the staves, a stack of oak and a stack of hoops. And somewhere behind it, in fresh-turned March ground, a row of saplings that were the talk of the village.

Then, in December, he was a witness at the baptism of his wife's niece. He was twenty-two, married, landed, and in good standing with his church. He had been in the records for seven months. Three months later he went into the woods.

"Notorious and public knowledge even among the children"

The rumour started with a woman named Femmetje Nyssens. Her husband was Teunis Nyssen — the same Teunis Nyssen who had stood witness for Garret at the church ten months before, and with whom Garret was now, the record says, having "private quarrels" [9]. What the quarrel was about is not written down. What Femmetje said is: she called him a tree-thief, and she said it, Garret would later complain, "in the presence of the full meeting" [1]. The consistory's own description of how far it travelled is the best sentence in the file. The rumours, it wrote, "were notorious and public knowledge even among the children" [10].

Breuckelen in 1662 had a new minister, and he was not a man to let a rumour run. Henricus Selyns had arrived from the Netherlands two years earlier, chosen — as the last chapter told — by a committee of two, the Burgomaster Martin Cregier and the Fiscal Nicasius De Sille, and approved by the Classis of Amsterdam in February 1660. He was in his mid-twenties, educated, exact, and about to be the keeper of one of the most complete church record books in colonial America. Some weeks before the Lord's Supper was to be celebrated, he and an elder, Wilhelmus Bredenbend, went round the congregation on the customary pastoral visit, inviting members to the Table. They came to the house at Gowanus. And here the record, for once, is gentle:

…not inside the house where he would have been embarrassed in the presence of his wife, but after we had gone outside, so that he was told in the most suitable and proper way about the rumours which are notorious and public knowledge even among the children.

Letter of the Breuckelen consistory to the Schouts and Schepens of Breuckelen, 5 April 1662 — First Book of Records, pp. 37–47, as transcribed in Cruise, pp. 59–60 [10].

Outside, away from Neeltje, the minister and the elder told him what the village was saying, and told him that they could not invite him to the Lord's Supper "unless there would be a Christian and penitent disclosure of what was already being said behind his back" [10]. If it was true, he should repent of it. If it was a lie, let it be a warning. Either way, he was barred from communion until it was cleared up.

Garret's answer is the reason this chapter exists. He did not go to Femmetje. He did not go to the consistory. He went to the court-messenger of Breuckelen and had Dominie Henricus Selyns, his minister, cited to appear before the Lower Court of Breuckelen at its next session, to answer for the rumours. And he did it on the Sabbath.

The consistory, 2 and 5 April 1662

The church's own account opens with the sound of a body of men who cannot believe what has been done to them:

Furthermore, it was brought to the attention of the Hon. Consistory, to their regret and disdain, that Gerrit Dirckzen Croesen van Wynschooten, a member of our congregation, has dared, with extreme wickedness and impudence (in violation of all Ordinances of the Church and customary procedures, without any right or justification, and at an inappropriate time, abusing even the Sabbath for the impudent scheme, a day reserved for the divine worship and for abstinence in preparation of the imminent Lord's Supper), to cite, and to have cited by the court-messenger, the person of Henricus Selyns, his lawful minister and teacher…

Consistory minutes, 2 April 1662 — First Book of Records, p. 245, as transcribed in Cruise, p. 54 [11].

A modern editor of the record book, in a footnote the family's genealogist reprints, put it more plainly: Garret "became so enraged when Selijns and Bredenbend discreetly questioned him about the rumours" that he "held the minister responsible for the accusations and filed a libel suit against them," and the minister's "indignation at having been cited in a secular court is evident from the fact that the Croesen affair covers no less than ten full pages in the original Protocol" [12]. Nothing else in the book's first ninety years got ten pages.

The consistory did not know what to do, which is itself telling. It voted to send Selyns across the river to consult "the Rev., pious, and very learned father Domine Johannes Megapolensis," the senior minister of the colony, on three questions: whether to summon Garret, whether to censure him, and whether to appear in the court where they had been cited [11]. Megapolensis's advice came back on 5 April: summon him at once; suspend him from the Supper; and write to the magistrates. They sent the court-messenger for Garret — "he will have to appear immediately" — and he came [9].

What follows is an interrogation, and the church wrote down both sides of it. He was asked what justification a member had for citing his minister in court. He answered, the clerk recorded, that "[the citation] was due to his own dullness and that [he] did not know any better, but that he had been advised by wiser people" — and then, warming up, that he had "even gone to Manhattan for that purpose, and had waited for more than half a day to see Henricus Selyns, shouting he would come to the Hon. Lord General's place where he was staying" [9]. The Lord General was Stuyvesant. A twenty-two-year-old cooper had crossed the river, found his minister's lodging at the Director-General's house, and stood outside it for half a day, shouting.

He "persisted in his innocence." He asked that the people accusing him be the ones barred from the Supper, and that Femmetje Nyssens be made to appear before the consistory. He was reminded of something he had reportedly said — "Consider me the offender" — and answered that "this was not based upon the truth but upon rumours." Selyns had told him, at the house, "I do not know, God knows. You can deceive me but not God," and Garret did not deny being told it. Then he lost his temper:

Eventually [he] tried to justify his case with indecent maledictions saying, "If it's true, I'll be damned!" and after he had been punished for that, [he] persisted equally strongly in his afore-mentioned swearing and self-damnation.

Consistory minutes, 5 April 1662 — as transcribed in Cruise, p. 57 [9].

They asked him why he had cited the minister before the Supper, when the court would not even sit until after it. He said "that he had sought his honour, the sooner the better." They asked why he had cited Selyns and not Femmetje, "or others who were talking about it every day." He said nobody had accused him but Selyns — which the clerk notes was untrue, since he had himself complained that Femmetje called him a tree-thief in front of the whole meeting. They asked when he had taken the trees. "In the middle of March," he said, "but that he did not know for sure." They said they knew very well: it was the day of prayers. He said it was towards evening. They asked where in the woods, and how many. He "did not want to tell before he was absolutely sure and that he did not know how many" [1].

He was sent outside while they deliberated, and called back in to hear the verdict. He was suspended from the Lord's Supper on three grounds, each of them about the lawsuit and none of them about the trees: "personal hatred against his lawful minister," "rejection and contempt of the consistory," and "breaking the promises he had made to the consistory while shaking their hands." As for his request that his accusers be barred instead, the answer was that he could first clear himself in the court he had dragged his minister into, "and the consistory will then do what they consider proper and reasonable" [1].

The letter to the magistrates

Then they wrote to the court. The letter, addressed to "the Schouts and Schepens of the village and jurisdiction of Breuckelen" and handed to Schout Adriaen Hegeman, is the part of the affair that historians of the colony have remembered, because it is one of the first times in America that a church told a court to mind its own business. It repeats the whole indictment — the wickedness, the impudence, the Sabbath — with one addition, in parentheses, after Garret's name: "(may God mend his manners!)" [10]. It asks the court to annul the citation, to prevent any like it in future, and "to leave those concerning Church matters to the Church meeting." And then, having said all that, it asks the court to investigate the trees anyway, carefully — "so that, if it is all lies, the aforesaid Crousen will be cleansed and cleared of the rumours about thievery and theft of certain trees," and if it is true, the church will do what its discipline requires. Selyns and Bredenbend signed it on 5 April 1662, "in our Church meeting" [10].

The old Brooklyn historian Stiles summarised the minister's position two centuries later: "the civil courts could not try offences arising purely out of the ecclesiastical relation; and … the complainant having submitted himself to the canons of the church, by becoming one of its members, was thereby precluded from taking the matter before the courts" [13]. The family's genealogist, a twentieth-century man, was unimpressed. "That probably would not work today. Garret was entitled to his day in court, and Femmetje should have had to answer to her part in the charges" [13].

And there the record stops. No verdict from the court survives. No finding about the trees. The consistory minutes move on to other business, and the family's genealogist, who read all of it, concluded that the affair "soon disappeared as everyone was happy to get out of this one" [14]. Whether Garret ever took communion again in Selyns's church is not written down. Selyns himself went back to the Netherlands in July 1664, a month before the English ships came, and did not return to the colony for eighteen years [14].

Eighteen days

What is written down is this. On 23 July 1662, in the Reformed Dutch Church of Breuckelen — the same church, with the same minister — Gerrit Croesen and Neeltje Jans baptized their first child, a son, Dirck. The witnesses were Grietje Jans, Neeltje's stepmother, and a neighbour named Adam Brouwer [15]. The consistory had suspended the father from the Table fifteen weeks earlier. It baptized his son anyway. The church's discipline was about the Supper, not the font, and a Dutch church in 1662 did not visit a father's contempt on his child.

Across the river, in the church inside the fort, another child had been baptized eighteen days before, on 5 July, with the Burgomaster and the Fiscal standing as her witnesses. Nobody at either font could have known it, but the boy and the girl would marry each other in this same Breuckelen church twenty-two years later, and the family whose name is on this book descends from the pair of them. Two baptisms, eighteen days and eight miles apart: one at the top of the colony, one in a village that was calling the father a thief.

Adam Brouwer, the witness, should be remembered too. He was an old friend of Neeltje's father, a fellow founder of the church, a former soldier of the Company in Brazil, and, in the genealogist's careful phrase, "fractious and troublesome" — and that, he adds, is being kind to him [16]. Seven years after he stood at this font, he and Garret would be in court against each other, and ten years after that his son would raid Garret's grape arbour and Adam would say, in front of a witness, that if he had been there Gerrit "should not have come off alive." That is the next chapter.

For now the cooper had his trees, wherever they came from, and his son, and his twenty-eight acres. In April 1664, with Selyns still in the pulpit, Neeltje joined the church herself, with Garret as one of her witnesses and the chief magistrate of Breuckelen as the other [17]. In 1670 a lawyer named John Sharp sued Garret for seventy-two guilders in unpaid fees and the court told him to pay up [18]. In 1675 the tax roll has him at Breuckelen with "1 poll, 2 oxen, 4 cows," a run of younger cattle, three hogs, and "14 morgens land and valley" — the twenty-eight acres, in Dutch measure — assessed at £99 10s [19]. He never learned to write. He was, the record shows, quick to feel his honour touched and quick to go to law about it, and he did not much care who was on the other side. The family's own genealogist, four generations on, would tell Michael that the rifts in this family ran "all the way back to the 1600s." This is what the 1600s look like: a man of twenty-two, barred from the Table over some saplings, standing outside the Governor's house for half a day, shouting for his minister to come down.
