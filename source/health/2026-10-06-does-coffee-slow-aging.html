Does coffee slow aging? A post from physician Nicholas Fabiano this week said coffee "preserves the main biological mechanisms of aging," with a screenshot of a journal review to back it. The review is real. It is also from 2024, which is the first thing to know about a paper that is being passed around as if it landed this morning. The second thing is that the post is a notch more confident than the paper's own abstract.

What the review says

The paper is "Impact of coffee intake on human aging: Epidemiology and cellular mechanisms," by Cátia Lopes and Rodrigo Cunha of the University of Coimbra, in Ageing Research Reviews (December 2024) [1]. It is a narrative review, which means the authors gathered and interpreted the literature rather than pooling it with a fixed statistical protocol. Its central claims, in the authors' words:

  • Regular consumption of "moderate doses" of coffee is associated with lower all-cause mortality, "an effect observed in over 50 studies in different geographic regions and different ethnicities."
  • The benefit "seems discrete (17% reduction)," which they translate into "an average increase in healthspan of 1.8 years of lifetime."
  • Coffee is also linked to lower cardiovascular, cerebrovascular, cancer and respiratory mortality, and to less loss of memory, less depression and less frailty in older people.

The cellular half of the title is where the viral wording comes from. The authors review human and human-tissue evidence that coffee and two of its main components, caffeine and chlorogenic acids, act on several of the biological processes thought to drive aging: genomic instability, damage to large molecules, metabolic and protein-handling problems. They call the effects "particularly robust" for stress adaptation and inflammation, and "unclear" for stem cells and regeneration [1]. So the abstract says "several of the main mechanisms, strongest on two, unclear on one." "The main mechanisms" is a fair paraphrase only if you stop reading before the last clause.

How big, and how sure?

The 17% figure is not unique to this review. A 2017 umbrella review in the BMJ pooled 201 meta-analyses of observational studies, covering 67 health outcomes, plus 17 meta-analyses of trials [2]. It found the largest apparent benefit at three to four cups a day compared with none: a relative risk of 0.83 for all-cause mortality (95% confidence interval 0.79 to 0.88), 0.81 for cardiovascular mortality, and 0.85 for cardiovascular disease. It also found that high versus low coffee intake went with an 18% lower risk of developing cancer. Lopes and Cunha's 17% is that same 0.83, read as a percentage.

That is a consistent signal, repeated across populations. It is also a signal from observational studies, where people who drink coffee differ from people who don't in ways that are hard to fully subtract: smoking, income, sleep, what else is on the plate, and whether someone cut back on coffee because they were already ill. The BMJ authors say it plainly: "Robust randomised controlled trials are needed to understand whether the observed associations are causal" [2]. A relative risk of 0.83 is not a small effect to ignore, but it is also the size of effect that a modest bias can produce.

Then there is the "1.8 years." It is an estimate the review derives from the mortality reduction, not something measured by following coffee drinkers and abstainers to the end of their lives. Treat it as the 17% restated in years, with all of the 17%'s caveats attached.

Where the evidence is weak or cuts the other way

Two parts of the BMJ review rarely make it into the viral version. First, in pregnancy, higher coffee intake was associated with low birth weight (odds ratio 1.31, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.67), preterm birth in the first and second trimesters, and pregnancy loss (1.46, 1.06 to 1.99). Second, coffee drinking was associated with a higher fracture risk in women but not in men; the reviewers suggested women at increased fracture risk "possibly be excluded" from any future trial. Other harmful associations "were largely nullified by adequate adjustment for smoking" [2], which is itself a reminder of how much work confounding does in this field.

The Lopes and Cunha abstract also closes by saying that further studies are needed to "detail these mechanistic benefits in aged individuals" and to learn "who gets the greater benefit with what schedules of which particular types and doses of coffee" [1]. In other words, the review does not tell you which coffee, how much or for whom. "Moderate" is doing a lot of the work.

What the evidence says. Across observational studies, moderate coffee drinking goes with about 17% lower all-cause mortality, with the biggest apparent effect at three to four cups a day, and the cellular evidence points to effects on inflammation and stress handling. That is a consistent association, not a demonstrated cause: no randomised trial has shown that coffee extends life, and the "1.8 years" is a conversion of the 17%, not a measured result. The exceptions, pregnancy and fracture risk in women, are real parts of the same evidence.
