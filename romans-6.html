The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. Having ended chapter 5 on grace reigning where sin increased, the letter takes up the objection that sentence invites, that the answer to sin is more sin, and answers it twice: first from baptism, with its burial, its likeness of death and its life with him; then from slavery, with the slave of sin, the slave of righteousness, and the pay each master gives.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 5; this library dates the letter no more closely than those chapters do. The only time-markers the chapter itself uses are “when you were slaves of sin,” “at that time” and “now” (verses 20 to 22), the readers’ own before and after.

y habiendo sido liberados del pecado, quedaron esclavizados a la justicia;

hablo en términos humanos por la debilidad de su carne. Porque así como presentaron sus miembros como esclavos a la inmundicia y a la iniquidad para la iniquidad, así ahora presenten sus miembros como esclavos a la justicia para santificación.

Porque cuando eran esclavos del pecado, eran libres respecto a la justicia.

Pero ahora, liberados del pecado y hechos esclavos de Dios, tienen su fruto para santificación, y el fin, vida eterna.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · Τί οὖν ἐροῦμεν; ἐπιμένωμεν τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ … μὴ γένοιτο … ἀπεθάνομεν τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ Ti oun eroumen? epimenōmen tē hamartia … mē genoito … apethanomen tē hamartia — Romans 6:1–2: “Shall we remain in sin so that grace may increase?” The objection, the “May it never be!”, and an answer that is a fact before it is an order: “we who died to sin”

“What shall we say, then?” (ti oun eroumen, Romans 6:1). The question opens a turn in the argument six times in Romans (ti oun eroumen, searched): at Romans 4:1 (“What, then, shall we say that Abraham … has found?”), here, at 7:7, at 8:31, and at Romans 9:14 and 9:30. The objection it carries is not new. Paul has already put it in the mouth of slanderers, “Let us do the evil things that the good things may come” (Romans 3:8), and what sets it off here is the last verse of the previous chapter: “where sin increased, grace abounded far more” (Romans 5:20). The verb of the objection is the verb of that verse, in the same form: pleonasē, “might increase,” stands in 5:20 (“so that the trespass might increase”) and here, and in no other verse of the New Testament (pleonasē, searched), so the objector takes the answer of chapter 5 and turns its own word into a plan: if more sin means more grace, let there be more sin.

“Remain in sin” (epimenō). The verb is epimenō, “stay on, remain,” the verb of Colossians 1:23 (“if indeed you remain in the faith”), Philippians 1:24 (“to remain in the flesh”) and Acts 21:10 (“as we stayed on for more days”). It is a verb of staying put in a place, and the place here is sin, set beside the other place the chapter before gave: “this grace in which we stand” (Romans 5:2). The question is whether one can stay at the one address in order to enjoy the other. The shelf reads it with a different English verb. KJV “Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?” and ASV “Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?” are joined by Geneva “Shall we continue still in sin, that grace may abound?” and Douay “shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?”, and by NWT 1984 “Shall we continue in sin, that undeserved kindness may abound?”; that is five of the seven with “continue” and “abound.” The NIV “Shall we go on sinning so that grace may increase?” turns the noun “sin” into a verb, and the TLB “shall we keep on sinning so that God can keep on showing us more and more kindness and forgiveness?” makes God the actor and adds “forgiveness,” neither of which the Greek has: its subject is “grace.” “Abound” has a cost in the five. They print it for all three verbs of 5:20 (“abound … abounded … abound” in the KJV, “abounded still more” in the NWT) and again here, so the two Greek verbs pleonazō and perisseuō run together; the NIV’s “increase” does the same with the other word. This translation keeps them apart (“increase” for pleonazō, “abound” for perisseuō), so that verse 1 sounds like the first half of 5:20 and not the second.

⚠ “Shall we remain” or “do we remain” (epimenōmen or epimenomen). The apparatus shows one vowel in dispute. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print epimenōmen, the subjunctive, which asks a deliberative question, “Shall we remain?”; Robinson–Pierpont prints epimenomen, the indicative, “Do we remain?” or “Are we staying?” (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on epimenōmen). All seven versions on the shelf ask the “shall we” question, which is the printed text. The same pair of readings comes back at verse 15 (hamartēsōmen against hamartēsomen, “Shall we sin?”), and the two questions are twins; the later Greek of the manuscripts pronounced the two vowels alike (checked: the loss of vowel length is attested in Koine Greek from the second century BC), which is the likeliest reason a scribe could write either.

“May it never be!” (mē genoito, Romans 6:2). Mē genoito is a wish in the optative, “may it not come to be,” and it stands in fifteen verses of the New Testament (mē genoito, searched): ten in Romans, one in Luke (Luke 20:16, the hearers’ answer to the parable of the tenants), one in 1 Corinthians and three in Galatians. Of the ten in Romans, three have been met (3:4, 3:6 and 3:31), two are in this chapter (here and verse 15), and five come after it (Romans 7:7 and 7:13, and three more, 9:14, 11:1 and 11:11). Where the English of the shelf says “God forbid,” the Greek has no “God.” KJV “God forbid”, ASV “God forbid” and Douay “God forbid” use the old oath, and Douay follows the Latin, which has the one word absit, “let it be far away” (checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org). NIV “By no means!”, TLB “Of course not!” and NWT 1984 “Never may that happen!” do without it. Geneva “God forbid.” has the same words, but the Geneva text on the shelf ends verse 1 with them and starts verse 2 at “How shall we.” The wording here is the one already used at 3:31.

“We who died to sin” (hoitines apethanomen tē hamartia). The reply to the objection is not a rule but a fact: apethanomen is an aorist, a single past event, “we died,” and the relative hoitines picks out the people to whom that is true, “we who.” The question that follows, “how shall we still live in it?”, asks how people who have died can go on residing in the place they have died out of: en autē, “in it,” with “it” being sin. The word hamartia has a dative with the verb, “died to sin,” and the dative says only that sin is the thing the death is related to. What relation, the chapter has to say; this verse does not. The clues come later: the one who has died “has been justified from sin” (verse 7), and Christ himself “died to sin once for all” (verse 10), and whatever that means for him, it cannot be the breaking of a habit of his own; so in this chapter “sin” looks like something that has a claim on the living and loses it at death, and not only a practice that is given up (that is a reading of the verses, not a statement in them). The shelf puts the verb in three forms. ASV “We who died to sin” keeps the aorist; KJV “we, that are dead to sin”, Geneva “we, that are dead to sin” and Douay “we that are dead to sin” make it a state, and the NIV “We are those who have died to sin” makes it a result that still holds. NWT 1984 “Seeing that we died with reference to sin” keeps the aorist and is the only version on the shelf that spells out the dative; it uses the same “with reference to” for sin in verses 10 and 11, and the Spanish Traducción del Nuevo Mundo does the same, in its own words. TLB “sin’s power over us was broken when we became Christians and were baptized to become a part of Jesus Christ” does not translate the verb: it explains the death with what verses 3 and 4 say about baptism, two verses early. Two questions are asked in this chapter and answered in two different ways. This one, whether grace gives license, is answered with death (verses 2–14); the second, at verse 15, whether the absence of law gives it, is answered with slavery (verses 16–23).

Verses 3–4 · ἐβαπτίσθημεν εἰς Χριστὸν Ἰησοῦν εἰς τὸν θάνατον αὐτοῦ … συνετάφημεν … διὰ τῆς δόξης τοῦ πατρός … ἐν καινότητι ζωῆς περιπατήσωμεν ebaptisthēmen eis Christon Iēsoun eis ton thanaton autou … synetaphēmen … dia tēs doxēs tou patros … en kainotēti zōēs peripatēsōmen — Romans 6:3–4: “baptized into his death,” “buried with him,” raised “through the glory of the Father,” and what it means to “walk in newness of life”

“Or do you not know” (ē agnoeite, Romans 6:3). The small word ē, “or,” sets the question against verse 2: either we died to sin, or you are unaware what your baptism was. The form “Or do you not know” (ē ouk oidate / ē agnoeite, searched) stands in seven verses of the New Testament, three of them in Romans: here, at Romans 7:1 and at Romans 11:2, and four in 1 Corinthians 6 (verses 2, 9, 16 and 19, not yet on these pages). It takes for granted that the readers know the teaching, and these readers are people Paul had not yet met: he “often intended to come to” them and “was prevented until now” (Romans 1:13). What he treats as shared ground, then, cannot be his own instruction in person; it is the teaching behind their baptism, the “pattern of teaching” to which they were “handed over” in verse 17 (a reading of the two verses together, not a statement in either). The shelf divides over the small word. ASV “Or are ye ignorant that”, NIV “Or don’t you know that” and NWT 1984 “Or do YOU not know that” keep it; KJV “Know ye not, that”, Geneva “Know ye not, that” and Douay “Know you not that” drop it, so that in those three the question stands on its own and no longer answers verse 2.

“Baptized into Christ Jesus … into his death” (ebaptisthēmen eis). The verb baptizō stands in Romans only here, in verses 3 and 4 (baptiz-, searched; the noun baptisma is in verse 4), so the letter that is about faith, law and grace speaks of baptism once, and for one purpose. The preposition is eis, “into,” and it is used twice in a row with two different objects: a person, “into Christ Jesus,” and an event, “into his death.” Several places on these pages baptize “into the name” of someone: of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit (Matthew 28:19), of the Lord Jesus (Acts 8:16 and 19:5) and, in the question Paul asks the Corinthians, of Paul himself (1 Corinthians 1:13 and 15). Here the object goes past the name to what happened to the one named. There is a likeness in the Gospels, which I report and do not claim Paul had in mind: Jesus calls his own death a baptism, “to be baptized with the baptism I am baptized with” (Mark 10:38, and again in verse 39) and “I have a baptism to be baptized with, and how I am hard-pressed until it is accomplished!” (Luke 12:50). The word “all” (hosoi, “as many as”) is carried by KJV “so many of us as were baptized” and flattened by the others, ASV “all we who were baptized”, NIV “all of us who were baptized” and NWT 1984 “all of us who were baptized”. KJV “baptized into Jesus Christ” and Geneva “baptized into Jesus Christ” print the names in the other order; ASV “baptized into Christ Jesus”, NIV “baptized into Christ Jesus” and NWT 1984 “baptized into Christ Jesus” print the order of the Greek. Douay “are baptized in Christ Jesus, are baptized in his death” puts “in” for both, as the Latin does (in Christo Iesu in morte ipsius, checked at vulgate.org).

“Buried with him … into that death” (synetaphēmen). The verb synthaptō, “bury together with,” stands in two verses of the New Testament (syntaph-, searched): here and Colossians 2:12 (not yet on these pages). It follows from verse 3 by oun, “then,” so the burial is offered as the sequel to the death, and it is the first of a run of compounds with syn-, “together with,” that carries through the chapter: synetaphēmen (verse 4), symphytoi (verse 5), synestaurōthē (verse 6) and syzēsomen (verse 8). The phrase here is eis ton thanaton, with the article, “into the death,” and the article points back to “his death” of verse 3, which is why this translation reads “that death.” Whether the picture of burial is drawn from the way the rite was done, going under the water, or only from what it means, the verse does not say; both have been argued (reported, not checked). The shelf drops or supplies the article. KJV “by baptism into death”, ASV “through baptism unto death” and NIV “through baptism into death” leave “the” out; Geneva “by baptism into his death” and NWT 1984 “through our baptism into his death” put “his” where the Greek has “the,” which is a good reading of what the article is for. Of the tenses, KJV “we are buried with him”, Geneva “We are buried then with him” and Douay “For we are buried together with him” say “are,” and ASV “We were buried therefore with him”, NIV “We were therefore buried with him” and NWT 1984 “Therefore we were buried with him” keep the aorist, a single past event, as the Greek has it.

“Through the glory of the Father” (dia tēs doxēs tou patros). The passive “was raised” does not name who raised him; the next phrase does, by naming the means: the glory of the Father. In the New Testament the phrase “the glory of the Father” stands in three verses (doxa tou patros, searched): here, and twice in the Gospels with “his,” where the Son of Man is to come “in the glory of his Father” (Matthew 16:27 and Mark 8:38). In those verses the glory is the setting of an arrival; here it is the force of a rising. Glory has been a place to hope for in this letter (“the glory of God,” Romans 3:23, 5:2); in this verse it is the power that does the raising, and Jesus uses the same word of power at the tomb of Lazarus, before the stone is moved: “Did I not tell you that if you believe, you will see the glory of God?” (John 11:40). I report that likeness and do not claim it as a citation. On the shelf, KJV “by the glory of the Father”, ASV “through the glory of the Father”, NIV “through the glory of the Father” and NWT 1984 “through the glory of the Father” keep the means. Geneva “to the glory of the Father” turns the glory into a goal, the thing the raising was for, which is a different sentence from the one the Greek wrote (dia with the genitive is “through”). TLB “with glorious power” turns the noun into an adjective and translates what the glory does rather than what it is.

“Walk in newness of life” (en kainotēti zōēs peripatēsōmen). The purpose of the burial is a walk. The verb peripateō, “walk about,” is a common word in Paul for the course of a life, and in Romans it stands in four verses (peripat-, searched): here, Romans 8:4 (“who walk not according to flesh but according to Spirit”), and Romans 13:13 (now on these pages) and 14:15 (not yet on these pages); the same figure is in Ephesians 2:2 (“in which you once walked”) and 2:10 (“so that we should walk in them”). The noun kainotēs, “newness,” stands in exactly two verses of the New Testament (kainotēt-, searched), both in Romans: here and Romans 7:6. The grammar is exact. The sentence says that the burial was so that we might walk (a purpose, aorist subjunctive), and that the walking takes place “in newness of life,” a sphere, not a possession: it is the new life that is walked in, not a new life that is handed over. Five versions on the shelf keep that shape: KJV “walk in newness of life”, ASV “walk in newness of life”, Geneva “walk in newness of life”, Douay “walk in newness of life” and NWT 1984 “walk in a newness of life”. The NIV “live a new life” drops the walking and turns “newness” into an adjective. The TLB “you were given his wonderful new life to enjoy” changes the verb of action into a verb of receiving. The Latin has the same shape as the five (in novitate vitae ambulemus, checked at vulgate.org). The “we too” (kai hēmeis) links the clause to the one before it, “just as Christ was raised”: the raising of Christ and the walk of the baptized are set side by side as a pair, and verse 5 states the likeness more precisely.

Verses 5–7 · σύμφυτοι γεγόναμεν τῷ ὁμοιώματι … ὁ παλαιὸς ἡμῶν ἄνθρωπος συνεσταυρώθη … τὸ σῶμα τῆς ἁμαρτίας … δεδικαίωται ἀπὸ τῆς ἁμαρτίας symphytoi gegonamen tō homoiōmati … ho palaios hēmōn anthrōpos synestaurōthē … to sōma tēs hamartias … dedikaiōtai apo tēs hamartias — Romans 6:5–7: “grown together” in the likeness of his death, the “old man” crucified with him, the “body of sin” abolished, and a dead person “justified from sin”

“Grown together in the likeness of his death” (symphytoi gegonamen tō homoiōmati, Romans 6:5). The adjective symphytos stands in one verse of the New Testament (symphyt-, searched), and in one verse of the Greek Old Testament in the Rahlfs word list, where it is said of a wood: “the dense forest has been pulled down” (ho drymos ho symphytos, Zechariah 11 in the Septuagint, read in context in the Rahlfs word list; the book is not yet on these pages). It is built on the verb phyō, “grow” (Liddell & Scott’s entry for the related verb symphyō gives “to make to grow together, unite”), and means things that have grown into one another. The English of the shelf has two pictures for it. KJV “planted together in the likeness of his death”, Douay “planted together in the likeness of his death” and Geneva “planted with him to the similitude of his death” say “planted,” and the Latin explains why: the Vulgate has conplantati, “planted together,” and similitudini, the dative of “likeness” (checked at vulgate.org). ASV “united with him in the likeness of his death” and NWT 1984 “united with him in the likeness of his death” say “united,” a word with no picture in it. This translation keeps the picture of growth that the Greek word has, which is a plant or a wood; the Greek does not say “planted.” The noun homoiōma, “likeness,” stands in six verses of the New Testament (homoiōm-, searched), four of them in Romans: Romans 1:23 (“a likeness of an image” of corruptible man), 5:14 (“the likeness of Adam’s transgression”), here, and 8:3 (“in the likeness of sinful flesh”); the other two are Philippians 2:7 (not yet on these pages) and Revelation 9:7 (where this translation has “the shapes of the locusts”). The word means a likeness that is not the thing itself, so the sentence does not say “we have died his death” but that we have grown together with something like it. What the likeness is, the verse does not say. NIV “united with him in a death like his” takes it to be a death of our own that resembles his; TLB “you died with him, so to speak, when he died” takes the death as figurative; the five that keep the noun leave the question where the Greek leaves it.

“But also of the resurrection” (alla kai tēs anastaseōs esometha). The second half is a future, esometha, with the small strong pair alla kai, “but also, yes also.” NIV “we will certainly also be united with him in a resurrection like his” and NWT 1984 “we shall certainly also be” carry the force with “certainly”; KJV “we shall be also in the likeness of his resurrection” and ASV “we shall be also in the likeness of his resurrection” leave it out. Whether the future is a future in time (a resurrection still to come) or a future of consequence (what follows, now, from the death) is read both ways. The chapter has the present on both sides of it: the baptized “walk in newness of life” now (verse 4), and are told to reckon themselves “alive to God” now (verse 11), while verse 8 repeats the future, “we shall also live with him.” The letter itself uses the future for the body when it comes to it: “the one who raised Christ from the dead will also make your mortal bodies alive” (Romans 8:11). Ephesians says the same togetherness in the past tense, “made us alive together with Christ … and raised us up together” (Ephesians 2:5 and 2:6). I report the two tenses side by side and do not choose.

“Our old man was crucified with him” (ho palaios hēmōn anthrōpos synestaurōthē, Romans 6:6). Verse 6 begins with a participle of knowing, touto ginōskontes, “knowing this,” which answers the question “do you not know” of verse 3 and is matched by eidotes, “knowing that,” at the start of verse 9. The verb systauroō, “crucify together with,” stands in five verses of the New Testament (systaur-, searched): in three of them it is said of the two men crucified beside Jesus (Matthew 27:44, Mark 15:32 and John 19:32), and in the other two of us, here and Galatians 2:19 (not yet on these pages). It is an aorist passive, a single event that was done: “was crucified.” The “old man” is ho palaios anthrōpos, a phrase that stands in three verses of the New Testament (palaios anthrōpos, searched): here, Ephesians 4:22 and Colossians 3:9 (those two not yet on these pages). The word anthrōpos is the word of “one man” for Adam in the chapter before (Romans 5:12, 15 and 19), and a reader coming from chapter 5 will hear the old man as the person one was in Adam’s line; the verse does not say so, and I mark it as a reading. KJV “our old man is crucified with him”, ASV “our old man was crucified with him”, Geneva “our old man is crucified with him” and Douay “our old man is crucified with him” keep the phrase; NIV “our old self was crucified with him” and NWT 1984 “our old personality was impaled with” give the person a modern word; TLB “Your old evil desires were nailed to the cross with him” changes the person into desires and the “we” into “you.” The NWT’s “impaled” is its word for the whole family, as its Gospel verses show (“impaled together with him” at Matthew 27:44 and Mark 15:32), and it goes with that version’s choice of “stake” for stauros; the verb in Romans is staurō-, and this translation keeps “crucify,” the word the KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and NIV use.

“The body of sin might be abolished” (hina katargēthē to sōma tēs hamartias). The phrase to sōma tēs hamartias, “the body of sin,” stands in this verse only (sōma tēs hamartias, searched), and the genitive can be read in several ways: the body that belongs to sin, that is ruled by sin, that is made of sin. NIV “the body ruled by sin” chooses one and NWT 1984 “our sinful body” chooses another; TLB “your sin-loving body” adds a third; the KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay keep the Greek’s own “of.” The verb is katargeō, which this translation renders “abolish” where it has met it in Romans, at 3:3, 3:31 and 4:14, and in 1 Corinthians 13:8, 10 and 11. It stands in six verses of Romans (katarg-, searched): the three just named, here, and Romans 7:2 and 7:6. The KJV has four different phrases for it in these four verses: “make … without effect” at 3:3, “make void” at 3:31, “made of none effect” at 4:14, and, here, KJV “might be destroyed”, as do Geneva “might be destroyed” and Douay “may be destroyed”. The English “destroyed” gives the wrong picture here, since the same chapter goes on to speak of “your mortal body” and of its “members” (verses 12 and 13) as still there; a thing taken out of service is not a thing gone. ASV “might be done away” and NIV “might be done away with” come closer, and NWT 1984 “might be made inactive” is the nearest to the idea of being put out of action, though it reads as a state and not as an end. The last clause, tou mēketi douleuein hēmas tē hamartia, is purpose: “so that we would no longer be slaves to sin.” The verb is douleuō, “serve as a slave,” and it opens the image that the second half of the chapter will use. KJV “we should not serve sin” and Douay “serve sin no longer” say “serve”; ASV “no longer be in bondage to sin”, NIV “no longer be slaves to sin” and NWT 1984 “no longer go on being slaves to sin” say what the status is.

“The one who has died has been justified from sin” (ho apothanōn dedikaiōtai apo tēs hamartias, Romans 6:7). The verb is dikaioō, “justify,” in the perfect passive: the verdict has been given and stands. It is the verb of Romans 3:24, 3:28, 4:2, 5:1 and 5:9, and what is new is the object: not “by faith,” and not a believer, but a dead person, cleared “from sin.” The same preposition after the verb, naming what one is cleared of, stands in one other verse: “from all the things from which you were not able to be justified by the Law of Moses” (Acts 13:38; dikaioō followed by apo, read from the hits; Matthew 11:19 and Luke 7:35 use the same verb with the same preposition, but there it is “wisdom is vindicated by her deeds” and “by all her children,” a different use, where the preposition marks the source of the vindication). In Acts it is the Law that could not clear; in Romans it is death that does, and with it the claim of the master. There is a saying in the Talmud that has the same shape, much later (Rabbi Yohanan, 180–279 CE) and given as a likeness, not as a source for Paul: Rabbi Yohanan reads Psalm 88:6 (not yet on these pages), “free among the dead,” to mean that “once a person dies, he becomes free [ḥofshi] from the mitzvot” (Niddah 61b; the same saying at Shabbat 30a; checked in the Sefaria text). Both say that a death ends an obligation. Here the shelf splits between two pictures. ASV “justified from sin” and Douay “justified from sin” keep the legal verb, the Douay following the Vulgate (iustificatus est a peccato, checked at vulgate.org); KJV “freed from sin”, Geneva “freed from sin” and NIV “set free from sin” use the verb of release, and NWT 1984 “acquitted from [his] sin” uses a court verb and adds “[his]” in brackets where the Greek has only “the sin.” The cost of “freed” is that the chapter has another verb for freeing, eleutheroō, in verses 18 and 22, and with “freed” here the two run together. TLB “you are freed from all its allure and its power over you” adds the explanation that the verse leaves to the reader. The verse is a reason (gar, “for”) for the purpose clause before it: the old man was crucified so that the body might be abolished and the slavery end, for one who has died is out of reach of the claim.

Verses 8–10 · ἀπεθάνομεν σὺν Χριστῷ … συζήσομεν αὐτῷ … οὐκέτι ἀποθνῄσκει, θάνατος αὐτοῦ οὐκέτι κυριεύει … ἐφάπαξ … ζῇ τῷ θεῷ apethanomen syn Christō … syzēsomen autō … ouketi apothnēskei, thanatos autou ouketi kyrieuei … ephapax … zē tō theō — Romans 6:8–10: “live with him,” why death is “no longer lord” over Christ, and what “once for all” and “lives to God” mean

“We believe that we shall also live with him” (pisteuomen hoti kai syzēsomen autō, Romans 6:8). The condition repeats verse 2 in the plain form: apethanomen syn Christō, “we died with Christ.” What follows it is not “we know” but “we believe,” and what is believed is a future, syzēsomen, “we shall live with.” The verb syzaō, “live together with,” stands in three verses of the New Testament (syzē-, searched): here, and 2 Corinthians 7:3 and 2 Timothy 2:11 (these two not yet on these pages). The last is the nearest in shape (ei gar synapethanomen, kai syzēsomen, a conditional of dying together and living together), and the letter there introduces it with pistos ho logos, a saying to be trusted; I report the likeness and do not decide which way anything was borrowed. The verse repeats the pattern of verse 5, where the future follows the “grown together” of the present, and so the same question as in verse 5 returns: whether this is life after the resurrection of the body or the life of the walk of verse 4, which the letter does not settle in so many words. KJV “Now if we be dead with Christ, we believe that we shall also live with him” and Geneva “if we be dead with Christ” make the death a state; ASV “if we died with Christ”, NIV “if we died with Christ” keep the aorist; NWT 1984 “if we have died with Christ” makes it a perfect. Douay “we shall live also together with Christ” repeats the name where the Greek has a pronoun, as the Latin does (simul etiam vivemus cum Christo, checked at vulgate.org). TLB “we know that you will share his new life” turns “we believe” into “we know,” the first person plural into a second person, and the verb of living into one of sharing. The Greek says “believe,” and what it believes in is left as a future.

“Knowing that Christ, raised from the dead, no longer dies” (eidotes hoti Christos egertheis ek nekrōn ouketi apothnēskei, Romans 6:9). The participle eidotes, “knowing,” is the second of the pair that opened verse 6 (ginōskontes), so the argument is carried by two acts of knowing: what happened to us (verse 6) and what is true of him (verse 9). Egertheis, “raised,” is passive, and the agent is the one named in verse 4, “the Father”; the verb that follows is a plain present, apothnēskei, “dies,” and the phrase “no longer dies” stands in this verse only in the New Testament (ouketi apothn-, searched). NIV “he cannot die again” turns the plain present into a statement about what is possible, which is a fair reading of the sense but a stronger sentence than the Greek writes. TLB “will never die again” turns it into a future; KJV “dieth no more”, ASV “dieth no more”, Geneva “dieth no more” and NWT 1984 “dies no more” keep it as it stands. Douay “rising again from the dead, dieth now no more” has a present participle that follows the Latin surgens, “rising,” and not the Greek passive “having been raised.”

“Death is no longer lord over him” (thanatos autou ouketi kyrieuei). The verb kyrieuō, “be lord over, rule as lord,” and its compound katakyrieuō stand together in eleven verses of the New Testament (kyrieu-, searched). The plain verb is the verb that Jesus uses of the kings of the nations, “lord it over them” (Luke 22:25), and in Romans it is said of death here, of sin in verse 14, of law at Romans 7:1, and of Christ himself at Romans 14:9 (the last not yet on these pages). In chapter 5 the verb of death’s power was basileuō, “reign” (5:14, 17), and death, which reigned from Adam to Moses, is not lord over the one who was raised. The word kyrios, the root of this verb, is the title that closes chapter 5 (“Jesus Christ our Lord”) and will close chapter 6 (“Christ Jesus our Lord”): the one death has no lordship over is Lord. KJV “death hath no more dominion over him”, ASV “death no more hath dominion over him” and Geneva “death hath no more dominion over him” all use “dominion,” a noun for a verb, and Douay “death shall no more have dominion over him” also changes the tense, to a future, as the Latin non dominabitur does; NIV “death no longer has mastery over him” and NWT 1984 “death is master over him no more” use “master,” and TLB “Death no longer has any power over him” weakens the picture of a person ruling to a quantity of power. This translation keeps the word “lord” so that the same root can be heard in verse 14 and at the end of the chapter.

“He died to sin once for all; he lives to God” (ephapax, zē tō theō, Romans 6:10). The Greek of the verse is built on two relative clauses with no noun, ho gar apethanen and ho de zē: literally “what he died” and “what he lives,” the same form of speech as ho de nyn zō en sarki at Galatians 2:20 (not yet on these pages). This translation has “as to the death he died” and “as to the life he lives.” The tenses differ: apethanen is an aorist, one event, and zē is a present, a continuing state, so the verse says by its grammar what it says in words: once, and still. ASV “For the death that he died, he died unto sin once: but the life that he liveth, he liveth unto God” supplies the two nouns the Greek leaves out; KJV “For in that he died, he died unto sin once: but in that he liveth, he liveth unto God” and Geneva “For in that he died, he died once to sin: but in that he liveth, he liveth to God” use “in that.” The adverb ephapax stands in five verses of the New Testament (ephapax, searched): here, 1 Corinthians 15:6 (where it means “at one time,” of the appearance to more than five hundred) and Hebrews 7:27, 9:12 and 10:10 (these four not yet on these pages). Four versions on the shelf say only “once”: KJV “he died unto sin once”, ASV “he died unto sin once”, Geneva “he died once to sin” and Douay “he died once” (the Latin has semel, “once,” checked at vulgate.org); three carry the sense of the Greek word with “for all”: NIV “he died to sin once for all”, NWT 1984 “with reference to sin once for all time” and TLB “He died once for all to end sin’s power”. The “to” after both verbs is the dative of verse 2 again: the one who “died to sin” here is Christ, and what the death was, the verse says by the pair that follows: he “lives to God.” The same dative of living is in “for all live to him” (Luke 20:38), and in the dative of Romans 14:8 and Galatians 2:19 (these two not yet on these pages); I report that likeness and do not claim a citation. The pair “dies to sin, lives to God” is what verse 11 will hand to the readers as an order.

Verse 11 · οὕτως καὶ ὑμεῖς λογίζεσθε ἑαυτοὺς εἶναι νεκροὺς μὲν τῇ ἁμαρτίᾳ ζῶντας δὲ τῷ θεῷ ἐν Χριστῷ Ἰησοῦ houtōs kai hymeis logizesthe heautous einai nekrous men tē hamartia zōntas de tō theō en Christō Iēsou — Romans 6:11: “reckon yourselves dead to sin and alive to God.” The ledger verb that credited Abraham, now turned into a command

“So you also, reckon yourselves” (houtōs kai hymeis logizesthe heautous). The verse turns on two small words. Houtōs kai, “so also,” ties the readers to the pair of verse 10: he died to sin and lives to God; so you also. And hymeis, “you,” is the plural “you” the chapter has not addressed since verse 3. Verses 4 to 10 speak of “we” and of “him”; the “you” comes back here with a command. A scan of the verb-endings of the whole Greek text of Romans 1 to 5 finds no verb of the plural-“you” type at all (scanned by verb-ending for -ete, -ate, -esthe and their relatives, setting aside este, “you are”); the first one in the letter is agnoeite, “do you not know,” in verse 3, and the first imperative is logizesthe here. Up to this point the letter has described, argued and quoted, and, apart from the disputed “let us have peace” of Romans 5:1, it has not told its readers to do anything. Verse 11 is the hinge between what is true of them and what they are asked to do, and verses 12 and 13 fill in the asking.

The ledger verb (logizomai). The verb is the one of Romans 4, where “Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness” (Romans 4:3), and where the wage “is not reckoned according to grace but according to debt” (4:4). It is also the verb of Romans 3:28 (“we reckon that a person is justified by faith”) and of 8:18 (“I reckon that the sufferings of the present season are not worth weighing against the glory”). In all of these the verb is one of calculation: it draws a conclusion from the figures and enters it. In chapter 4 the one who reckons is God; here it is the readers, who are told to draw the conclusion from verses 3 to 10 and to hold it. I read the command, then, as neither “feel dead” nor “pretend,” and not “make yourselves dead,” since the verb asks for a judgement about what has already happened. The form is a present imperative, which asks for a continuing practice more naturally than a single act: “go on reckoning.” The sentence has an object and a complement, heautous einai nekrous, “yourselves to be dead,” and the participle zōntas, “alive,” completes the pair with a dative of its own, tō theō, “to God.” The two parts are balanced by men … de (“on the one hand … on the other”): KJV “dead indeed unto sin” and NWT 1984 “dead indeed with reference to sin” show the men with “indeed”; the other versions let it go. The verb itself divides the shelf into four. KJV “reckon ye also yourselves to be dead”, ASV “reckon ye also yourselves to be dead”, Douay “reckon, that you are dead to sin” and NWT 1984 “reckon yourselves to be dead” keep the ledger word; NIV “count yourselves dead to sin” keeps the sense with a plainer word; Geneva “think ye also, that ye are dead to sin” makes it an opinion; TLB “look upon your old sin nature as dead” changes the object, so that what is to be reckoned dead is no longer “yourselves” but the nature.

⚠ “To be” and “our Lord” (the apparatus). The verse carries two notes in the SBLGNT apparatus, and both can be seen in English. The first is the word einai, “to be”: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print einai nekrous men, “to be dead indeed”; Robinson–Pierpont prints nekrous men einai, the same words in another order; and the Greek text behind the NIV has nekrous men with no “to be” at all. which fits the NIV’s “count yourselves dead,” with nothing between “yourselves” and “dead.” The second is the end of the verse. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 end at en Christō Iēsou, “in Christ Jesus”; Robinson–Pierpont adds tō kyriō hēmōn, “our Lord.” The shelf divides along that line. ASV “in Christ Jesus”, NIV “in Christ Jesus” and NWT 1984 “by Christ Jesus” stop there, while KJV “through Jesus Christ our Lord”, Geneva “in Jesus Christ our Lord”, Douay “in Christ Jesus our Lord” and TLB “through Jesus Christ our Lord” carry the added words, so that four of the seven end the verse as verse 23 and 5:21 end. The preposition is en, “in”: the KJV and TLB’s “through” and the NWT’s “by” change a place, “in Christ Jesus,” into a means.

Verses 12–14 · μὴ βασιλευέτω ἡ ἁμαρτία ἐν τῷ θνητῷ ὑμῶν σώματι … μηδὲ παριστάνετε τὰ μέλη ὑμῶν ὅπλα ἀδικίας … παραστήσατε ἑαυτοὺς τῷ θεῷ ὡσεὶ ἐκ νεκρῶν ζῶντας … ἁμαρτία γὰρ ὑμῶν οὐ κυριεύσει … ὑπὸ χάριν mē basileuetō hē hamartia en tō thnētō hymōn sōmati … mēde paristanete ta melē hymōn hopla adikias … parastēsate heautous tō theō hōsei ek nekrōn zōntas … hamartia gar hymōn ou kyrieusei … hypo charin — Romans 6:12–14: “do not let sin reign in your mortal body,” “present yourselves to God as alive from the dead,” and the promise that sin will not be lord, because you are “under grace”

“Therefore let not sin reign in your mortal body” (mē oun basileuetō hē hamartia, Romans 6:12). The oun, “therefore,” draws the command out of verses 1 to 11, and the verb comes straight from chapter 5: sin “reigned in death” (Romans 5:21), death “reigned” (5:14 and 17), and here the readers are told not to let sin go on reigning. The command is in the third person and in the present, basileuetō, “let it not be reigning.” It also tells the reader something about verse 6. If the “body of sin” had been done away in the sense that sin was simply gone, there would be nothing to forbid; a command not to let sin reign takes it for granted that sin can still try, and that the place it tries is “your mortal body.” The word thnētos, “mortal,” is the word of “your mortal bodies” in Romans 8:11, where the Spirit “will also make your mortal bodies alive”; the body of this verse is the same body that is promised life there, which agrees with reading verse 6 as the end of sin’s hold on the body and not of the body itself. The last clause names what reigning would look like: eis to hypakouein tais epithymiais autou, “so as to obey its desires.” The word “its” (autou) is a masculine or neuter form (the two look alike in the genitive), and “sin” is feminine, which would need autēs; the nearest word it can agree with is “body,” so the Greek speaks of the desires of the body, and does not say “the desires of sin.” The obedience-word hypakouō is here for the first time in the chapter, and it runs through the next verses to its noun in verse 16, hypakoē, which is the “obedience” of Romans 1:5 and 5:19.

⚠ “Obey the desires” or “obey it in its desires” (the apparatus). The apparatus has a note on hypakouein: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 end the verse there and then read tais epithymiais autou; Robinson–Pierpont adds two words, autē en, so that the clause becomes “to obey it in its desires.” The addition makes sin the one that is obeyed, and the printed text leaves the word “sin” out of the obedience clause altogether. The shelf divides. KJV “that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof” and Geneva “that ye should obey it in the lusts thereof” print the longer reading; ASV “that ye should obey the lusts thereof”, Douay “so as to obey the lusts thereof”, NIV “so that you obey its evil desires”, TLB “do not give in to its sinful desires” and NWT 1984 “that YOU should obey their desires” (with “bodies” in the plural before it) print the shorter one. The Greek word epithymia is simply “desire,” and this translation keeps it as such (as at Romans 1:24, “in the desires of their hearts,” and Ephesians 2:3, “in the desires of our flesh”); “lusts” in the KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay had a wider sense when they were printed than it has now (reported, not checked), and “evil” in the NIV and “sinful” in the TLB are added by the translators, since the Greek word does not carry them.

“Do not keep presenting … present yourselves” (mēde paristanete … parastēsate, Romans 6:13). The two commands are in two tenses. The first is a present, paristanete, with mēde, “nor,” and a present prohibition is commonly read as “do not go on doing it”; the second is an aorist, parastēsate, which is commonly read as one decisive act (the usual reading of the two tenses, reported, not checked in a grammar). NWT 1984 “Neither go on presenting YOUR members to sin” is the only version on the shelf that shows the present in English. The verb is paristēmi, “place beside, present,” and it stands in six verses of Romans (parist-, searched): five times in three verses of this chapter (verses 13, 16 and 19) and in Romans 12:1, 14:10 and 16:2 (these last two not yet on these pages). It is also the verb of two verses in Acts that are the nearest to this one in wording. The risen Jesus “presented himself alive” to his disciples (Acts 1:3, parestēsen heauton zōnta), and Peter, having raised Tabitha, “presented her alive” (Acts 9:41, parestēsen autēn zōsan). The verb has “alive” or “living” attached to what is presented in four verses of the New Testament (parist- with zō-, searched): those two, this verse, and Romans 12:1. Romans 6:13 asks the readers to do with themselves what the Acts verses say was done with a risen man and a raised woman: parastēsate heautous … zōntas, “present yourselves … alive.” I report the likeness and do not claim that it was meant. The comparison clause is hōsei ek nekrōn zōntas, “as alive from the dead.” The little word hōsei, which the Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 texts print and Robinson–Pierpont prints as hōs, can mark a comparison, “as if,” and in later Greek often means no more than “as” (reported, not checked); the readers are not called dead, since they are alive, and the phrase says what kind of living they have. KJV “as those that are alive from the dead”, ASV “as alive from the dead” and NWT 1984 “as those alive from the dead” keep the phrase; NIV “as those who have been brought from death to life” and TLB “for you are back from death” make it a report of what happened.

“Weapons of unrighteousness” (hopla adikias). The “members” (melē, the limbs and organs of the body) are to be given to sin or to God as hopla. The noun stands in five verses of the New Testament (hopl-, searched): the arrest party that comes “with torches and lamps and weapons” (John 18:3), here, and Romans 13:12 (now on these pages), 2 Corinthians 6:7 and 10:4 (these three not yet on these pages); in each of the others it is a weapon or armor, and the phrase “weapons of righteousness” (hopla dikaiosynēs) stands in two verses, here and 2 Corinthians 6:7 (not yet on these pages). The shelf mostly reads it more mildly. KJV “instruments of unrighteousness”, ASV “instruments of unrighteousness”, Douay “instruments of iniquity”, NIV “an instrument of wickedness” and TLB “tools of wickedness” say “instrument” or “tool”; only Geneva “weapons of unrighteousness” and NWT 1984 “weapons of unrighteousness” say “weapons.” The Douay is following the English tradition here and not its Latin, which has arma iniquitatis, “weapons of iniquity” (checked at vulgate.org). The picture is of a soldier handing his arms over to a commander, and the chapter will come back to the soldier at its last verse, where the master pays: “the wages of sin is death” (verse 23), the word opsōnia being the soldier’s ration-money (Luke 3:14).

“Sin will not be lord over you … under grace” (hamartia gar hymōn ou kyrieusei, Romans 6:14). After a run of commands the verse is a statement, and it is in the future, ou kyrieusei, “will not be lord.” It is a promise with a reason: gar, “for,” and the reason is not the readers’ resolve but their position, “you are not under law but under grace.” The preposition is hypo, “under,” the preposition of Romans 3:9, where Jews and Greeks alike were charged as “all under sin”: the readers have been moved from one “under” to another. And “law” has no article in the Greek (hypo nomon), the form of 3:31 and of the verse that follows. Five of the seven put “the” before “law”: KJV “not under the law, but under grace”, Geneva “not under the Law, but under grace”, Douay “not under the law, but under grace” and NIV “not under the law, but under grace”, and, taking the phrase apart, TLB “no longer tied to the law where sin enslaves you”; only ASV “not under law, but under grace” and NWT 1984 “not under law but under undeserved kindness” leave it out as the Greek does. With the article the phrase can sound like a statement about one law, the Law of Moses; without it, it names a condition. Whether Paul means the Law of Moses or the principle is read both ways, and I do not decide. The future is the other thing to see. NWT 1984 “sin must not be master over YOU” makes the verse a command; KJV “shall not have dominion over you”, ASV “shall not have dominion over you”, Geneva “shall not have dominion over you” and Douay “shall not have dominion over you” keep a future that can be read as a promise, and NIV “sin shall no longer be your master” and TLB “Sin need never again be your master” add “no longer” and “never again.” A Greek future can sometimes stand for a command, but the verse gives as its reason a fact, and a fact is what supports a promise; so I have kept the statement.

Verses 15–16 · Τί οὖν; ἁμαρτήσωμεν ὅτι οὐκ ἐσμὲν ὑπὸ νόμον ἀλλὰ ὑπὸ χάριν; μὴ γένοιτο … ᾧ παριστάνετε ἑαυτοὺς δούλους εἰς ὑπακοήν, δοῦλοί ἐστε ᾧ ὑπακούετε Ti oun? hamartēsōmen hoti ouk esmen hypo nomon alla hypo charin? mē genoito … hō paristanete heautous doulous eis hypakoēn, douloi este hō hypakouete — Romans 6:15–16: the second objection, “Shall we sin because we are not under law?”, and an answer from slavery: you are the slaves of whoever you obey

“What then? Shall we sin?” (ti oun? hamartēsōmen, Romans 6:15). The objection of verse 1 asked whether to stay in sin; this one asks whether to sin, and the verbs show the difference. In verse 1 the verb was epimenō, a verb of staying on; here it is hamartēsōmen, an aorist subjunctive, which names a single act: “Shall we commit a sin?” The second objector, as the aorist suggests, asks for less, one lapse, and the ground is not the abundance of grace but the end of the Law: ouk esmen hypo nomon alla hypo charin, “we are not under law but under grace.” Those are the closing words of verse 14, word for word but for the person (“you are not” becomes “we are not”), and the phrase hypo charin, “under grace,” stands in these two verses and nowhere else in the New Testament (hypo charin, searched); hypo nomon, “under law,” stands in eight verses (hypo nomon, searched): these two, 1 Corinthians 9:20, and Galatians 3:23, 4:4, 4:5, 4:21 and 5:18 (those six not yet on these pages). The objector has taken the statement of verse 14 and run it through the same machine as the statement of 5:20: if grace is where we stand, anything goes. The shelf renders the question in three ways. KJV “shall we sin, because we are not under the law, but under grace?”, ASV “shall we sin, because we are not under law, but under grace?” and NIV “Shall we sin because we are not under the law but under grace?” ask it plainly. NWT 1984 “Shall we commit a sin” makes the aorist audible, and puts “What follows?” for “What then?”. TLB “Does this mean that now we can go ahead and sin and not worry about it?” is the freest, and its parenthesis about salvation, “does not depend on keeping the law but on receiving God’s grace,” is the translator’s own explanation, which the Greek leaves for the next verses to give. Then the same exclamation as in verse 2: KJV “God forbid”, ASV “God forbid”, Geneva “God forbid” and Douay “God forbid”, NIV “By no means!”, TLB “Of course not!” and NWT 1984 “Never may that happen!”. Two questions have now been answered with the same two Greek words, mē genoito, and the replies that follow are different in kind: the first was answered by death (verses 2 to 14), this one by slavery (verses 16 to 23).

⚠ “Shall we sin” or “will we sin” (hamartēsōmen or hamartēsomen). The apparatus has the same one-vowel difference as at verse 1. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print hamartēsōmen, an aorist subjunctive, the deliberative “Shall we sin?”; Robinson–Pierpont prints hamartēsomen, a future indicative, “Are we going to sin?” (the SBLGNT apparatus, note on hamartēsōmen). The English of the shelf cannot show the difference, since “shall we” stands for both. It is the same pair of vowels that made the difference at verse 1, and the later pronunciation made the two alike (checked: the loss of vowel length is attested in Koine Greek from the second century BC).

“Do you not know” (ouk oidate, Romans 6:16). This is the second of Paul’s “do you not know” formulas in the chapter. The first, agnoeite at verse 3, asked about baptism; this one, ouk oidate, asks about a rule of slavery: the one you obey is the one whose slave you are. Both forms of the question come back later in the letter, agnoeite at Romans 7:1 and ouk oidate at Romans 11:2. Slavery is the picture of the second half of the chapter, and the noun doulos is the word Paul put at the head of the letter for himself, “slave” of Christ Jesus (Romans 1:1). The structure of the sentence is a chain: hō paristanete heautous doulous eis hypakoēn, “to whom you present yourselves as slaves for obedience,” and then douloi este hō hypakouete, “you are slaves to the one you obey.” Presenting yourself is what makes the obedience; obeying is what shows whose slave you are. It does not say that anyone chooses a master in the free way a customer chooses a shop; it says that the act of giving oneself over to a master’s orders is what defines the relation. Self-sale into slavery did happen in the ancient world, though for a Roman citizen it was illegal in theory (checked), and it may be the background of the phrase “present yourselves as slaves”; the verse does not say. The verb paristēmi comes back from verse 13, where it was the “presenting” of members as weapons; here it is the presenting of the whole self as a slave. Four English verbs are in use across the shelf for the five Greek occurrences in verses 13, 16 and 19, each held steady through the chapter: ASV “present yourselves as servants” and NWT 1984 “keep presenting yourselves” say “present” throughout the chapter; KJV “ye yield yourselves servants” says “yield”; Geneva “ye give yourselves as servants” says “give”; NIV “you offer yourselves to someone as obedient slaves” says “offer”; the Douay “you yield yourselves servants” says “yield” here but “present yourselves” in verse 13, though the Latin has the same verb in both (exhibeatis, exhibete, checked at vulgate.org); the TLB “you can choose your own master” leaves the verb altogether.

“Slaves” and “servants.” Four of the seven shelf versions call them “servants”: KJV “his servants ye are to whom ye obey”, ASV “his servants ye are whom ye obey”, Geneva “his servants ye are to whom ye obey” and Douay “his servants you are whom you obey”. Three say “slaves”: NIV “you are slaves of the one you obey”, NWT 1984 “YOU are slaves of him because YOU obey him” and, in its own way, TLB “you will be his slave”. In this passage “servant” costs more than anywhere else, for the argument here depends on the master’s ownership: a servant is hired and can leave, a slave is owned, and the point of the verse (and of the next five) is that you cannot be neutral about whose you are. The dictionary entry for doulos makes the same case for the word everywhere. The last clause also divides the shelf. NIV “which leads to death, or to obedience, which leads to righteousness” turns the Greek eis into a relative clause, and NWT 1984 “of sin with death in view or of obedience with righteousness in view” turns it into “with … in view.” KJV “of sin unto death, or of obedience unto righteousness” keeps the preposition as it is. The Douay has Douay “of obedience unto justice”, where the Latin has iustitiam (checked at vulgate.org), and so the English keeps the root that joins “justify” of verse 7 to the noun, a link that “justified” and “righteousness” hide from the English reader and that this translation holds in the notes instead. The master named at the end of the verse is not God but “obedience,” and the destination of that master is “righteousness”; in verses 18 and 22 the language moves on to “righteousness” as the master and then to “God.” The only rare word in the verse is ētoi, “either,” which stands in this verse only in the whole New Testament (the exact word, searched across the Greek text).

Verses 17–19 · χάρις δὲ τῷ θεῷ ὅτι ἦτε δοῦλοι τῆς ἁμαρτίας ὑπηκούσατε δὲ ἐκ καρδίας εἰς ὃν παρεδόθητε τύπον διδαχῆς … ἐλευθερωθέντες … ἐδουλώθητε … ἀνθρώπινον λέγω … εἰς ἁγιασμόν charis de tō theō hoti ēte douloi tēs hamartias hypēkousate de ek kardias eis hon paredothēte typon didachēs … eleutherōthentes … edoulōthēte … anthrōpinon legō … eis hagiasmon — Romans 6:17–19: thanks to God for a handing-over, a “pattern of teaching,” freed and enslaved in one sentence, and Paul’s apology: “I speak in human terms”

“Thanks be to God that you were slaves of sin, yet you obeyed” (charis de tō theō hoti ēte douloi tēs hamartias hypēkousate de, Romans 6:17). The thanks is not for the slavery. The Greek puts the two halves side by side with a de, “but, yet,” and the thanks goes to what comes after it: you were slaves of sin, yet you obeyed. English can misread the sentence the other way, as a thanksgiving for having been slaves, and the shelf is divided by how it guards against that. ASV “whereas ye were servants of sin”, NIV “though you used to be slaves to sin” and TLB “though you once chose to be slaves of sin” put the slavery into a clause that gives way; KJV “that ye were the servants of sin, but ye have obeyed”, Geneva “that ye have been the servants of sin, but ye have obeyed”, Douay “that you were the servants of sin, but have obeyed” and NWT 1984 “that YOU were the slaves of sin but YOU became obedient” keep the two halves apart, as the Greek does. The TLB’s “chose” is its own: the verse has no word for choosing, and the sentence of verse 16 was about whom one obeys, not about picking a master. The word charis, “thanks,” is the word that has been “grace” in this chapter (“under grace,” verses 14 and 15); English splits one Greek word into two, and the reader who hears the thanks hears the grace. The verb of the obedience is hypakouō, the verb of verse 12, where the readers were told not to obey the body’s desires; the phrase ek kardias, “from the heart,” stands in two verses of the New Testament (ek kardias, searched): here, and 1 Peter 1:22, “love one another earnestly from the heart.”

“The pattern of teaching to which you were handed over” (eis hon paredothēte typon didachēs). The relative is attracted into the case of its noun, so that the Greek says literally “into which pattern of teaching you were handed over.” The direction matters. The readers are not given the teaching; they are handed over to it, as a slave is handed to an owner. Four of the seven keep that direction: ASV “to that form of teaching whereunto ye were delivered”, Geneva “whereunto ye were delivered”, Douay “into which you have been delivered” and NWT 1984 “to which YOU were handed over”. KJV “that form of doctrine which was delivered you” reverses it, so that the teaching is handed to the readers. NIV “the pattern of teaching that has now claimed your allegiance” keeps the idea of a claim on them and drops the verb, and TLB “the teaching to which God has committed you” names God as the one who did the handing, which the passive does not. The verb is paradidōmi, and in Romans it stands in six verses (paredō-, searched): the three of chapter 1 where “God gave them over” to uncleanness, to passions of dishonor and to an unapproved mind (Romans 1:24, 26 and 28; the English there is “gave over”), the two where Christ is “handed over” (4:25 and 8:32), and this one. Chapter 1 has people handed over to a master; this verse hands the readers to another. The noun typos stands in two verses of Romans (typ-, searched): here, and Adam as “a type of the one who was to come” (Romans 5:14). This translation says “type” there, where the word is a figure that points ahead, and “pattern” here, where “type of teaching” would read as “kind of teaching,” which is not what the Greek says; the Greek compound typos didachēs stands in this verse alone. The word for teaching is didachē, rendered “teaching” as at Mark 1:27, and the KJV, Geneva and Douay have “doctrine,” an older and narrower word for a body of instruction.

“Set free from sin … enslaved to righteousness” (eleutherōthentes … edoulōthēte, Romans 6:18). Both verbs are aorist passives, and neither names who did it: you were set free, you were enslaved. The verb eleutheroō, “set free,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament (eleutheroō, searched, the augmented form ēleutherōsen included): here, verse 22, John 8:32 (“the truth will set you free”) and 8:36 (“if the Son sets you free, you will be free indeed”), Romans 8:2 (“set you free from the law of sin and of death”) and 8:21 (“set free from the slavery of decay”), and Galatians 5:1 (not yet on these pages). In John the freedom is set against a slavery the hearers deny, “everyone who commits sin is a slave of sin” (John 8:34), and the likeness is plain; I report it and do not claim a source. What is strange here is the paired verb: the freedom is described in the same sentence as a new slavery, and Paul says in the next verse that he knows the picture strains. The English of five versions turns the second verb into something that happened to the readers on their own: KJV “ye became the servants of righteousness”, ASV “ye became servants of righteousness”, NIV “have become slaves to righteousness”, NWT 1984 “YOU became slaves to righteousness” and TLB “you have become slaves to your new master, righteousness”, while Geneva “ye are made the servants of righteousness” and Douay “we have been made servants of justice” keep the passive (the Douay also has “we” where the Latin has “you,” servi facti estis, checked at vulgate.org). The TLB adds “old master” and “new master,” two words the Greek does not have.

“I am speaking in human terms because of the weakness of your flesh” (anthrōpinon legō dia tēn astheneian tēs sarkos hymōn, Romans 6:19). It is an apology for the picture. Paul uses the formula three times (anthrōpinon legō / kata anthrōpon legō, searched): here, in Romans 3:5 (“I speak in a human way”) and in Galatians 3:15 (not yet on these pages). The slave and the master are borrowed from the world of the readers because it is what they can see. The reason he gives is “the weakness of your flesh,” tēn astheneian tēs sarkos, in the family of words for being weak that runs through the letter: “while we were still weak” (Romans 5:6), the law “weak through the flesh” (8:3), the Spirit helping “our weakness” (8:26). Whether the weakness is a limit of understanding, so that a picture is needed, or the weakness of the flesh in the sense of 8:3, so that a command is needed, the words do not say. NIV “because of your human limitations” and TLB “because it is easy to understand” take the first; KJV “because of the infirmity of your flesh”, ASV “because of the infirmity of your flesh”, Douay “because of the infirmity of your flesh”, Geneva “because of the infirmity of your flesh” and NWT 1984 “because of the weakness of YOUR flesh” leave the question as the Greek does.

“Uncleanness … lawlessness … sanctification” (akatharsia, anomia, hagiasmos). The two names of the old master are words the letter has used. Akatharsia, “uncleanness,” is the thing to which “God gave them over” in Romans 1:24, and in Romans it stands at 1:24 and here only (akatharsia, searched); anomia, “lawlessness,” is the “lawless acts” that are forgiven in Romans 4:7, and in Romans the noun stands at 4:7 and here only (anomi-, searched). The phrase tē anomia eis tēn anomian, “to lawlessness for lawlessness,” has the noun twice, once as the master and once as the end. KJV “to iniquity unto iniquity”, ASV “to iniquity unto iniquity” and Douay “uncleanness and iniquity, unto iniquity” keep the doubling; NWT 1984 “lawlessness with lawlessness in view” keeps it as a purpose; Geneva “to iniquity, to commit iniquity” makes the second a verb; NIV “ever-increasing wickedness” reads the doubling as growth, which is a reading and a good one, and TLB “all kinds of sin” drops both. The end of the new service is eis hagiasmon. The noun hagiasmos stands in ten verses of the New Testament (hagiasm-, searched): here and verse 22, 1 Corinthians 1:30 (“righteousness and sanctification and redemption”), 1 Peter 1:2 (“in sanctification of the Spirit”), and six more in 1 Thessalonians 4:3, 4 and 7, 2 Thessalonians 2:13, 1 Timothy 2:15 and Hebrews 12:14 (these six not yet on these pages). It is the process or the state of being made holy, from hagiazō, and it is a different word from hagiosynē, the quality, which this translation renders “holiness” at Romans 1:4 (“the Spirit of holiness”). So here it is “sanctification.” ASV “unto sanctification” and Douay “unto sanctification” keep the two words apart; KJV “unto holiness”, Geneva “in holiness”, NIV “leading to holiness” and NWT 1984 “with holiness in view” use the English word that serves for both Greek nouns, and TLB “all that is right and holy” turns the noun into adjectives.

Verses 20–21 · ἐλεύθεροι ἦτε τῇ δικαιοσύνῃ … τίνα οὖν καρπὸν εἴχετε τότε ἐφʼ οἷς νῦν ἐπαισχύνεσθε; τὸ γὰρ τέλος ἐκείνων θάνατος eleutheroi ēte tē dikaiosynē … tina oun karpon eichete tote eph’ hois nyn epaischynesthe? to gar telos ekeinōn thanatos — Romans 6:20–21: the only freedom a slave of sin had, “free in regard to righteousness,” and the question of the fruit: what did you get, and what are you ashamed of?

“Free in regard to righteousness” (eleutheroi ēte tē dikaiosynē, Romans 6:20). The sentence is bitter. A person in the service of sin had a freedom after all, and it was a freedom from the other master: righteousness had no claim on a slave of sin. The Greek does it with a bare dative, tē dikaiosynē, a dative of relation, the construction of “dead to sin” and “alive to God” (verses 2, 10, 11), so the verse says “free in respect of righteousness,” and the shelf is divided over how much to add. ASV “free in regard of righteousness” and NWT 1984 “free as to righteousness” keep the dative as a relation. KJV “free from righteousness” and Geneva “freed from righteousness” put in a “from” that the Greek does not have, and the effect is to make this phrase look the same as verse 18, where the Greek really does have “from” (apo, “set free from sin”); the KJV prints “made free from sin” there. NIV “free from the control of righteousness” adds “the control,” which is a reading of what the freedom was: a slave of sin was out of the reach of righteousness’s authority. Douay “you were free men to justice” follows the Latin dative (liberi fuistis iustitiae, checked at vulgate.org) and leaves the sense to the reader; TLB “you didn’t bother much with goodness” turns the status into a habit and the noun into “goodness.” The imperfect tense, ēte, with hote, “when,” is a past that went on, and the verse is tied to the one before it by gar, “for,” which gives a reason for the command of verse 19: the two services do not overlap, since while one served sin one was free of righteousness.

“What fruit were you having then?” (tina oun karpon eichete tote, Romans 6:21). The word karpos, “fruit,” stands in six verses of Romans (karp-, searched): the first is Paul’s wish to have “some fruit among you also” (Romans 1:13), then this verse and the next, and Romans 7:4 and 7:5, and Romans 15:28 (the last not yet on these pages). The question is the language of a ledger and of a harvest at once: a master collects what the slave produces, so “fruit” is also the slave’s output, and the verse asks what the output came to. The Greek can be pointed in two ways. In the printed text one question runs to the end of the clause, tina oun karpon eichete tote eph’ hois nyn epaischynesthe;, “What fruit, then, were you having at that time from the things of which you are now ashamed?” The other way stops the question after tote and makes what follows its answer: “What fruit, then, were you having at that time? Things of which you are now ashamed.” (Early manuscripts carry no question marks, so both pointings are open to the Greek; reported, not checked.) NWT 1984 “What, then, was the fruit that YOU used to have at that time? Things of which YOU are now ashamed.” reads it the second way, as a question and an answer. KJV “What fruit had ye then in those things whereof ye are now ashamed?”, ASV “What fruit then had ye at that time in the things whereof ye are now ashamed?”, Geneva “What fruit had ye then in those things, whereof ye are now ashamed?” and Douay “What fruit therefore had you then in those things, of which you are now ashamed?” read it the first way, and so does the NIV “What benefit did you reap at that time from the things you are now ashamed of?”, which also changes “fruit” to “benefit.” The TLB “And what was the result? Evidently not good” replaces the question with its own answer. The two readings come to the same place, since either way what the readers produced is what they now feel shame about.

“Now ashamed” (nyn epaischynesthe). The verb epaischynomai, “be ashamed (of),” stands in nine verses of the New Testament (epaischyn-, searched, setting aside the adjective anepaischyntos, “unashamed,” in 2 Timothy 2:15, not yet on these pages): the Son of Man is to be ashamed of those who are ashamed of him (Mark 8:38 and Luke 9:26), Paul writes at the head of this letter, “I am not ashamed of the gospel” (Romans 1:16), and the word falls three times in 2 Timothy 1 (verses 8, 12 and 16): as a charge to Timothy, as a statement by Paul, and of Onesiphorus. The others are Hebrews 2:11 (not yet on these pages) and Hebrews 11:16 (“God is not ashamed of them, or to be called their God”). What the readers are ashamed of is the opposite of what Paul says he is not ashamed of, and the two stand at the two ends of the argument: the gospel, which is God’s power for salvation (1:16), and the former life, which they now blush to remember. There is a small point in the tense. The verb is a present, epaischynesthe, “you are ashamed,” and so the shame is a present feeling about a past fact, and the verse does not command it; it takes it for granted.

“The end of those things is death” (to gar telos ekeinōn thanatos). The noun telos means both “end” in the sense of “termination” and “end” in the sense of “goal,” as the English word does, and this verse and the next both use it: here the end of the old service is death, and in verse 22 the end of the new one is eternal life. The verse has no verb, only telos and thanatos with the “for” in front. KJV “the end of those things is death”, ASV “the end of those things is death”, Geneva “the end of those things is death”, NWT 1984 “the end of those things is death” and Douay “the end of them is death” keep the noun sentence, as the Latin does (finis illorum mors est, checked at vulgate.org). NIV “Those things result in death!” supplies a verb and an exclamation, and TLB “all of them end in eternal doom” supplies a verb and two words that are not in the Greek: the Greek says thanatos, “death,” here as it has said it in verses 9 and 16, and the word “eternal,” with “doom,” belongs to the translator. The pairing of death as the end of the one service and the gracious gift of life as the end of the other is built here, and the two ends are named together in verse 23.

Verses 22–23 · νυνὶ δέ … ἔχετε τὸν καρπὸν ὑμῶν εἰς ἁγιασμόν, τὸ δὲ τέλος ζωὴν αἰώνιον … τὰ γὰρ ὀψώνια τῆς ἁμαρτίας θάνατος, τὸ δὲ χάρισμα τοῦ θεοῦ ζωὴ αἰώνιος nyni de … echete ton karpon hymōn eis hagiasmon, to de telos zōēn aiōnion … ta gar opsōnia tēs hamartias thanatos, to de charisma tou theou zōē aiōnios — Romans 6:22–23: “But now,” enslaved to God, and the verse about wages: “the wages of sin is death, but the gracious gift of God is eternal life”

“But now” (nyni de, Romans 6:22). The pair nyni de, “but now,” is a turn of time in Paul’s writing, and it stands in eighteen verses of the New Testament (nyni de, searched), six of them in Romans: 3:21, where it opened the new way of righteousness (“But now, apart from law, God’s righteousness has been made visible”), here, Romans 7:6 and 7:17, and Romans 15:23 and 15:25 (these two not yet on these pages). The shipped letters use it for the same kind of turn: “But now, in Christ Jesus, you who were once far off have been brought near” (Ephesians 2:13), “but now he has reconciled you” (Colossians 1:22), “who once was useless to you but now is useful” (Philemon 11), and “But now there remain faith, hope, love” (1 Corinthians 13:13). Here it closes the accounting that verse 20 began: then, you were slaves of sin and free in regard to righteousness; now, you have been set free from sin and enslaved to God. The two participles are the two of verse 18, eleutherōthentes and doulōthentes, now with the master named. Verse 18 said that you were enslaved to “righteousness”; here the master is God, and the name has moved from a quality to a person. KJV “being made free from sin, and become servants to God”, ASV “being made free from sin and become servants to God”, Douay “being made free from sin, and become servants to God” and Geneva “being freed from sin, and made servants unto God” keep the two participles; NIV “you have been set free from sin and have become slaves of God”, NWT 1984 “you were set free from sin but became slaves to God” and TLB “you are free from the power of sin and are slaves of God” turn them into finite verbs, and the TLB’s “the power of” is its addition.

“You have your fruit … and the end, eternal life” (echete ton karpon hymōn eis hagiasmon, to de telos zōēn aiōnion). The tense has changed from verse 21. There the readers “were having” fruit (eichete, imperfect); here they “have” it (echete, present), and the fruit is already on the way to something, eis hagiasmon, “for sanctification.” The grammar of the last clause carries a difference that the English hides. In verse 21 the end is the subject of a statement about “those things”: to telos ekeinōn thanatos, “the end of those things is death,” with thanatos in the nominative. In verse 22 the words are to de telos zōēn aiōnion, and zōēn aiōnion is unmistakably accusative (the neuter telos reads the same in either case): it stands in apposition to “the end,” which is itself something the readers have. Death is where the old service ends; eternal life is, in the grammar, a possession already held. Hagiasmos is “sanctification” for the same reason as in verse 19, and the shelf splits the same way: ASV “unto sanctification” keeps the word, while KJV “unto holiness”, Geneva “in holiness”, NIV “leads to holiness” and NWT 1984 “in the way of holiness” use “holiness.” The TLB “his benefits to you include holiness and everlasting life” makes the pair a list of benefits that are the master’s, and puts the two nouns on the same footing, which the Greek (a fruit that leads to sanctification and an end that is life) does not. The end is zōē aiōnios, which this translation renders “eternal life” each time it has come, at Romans 2:7 and 5:21, and in this verse and the next. Three versions on the shelf split the Greek word in two between neighbouring verses, and print “everlasting” in verse 22 and “eternal” in verse 23: KJV “the end everlasting life”, then in verse 23 “the gift of God is eternal life”; Geneva “the end, everlasting life”, then “eternal life”; and TLB “holiness and everlasting life”, then “eternal life”. The ASV and NIV print “eternal” in both verses, and the NWT and Douay “everlasting” in both.

“The wages of sin is death” (ta gar opsōnia tēs hamartias thanatos, Romans 6:23). The noun opsōnion is the pay of a soldier, originally the money for his rations, and it stands in four verses of the New Testament (opsōni-, searched): John the Baptist's reply to the soldiers who ask what to do, “be content with your wages” (Luke 3:14), here, and 1 Corinthians 9:7 and 2 Corinthians 11:8 (those two not yet on these pages). The chapter has used a soldier’s word before: the members of the body are hopla, “weapons,” to be handed to one commander or the other (verse 13). The last verse pays the soldiers. Sin is a paymaster with one coin; the noun is plural in the Greek, as a soldier’s pay was, and what the pay comes to is death. The noun is not the word of Romans 4:4, where the “wage” of a worker (misthos) “is not reckoned according to grace but according to debt”; a soldier’s pay is owed in the same way as that wage. Against the wages stands to de charisma tou theou, “but the gracious gift of God,” and the noun is the one of Romans 5:15 and 16 (“the gracious gift is not like the trespass”), and of the “spiritual gift” Paul wanted to share at Romans 1:11. The sentence has no soldier on the gift side. A wage is paid because it is owed; a charisma is the result of charis, grace, and nothing is owed.

The shelf at verse 23. KJV “the wages of sin is death; but the gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord” and NIV “the wages of sin is death, but the gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord” are the two most familiar forms, and they differ in two small things that are in the Greek. The first is the name order, which the KJV and Geneva print as “Jesus Christ” (Geneva “eternal life, through Jesus Christ our Lord”) and the Greek printed here has as “Christ Jesus.” The second is the preposition, en, “in,” which the KJV, Geneva and TLB (TLB “the free gift of God is eternal life through Jesus Christ our Lord”) make “through,” and the NWT makes “by” (NWT 1984 “the gift God gives is everlasting life by Christ Jesus our Lord”), a means in place of a sphere, exactly as at verse 11. ASV “the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord” has the Greek’s own words. Douay “the grace of God, life everlasting, in Christ Jesus our Lord” has “grace” for charisma, as the Latin does (gratia autem Dei vita aeterna, checked at vulgate.org), so that the English loses the difference between charis and charisma that the Greek of this chapter and the last has kept. The ending, “in Christ Jesus our Lord,” is also the ending of Romans 8:39, and stands where chapter 5 ended with “through Jesus Christ our Lord” (5:21). Chapter 5 ended on “our Lord,” chapter 6 ends on it, and so does chapter 8.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “May it never be!” for mē genoito (vv. 2 and 15), as at Romans 3:4, 3:6 and 3:31; “remain” for epimenō (v. 1), as at Colossians 1:23 and Philippians 1:24; “increase” for pleonazō (v. 1), as at 5:20; “baptized into” (v. 3), as at Matthew 28:19 and Acts 19:5; “walk” for peripateō (v. 4), as at 8:4; “likeness” for homoiōma (v. 5), as at 1:23, 5:14 and 8:3; “crucified with” for systauroō (v. 6), as the same verb is said of the two beside Jesus at Matthew 27:44, Mark 15:32 and John 19:32; “abolish” for katargeō (v. 6), as at 3:3, 3:31 and 4:14; “slave” for doulos and douleuō (vv. 6, 16–22), as at 1:1; “justified” for dikaioō (v. 7), as at 3:24, 3:28 and 5:1; “lord” for kyrieuō (vv. 9 and 14), kept apart from “reign” for basileuō (v. 12), as at 5:14, 17 and 21; “reckon” for logizomai (v. 11), as at 3:28, 4:3 and 8:18; “mortal” for thnētos (v. 12), as at 8:11; “desires” for epithymia (v. 12), as at 1:24; “weapons” for hopla (v. 13), as at John 18:3; “present” for paristēmi, one English verb for all five occurrences (vv. 13, 16 and 19), as at Acts 1:3 and 9:41; “handed over” for paradidōmi (v. 17), as at 4:25 and 8:32, where the same verb is “gave over” at 1:24, 26 and 28; “set free” for eleutheroō (vv. 18 and 22), as at 8:2 and 21; “uncleanness” for akatharsia (v. 19), as at 1:24; “lawlessness” for anomia (v. 19), as at 4:7; “fruit” for karpos (vv. 21 and 22), as at 1:13; “wages” for opsōnia (v. 23), as at Luke 3:14; “eternal life” (vv. 22 and 23), as at 2:7 and 5:21; and “gracious gift” for charisma (v. 23), as at 5:15 and 16. Three pairs are split on purpose: typos is “pattern” at verse 17 and “type” at 5:14; hagiasmos is “sanctification” (vv. 19 and 22) against “holiness” for hagiosynē at 1:4; and charis is “grace” in verses 14 and 15 and “thanks” in verse 17, where the note says why.

Echoes paid. The objection built on “where sin increased” (5:20) — v. 1, in the same verb form. “May it never be!” (3:31) — vv. 2 and 15. Death and sin as reigning powers (5:14, 21) — v. 9 and v. 12. The likeness of Adam’s transgression (5:14) — the likeness of his death, v. 5. “Justified” (3:24, 5:9) — the dead person justified from sin, v. 7, and, in Acts 13:38, what the Law could not do. The ledger verb of Abraham (4:3) — turned into a command, v. 11. “All under sin” (3:9) — “under grace,” vv. 14 and 15. “The obedience of faith” (1:5) and the obedience of the one (5:19) — the obedience that makes a slave, v. 16. God’s giving-over to uncleanness (1:24) — the readers’ hand-over to a pattern of teaching, v. 17, and their old service to uncleanness, v. 19. “Lawless acts” forgiven (4:7) — the old master’s name, v. 19. “I speak in a human way” (3:5) — v. 19. Weakness (5:6) — v. 19. “Slave of Christ Jesus” (1:1) — the slaves of vv. 16–22. “Some fruit among you” (1:13) and “I am not ashamed of the gospel” (1:16) — the fruit and the shame of v. 21. The wage that is owed, against grace (4:4) and the gracious gift (5:15–16) — the closing contrast, v. 23. “Eternal life … our Lord” (5:21) — vv. 22–23.

Echoes planted. Death as the end of a claim (v. 7) is worked out in the picture of a husband who dies at Romans 7:1–6, where the same kyrieuō and katargeō of verses 9 and 6 return (the former at 7:1, the latter at 7:2 and 7:6), with “newness” once more at Romans 7:6. “Set free” (v. 18) is taken up at 8:2 and 8:21; the mortal bodies of verse 12 are made alive at 8:11; walking (v. 4) returns at 8:4; the “together with” verbs of verses 4–8 return in “heirs together with Christ” and “glorified together” at 8:17; and the chapter’s last words, “in Christ Jesus our Lord,” are the last words of 8:39. The presenting of one’s members and oneself to God (v. 13) is repeated as the presenting of bodies at Romans 12:1. Christ as lord of the dead and the living is Romans 14:9, and living to the Lord is Romans 14:8 (those two not yet on these pages). Crucified with Christ, and living to God (vv. 6 and 10) is the sentence of Galatians 2:19 (not yet on these pages), and buried with him in baptism (v. 4) is Colossians 2:12 (not yet on these pages). Died with him, and shall live with him (v. 8) is the saying of 2 Timothy 2:11 (not yet on these pages). Ephapax, “once for all” (v. 10), is the key word of Hebrews 7:27, 9:12 and 10:10 (these three not yet on these pages). The “weapons of righteousness” of verse 13 are in 2 Corinthians 6:7 (not yet on these pages). Hagiasmos (vv. 19 and 22) is the word of 1 Thessalonians 4:3–7 (not yet on these pages).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether verses 1 and 15 ask “shall we?” or “do we?” (a one-vowel difference in the apparatus); what “died to sin” means for the readers, and for Christ himself in verse 10; what “the likeness of his death” is in verse 5, a death of our own that resembles his or the rite that stands for it; whether the futures of verses 5 and 8 look to a resurrection still to come or to what follows now from the death; whether the “old man” is the person one was in Adam, and what kind of genitive makes the “body of sin” (verse 6); whether the burial of verse 4 is drawn from the way the rite was done; whether the “weakness of your flesh” of verse 19 is a limit of understanding or of will; whether the question of verse 21 runs to the end of the clause or stops after “then”; whether “under law” in verses 14 and 15 means the Law of Moses or any law; and whether verse 14 is a promise or a command. The SBLGNT apparatus has six notes in this chapter, at verses 1, 11 (two), 12, 13 and 15, and each is treated in the note for its verse.
