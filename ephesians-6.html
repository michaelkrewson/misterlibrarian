Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–4 · children and parents, the first commandment with a promise, “on the earth,” and bringing children up

“Children, obey your parents in the Lord.” The household of 5:21 goes on, and the children are spoken to directly, as hearers of the letter when it was read in the congregation. Hypakouō, “obey,” is to listen and do (dictionary); it is the verb of v5 as well. Dikaion, “right,” is the word for what is just. The KJV reads “Children, obey your parents in the Lord: for this is right”; the NWT 1984 reads “for this is righteous”; the TLB adds “because God has placed them in authority over you,” words the Greek does not have.

“Honor your father and mother … on the earth” — the commandment, quoted from the Greek. This is the commandment of Exodus 20:12 and Deuteronomy 5:16, which Jesus quotes at Matthew 15:4. Paul quotes it as the Greek Old Testament has it: “so that it may go well with you” stands in the Hebrew of Deuteronomy 5:16 and in the Greek of Exodus 20:12, but not in the Hebrew of Exodus. And he stops short. Both commandments end “on the ground that Jehovah your God is giving you”; Paul ends at “on the earth.” Gē means both “land” and “earth,” and with the gift of the land left out, most readers take the promise to reach as far as the earth does; others still hear the land. Makrochronios, “long-lived,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The KJV reads “and thou mayest live long on the earth”; the NWT 1984 reads “you may endure a long time on the earth”; the TLB reads “yours will be a long life, full of blessing,” and the earth is gone.

⚠ “The first commandment with a promise.” The second commandment already speaks of God “doing loyal-love to the thousandth generation of those loving me” (Exodus 20:6), so readers ask in what sense this one is first. Some answer that Exodus 20:6 describes God and attaches no promise to a command; others take “first” as “foremost,” the first command a child learns; others as the first of the commandments about other people. This page sets out the readings and does not choose. The ASV reads “(which is the first commandment with promise)”; the NWT 1984 reads “which is the first command with a promise”; the TLB reads “This is the first of God’s Ten Commandments that ends with a promise,” one of the readings printed as the text.

“Do not provoke your children to anger, but bring them up.” Parorgizō stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Romans 10:19, Moses’ “I will provoke you to anger”; its noun is the “provocation” of Ephesians 4:26 (dictionary). Pateres, “fathers,” can mean both parents, as it does at Hebrews 11:23, where Moses is hidden “by his parents.” “Bring them up” is ektrephō, the “nourishes” of Ephesians 5:29: what a man does for his own flesh he does for his children. Paideia is the training of a child, discipline; nouthesia, “admonition,” putting something in mind, stands in three verses: 1 Corinthians 10:11, where it is “a warning to us,” this one, and Titus 3:10. The KJV reads “bring them up in the nurture and admonition of the Lord”; the NIV reads “Fathers, do not exasperate your children”; the NWT 1984 reads “the discipline and mental-regulating of Jehovah,” where this translation prints “the Lord,” since the Lord of these verses may be Christ (v5–6).

Verses 5–9 · slaves and masters, how the passage has been used, eye-service, and one Master in the heavens

⚠ “Slaves, obey your masters according to the flesh.” Douloi are people owned by other people (dictionary), and this translation says “slaves.” The same word comes back in v6, “slaves of Christ,” the title Paul gives himself. Their owners are masters only “according to the flesh.” Fear and trembling were Paul’s own at Corinth: “in fear, and in much trembling” (1 Corinthians 2:3). Haplotēs, “simplicity,” is the undivided heart of Romans 12:8. The KJV reads “Servants, be obedient to them that are your masters according to the flesh”; the NWT 1984 reads “YOU slaves, be obedient to those who are [YOUR] masters in a fleshly sense”; the NIV reads “Slaves, obey your earthly masters with respect and fear.”

⚠ How this passage has been used. These verses and their parallel at Colossians 3:22 were cited by defenders of slavery, in the American South among others, as the Bible’s own word to the enslaved: obey. A Bible abridged for enslaved people in the British West Indies, printed in London in 1807, kept this verse while it left out most of the Old Testament, the escape from Egypt among it. That use is part of the record. The passage itself speaks to slaves directly, as people who act and who will be repaid by the Lord (v8). It tells masters to do “the same things,” to give up their threats, and it puts them under a Master in the heavens with whom “there is no partiality” (v9). It does not call for an end to slavery, and it does not defend it. Elsewhere Paul asks that the slave Onesimus be received “no longer as a slave but more than a slave, a beloved brother” (Philemon 1:16), and writes “there is no slave nor free” (Galatians 3:28). Readers divide: some read rules for living inside an order the writer did not expect to change; others, abolitionists among them, read an order already undone from within. This page sets out the readings and does not choose.

“Not by way of eye-service, as people-pleasers.” Ophthalmodoulia, “eye-service,” work done for the master’s eye, stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Colossians 3:22. Anthrōpareskos, “people-pleaser,” stands in the same two verses. The pair is the one of Galatians 1:10: “If I were still pleasing men, I would not be a slave of Christ.” “From the soul” is ek psychēs, not the “heart” of v5. The KJV reads “Not with eyeservice, as menpleasers”; the NIV reads “Obey them not only to win their favor when their eye is on you”; the NWT 1984 reads “doing the will of God whole-souled.” At v7 the NWT 1984 reads “as to Jehovah”; this translation prints “the Lord,” since vv5–6 name Christ.

“Both their Master and yours is in the heavens.” One Greek word, kyrios, is “masters” and “Master” and “Lord” in these verses (dictionary); the masters are reminded that they have one. Prosōpolēmpsia, “partiality,” literally “receiving the face,” stands in four verses of the New Testament: Romans 2:11, “there is no partiality with God,” this one, Colossians 3:25 and James 2:1 (dictionary). “Whether slave or free” in v8 is the pair of Galatians 3:28. The KJV reads “neither is there respect of persons with him”; the NIV reads “there is no favoritism with him”; the TLB adds “you yourselves are slaves to Christ.”

Verses 10–17 · the full armor of God, the schemes of the devil, the world-rulers, and Isaiah’s armor

“Finally, be empowered in the Lord.” Endynamoō is the verb of Philippians 4:13, “the one who empowers me,” and “the might of his strength” is the phrase of Ephesians 1:19. Tou loipou, “finally,” is the “From now on” of Galatians 6:17, and readers take it here either way. The Byzantine text reads “Finally, my brothers,” and so the KJV: “Finally, my brethren, be strong in the Lord, and in the power of his might”; the ASV reads “Finally, be strong in the Lord, and in the strength of his might”; the NWT 1984 reads “go on acquiring power in [the] Lord.”

“The full armor of God” — God’s own. Panoplia, the whole kit of a heavy-armed soldier, stands in three verses of the New Testament: Luke 11:22, where the stronger man takes away the strong man’s “full armor, in which he had trusted,” and here at v11 and v13 (dictionary). In Isaiah 59:17 it is God who arms himself: in the Greek Old Testament he “put on righteousness as a breastplate, and set a helmet of salvation on his head.” The armor is his, lent. Paul has used the figure before: “let us put on the armor of light” (Romans 13:12), and “the weapons of righteousness” (2 Corinthians 6:7). The KJV reads “Put on the whole armour of God”; the NWT 1984 reads “the complete suit of armor from God,” reading the genitive as the giver.

“The schemes of the devil.” Methodeia, a planned approach, a stratagem, stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and Ephesians 4:14, “the scheming of error” (dictionary). Diabolos is the slanderer (dictionary), the devil to whom the readers were told “do not give place” (Ephesians 4:27). The KJV reads “the wiles of the devil”; the Geneva reads “the assaults of the devil”; the TLB reads “all strategies and tricks of Satan.”

⚠ “Not against blood and flesh” — the world-rulers of this darkness. Palē, “wrestling,” hand-to-hand, stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; the Greek has “blood and flesh” in that order. “The rulers” and “the authorities” are those of Ephesians 1:21 and Ephesians 3:10 (dictionary), and “the heavenly places” are those of Ephesians 1:3. Kosmokratōr, “world-ruler,” stands in one verse, this one (dictionary); Greek writers are reported to have given the title to gods. “The spiritual forces of wickedness” supplies “forces” for a Greek neuter plural, “the spiritual things of wickedness.” Most of the tradition reads personal evil powers here; some modern readers include the structures through which such powers are thought to work. This page reports both. The Byzantine text reads “the darkness of this age,” and so the KJV: “against the rulers of the darkness of this world, against spiritual wickedness in high places”; the ASV reads “the world-rulers of this darkness”; the NIV reads “the spiritual forces of evil in the heavenly realms.”

“The evil day … to stand.” “Stand” comes four times in vv11–14: to stand against, to resist, to stand, stand. The soldier holds ground; he is not told to advance. “The evil day” recalls Ephesians 5:16, “the days are evil.” The KJV reads “and having done all, to stand”; the Douay, from the Vulgate, reads “and to stand in all things perfect.”

“Truth, righteousness, the readiness of the gospel of peace” — Isaiah’s armor. Three passages of Isaiah lie behind these verses, and none of the three is translated here yet. In the Greek of Isaiah 11:5 the coming king has righteousness girding his loins; Isaiah 59:17 gives the breastplate of righteousness; and Isaiah 52:7 gives “the feet of one who announces peace,” a verse Paul quotes at Romans 10:15, “How beautiful are the feet of those who announce the good news of the good things!” Thōrax, “breastplate,” stands in four verses: this one, 1 Thessalonians 5:8 and two in Revelation 9 (none of those translated here yet). Hetoimasia, “readiness,” stands in one verse; some read it as a firm footing, a sense the word is reported to have in the Greek Old Testament. “The gospel of peace” is the “good news of peace” of Ephesians 2:17. The KJV reads “And your feet shod with the preparation of the gospel of peace”; the NWT 1984 reads “the equipment of the good news of peace”; the NIV reads “the readiness that comes from the gospel of peace.”

“The shield of faith … the flaming arrows of the evil one.” Thyreos stands in one verse: the long shield, named from thyra, “door,” for its shape. The Byzantine text reads epi pasin, “above all,” for en pasin, “in all things,” and so the KJV: “Above all, taking the shield of faith, wherewith ye shall be able to quench all the fiery darts of the wicked”; the ASV reads “the fiery darts of the evil one”; the NWT 1984 reads “the large shield of faith.”

⚠ “The sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God.” Perikephalaia, “helmet,” stands in two verses: this one and 1 Thessalonians 5:8, and it comes from Isaiah 59:17. Machaira is the short sword (dictionary), the one piece made to strike. Rhēma is the spoken word, as at Ephesians 5:26 (dictionary). “Which” is neuter in Greek: it agrees with “Spirit,” not with “sword,” so either the Spirit is named as the word of God, or, as most read it, the word is the sword the Spirit gives. This page reports both. The KJV reads “And take the helmet of salvation, and the sword of the Spirit, which is the word of God”; the NWT 1984 reads “the sword of the spirit, that is, God’s word.”

Verses 18–20 · praying at every time, boldness, and an ambassador in a chain

“Praying at every time in the Spirit.” The participles hang on “Stand” in v14: prayer is part of the standing. Proseuchē is prayer and deēsis a request, a petition (dictionary). “Staying awake” is the watchfulness of a sentry. The KJV reads “Praying always with all prayer and supplication in the Spirit”; the NIV reads “for all the Lord’s people”; the NWT 1984 reads “in behalf of all the holy ones.”

“To make known with boldness the mystery of the gospel.” Parrēsia is free and open speech (dictionary), the “boldness and access” of Ephesians 3:12; it returns as a verb in v20. The mystery is the one of 3:3–9, the nations made fellow heirs (dictionary). The KJV reads “that I may open my mouth boldly, to make known the mystery of the gospel”; the NWT 1984 reads “the sacred secret of the good news”; the TLB adds “his salvation is for the Gentiles too.”

⚠ “An ambassador in a chain.” Presbeuō, “to be an ambassador,” stands in two verses of the New Testament: this one and 2 Corinthians 5:20, “we are ambassadors on behalf of Christ” (dictionary). By custom an envoy was not to be touched; this one is chained. Halysis is singular, as at Acts 28:20, “this chain,” and 2 Timothy 1:16, “my chain” (dictionary); he is the prisoner of Ephesians 3:1 and Ephesians 4:1. The KJV reads “For which I am an ambassador in bonds”; the ASV reads “an ambassador in chains”; the Douay, from the Vulgate, keeps the singular: “an ambassador in a chain.”

Verses 21–24 · Tychicus, a close echo of Colossians, and grace “in incorruption”

“Tychicus, the beloved brother and faithful servant.” Tychikos stands in five verses of the New Testament: Acts 20:4, where he is one of the men “of Asia” travelling with Paul, this one, Colossians 4:7, 2 Timothy 4:12 and Titus 3:12 (the last two yet to come in this translation) (encyclopedia). Verse 22 is word for word the Greek of Colossians 4:8, and verse 21 is close to 4:7, which adds “and fellow slave”; the two letters seem to have travelled together. Diakonos is “servant,” as at Ephesians 3:7. The KJV reads “a beloved brother and faithful minister in the Lord”; the NIV reads “the dear brother and faithful servant in the Lord”; the Geneva prints a closing line, “Written from Rome unto the Ephesians, and sent by Tychicus,” which is not part of the letter’s Greek.

“Peace to the brothers, and love with faith.” The letter opened “grace to you and peace” (Ephesians 1:2); it closes with peace and then grace, spoken of the readers in the third person, “to the brothers,” “with all who love.” The letter greets its readers without giving names, unlike Romans 16:3 and the verses after it. That fits the reading of the letter as one sent round several congregations, which the missing “in Ephesus” at 1:1 in the earliest manuscripts also suggests; it is a reading, not a settled fact. The KJV reads “Peace be to the brethren, and love with faith”; the NIV reads “Peace to the brothers and sisters.”

⚠ “In incorruption.” Aphtharsia stands in seven verses of the New Testament (dictionary); it is the “incorruption” of the raised body at 1 Corinthians 15:42 and the “immortality” of Romans 2:7 and 2 Timothy 1:10. It is the last word of the letter, and readers attach it three ways: to the love, a love that does not decay; to the grace, grace with immortality; or to the Lord who is loved. This page does not choose. The Byzantine text and the Vulgate add “Amen.” The KJV reads “in sincerity. Amen”; the ASV reads “with a love incorruptible”; the NIV reads “with an undying love”; the Douay reads “in incorruption. Amen”; the Geneva reads “to their immortality.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “slaves” for douloi at 6:5–8, as at Galatians 1:10; “on the earth,” where Paul leaves out the land’s gift; “provoke to anger,” as at Romans 10:19; “bring them up,” the verb of 5:29; “no partiality,” as at Romans 2:11; “be empowered,” as at Philippians 4:13; “the might of his strength,” as at 1:19; “full armor,” as at Luke 11:22; “schemes,” as at 4:14; “the rulers” and “the authorities,” as at 3:10; “blood and flesh” in the Greek order; “boldness,” as at 3:12; “in a chain,” singular, as at Acts 28:20; and “incorruption,” as at 1 Corinthians 15:42.

Echoes paid. The household of Ephesians 5:21 finished; bring them up from Ephesians 5:29; the provocation of Ephesians 4:26; the schemes of Ephesians 4:14 and the devil of Ephesians 4:27; the rulers and authorities in the heavenly places of Ephesians 1:21 and Ephesians 3:10; the might of his strength from Ephesians 1:19; the evil days of Ephesians 5:16; the gospel of peace from Ephesians 2:17; the word of Ephesians 5:26; the mystery and the boldness of 3:3 and Ephesians 3:12; the prisoner of Ephesians 3:1 and Ephesians 4:1; the chain of Acts 28:20; the ambassadors of 2 Corinthians 5:20; Tychicus from Acts 20:4; and the opening grace and peace of Ephesians 1:2, returned in reverse.

Echoes planted. The same household instructions in Colossians 3:18–4:1, and Tychicus with Onesimus at Colossians 4:7–9; the breastplate and helmet again at 1 Thessalonians 5:8; God’s own armor at Isaiah 59:17; and the feet of the one who announces peace at Isaiah 52:7.

This is the last chapter of Ephesians. The next letter is Philippians 1, already on these pages.
