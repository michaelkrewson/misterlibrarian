Paul's last charge to Timothy: 'Preach the word'; 'I am already being poured out as a drink offering'; 'I have fought the good fight, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith'; Demas gone, Luke alone with him, the cloak and the parchments; the first defense and the lion's mouth; and the last greetings.
Undated by the text; on the traditional reading, Paul's last imprisonment at Rome, mid-60s AD; many scholars date the Pastoral letters later.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–5 · “Preach the word” — in season and out of season, and ears that itch

“I solemnly charge you before God and Christ Jesus, who is to judge the living and the dead, and by his appearing and his kingdom.” “Solemnly charge” is diamartyromai, as at 1 Timothy 5:21, “I solemnly charge you before God and Christ Jesus,” and 2 Timothy 2:14 (dictionary). Three words of the verse and the next stand together in Peter’s speech at Acts 10:42: “he charged us to proclaim to the people and to testify solemnly that this is the one determined by God as judge of living and dead.” In the printed text “his appearing and his kingdom” are what the charge is sworn by; the Byzantine text reads kata, “at his appearing,” and has “the Lord Jesus Christ” for “Christ Jesus.” “Appearing” is epiphaneia, as at 1 Timothy 6:14 and 2 Timothy 1:10 (dictionary). The KJV reads “who shall judge the quick and the dead at his appearing and his kingdom”; the ASV reads “and by his appearing and his kingdom”; the NWT 1984 reads “and by his manifestation and his kingdom.”

“Preach the word; stand ready in season and out of season.” “Preach” is kēryssō, the herald’s verb (dictionary). Eukairōs akairōs is a play on kairos, “the right moment” (dictionary): when it suits and when it does not. Readers take the inconvenience as the hearers’ or as the preacher’s. Eukairōs stands in two verses: Judas seeking how he might “conveniently” hand Jesus over at Mark 14:11, and this one. “Stand ready” is ephistēmi, the verb that returns in verse 6, where the time “has come.” The KJV reads “be instant in season, out of season”; the ASV reads “be urgent in season, out of season”; the NWT 1984 reads “be at it urgently in favorable season, in troublesome season.”

“Reprove, rebuke, exhort, with all patience and teaching.” “Reprove” is elenchō, the verb behind the “reproof” of 2 Timothy 3:16; “rebuke” is epitimaō (dictionary); “exhort” is parakaleō. “Patience” is makrothymia, as at 2 Timothy 3:10 (dictionary). The KJV reads “reprove, rebuke, exhort with all longsuffering and doctrine”; the NIV reads “correct, rebuke and encourage”; the Douay, following the Latin order, reads “reprove, entreat, rebuke in all patience and doctrine.”

⚠ “They will heap up teachers for themselves, having itching ears.” Episōreuō, “to heap up,” stands in one verse of the New Testament; it is a compound of the verb of the women “loaded with sins” at 2 Timothy 3:6 (dictionary). Knēthō, “to itch, to tickle,” stands in one verse too (dictionary). The Greek is “itching as to the hearing,” ears that want to be scratched with pleasant things; “ears” is akoē, and it returns in the next verse (dictionary). “Sound teaching” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 1:10 (dictionary). The KJV reads “after their own lusts shall they heap to themselves teachers, having itching ears”; the NWT 1984 reads “they will accumulate teachers for themselves to have their ears tickled”; the NIV reads “to say what their itching ears want to hear.”

“They will turn their ears away from the truth and wander off to myths.” Mythos stands in five verses: 1 Timothy 1:4, 1 Timothy 4:7, this one, Titus 1:14 and 2 Peter 1:16 (dictionary). “Wander off” is ektrepomai, the verb of 1 Timothy 1:6, “have wandered off into empty talk,” and of 1 Timothy 5:15, “wandered off after Satan.” The KJV reads “and shall be turned unto fables”; the NWT 1984 reads “turned aside to false stories”; the NIV reads “turn aside to myths.”

“Be sober in everything, suffer hardship, do the work of an evangelist, carry out your ministry fully.” “Be sober” is nēphō, as at 1 Thessalonians 5:6 (dictionary); “suffer hardship” is the verb of 2 Timothy 2:9. Euangelistēs stands in three verses: Philip “the evangelist” at Acts 21:8, the evangelists among the gifts of Ephesians 4:11, and this one (dictionary). “Carry out fully” is plērophoreō, and it returns at verse 17. The Latin adds “Be sober” at the end, and so the Douay: “fulfill thy ministry. Be sober.” The ASV reads “But be thou sober in all things, suffer hardship, do the work of an evangelist, fulfil thy ministry”; the Geneva reads “cause thy ministry to be thoroughly liked of.”

Verses 6–8 · “Poured out as a drink offering” — the good fight, the course, and the crown

⚠ “I am already being poured out as a drink offering.” Spendō stands in two verses: Philippians 2:17, “even if I am poured out as a drink offering upon the sacrifice and service of your faith,” and this one (dictionary). In Philippians the pouring is “even if”; here it is “already.” The drink offering is the wine poured out beside the sacrifices, as at Numbers 28:7 (dictionary). The KJV reads “For I am now ready to be offered”; the NIV reads “already being poured out like a drink offering”; the Douay reads “I am even now ready to be sacrificed.”

“The time for me to cast off has come.” Analysis, “a loosing,” stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). Its verb is the desire “to cast off and be with Christ” at Philippians 1:23, and the master who “returns from the wedding feast” at Luke 12:36: a ship loosed from its moorings, a camp struck and moved on. “Has come” is the ephistēmi of verse 2. The KJV reads “the time of my departure is at hand”; the NWT 1984 reads “the due time for my releasing is imminent”; the Douay reads “the time of my dissolution is at hand.”

⚠ “I have fought the good fight, I have finished the course, I have kept the faith.” Three perfects, done and standing. “Fought the good fight” is agōn with its own verb, the contest of the games, and the same words as the command of 1 Timothy 6:12, “Fight the good fight of the faith”: the order given there, the report given here. “The course” is dromos, as at Acts 20:24, “so as to finish my course” (dictionary). Readers take “kept the faith” as faithfulness kept, as one keeps one’s word, or as the teaching kept, the deposit guarded of 2 Timothy 1:14. The KJV reads “I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith”; the NWT 1984 reads “I have fought the fine fight, I have run the course to the finish, I have observed the faith”; the NIV reads “I have finished the race.”

“There is laid up for me the crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, will award me on that day.” “Laid up” is the verb of the hope “laid up for you in the heavens” at Colossians 1:5. The crown is the victor’s wreath (dictionary), the “perishable crown” of 1 Corinthians 9:25 made imperishable; James 1:12 has “the crown of life” promised “to those who love him.” Readers take “of righteousness” as the crown that righteousness wins or the crown that righteousness is. “Will award” is apodidōmi, the verb of verse 14, “will repay.” The judge is the one of verse 1, and “his appearing” is Christ’s, as at 2 Timothy 1:10. “Have loved his appearing” is a perfect, set against Demas in verse 10, “having loved the present age.” The KJV reads “Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness”; the NWT 1984 reads “From this time on there is reserved for me the crown of righteousness”; the NIV reads “to all who have longed for his appearing.”

Verses 9–13 · Demas, Luke and Mark — and the cloak, the books and the parchments

“Demas has forsaken me, having loved the present age.” Dēmas stands in three verses: with Luke at Colossians 4:14, among the “fellow workers” at Philemon 1:24, and here (encyclopedia). “Forsaken” is enkataleipō, the verb of the cry from the cross at Mark 15:34, “why have you forsaken me?”, and of verse 16 (dictionary). “The present age” is the phrase of 1 Timothy 6:17. Krēskēs, Crescens, stands in one verse (encyclopedia). Some manuscripts are reported to read Gallia, Gaul, for Galatia, and Eusebius says that “Crescens was sent to Gaul” (Church History 3.4.9, read in McGiffert’s English); the manuscripts are reported here, not checked. Dalmatia is on the eastern shore of the Adriatic, in the region of the Illyricum Paul names at Romans 15:19 (encyclopedia). The KJV reads “For Demas hath forsaken me, having loved this present world”; the NWT 1984 reads “because he loved the present system of things”; the NIV reads “because he loved this world, has deserted me.”

“Luke alone is with me. Pick up Mark and bring him with you, for he is useful to me for ministry.” Luke is “the beloved physician” of Colossians 4:14 (encyclopedia). Mark is the John Mark over whom Paul and Barnabas parted at Acts 15:37 and 15:39, and who is with Paul again at Colossians 4:10 (encyclopedia). “Useful” is euchrēstos, the word of 2 Timothy 2:21 and of Onesimus at Philemon 1:11 (dictionary). The KJV reads “Only Luke is with me. Take Mark, and bring him with thee: for he is profitable to me for the ministry”; the NIV reads “he is helpful to me in my ministry.”

“And Tychicus I sent to Ephesus.” Tychicus is “of Asia” at Acts 20:4 and the bearer of news at Ephesians 6:21 and Colossians 4:7 (encyclopedia). The aorist is read two ways: Tychicus already gone, or the letter-writer’s “I am sending,” with Tychicus carrying this letter as he carries news in Ephesians and Colossians. The ASV reads “But Tychicus I sent to Ephesus”; the KJV reads “And Tychicus have I sent to Ephesus.”

⚠ “The cloak that I left at Troas with Carpus, and the books, especially the parchments.” Phailonēs, a heavy travelling cape, stands in one verse of the New Testament; it is the Latin paenula in Greek letters, and the Vulgate prints penulam (dictionary). Membrana stands in one verse too, another Latin word, “skin, parchment” (dictionary). The books are biblia, scrolls. Readers have guessed what was in them: the Scriptures, Paul’s own papers, documents for his case; the text does not say. Troas is the port of Acts 16:8 and 2 Corinthians 2:12 (encyclopedia); Carpus is named only here (encyclopedia). The KJV reads “The cloke that I left at Troas with Carpus”; the NWT 1984 reads “and the scrolls, especially the parchments”; the NIV reads “and my scrolls, especially the parchments.”

Verses 14–18 · Alexander the coppersmith, the first defense, and “the lion’s mouth”

“Alexander the coppersmith did me much harm … he strongly opposed our words.” Chalkeus, a worker in copper or bronze, stands in one verse of the New Testament. An Alexander is handed over to Satan with Hymenaeus at 1 Timothy 1:20, and an Alexander is put forward by the Jews in the riot at Ephesus at Acts 19:33; whether either is this man is not decided (encyclopedia). “Opposed” is anthistēmi, the verb of Jannes and Jambres at 2 Timothy 3:8. The KJV reads “Alexander the coppersmith did me much evil”; the NIV reads “Alexander the metalworker did me a great deal of harm”; the ASV reads “for he greatly withstood our words.”

⚠ “The Lord will repay him according to his works.” The words are the formula of Psalm 62:12 (62:13 in the Hebrew, 61:13 in the Greek), “you repay each according to his work,” and of Proverbs 24:12 (yet to come in this translation; Hebrew and Greek checked). The printed text has the future, apodōsei, a statement; the Byzantine text has the optative, apodōiē, a wish, and so the KJV: “the Lord reward him according to his works.” The NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah will repay him according to his deeds.” The Hebrew of those two texts does not carry the name: Psalm 62 has Adonai, “Lord,” and Proverbs 24:12 has only “he who weighs the hearts,” where the Greek supplies kyrios. In this chapter the one who repays is the Lord who awards in verse 8, so this translation keeps “the Lord.” The ASV reads “the Lord will render to him according to his works.”

⚠ “At my first defense … all forsook me; may it not be counted against them.” “Defense” is apologia, the courtroom word of Acts 22:1 and Acts 25:16 (dictionary). Readers take the first defense as a first hearing in the trial still under way, or as an earlier trial. Eusebius (Church History 2.22.2, read in McGiffert’s English) takes the second view: “it is said” that after his defense Paul “was sent again upon the ministry of preaching,” and suffered martyrdom on coming to Rome “a second time.” “Forsook” is the verb of verse 10. “Counted” is logizomai (dictionary), and the wish is close to Stephen’s at Acts 7:60, “Lord, do not set this sin against them.” The KJV reads “but all men forsook me: I pray God that it may not be laid to their charge”; the Douay reads “may it not be laid to their charge”; the NIV reads “but everyone deserted me. May it not be held against them.”

⚠ “The Lord stood by me and empowered me … and I was rescued out of the lion’s mouth.” “Stood by me” is paristēmi, the same form as at Acts 27:23, “there stood by me this night an angel” (dictionary); at Acts 23:11, “the Lord stood by him,” the verb is ephistēmi, the verb of verses 2 and 6; “empowered” is the verb of 2 Timothy 2:1 and 1 Timothy 1:12 (dictionary). “The proclamation” is kērygma, as at 1 Corinthians 1:21, and “fully carried out” is the verb of verse 5. “Out of the lion’s mouth” is the cry of Psalm 22:21 (22:22 in the Hebrew, 21:22 in the Greek), “save me from the lion’s mouth,” and the Greek of 1 Maccabees 2:60 has the same verb and phrase of Daniel, “rescued out of the mouth of lions” (neither on these pages; Greek checked). Readers take the lion as a real danger of death; as Nero, as Eusebius does, “as is probable on account of the latter’s cruelty” (Church History 2.22.4, the same English); or as a proverb for mortal danger. The KJV reads “Notwithstanding the Lord stood with me, and strengthened me”; the NWT 1984 reads “the Lord stood near me and infused power into me”; the ASV reads “and I was delivered out of the mouth of the lion.”

“The Lord will rescue me from every evil work and will save me for his heavenly kingdom.” “Rescue” is rhyomai, as at 2 Timothy 3:11 and in verse 17; the line is close to the prayer of Matthew 6:13, “rescue us from the evil one.” The doxology, “to him be the glory,” is said to “the Lord” of the sentence, and readers take it as said to Christ or to God. The Latin turns the first verb into a past, and so the Douay: “The Lord hath delivered me from every evil work.” The ASV reads “The Lord will deliver me from every evil work, and will save me unto his heavenly kingdom”; the NIV reads “will bring me safely to his heavenly kingdom.”

Verses 19–22 · Prisca and Aquila, Linus and Claudia — “come before winter”

“Greet Prisca and Aquila and the household of Onesiphorus.” Paul writes Prisca, as at Romans 16:3 and 1 Corinthians 16:19; Luke writes Priscilla (encyclopedia, encyclopedia). The household of Onesiphorus is the one of 2 Timothy 1:16 (encyclopedia). The KJV reads “Salute Prisca and Aquila, and the household of Onesiphorus”; the NIV reads “Greet Priscilla and Aquila.”

“Erastus remained at Corinth, and Trophimus I left sick at Miletus.” An Erastus is sent ahead with Timothy at Acts 19:22, and an Erastus is “the city’s treasurer” at Romans 16:23; whether they are one man is not decided (encyclopedia). Trophimos stands in three verses: among the companions “of Asia” at Acts 20:4, “Trophimus the Ephesian” at Acts 21:29, and here (encyclopedia). “Sick” is astheneō (dictionary). The KJV reads “Erastus abode at Corinth: but Trophimus have I left at Miletum sick”; the ASV reads “Erastus remained at Corinth: but Trophimus I left at Miletus sick.”

“Do your best to come before winter.” The request of verse 9 again, now with a season: sea travel became hazardous in autumn, as at Acts 27:9, “the voyage by now being hazardous because the Fast also had already gone by,” and readers connect it with the cloak of verse 13. Linos stands in one verse of the New Testament (encyclopedia), and Eubulus, Pudens and Claudia are named only here as well. Irenaeus says that the apostles “committed into the hands of Linus the office of the episcopate” at Rome, and “of this Linus, Paul makes mention in the Epistles to Timothy” (Against Heresies 3.3.3, read in the English of the Ante-Nicene Fathers). The KJV reads “Do thy diligence to come before winter”; the NWT 1984 reads “Do your utmost to arrive before winter”; the NIV reads “and all the brothers and sisters.”

“The Lord be with your spirit. Grace be with you all.” “Your spirit” is singular, Timothy’s; “you all” is plural, as in the last line of the first letter, 1 Timothy 6:21. The Byzantine text adds “Jesus Christ” and “Amen.” The Geneva, after the Textus Receptus, prints a closing note that is not part of the letter: “The second Epistle written from Rome unto Timothy, the first Bishop elected of the Church of Ephesus, when Paul was presented the second time before the Emperor Nero.” The ASV reads “The Lord be with thy spirit. Grace be with you”; the NIV reads “Grace be with you all.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “solemnly charge,” as at 1 Timothy 5:21; “appearing,” as at 1:10; “stand ready” and “has come” for one verb; “reprove,” the verb of 3:16’s reproof; “heap up,” as at 3:6; “ears” twice for one word; “myths” and “wander off,” as in 1 Timothy; “carry out fully” and “fully carried out” for one verb; “poured out as a drink offering” and “cast off,” as in Philippians; “the good fight,” as at 1 Timothy 6:12; “the course,” as at Acts 20:24; “award” and “repay” for one verb; “loved” twice; “forsaken,” as at Mark 15:34; “useful,” as at 2:21; “opposed,” as at 3:8; “the Lord” at verse 14, where the NWT prints “Jehovah”; “empowered,” as at 2:1; and “rescue,” as at 3:11.

Echoes paid. The solemn charge of 1 Timothy 5:21 and the judge of living and dead of Acts 10:42; the reproof of 2 Timothy 3:16; heaped from 2 Timothy 3:6; the myths of 1 Timothy 1:4; the drink offering of Philippians 2:17 and the casting off of Philippians 1:23; the good fight of 1 Timothy 6:12 and the course of Acts 20:24; Demas and Luke of Colossians 4:14; Mark from Acts 15:39; useful from 2 Timothy 2:21; Alexander of 1 Timothy 1:20; opposed from 2 Timothy 3:8; empowered from 2 Timothy 2:1; the Lord who rescued me at 2 Timothy 3:11; Prisca of Romans 16:3; Onesiphorus of 2 Timothy 1:16; and Trophimus of Acts 21:29.

Echoes planted. “The appearing” again at Titus 2:13; Tychicus at Titus 3:12; and Apollos at Titus 3:13 (all yet to come in this translation).

This is the last chapter of 2 Timothy. Next in the Pastoral letters: Titus 2. Older men and older women, younger women and younger men, slaves, and “the grace of God has appeared.”
