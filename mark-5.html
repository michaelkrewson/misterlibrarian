Crossing to the region of the Gerasenes, Jesus casts a legion of demons out of a man living among the tombs and into a herd of pigs, which rush into the sea and drown. Back across the lake, a synagogue ruler named Jairus begs him to heal his dying daughter; on the way, a woman who has bled for twelve years touches his cloak and is healed. Word comes that the girl has died, but Jesus raises her anyway: 'Talitha cumi — Little girl, I say to you, arise.'
c. AD 28-29, corresponding to the same two miracle stories told in Matthew 8-9, here given in fuller detail — Mark names the demoniac's legion and the drowned herd's count, about two thousand pigs, details Matthew omits.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–13 · εἰς τὴν χώραν τῶν Γερασηνῶν eis tēn chōran tōn Gerasēnōn — whose country, exactly?

⚠ The region's own name is disputed, and the dispute is old. KJV/RV60 read “Gadarenes,” following the Textus Receptus; ASV, NIV and NVI read “Gerasenes,” following the earlier manuscripts — the reading kept here. A third candidate, Gergesa, shows up in some witnesses too. The geography explains the argument: Gerasa (modern Jerash) sits some thirty miles from the lake, an odd anchor for a story that ends with pigs running downhill into the water in the same verse; Gadara is closer, about six miles off; a small site directly on the shore, sometimes identified as Gergesa, fits the terrain best of all. This translation follows the earlier text and lets the geographical awkwardness stand rather than solving it by picking the tidiest name.

⚠ Nobody could bind him, and Mark makes sure the reader knows it took real, repeated effort to fail. Shackles and chains, more than once, torn apart from the inside — the description exists to establish a specific kind of strength before Jesus ever speaks a word to him.

“Legion” is a loaded word to put in an unclean spirit's mouth in a Roman province: a legion was some five to six thousand soldiers, the empire's own unit of occupying force, borrowed here to describe the size of the crowd inside one man. The word choice is not neutral in a region that had Roman legions stationed nearby.

⚠ The negotiation itself is strange and Mark does not smooth it. The spirits beg to stay in the region (v10), then beg to enter the pigs (v12) — a request Jesus grants without recorded comment — and the herd's destruction (about two thousand, v13) is the direct, immediate result. The text does not explain why the second request is allowed when the first is not, or comment on the pigs' owners at all.

Verses 14–20 · ὕπαγε εἰς τὸν οἶκόν σου… καὶ ἀπάγγειλον hypage eis ton oikon sou… kai apangeilon — the one man Jesus tells to talk

⚠ The town is more afraid of the healed man than the possessed one. Verse 15 names the fear directly, at the exact moment the man is sitting, dressed, and sane — not while he was breaking chains among the tombs. What frightens them is not danger but a change in kind they cannot account for, and their response is to ask Jesus to leave (v17), the only request in this chapter he does not argue with.

⚠ This is the one clear exception to the silence Mark has kept building since chapter 1. Every other cleansed or delivered person in this Gospel so far has been told to say nothing, or has spread the word against instruction. Here Jesus does the opposite on purpose: “go home… and report” (v19). The man is a Gentile, in Gentile territory, with no synagogue audience and no crowd of scribes reading Messianic claims into a healing — the one setting in the Gospel so far where public proclamation costs nothing politically, and Jesus lifts the usual restriction exactly there.

The Decapolis (v20) — ten Greek cities east of the Jordan — is named as the man's own mission field, the first person in this Gospel commissioned to preach who is neither a fisherman nor a disciple Jesus called in person.

Verses 21–24 · τὸ θυγάτριόν μου ἐσχάτως ἔχει to thygatrion mou eschatōs echei — a ruler falls at the feet of a man his own institution distrusts

Jairus is named — unusual in Mark, where most people Jesus heals go unnamed — and titled archisynagōgos, a synagogue ruler, one of the very class of official who has already been arguing with Jesus since chapter 2 and plotting against him since chapter 3. He falls at Jesus's feet anyway, in public, for his daughter.

⚠ “My little daughter” — thygatrion, a diminutive, the kind of word a parent uses rather than an official. The urgency in v23 is medical and immediate: at the point of death, not merely ill. The crowd that follows (v24) is the same crowd that will delay him for the rest of this scene.

Verses 25–34 · τίς μου ἥψατο τῶν ἱματίων tis mou hēpsato tōn himatiōn — touched by a crowd, touched by one woman

⚠ Twelve years of bleeding (v25) sits inside a story about a twelve-year-old girl (v42) without Mark ever remarking on the coincidence — two lives measured by the same number, one running out as the other has just begun.

The woman's condition made her ritually unclean under the law (Leviticus 15:25-27, now on these pages), which means every person she brushed against in this crowd (v27) she technically made unclean in turn — a detail the text does not spell out but the original audience would have felt immediately. She touches Jesus anyway, from behind, expecting to go unnoticed.

“Who touched my garments?” against the disciples' — almost incredulous — “you see the crowd pressing you” (v31) draws the exact line this scene is built on: a crowd presses Jesus by accident, constantly; one person touches him on purpose, once, and the story stops for her alone. Power (dynamis) leaving him (v30) is described almost physically, a quantity that moves and can be perceived leaving, not a metaphor for attention.

“Daughter” (v34) is the only time in this Gospel Jesus calls a grown woman by this word — the same relationship-word Jairus has just used of the girl he is racing home to. Mark does not comment on the echo. It simply sits between the two halves of one interrupted errand.

Verses 35–43 · Ταλιθα κουμ talitha koum — three witnesses, one phrase left untranslated on purpose

⚠ “Peter and James and John” (v37) appear together here for the first time as a set — the same three, and only these three, Jesus will take up the mountain at the transfiguration (9:2) and further into the garden at Gethsemane (14:33). This scene is the first time Mark marks them out as a distinct inner group, before either of the later scenes gives a reason why.

The mourners' laughter (v40) at “the child is not dead but sleeping” is not politeness failing; it is people who have just been wailing (v38) switching instantly to scorn for a claim they take as obviously false — and Jesus puts all of them outside before he does anything else.

“Talitha koum” is Aramaic, the language Jesus actually spoke, kept untranslated in the Greek text and then translated immediately afterward — the same pattern this Gospel already used for “Boanerges” at 3:17. Mark preserves the actual sound of what was said before explaining it, twice now, as if the original words themselves are part of what is being reported.

⚠ The order to keep silent (v43) returns immediately after the one chapter that just broke it — a Gentile in Gentile territory was told to proclaim everywhere (v19); a Jewish official's daughter, raised in a Jewish household, is met with the opposite instruction. The text draws the line differently each time and never states the rule connecting the two decisions.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: "Gerasenes" (v1) over KJV/RV60's "Gadarenes," following the earlier manuscripts rather than the Textus Receptus; "Legion" (v9) kept as the loaded Roman military term it is, not softened to a generic "many"; "Daughter" (v34) kept as the same relationship-word Jairus uses of his own child (v23), not varied for elegance; and "Talitha koum" (v41) preserved in Aramaic and then translated, matching this Gospel's own treatment of "Boanerges" at 3:17.

Echoes paid. The inner three, Peter and James and John (v37), named together here for the first time before either later scene explains why; the silence command (v43), returning immediately after the one chapter that broke it for a Gentile audience (v19); and power going out from him (v30), described as something that moves and can be perceived leaving, not a metaphor.

Echoes planted. Peter, James, and John will be taken up the mountain at the transfiguration and further into Gethsemane — the same three, marked out first in this ordinary house in Capernaum. The twelve years shared by the woman's affliction and the girl's age (vv25, 42), left uncommented. And the Decapolis mission (v20), the first person in this Gospel sent to preach who was neither called in person nor a fisherman.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Which region this chapter is actually set in (v1), a genuine and old textual crux, not a translation choice. Why the spirits' second request is granted when the first is refused (vv10-13), left without comment in the Greek. And why silence is commanded here (v43) but lifted entirely at v19, two decisions in one Gospel with no stated rule connecting them.
