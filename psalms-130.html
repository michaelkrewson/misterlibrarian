“Out of the depths I have called you, Jehovah”: one of the songs of ascents, in eight verses. A voice from the deeps asks to be heard, asks who could stand if God kept iniquities, says that forgiveness is with him so that he may be feared, waits for him more than watchmen for the morning, twice, and turns to tell Israel to hope, because with Jehovah are steadfast love and plentiful redemption, and he will redeem Israel from all his iniquities.
The heading names no author and no event, and the psalm does not either; it is not dated, and it is filed here under the monarchy as an undated psalm, as Psalm 121 is. It is one of the fifteen songs of ascents, Psalms 120 to 134.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verse 1 · Psalm 130:1 “Out of the depths I have called you, Jehovah” — a song of ascents, and the depths that are the sea’s

⭐ “Out of the depths”. Mima’amaqqim is a plural, “from the deeps” (dictionary), and this exact form stands in no other verse. Its relatives are the depths of water: ma’amaqqei, “the depths of the sea” (Isaiah 51:10) and “the depths of the waters” (Psalm 69:3 and 69:15), and of Tyre broken “in the depths of the waters” (Ezekiel 27:34). So the first line is the cry of someone who is under. The KJV has “Out of the depths have I cried unto thee”, the NIV “Out of the depths I cry to you”, the NWT 1984 “Out of the depths I have called upon you”, the Douay “Out of the depths I have cried to thee” and the Geneva “Out of the deep places have I called unto thee”. The TLB supplies “from the depths of despair”, which the Hebrew does not say; it says only depths. The verb is qara, to call, the one that opens the first of the fifteen: “I called, and he answered me” (Psalm 120:1).

⭐ The heading, and the numbers. “A song of ascents” is the heading of fifteen psalms in a row, 120 to 134 (dictionary); the form shir hamma’alot stands in fourteen verses of the Hebrew Bible, and Psalm 121 has the variant shir lamma’alot (Psalm 121:1, now on these pages). Unlike Psalm 19, where the heading is a verse of its own, here the Hebrew counts the heading and the first line as one verse, so the verse numbers on this page are the same as in the seven versions on the shelf: the KJV, the ASV, the NIV, the NWT 1984, the TLB, the Geneva and the Douay all print the first line of the psalm as verse 1.

Verse 2 · Psalm 130:2 “let your ears be attentive to the voice of my supplications”

⭐ “Hear my voice”. Shim’ah is a command with an added syllable, “do hear”, and the voice is qol, spoken twice, “my voice” and “the voice of my supplications”. Tachanunai are supplications, requests for favor (dictionary). The KJV has “supplications”, the ASV “supplications”, the NIV “my cry for mercy”, the NWT 1984 “my entreaties”, the Douay “supplication” and the Geneva “my prayers”. The TLB shrinks the line to “Hear me! Answer! Help me!”.

⭐ “Let your ears be attentive”. Oznekha qashuvot: the pair “your ears, attentive” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one and Solomon’s prayer when the house is finished, “let your eyes be open and your ears attentive to the prayer of this place” (2 Chronicles 6:40). The next chapter has it again with “my” for “your”, in God’s answer, “now my eyes will be open and my ears attentive” (2 Chronicles 7:15, now on these pages). The psalmist asks for the thing that God promises to the house of prayer. The KJV has “let thine ears be attentive”, the NWT 1984 “May your ears prove to be attentive”, the Geneva “let thine ears attend” and the Douay “Let thy ears be attentive”.

Verses 3–4 · Psalm 130:3–4 “If you keep iniquities, Yah, who can stand?” and “with you is forgiveness, so that you may be feared”

⭐ “Keep iniquities”. Avonot are iniquities (dictionary), and tishmar is the verb shamar, to keep, guard, watch (dictionary), the same verb as the “watchmen”, shomrim, of verse 6: God as a watchman of the guilty is set against the psalmist as a watchman of the dawn. Four of the seven print “mark”, and the other three each go their own way. The KJV has “shouldest mark iniquities”, the ASV “shouldest mark iniquities”, the Douay “wilt mark iniquities” and the Geneva “straightly markest iniquities”; the NIV has “kept a record of sins”, the NWT 1984 “If errors were what you watch”, and the TLB “if you keep in mind our sins”. Yah is the short form of the name (dictionary), joined to the verb by a hyphen, and then comes Adonai, the Lord. The ASV prints the first “Jehovah” and the second “Lord”; the KJV prints both “Lord”; the NWT 1984 prints “O Jah, O Jehovah”.

⭐ “Who can stand?” Mi ya’amod is a question that stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible, and in the other three it has a “before”: “who can stand before you from the time of your anger?” (Psalm 76:8), “who can stand before his cold?” (Psalm 147:17) and “who can stand before jealousy?” (Proverbs 27:4), none of them translated here yet. Here it has none. Nothing is named to stand before, and the sense is the plainest one: who would be left standing if the record were read.

⚠ “So that you may be feared”. Hasselichah is forgiveness, with the article (dictionary), and the singular stands in this one verse of the Hebrew Bible; the plural, selichot, is in Nehemiah 9:17. The verb behind it is the verb of “who forgives all your iniquity” (Psalm 103:3, now on these pages). The surprise is the purpose clause, lema’an tivvare: forgiveness is there “so that you may be feared”, with the fear of yare (dictionary) as its purpose. Four on the shelf keep the clause: the KJV has “that thou mayest be feared”, the ASV “That thou mayest be feared”, the NWT 1984 “In order that you may be feared” and the Geneva “that thou mayest be feared”. The NIV makes it “so that we can, with reverence, serve you”, the Douay, which translates the Latin, has “and by reason of thy law, I have waited for thee”, and the TLB “But you forgive! What an awesome thing this is!”. Four, one, one and one make the seven.

Verses 5–6 · Psalm 130:5–6 “I wait for Jehovah, my soul waits” — and “watchmen for the morning”

⭐ Wait, wait, hope. The verse is built of two verbs for waiting. Qavah is to wait for, to look for (dictionary), and it is spoken twice, qiviti, “I wait”, and qivta nafshi, “my soul waits” (dictionary); yachal is to hope (dictionary), and it comes in the third clause, hochalti, “I hope”, with “for his word” in front of it. “I wait for Jehovah” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, Psalm 40:2, and Jacob’s cry in the middle of his blessings, “For your salvation I have waited, Jehovah” (Genesis 49:18, now on these pages). The KJV has “I wait for the Lord, my soul doth wait, and in his word do I hope”, the ASV “I wait for Jehovah, my soul doth wait, And in his word do I hope”, and the NWT 1984 “I have hoped, O Jehovah, my soul has hoped, And for his word I have waited”, which swaps the two verbs. The NIV turns the soul into “my whole being waits”, and the Douay, from the Latin, has “My soul hath hoped in the Lord”.

⭐ “Watchmen for the morning”. Nafshi la-Adonai mishomrim laboqer, shomrim laboqer: the Hebrew has no verb in the first half, “my soul is for the Lord”, and then “more than watchmen for the morning” twice. “Watchmen for the morning” stands in this one verse, said twice, and the second saying adds nothing but time. The KJV has “more than they that watch for the morning: I say, more than they that watch for the morning”, with an “I say” the Hebrew does not have; the ASV has “More than watchmen wait for the morning; Yea, more than watchmen for the morning”; the NIV “more than watchmen wait for the morning, more than watchmen wait for the morning”; the NWT 1984 “More than watchmen for the morning, Watching for the morning”; and the Geneva “more than the morning watch watcheth for the morning”. The TLB drops the repeat, “more than sentinels long for the dawn”, and the Douay has a different line, “From the morning watch even until night, let Israel hope in the Lord”.

Verses 7–8 · Psalm 130:7–8 “Israel, hope in Jehovah” — steadfast love, plentiful redemption, and “from all his iniquities”

⭐ From “I” to “Israel”. The poem has said “I” and “my” for six verses, and here it turns to a people, “Israel, hope in Jehovah”. “Israel, hope in Jehovah” stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and in the last verse of the next psalm, Psalm 131:3, where it is followed by “from now and forever”. The reason given is a pair of words: chesed, steadfast love (dictionary), and pedut, redemption, with a word of abundance, harbeh, “plentiful”, in front of the second. This translation prints “steadfast love”, as at Exodus 34:7 (now on these pages). The shelf prints chesed three ways: the KJV has “there is mercy”, the Douay “there is mercy” and the Geneva “is mercy”; the ASV has “lovingkindness”, the NWT 1984 “loving-kindness” and the NIV “unfailing love”. For the redemption, the KJV has “plenteous redemption”, the ASV “plenteous redemption”, the Douay “plentiful redemption”, the NIV “full redemption”, the Geneva “great redemption” and the NWT 1984 “abundantly so is there redemption”.

⭐ “He will redeem Israel from all his iniquities”. Pedut, the noun, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible once the hits are read: here, Psalm 111:9 and Isaiah 50:2, and the verb is padah, to buy back with a price (dictionary). The last line returns to the word the poem opened its question with: the “iniquities” (avonot) that could not be survived in verse 3 are the ones Israel is redeemed “from all” of in verse 8, so the first question and the last answer are about the same thing. Psalm 103 says the same: “who forgives all your iniquity” and “He has not done to us according to our sins, nor dealt with us according to our iniquities” (Psalm 103:3 and 10, now on these pages), and Titus has the shape of the verse in Greek, “who gave himself for us, to redeem us from all lawlessness” (Titus 2:14, now on these pages). The KJV has “from all his iniquities”, the ASV “From all his iniquities”, the Douay “from all his iniquities” and the Geneva “from all his iniquities”; the NIV has “from all their sins”, the NWT 1984 “out of all his errors”, and the TLB “from her slavery to sin”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “the depths” for ma’amaqqim; “supplications” for tachanunim; “iniquities” for avonot, as at Exodus 34:7; “keep” for shamar, as at Psalm 121; “Yah” for the short name, as at Isaiah 26:4; “forgiveness” for selichah; “wait” for qavah, as at Psalm 27:14, and “hope” for yachal; “steadfast love” for chesed; “redeem” and “redemption” for padah and pedut; “the Lord” for Adonai and “Jehovah” for the name. The Hebrew has no written and read pairs in this psalm.

Echoes paid. The song of ascents of Psalm 121:1; Jacob’s “I have waited, Jehovah” (Genesis 49:18); the steadfast love and the forgiving of iniquity in Exodus 34:7; “who forgives all your iniquity” and “according to our iniquities” (Psalm 103:3, 10); the keeping of Psalm 121; “Yah” in Isaiah 26:4; “wait for Jehovah” in Psalm 27:14 and Isaiah 40:31; Titus 2:14; and God’s answer to Solomon’s prayer in 2 Chronicles 7:15.

Echoes planted. The first of the ascents (Psalm 120:1); the depths of the waters (Isaiah 51:10, Psalm 69:3 and 69:15, Ezekiel 27:34); the attentive ears of Solomon’s prayer (2 Chronicles 6:40); “who can stand” (Psalm 76:8, 147:17, Proverbs 27:4); the plural forgivings of Nehemiah 9:17; “I wait for Jehovah” in Psalm 40:2; the redemption of Psalm 111:9 and Isaiah 50:2; and the next song of ascents, which ends with the same line to Israel (Psalm 131:3). None of these is translated here yet.
