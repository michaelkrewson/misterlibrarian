Festus, having entered the province, goes up after three days from Caesarea to Jerusalem, where the chief priests and the leading men of the Jews give him information against Paul and ask as a favor that he send for Paul to Jerusalem, while they make an ambush to kill him on the way. Festus answers that Paul is being kept at Caesarea and that the powerful among them should come down and accuse him there. After not more than eight or ten days he sits on the judgment seat at Caesarea; the Jews who have come down bring heavy accusations they cannot prove, and Paul says he has committed no offense against the law of the Jews, the temple or Caesar. Festus, wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews, asks whether he will go up to Jerusalem and be judged there before him. Paul answers that he is standing at Caesar’s judgment seat, where he must be judged; that he does not beg off from dying if he has done anything worthy of death, but that no one can give him over to them as a favor; and he says ‘I appeal to Caesar.’ After talking with the council Festus answers, ‘You have appealed to Caesar; to Caesar you shall go.’ After some days King Agrippa and Berenice arrive at Caesarea to greet Festus, and Festus lays the matters about Paul before the king: a man left a prisoner by Felix, against whom the chief priests and elders gave information and asked for a sentence of condemnation, to whom he answered that it is not a custom of the Romans to give a person over before the accused meets his accusers; the accusers, when they came, brought none of the evil things he supposed, but questions about their own religion and about a certain Jesus who had died, whom Paul asserted to be alive; and, at a loss about the inquiry, he asked whether Paul would be judged at Jerusalem, but Paul appealed to be kept for the decision of the Emperor. Agrippa says he too wishes to hear the man, and on the next day, with great display, he and Berenice enter the audience room with tribunes and the men of distinction in the city, and Paul is brought in. Festus tells the assembly that the whole multitude of the Jews has petitioned him, shouting that Paul must not live any longer; that he found nothing worthy of death; that, Paul having appealed to the Emperor, he decided to send him; that he has nothing certain to write to his lord; and that he has brought Paul out so that, after the examination, he may have something to write, for it seems unreasonable to send a prisoner without indicating the charges against him.
Undated by the text: the chapter names no year. Its time-markers are ‘after three days’ (v1), ‘not more than eight or ten days’ (v6), ‘some days’ (v13), ‘many days’ (v14) and ‘the next day’ (vv17, 23). The text gives no year for Festus’s arrival, and this library dates the events no more closely than the book does. It follows Acts 24, where Felix is succeeded by Porcius Festus and leaves Paul bound; Acts 26 follows, with Paul before Agrippa.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–5 · Φῆστος … ἐπαρχείᾳ … ἐνεφάνισαν … χάριν … ἐνέδραν … τηρεῖσθαι … δυνατοὶ … ἄτοπον Phēstos … eparcheia … enephanisan … charin … enedran … tēreisthai … dynatoi … atopon — Acts 25:1–5: what does Festus do in his first days as governor, why do the chief priests ask for Paul “as a favor,” and what is the “ambush” again?

“Having entered the province” (Acts 25:1). Phēstos oun epibas tē eparcheia: Festus, “then,” having stepped onto his province, goes up to Jerusalem three days later. The noun eparcheia stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 23:34, where Felix, having read the tribune’s letter, “asked what province he was from.” Felix asked a prisoner which province he came from; the next governor is introduced stepping into his own. The participle epibas is from epibainō, “to step onto,” the verb of boarding a ship at 21:2 (“we went aboard”) and of setting foot in a place at 20:18 (“I set foot in Asia”) and 21:4 (the disciples tell Paul “not to set foot in Jerusalem”); it comes once more at 27:2 (not yet on these pages). The Greek names the province, not the city: Caesarea appears only as the place he goes up from. Six of the seven shelf versions print “province”: NWT 1984 “after entering upon the [government of the] province” (the bracket marks words its translators supplied), ASV “having come into the province,” KJV “was come into the province,” Geneva “was then come into the province,” Douay “was come into the province” and NIV “arriving in the province.” TLB “arrived in Caesarea to take over his new responsibilities” drops the province and makes the city the place of arrival, which the Greek does not say. “After three days” is meta treis hēmeras, a phrase that stands in five verses of the New Testament; the other four are Jesus’ own “after three days” before rising, in Matthew 27:63 and in Mark 8:31, 9:31 and 10:34. Luke draws no link, and here it is a governor’s interval between a landing and a road. Luke does not say why Festus went up so soon. When he took office and in what year is disputed among historians, and the library entry for Festus sets out what Josephus says and leaves the date open; that is reported there, not decided on this page.

“Gave information” (Acts 25:2). Enephanisan … kata tou Paulou, “they gave information against Paul,” uses the aorist of emphanizō, the verb of laying information before an official. In Acts it stands in five verses, emphanizō in the forms of 23:15, 23:22, 24:1, here and 25:15. At 23:15 the plotters tell the Sanhedrin to “make it clear to the tribune” that he should bring Paul down; at 24:1 Ananias, the elders and Tertullus “gave the governor information against Paul,” with the same verb and the same kata tou Paulou as here, and Felix as the hearer where Festus is now. The same charge-bearing is now brought to the second governor. The shelf renders the verb six ways. NWT 1984 “gave him information against Paul” is the nearest; ASV and KJV “informed him against Paul” are close behind; Geneva “appeared before him against Paul” and Douay “went unto him against Paul” describe the visit and leave out the speaking; NIV “presented the charges against Paul” makes it a formal charge, and TLB “gave him their story about Paul” changes “against” to “about.” The Greek has kata with the genitive, “against,” and no charge is quoted; Luke reports only that the information was given.

“The chief priests and the leading men” (Acts 25:2). The printed text has hoi archiereis kai hoi prōtoi tōn Ioudaiōn. The apparatus gives one substantive variant: hoi archiereis, “the chief priests,” is the reading of Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and Robinson–Pierpont has the singular ho archiereus, “the high priest.” The shelf is split by it: KJV “the high priest” and Geneva “the high Priest” print the singular (two of the seven), and the other five print the plural. Unlike 24:1, where Ananias is named, nobody is named here; the entry for Ananias covers the one the book does name. Prōtoi is the plural “first,” used of the men of standing in a community. The shipped text has the same plural at Mark 6:21 (“the leading men of Galilee”), Luke 19:47 (“the leading men of the people,” beside the chief priests and scribes who are seeking to destroy Jesus), Acts 13:50 (“the leading men of the city”) and, of women, 17:4 (“leading women”). In the singular it is Publius, “the first man of the island,” at 28:7 (not yet on these pages). Paul himself will call together tous ontas tōn Ioudaiōn prōtous, “those who were the first of the Jews,” at 28:17 (not yet on these pages). Festus will later tell the story of this meeting with different nouns: at 25:15 he says it was “the chief priests and the elders of the Jews” who gave the information. One occasion therefore has two descriptions in a single chapter, and the book does not reconcile them. For “leading men,” NWT 1984 “principal men,” ASV “the principal men” and Douay “principal men” are three of the seven; KJV and Geneva “the chief of the Jews” make a second group of two; NIV “the Jewish leaders” and TLB “other Jewish leaders” a third.

“Were urging … asking as a favor” (Acts 25:2–3). Parekaloun is imperfect, “they were urging,” the request pressed again and again; NWT 1984 “began to entreat” reads the imperfect as the start of a pressing. Then comes aitoumenoi charin kat’ autou. The participle aitoumenoi, “asking,” stands in four verses of the New Testament: here, 25:15, where Festus repeats that they were “asking” for a condemnation against Paul, Colossians 1:9, and Luke 23:23, where the crowd “kept pressing him with loud voices, demanding that he be crucified.” The noun is the one in Felix’s motive at 24:27 (there in the form charita; see the entry charin katatithemi), and the same word comes back at 25:9, where Festus himself is described as “wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews,” and in the verbs at 25:11 and 25:16. So the favor asked of Festus here is the favor he is later said to want to give. The preposition is kata again: the favor is “against him.” Five of the seven keep the hostile direction: NWT 1984 “asking for themselves as a favor against the [man],” ASV “asking a favor against him,” KJV “desired favour against him,” Geneva “desired favor against him” and Douay “Requesting favour against him.” NIV “as a favor to them” turns the sentence round so that the favor is done to the priests, and TLB “begged him to bring Paul to Jerusalem at once” has no favor and adds “at once.” The verb metapempsētai, “send for,” is the middle of metapempō, which stands in eight verses of the New Testament, all in Acts: 10:5, 10:22, 10:29, 11:13, 20:1, 24:24, 24:26 and here. At 24:26 it is Felix, “sending for” Paul for talks; here the priests want Festus to send for him, to their city. The shelf follows: ASV, KJV and Geneva “send for him to Jerusalem,” NWT 1984 “send for him to come to Jerusalem,” Douay “command him to be brought to Jerusalem” and NIV “have Paul transferred,” the last two moving from summoning to commanding and transferring.

“Made an ambush” (Acts 25:3). Enedran poiountes anelein auton kata tēn hodon, “while they made an ambush to kill him on the way.” The noun enedra stands in two verses of the New Testament, 25:3 and 23:16, where Paul’s nephew “hears of the ambush”; see the entry enedra. The plot of 23:12–21 was forty men under an oath, and a request that the tribune bring Paul down to the Sanhedrin “as though” to examine him more exactly (23:15). The structure is repeated here: first a request to move the prisoner, then the ambush on the road. The two words anelein auton, “to kill him,” are the ones the Jews of Damascus take counsel to do at 9:23, the Hellenists attempt at 9:29, and the plotters speak at 23:15. Whether the forty of chapter 23 are meant here, or whether the plan has lasted the two years of 24:27 or been made again, the text does not say; it says only that an ambush was being made as the request was made. The participle is present, and the verb is poieō, “make,” which is why this translation prints “made an ambush” and not “set.” The shelf varies: NWT 1984 “laying an ambush” and NIV “preparing an ambush” keep the noun; ASV “laying a plot” names the plan; KJV and Douay “laying wait,” and Geneva “laid wait,” turn the noun into a verb; TLB “waylay” is the English verb for it.

“Kept at Caesarea” (Acts 25:4). Apekrithē tēreisthai ton Paulon eis Kaisareian, “answered that Paul was being kept at Caesarea.” Festus does not say yes or no to the request; he says where Paul is and where he himself is going. The Greek has no word of refusal, though the sense of the answer is that Paul stays. The passive of tēreō in the forms tēreisthai, etēreito and tērēthēnai stands in four verses of the New Testament: 12:5, Peter “kept in the prison”; 24:23, where Felix orders Paul “kept” with some freedom; here; and 25:21, where Festus orders him kept for the Emperor’s decision. The preposition is eis, “into,” with a verb of staying; the apparatus gives eis Kaisareian to Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 and en Kaisareia, “in Caesarea,” to Robinson–Pierpont, which is the smoother reading. That eis can stand where en would be expected is reported here as a known feature of the Greek of this period, not checked on this page. The shelf: NWT 1984 “was to be kept” and KJV and Geneva “should be kept” shade the statement into a decision about Paul’s future; ASV “was kept in charge” and Douay “was kept in” stay with the present state; NIV “is being held” is also present. TLB has “since Paul was at Caesarea” and no word for keeping. The second clause is heauton de mellein en tachei ekporeuesthai, “he himself was about to go out shortly.” Mellein is “be about to”; NWT 1984 “about to depart shortly” and ASV “about to depart thither shortly” keep it, where KJV “would depart shortly,” Geneva “would shortly depart” and Douay “would very shortly depart” turn it into a plain future; NIV “am going there soon” and TLB “returning there soon” use “soon.” The phrase en tachei stands in eight verses of the New Testament. Where it is a command it is “quickly,” as when the angel tells Peter “Rise quickly” (12:7) and when the voice tells Paul to “get out of Jerusalem quickly” (22:18); the others are Luke 18:8, here, Romans 16:20 and 1 Timothy 3:14 (neither yet on these pages), Revelation 1:1 and 22:6. For a governor’s plans for a journey, “shortly” is the English; the Greek is the same phrase.

“The powerful among you” (Acts 25:5). Hoi oun en hymin, phēsin, dynatoi: “Therefore let the powerful among you, he says …” The form dynatoi stands in four verses of the New Testament: here, 1 Corinthians 1:26, where Paul tells the brothers to look at their calling: “not many wise according to the flesh, not many powerful, not many well-born” and Romans 15:1 and 2 Corinthians 13:9 (neither yet on these pages), where the word is set against weakness. Only 1 Corinthians 1:26 shares this verse’s sense of weight in the community. The Greek does not say who these men are, only that they are the ones with power or ability among “you” (plural, the Jews who have come to him). The shelf splits three ways. KJV and Geneva “which among you are able” and Douay “that are able” take it as ability (three of the seven); ASV “that are of power,” NWT 1984 “those who are in power” and TLB “those with authority” take it as power (three); NIV “some of your leaders” is the one that names a role. The word order of this clause varies in the apparatus (Robinson–Pierpont puts dynatoi first), and the meaning does not. Synkatabantes, “going down together,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, synkatabainō only here, so the New Testament has no second use to compare. The prefix syn- says “with” and the Greek gives no pronoun; this translation prints “with me,” as do ASV “go down with me,” KJV “go down with me” and NWT 1984 “come down with me,” while Geneva “come down with us” widens it to Festus and his staff, which the Greek does not say either. The “down” is the road from Jerusalem to the coast, the way Ananias “came down” at 24:1. Festus’s offer is a trial before him at Caesarea with the accusers present, and the thing they are to prove is atopon.

“Out of place” (Acts 25:5). Atopon is “out of place,” built from a-, “not,” and topos, “place”: what does not belong. It names behavior that is off, not a legal offense. It stands in four verses of the New Testament, atopos in the forms of Luke 23:41, here, 28:6 (not yet on these pages) and 2 Thessalonians 3:2 (not yet on these pages). At Luke 23:41 the second criminal says of Jesus “this man has done nothing out of place”; at 28:6 the islanders wait for Paul to swell up or fall dead and see “nothing out of place” happen to him. The word is Luke’s for the innocent, and Festus uses it of Paul, though he is not saying he believes him innocent: he says if there is anything. The apparatus lists Robinson–Pierpont with toutō, “this,” at the place where the printed text has atopon; the full wording of that edition is not given in the apparatus line, so the difference is reported and not described further. The shelf is wide: NWT 1984 “out of the way,” ASV “amiss,” KJV and Geneva “wickedness,” Douay “crime” and NIV “done anything wrong.” Three of those, “wickedness,” “crime” and “wrong,” make it a moral or legal offense; the Greek says only that something would be out of place. TLB turns Festus’s speech into a report (“those with authority in this affair should return with him for the trial”) and has no word for it at all. What the accusers bring when they come is in 25:7, the “heavy accusations” that Luke says they were not able to prove.

Verses 6–9 · οὐ πλείους ὀκτὼ ἢ δέκα … ἐπὶ τοῦ βήματος … βαρέα αἰτιώματα … ἃ οὐκ ἴσχυον ἀποδεῖξαι … οὔτε εἰς τὸν νόμον … οὔτε εἰς τὸ ἱερὸν … οὔτε εἰς Καίσαρά τι ἥμαρτον … χάριν καταθέσθαι … κριθῆναι ἐπʼ ἐμοῦ ou pleious oktō ē deka … epi tou bēmatos … barea aitiōmata … ha ouk ischyon apodeixai … oute eis ton nomon … oute eis to hieron … oute eis Kaisara ti hēmarton … charin katathesthai … krithēnai ep’ emou — Acts 25:6–9: what happens at Paul’s hearing before Festus at Caesarea, what are the “heavy accusations,” why does Paul deny offending against the law, the temple and Caesar, and what does it mean that Festus wants to “lay up a favor” with the Jews?

“Not more than eight or ten days” (Acts 25:6). Diatripsas de en autois hēmeras ou pleious oktō ē deka, literally “having spent among them days, not more than eight or ten.” The participle diatripsas, “having spent,” has a near relative later in the chapter: the visitors of 25:13 are “spending many days” at Caesarea (25:14), the same verb in the imperfect. The Greek gives a length of stay and not a date. It does not say whether the three days of 25:1 fall inside the count, and “among them” names no city: the people are the leaders of 25:2–5, and the city is Jerusalem only from the “went up” of 25:1 and the “went down to Caesarea” of this verse. The apparatus has a note on the number (939). The printed text, ou pleious oktō, is the reading of Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28; Robinson–Pierpont prints pleious alone in its place, which, with ē deka left as it is, would read “more than ten”; and the apparatus lists the NIV beside the bare oktō, the word “eight” without “not more than.” The shelf runs along the same line. NWT 1984 “not more than eight or ten days,” ASV “not more than eight or ten days” and Douay “no more than eight or ten days” keep the whole phrase; NIV “eight or ten days” and TLB “Eight or ten days later” keep the two numbers and drop the ceiling, so five of the seven print “eight or ten.” KJV “more than ten days” prints the ten alone, as a floor, which is the sense of the Robinson–Pierpont reading as the apparatus gives it; whether that reading lay behind it is not checked on this page. Geneva “no more than ten days” also prints the ten alone, as a ceiling, so the two versions that drop “eight” point opposite ways. This translation keeps the printed text, ou pleious as “not more than,” and leaves the approximation as Luke wrote it.

“The judgment seat” (Acts 25:6). Kathisas epi tou bēmatos, “sitting on the judgment seat.” Bēma, the raised platform from which a judge speaks or decides (see bēma), stands in twelve verses of the New Testament. Eight are in Acts: 7:5, where it is only a measure of ground (“not even a foot’s breadth,” 7:5), 12:21, 18:12, 18:16, 18:17, and here, 25:10 and 25:17. The other four are Pilate’s seat at Matthew 27:19 and John 19:13, and Romans 14:10 and 2 Corinthians 5:10 (neither yet on these pages), where the seat is God’s and Christ’s. The three-word participle phrase kathisas epi tou bēmatos, “having sat on the judgment seat,” stands in three verses, 12:21, here and 25:17, so Luke describes Festus’s sitting in the words he used for Herod’s. At 12:21 Herod, “having put on royal apparel and sat on the judgment seat, was making a public speech,” and two verses later an angel of Jehovah struck him (12:23); Luke does not draw the line between the two seats, and nothing in 25:6 hints at a fate for Festus. A closer scene is Corinth, where the Jews “brought him to the judgment seat” before the proconsul Gallio (18:12): that hearing ends at 18:16 with Gallio driving the accusers from the seat, and Paul is once more on the receiving end of a Roman seat here. The order that follows is ekeleusen ton Paulon achthēnai, “he ordered Paul to be brought,” and Festus will repeat it in his own words at 25:17. For the seat itself the shelf is close: KJV “sitting on the judgment seat,” Geneva “sat in the judgment seat,” Douay “sat in the judgment seat,” ASV “judgment-seat” and NWT 1984 “sat down on the judgment seat” print the furniture, five of the seven. NIV “convened the court” and TLB “opened Paul’s trial” print the event and leave the seat out, though the Greek names only the seat and the sitting. Caesar’s own seat comes into the next verses (25:10).

“Heavy accusations” (Acts 25:7). Pollà kai barea aitiōmata katapherontes ha ouk ischyon apodeixai, literally “bringing down many and heavy accusations which they were not strong enough to prove.” The noun aitiōma stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and is an uncommon form beside the ordinary aitia that the rest of the chapter uses (25:18, 25:27); the sense, “a charge laid,” is clear from the sentence, and the rareness of the word is the only thing there is to report about it. The adjective is barys, “heavy.” The root bar- in this sense stands in three verses of Acts: 20:29, where Paul tells the Ephesian elders that “grievous wolves” will come in among them (20:29), here, and 28:27 (not yet on these pages), where it is the adverb. This translation prints “grievous” at 20:29 and “heavy” here for the same adjective; “heavy” keeps the picture of weight that the verb katapherontes (“carrying down”) completes, as a load carried down on a man. That verb stands in three verses of the New Testament in all its forms: Eutychus “being borne down by deep sleep” (20:9), here, and 26:10, where Paul says of his old work against the believers katēnegka psēphon, which is read as “I cast my vote” (reported here, not checked on this page). The apparatus has two notes on the verse. One (940) is the pronoun auton, “around him,” which Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print and Robinson–Pierpont omits. The other (941) is the participle: for katapherontes Robinson–Pierpont reads pherontes kata tou Paulou, “bringing against Paul,” with the “down” spelled out as a preposition and Paul named. On the shelf KJV “laid many and grievous complaints against Paul” and Geneva “laid many and grievous complaints against Paul” name Paul at this point, where the printed Greek has only the prefix. The adjective splits the shelf into two groups: “grievous” in ASV “many and grievous charges,” KJV and Geneva “many and grievous complaints” and Douay “many and grievous causes” (four of the seven), and “serious” in NWT 1984 “many and serious charges,” NIV “many serious charges” and TLB “many serious accusations” (three).

“Which they were not able to prove” (Acts 25:7). Apodeixai is the aorist infinitive of apodeiknymi, “to show forth, to demonstrate.” The verb and its noun apodeixis together stand in five verses of the New Testament: Acts 2:22, where Jesus is “a man publicly attested to you by God”; here; 1 Corinthians 2:4, 1 Corinthians 4:9 and 2 Thessalonians 2:4 (none yet on these pages), where the noun is a “demonstration” of Spirit and power, the apostles are “exhibited” last of all, and the lawless man “displays himself” as God. Here it is proof in a court; the other four are attestation, demonstration, exhibition and display. What Luke reports in his own voice is what Paul told Felix of the accusers: “nor can they prove to you the things of which they now accuse me” (24:13), where the verb is parastēsai, a different word for the same demand. Between 24:13 and 25:7 the sentence has moved from Paul’s claim to the narrator’s report; the Greek does not say what the evidence was, only that it was not shown. The imperfect ouk ischyon, “they were not able,” sets it as a state that held while they brought the charges. For the clause the shelf is close: ASV “which they could not prove,” KJV “which they could not prove” and Douay “which they could not prove” agree, NIV “could not prove them” and TLB “couldn’t prove” agree with them, so that five of the seven print “prove.” NWT 1984 “unable to show evidence” is the closest to the verb’s picture of showing, and Geneva “whereof they could make no plain proof” turns the verb into a noun.

“Neither against the law … nor against the temple … nor against Caesar” (Acts 25:8). Oute eis ton nomon tōn Ioudaiōn oute eis to hieron oute eis Kaisara ti hēmarton: three “neither … nor” members, each with eis, and the verb last, after the third. The first two answer charges the book has already printed. The cry at the temple in 21:28 was that Paul “teaches everyone everywhere against the people and the law and this place,” and Tertullus told Felix that Paul “even tried to profane the temple” (24:6). Hieron is the whole temple complex, not the inner sanctuary (see hieron). The third member, Caesar, is not in either of those lists, and not in Tertullus’s other words: “a plague and one stirring up dissensions among all the Jews throughout the inhabited world, and a ringleader of the party of the Nazarenes” (24:5; see stasis). Whether “dissensions” was heard as an offense against Caesar the text does not say, and neither does it say what the accusers in 25:7 charged; it gives only Paul’s answer, which is shaped as if Caesar had been named. Kaisar, “Caesar,” in the forms Kaisaros, Kaisara and Kaisari, stands in nine verses of Acts, and before this verse in one only: Jason’s accusers at Thessalonica say the believers “are acting against the decrees of Caesar, saying that there is another king, Jesus” (17:7). The other seven are 25:10, 25:11, 25:12 and 25:21 in this chapter, 26:32, and 27:24 and 28:19 (not yet on these pages). Outside Acts the same word appears in accusations at Luke 23:2 (“forbidding the paying of taxes to Caesar”) and John 19:12 (“you are no friend of Caesar”); the three are placed side by side here, not joined. The verse’s introduction is apologoumenou, “making a defense,” the verb whose noun is apologia (22:1; see apologia), and the same verb is Paul’s own at 24:10; at 26:2 it comes again before Agrippa. The apparatus (942) has the genitive absolute in two forms: tou Paulou apologoumenou, “Paul making a defense,” in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and apologoumenou autou, “he making a defense,” in Robinson–Pierpont. The shelf follows the same split: NWT 1984 “But Paul said in defense,” ASV “while Paul said in his defense,” Douay “Paul making answer for himself,” NIV “Then Paul made his defense” and TLB “Paul denied the charges” name Paul (five of the seven), where KJV “While he answered for himself” and Geneva “Forasmuch as he answered” print the pronoun; Geneva also puts Paul’s words into indirect speech, “that he had neither offended anything against the law of the Jews.”

“Offense” (Acts 25:8). Ti hēmarton, “have I sinned in anything,” is the aorist of hamartanō, and in Acts the verb stands in one verse, this one. The noun hamartia is common in Acts, but as the thing forgiven or washed away: “that your sins may be blotted out” (3:19), “do not set this sin against them” (7:60), “wash away your sins” (22:16). The verb here is the legal use of the same root: a wrong done to someone, or against something, named with eis. That construction, hamartanō eis, is how the son in Luke 15 confesses, “I have sinned against heaven and before you” (15:18, 15:21), and how Jesus speaks of a brother who “sins against you” (Matthew 18:15, 18:21, Luke 17:4). Greek has one verb here for the moral and the legal wrong, and the shelf has to choose between English words that separate them. NWT 1984 “have I committed any sin” and ASV “have I sinned at all” keep the root, two of the seven. KJV “have I offended any thing at all” and Douay “have I offended in any thing” choose the legal word, as does Geneva “neither offended anything,” three of the seven. NIV “I have done nothing wrong against the Jewish law” speaks of wrong in general, and TLB “I have not opposed the Jewish laws or desecrated the Temple or rebelled against the Roman government” turns the three members into three verbs and “Caesar” into “the Roman government,” so that Paul’s own wording, which names a person, is not in it. This translation prints “offense,” the one English word that holds a legal charge and a moral fault together, with the preposition kept as “against.” See hamartanō and aitiōma.

“Wishing to lay up a favor” (Acts 25:9). Thelōn tois Ioudaiois charin katathesthai, the idiom of 24:27, where Felix, “wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews,” left Paul bound (thelōn te charita katathesthai tois Ioudaiois). Luke gives the successor the motive of the man he replaced, in the same words but for the form of the noun: charita at 24:27, charin here (see charin katatithemi). That is also the word of 25:3, where the leaders ask a charin against Paul; so the favor asked for there and the favor Festus now wants to bank are the same noun. The apparatus lists two small variants here: the word order thelōn tois Ioudaiois against Robinson–Pierpont’s tois Ioudaiois thelōn (943), which does not change the English, and krithēnai against Robinson–Pierpont’s krinesthai (944), the aorist against the present infinitive of the same verb, a difference of aspect that the shelf does not show; the English cannot show it, and Festus’s retelling at 25:20 uses the present form, krinesthai. The shelf is divided on the favor. “Favor” is in NWT 1984 “desiring to gain favor with the Jews,” ASV “desiring to gain favor with the Jews,” Geneva “willing to get favor of the Jews” and NIV “wishing to do the Jews a favor” (four of the seven); KJV “willing to do the Jews a pleasure” and Douay “willing to shew the Jews a pleasure” make it a pleasure (two); and TLB “anxious to please the Jews” has no noun at all. None of the seven keeps the picture of laying something down for later. The question is Theleis eis Hierosolyma anabas ekei peri toutōn krithēnai ep’ emou?, “Do you wish to go up to Jerusalem and be judged there before me about these things?” The preposition is epi with the genitive, “before,” in the same sense as Paul’s “I am being judged before you today” to Felix (24:21), so it is Festus himself, not a court of the Jews, whom the verse names as the one before whom Paul would be judged. Whether Festus means to sit in Jerusalem with the leaders beside him, or to hand the matter to them, the verse does not say, and the next verses make Paul’s answer the thing to read (25:10). The city is the one in the request of 25:3, where the favor asked was that Paul be sent for “to Jerusalem,” with an ambush on the way; whether Festus knew of the ambush, 25:3 does not say. On the shelf, NWT 1984 “be judged there before me” and ASV “there be judged of these things before me” print “judged,” and NIV “stand trial before me there on these charges” and TLB “stand trial before me” print “stand trial”; the TLB drops “there,” the one word that names the place of the trial.

Verses 10–12 · Ἐπὶ τοῦ βήματος Καίσαρος … οὐ παραιτοῦμαι … χαρίσασθαι … Καίσαρα ἐπικαλοῦμαι … συμβουλίου Epi tou bēmatos Kaisaros … ou paraitoumai … charisasthai … Kaisara epikaloumai … symbouliou — Paul’s appeal to Caesar (Acts 25:10–12): what does “I appeal to Caesar” say, what is it to “beg off” from dying, and who is Festus’s “council”?

“Caesar’s judgment seat” (Acts 25:10). Festus has just asked whether Paul wishes to go up to Jerusalem “and be judged there before me” (25:9, krithēnai ep’ emou). Paul answers in the same grammar and moves the ground: Epi tou bēmatos Kaisaros hestōs eimi, hou me dei krinesthai, “I am standing at Caesar’s judgment seat, where I must be judged.” The verb is the same krinō and the preposition is the same epi, but the owner of the court has changed from “me” to Caesar. The seat is the one Festus took the day before (25:6, kathisas epi tou bēmatos), and Paul names it by its owner, not by the man sitting on it. The noun bēma stands in eight verses of Acts: 7:5, where it is only a foot’s breadth of ground, 12:21, 18:12, 18:16, 18:17, and 25:6, 25:10 and 25:17 (see bēma). Two of the earlier sittings are close to this one. Herod, after going down from Judea to Caesarea (12:19), sat on it to make his speech, in the same city where Festus sits now. And at Corinth Gallio said “I do not wish to be a judge of these things” (18:15) and drove the accusers away from it (18:16); Festus has just offered to hear the matter himself. The verb of necessity is dei, “it is necessary,” and this translation prints “must.” The Lord’s word to Paul in the barracks was also a dei: “you must bear witness in Rome also” (23:11, houtō se dei … martyrēsai). Whether Paul has that night in mind, Luke does not say, and the same dei returns in an angel’s mouth at 27:24 (not yet on these pages), “you must stand before Caesar.” On the shelf, NWT 1984 “the judgment seat of Caesar,” ASV “Caesar’s judgment-seat,” and KJV, Geneva and Douay “Caesar’s judgment seat” keep the seat (five of seven). NIV “Caesar’s court” names the institution, and TLB “a hearing before the emperor himself” turns a statement of where Paul stands into a demand, which the Greek does not say. For “must,” NWT 1984 “ought to be judged,” ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “ought to be judged” and NIV “ought to be tried” print the weaker word (six of seven). The apparatus marks the word order only: Westcott–Hort print Hestōs epi tou bēmatos Kaisaros, with “standing” first, and Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont print the order given here. The English is the same either way.

“Wronged the Jews in nothing” (Acts 25:10). Ioudaious ouden ēdikēsa: the verb adikeō, “to do wrong, to treat unjustly,” takes “the Jews” as its object and ouden as an adverb, “in nothing.” The verb stands in five verses of Acts: 7:24, where Moses sees one of his people being wronged, 7:26, “why do you wrong one another?”, 7:27, where “the one wronging his neighbor” answers “Who appointed you ruler and judge over us?”, and here, at 25:10 and 25:11. Adikeō is thus Stephen’s verb for the quarrel in Egypt before it is Paul’s for the quarrel in Jerusalem. The noun that goes with it is a different word, adikēma, at 18:14, where Gallio speaks of “some wrong or wicked villainy,” and 24:20, where Paul asks what wrongdoing the men found when he stood before the Sanhedrin (see adikēma). Paul’s denial at 25:8 was worded with another verb, hēmarton, “I have committed an offense,” against the law, the temple and Caesar; here the object is the Jews themselves. The apparatus divides on the tense: Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont print the aorist ēdikēsa, “I wronged,” and Westcott–Hort and Tregelles print the perfect ēdikēka, “I have wronged.” The perfect puts the result in the present, and the aorist reports the act; the English “I have wronged” does for either. On the shelf, ASV and KJV “have I done no wrong,” NWT 1984 “I have done no wrong to the Jews,” Geneva “I have done no wrong” and NIV “not done any wrong” keep the verb as “wrong” (five of seven). Douay “no injury” keeps the verb in a different English word, and TLB “I am not guilty” changes it into a verdict, which is the claim of the next verse, not of this one.

“As you yourself also know quite well” (Acts 25:10). Hōs kai sy kallion epiginōskeis. Kallion stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. It is the comparative of kalōs, “well,” so literally “better.” Whether it means “very well,” the comparative used loosely, or “better than you say, better than your question lets on,” the Greek does not mark. Lexicons and grammars are reported to read it both ways, which is not checked on this page. This translation prints “quite well.” The verb epiginōskō, “to come to know, to recognize,” is the verb of the two earlier exchanges with Felix. Tertullus said Felix would be able, by examining Paul, epignōnai, “to learn about all these things” (24:8), and Paul answered that Felix was able epignōnai, “to ascertain” how few days it had been since he went up to Jerusalem (24:11). Now Paul says that Festus is already finding out, and the text does not say what Festus has found out. The scene before it says only that the Jews brought many heavy accusations “that they were not able to prove” (25:7). On the shelf, ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “very well knowest,” NIV “you yourself know very well” and TLB “You know very well” print “very well” (six of seven); NWT 1984 “finding out quite well” is the one that keeps both the present tense of the verb and the “quite.”

“Beg off” from dying (Acts 25:11). Ei men oun adikō kai axion thanatou pepracha ti, ou paraitoumai to apothanein: “If, then, I am doing wrong and have done anything worthy of death, I do not beg off from dying.” The two-word string axion thanatou stands in three verses of the New Testament: Luke 23:15, Acts 23:29 and here. At 23:29 Lysias reports having found “no accusation worthy of death or bonds,” and at 25:25 Festus will say “I found that he had done nothing worthy of death,” with the same two Greek words standing apart. Paul’s sentence is the conditional that both of them answer. The verb paraitoumai stands in ten verses of the New Testament, and the clearest comparison is a dinner invitation: the guests in Luke 14:18–19 each say “I ask you, consider me excused,” in the perfect participle parēitēmenon. At Mark 15:6, where the crowd’s request for a prisoner is in question, the Greek text marks a variable reading: Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 print parēitounto, and Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont print the plain “asked,” ētounto. In the letters it is an imperative, “decline,” to Timothy and Titus, and in Hebrews 12:19 those who heard the voice of words “begged” that no further word be added to them, and 12:25 warns not to “refuse” the one speaking: 1 Timothy 4:7, 5:11, 2 Timothy 2:23, Titus 3:10 and Hebrews 12:19 and 12:25 (not yet on these pages). The word asks off: to plead to be let go, hence to decline. Paul says he will not plead to be spared the sentence if the sentence is earned. On the shelf, KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay “I refuse not to die,” NIV “I do not refuse to die” and TLB “refuse to die” print “refuse” (six of seven). NWT 1984 “beg off from dying” is the only one that keeps the asking. The connective is also marked: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print oun, “then,” and Robinson–Pierpont print gar, “for.” ASV “If then” matches the first, and KJV, Geneva and Douay “For if” match the second.

“Give me over to them as a favor” (Acts 25:11). Oudeis me dynatai autois charisasthai, literally “not one person is able to grant me to them.” The verb charizomai is the verb of charis, “favor,” and it comes back to the word Festus was described with two verses earlier: he was “wishing to lay up a favor” with the Jews (25:9, charin katathesthai), as Felix had been at 24:27 (see charin katatithemi). Paul does not name Festus. He says oudeis, “not one,” and the only person in the room with the power he is denying is the governor. The verb charizomai, in the forms that stand in Acts, is found in four verses: 3:14, where the crowd “asked for a murderer to be granted to you,” here, 25:16, where Festus tells Agrippa that it is not a custom of the Romans “to give over any person as a favor” before the accused has met his accusers, and 27:24 (not yet on these pages), where God “has granted” Paul the lives of those sailing with him. The same verb thus names a prisoner granted to a crowd, a prisoner who cannot be granted to one, and men granted to a prisoner. On the shelf, NWT 1984 “hand me over to them as a favor” and Geneva “to pleasure them” keep the favor (two of seven). KJV “deliver me unto them,” ASV “give me up unto them,” Douay “deliver me to them,” NIV “hand me over to them” and TLB “turn me over to these men to kill me” leave it out, and TLB adds a purpose the Greek does not give. Dynatai is “is able, can,” and KJV “may” and NIV “has the right” turn it into a statement of permission; whether Paul means power or right, the verb alone does not say.

“I appeal to Caesar” (Acts 25:11). Kaisara epikaloumai. The middle of epikaleō means “to call upon” (it is the verb of “calling on his name” at 22:16; see epikaleō), and with Caesar as object it is the verb of an appeal to a court. In that legal sense it stands in six verses of Acts, epikaloumai here, epikeklēsai in Festus’s answer at 25:12, 25:21 and 25:25, where Festus reports the appeal, and 26:32, and 28:19 (not yet on these pages). At 26:32 Agrippa will say that the man could have been released had he not appealed to Caesar, and at 28:19 Paul will say he was compelled to appeal, “not as having anything to accuse my nation of.” Paul’s earlier statements about being a Roman are at 16:37, 22:25–29 and 23:27, but 25:10–12 does not mention citizenship, and Luke does not say what law the appeal rests on. What the appeal was in Roman law is partly answered by later Roman sources, read here in the Latin of the Digest, which quotes earlier jurists. Ulpian (On the Office of the Proconsul, book 8) says that the Julian law on public violence binds anyone who, holding imperium or power, kills or flogs a Roman citizen adversus provocationem, “against his appeal,” or orders anything done, or a thing put round his neck so that he be tortured (Digest 48.6.7); and Maecianus gives the same law as forbidding anyone to bind a defendant or hinder him from being present at Rome within a set time (48.6.8). A citizen’s appeal against a magistrate’s power is therefore attested in those texts, and protected by statute. The Digest’s title on appeals (49.1) is built around appeals from a verdict, and says of the emperor that “it is foolish to remark that one may not appeal from the emperor, since he is the one appealed to” (49.2.1, Ulpian: stultum est illud admonere a principe appellare fas non esse, cum ipse sit qui provocatur); an appeal before any verdict is allowed there only narrowly, by Scaevola when a judge orders an inquiry (quaestio) in a civil case, or in a criminal case “if he does this against the laws” (49.5.2: ante sententiam appellari potest … si contra leges hoc faciat). Paul’s appeal at 25:11 comes before any verdict, and Luke gives it no stated ground. Pliny, governor of Bithynia, writes to the emperor that Christians who were Roman citizens he marked down to be sent to the city (Epistles 10.96.4: quia cives Romani erant, adnotavi in urbem remittendos): a governor sending citizens to Rome on his own note, with no appeal mentioned. These are later jurists and a later letter, quoted here with their dates reported and not checked, and none describes Festus’s own procedure; so they do not settle whether the appeal was a right Festus had to allow or one he could refuse, or what part his council played. The Cicero and Livy passages read at 22:25 concern the flogging and binding of citizens, not appeal, and do not decide it either. On the shelf, seven of the seven print the verb as “appeal,” and NWT 1984 and NIV add an exclamation mark, “I appeal to Caesar!”, where the Greek printed here ends in a full stop.

“After talking with the council” (Acts 25:12). Tote ho Phēstos syllalēsas meta tou symbouliou apekrithē. Symboulion stands in eight verses of the New Testament. In seven of them it is a plan or a decision reached together, with “took” or “made”: Matthew 12:14, 22:15, 27:1, 27:7, 28:12, Mark 3:6 and 15:1. Only here is the preposition meta, “with,” followed by it, so that the word names the people consulted and not the plan. Who they were, Luke does not say. That governors had advisers who sat with them is shown, for the jurists of the Digest, by its title on assessors (De officio adsessorum, 1.22), which lists what an adsessor does at a hearing (1.22.1: in hearings, applications, petitions, edicts, decrees and letters), speaks of an adviser who “sits with” the magistrate (adsidere, 1.22.5, where he is a consiliarius) and of the consilium of an official (1.22.6); that is later jurists’ account, not a description of Festus’s court. It is not the Sanhedrin, whose Greek is synedrion (24:20; see Sanhedrin), and Mark 15:1 has both words in one sentence, the chief priests making a symboulion with the elders and scribes “and the whole synedrion.” The verb syllaleō, “to talk with, to confer,” stands in six verses: Matthew 17:3, Mark 9:4, Luke 4:36, 9:30, 22:4, where Judas “conferred with the chief priests and the captains of the temple about how he might hand him over,” and here. On the shelf, KJV, ASV and Douay “conferred with the council” and NIV “conferred with his council” print “council,” and so does Geneva “the Council” (five of seven). NWT 1984 “assembly of counselors” and TLB “advisors” make the word a group of persons, which fits its use here. Festus’s answer is a perfect and a future, Kaisara epikeklēsai, epi Kaisara poreusē: the appeal stands made, and the journey follows. KJV, Geneva “Hast thou appealed unto Caesar?” and Douay “Hast thou appealed to Caesar?” print the first clause as a question, three of seven, where the Greek printed here has a full stop and no question word, so whether Festus checks the appeal or accepts it is a matter of tone the text does not mark. For the second clause, ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay “shalt thou go” and TLB “you shall go” print “shall” (six of seven, counting NWT 1984 “you shall go”), and NIV “you will go” has “will,” as does NWT 2013 “you will go.” The apparatus has no mark in 25:12. The hearing ends without a verdict: Festus does not say whether the charges failed, only where Paul is going, and what follows is an interval and a royal visit (25:13).

Verses 13–16 · Ἀγρίππας ὁ βασιλεὺς καὶ Βερνίκη … ἀνέθετο … καταλελειμμένος … καταδίκην … ἔθος Ῥωμαίοις … κατὰ πρόσωπον Agrippas ho basileus kai Bernikē … anetheto … kataleleimmenos … katadikēn … ethos Rhōmaiois … kata prosōpon — Agrippa and Berenice in Acts 25:13–16: who are they, what does Festus “lay before” the king, and what is the Roman “custom” of meeting your accusers face to face?

“Agrippa the king and Berenice” (Acts 25:13). Luke names the pair and says nothing else about them: no father, no “Herod,” no word that they are brother and sister. The name Bernikē stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, 25:23 and 26:30. Her family comes from Josephus, read in the Greek. In the Antiquities (20.104) Herod, the brother of the great king Agrippa, dies in the eighth year of Claudius and leaves sons by Berenice, “the daughter of his brother” (ek Berenikēs de tēs tadelphou thygatros), and Claudius gives his realm to Agrippa “the younger” (Agrippai tōi neōterōi). At 20.145 she is long a widow of the man who “had been both her husband and her uncle,” and Josephus says that a rumor (phēmē) was current that she was “with her brother” (tadelphōi syneiē), that she persuaded Polemon, king of Cilicia, to be circumcised and marry her, and that she left him “through licentiousness, as they said” (20.146, di’ akolasian, hōs ephasan). Josephus marks those items as report, and Luke mentions none of them. The same stretch of the Antiquities (20.139–143) has her sister Drusilla given by their brother Agrippa to Azizus and then taken as wife by Felix; on the identification the entry reports, that makes the king now at Caesarea the brother of Felix’s wife (24:24; see Drusilla). That this Agrippa is the son of the Agrippa who dies at Caesarea in Acts 12 (Herod Agrippa I) is likewise the reference works’ identification, not a statement of Luke’s; the entries Agrippa II and Berenice set out what is reported. On the spelling: Luke writes Bernikē, Josephus writes Berenikē in each passage above, this translation prints “Berenice,” the English form for the woman Josephus describes, and seven of the seven shelf versions print “Bernice.”

“Arrived … to greet” (Acts 25:13). Katēntēsan eis Kaisareian aspasamenoi ton Phēston is, in form, “arrived at Caesarea, having greeted Festus”: the aorist participle aspasamenoi follows the verb of arrival and does not itself say whether the greeting is the purpose of the trip or what they did on arriving. The same two verbs stand together at 21:7, katēntēsamen eis Ptolemaida, kai aspasamenoi tous adelphous, where the travelers arrive at Ptolemais and, having greeted the brothers, stay a day with them; those two are the only verses of Acts that carry a form of katantaō in the sense of arriving and a form of aspazomai together (the two searches were run separately and the lists compared). The greeting verb stands in five verses of Acts: 18:22 (“greeted the congregation” at Caesarea), 20:1 (printed “embraced” on these pages, a farewell), 21:7, 21:19 and here. The shelf mostly reads the participle as a purpose. KJV, Geneva and Douay “to salute Festus,” NIV “to pay their respects to Festus,” NWT 1984 “for a visit of courtesy to Festus” and TLB “for a visit with Festus” make it the reason for coming (six of the seven); ASV “and saluted Festus” keeps it as an act after arriving. This translation’s “to greet” is on the purpose side. Geneva and Douay “came down to Caesarea” carry the kata- of the verb as “down”; the other five print an arrival without it.

“Laid before” (Acts 25:14). Anetheto is the middle aorist of anatithēmi, “to set before” someone, in the sense of putting a matter to him for his view. The simple verb stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Galatians 2:2 (not yet on these pages), where Paul went up and “laid before” them the gospel he preaches among the nations, privately to those of repute; the compound prosanethemēn is a different form, at Galatians 1:16, where Paul says he did not “consult” with flesh and blood, and at 2:6 (not yet on these pages). What Festus wants from the king is not stated here; the reason comes at 25:26, “so that when the examination has taken place I may have something to write.” The shelf spreads over four verbs. ASV “laid Paul’s case before the King” and NWT 1984 “laid before the king” keep the picture of setting something in front of a person; KJV and Geneva “declared Paul’s cause unto the king” make it an announcement; NIV and TLB “discussed Paul’s case with the king” make it a conversation, which the verb allows but does not say; Douay “told the king of Paul” makes it a report. The matter is ta kata ton Paulon, “the matters about Paul” (NWT 1984 “the matters respecting Paul”), where the others print a “case” or a “cause.” The days are pleious hēmeras, literally “more days,” and Luke had written ou pleious, “not more,” of Festus’s own stay in Jerusalem (25:6); seven of the seven shelf versions print a plain quantity here (“many,” “several,” “a number of”) and not a comparative. The apparatus marks one variable reading in this verse: the verb dietribon, “they were spending,” is plural in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, and Robinson–Pierpont has the singular dietriben. The plural takes the royal pair as its subject; the singular has a single subject, and the apparatus line does not say which person it is.

“Left a prisoner” (Acts 25:14). Anēr tis estin kataleleimmenos hypo Phēlikos desmios: a perfect passive participle, kataleleimmenos, “left behind,” a state that Felix’s leaving has put Paul in. The participle in this form stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one (kataleleimmenos). It is the same verb that closes the last chapter: Felix, wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews, “left Paul bound” (24:27, katelipe with dedemenon). The noun is now desmios, “prisoner” (see desmios), the word the centurion used of Paul at 23:18 and Festus will use again at 25:27. Festus gives the king the fact and none of Felix’s motive, and calls Paul “a certain man,” anēr tis; he will use the same small word of Jesus at 25:19, “a certain Jesus.” The shelf divides over what is left. ASV “left a prisoner by Felix,” NWT 1984 “left prisoner by Felix” and Douay “was left prisoner by Felix” print the man as the prisoner; so does NIV “whom Felix left as a prisoner.” KJV “left in bonds by Felix” names the chain and Geneva “left in prison by Felix” names a place, neither of which the noun does. TLB “whose case was left for me by Felix” moves the leaving from the man to his case, so that Felix hands Festus a file where the Greek has him leave a person.

“Sentence of condemnation” (Acts 25:15). Festus retells 25:2–3 in one clause. The verb is the same as at v2, enephanisan, “gave information,” the verb whose imperative the plotters use at 23:15 (emphanisate, “make it clear”) and whose aorist is the high priest’s party’s before Felix at 24:1. The party is now “the chief priests and the elders of the Jews,” where the narrator had written “the chief priests and the leading men” at v2, and where Ananias had come down with “some elders” at 24:1. The participle aitoumenoi kat’ autou, “asking against him,” is the one the narrator used at 25:3, but the thing asked has changed: there it was charin, “a favor,” and a transfer to Jerusalem, and here it is katadikēn, “condemnation.” Festus does not mention the ambush of v3. The noun katadikē stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one; the verb katadikazō stands in four verses, Matthew 12:7 and 12:37, Luke 6:37 and James 5:6 (not yet on these pages), and the noun is formed from the same kata- plus dikē that makes the verb. The apparatus has one note here: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 read katadikēn, and Robinson–Pierpont has the plain dikēn (as lexicons give it, justice, penalty or judgment), without the prefix. The shelf shows both ends of that range without settling which Greek each version held, which is reported here, not checked. KJV “desiring to have judgment against him,” Geneva “desired to have judgment against him” and ASV “asking for sentence against him” name a judgment or sentence without saying which way it goes. Douay “desiring condemnation against him” and NWT 1984 “a judgment of condemnation” and NIV “asked that he be condemned” print the verdict, and TLB “asked me to have him killed” names the penalty, which neither katadikē nor dikē does. This translation prints “sentence of condemnation,” with the prefix kept.

“Not a custom of the Romans … as a favor” (Acts 25:16). Ouk estin ethos Rhōmaiois charizesthai tina anthrōpon. The word is ethos, “custom,” and not nomos, “law.” The same word is on the lips of the accusers at Philippi, who say that the men are proclaiming “customs that it is not lawful for us to accept or to practice, being Romans” (16:21, ethē), and Luke has Paul say at 26:3 that Agrippa is expert in the Jews’ customs and, at 28:17, that he did nothing against “the ancestral customs” (not yet on these pages). Later Roman sources put the principle as a rule, in the Digest: Marcian quotes a rescript of the emperors Severus and Antoninus that no one is to be punished in his absence, “for the reason of fairness does not allow anyone to be condemned with his case unheard” (48.17.1: neque enim inaudita causa quemquam damnari aequitatis ratio patitur, with hoc iure utimur, “this is the law we follow”); and Ulpian quotes Trajan’s rescript to Julius Fronto that an absent man is not to be condemned on criminal charges (48.19.5: absentem in criminibus damnari non debere). Those texts are about the absent or unheard accused and say nothing of facing the accusers, which is Festus’s wording, and both are later than Festus, with their dates reported here and not checked. So the principle is attested in later rescripts, not in the form Luke gives, and no source read here is Festus’s authority for it; Luke calls it a custom and Festus names none. The shelf divides. ASV “the custom of the Romans,” NIV “the Roman custom” and Douay “the custom of the Romans” keep it a custom (three of seven); KJV and Geneva “the manner of the Romans” give a wider word (two of seven); NWT 1984 “Roman procedure” makes it a rule of court; TLB “Roman law” makes it law outright. The verb charizesthai is the middle of charizomai, “to give freely, grant as a favor,” and carries the root of Festus’s own motive: charin at 25:3 (the priests’ request), charin katathesthai at 25:9 (see charin katatithemi) and charisasthai at 25:11, where Paul says he cannot be “given over” to the Jews as a favor. Festus now gives Agrippa as Roman custom a principle close to Paul’s at v11; Luke draws no line between that and v9. The favor survives in the shelf in two of the seven: NWT 1984 “as a favor” and Geneva “for favor.” KJV “to deliver any man to die,” ASV “to give up any man” and NIV “to hand over anyone” drop it. The apparatus has one note on the verse: after anthrōpon, Robinson–Pierpont adds eis apōleian, “to destruction,” which the other three editions lack. The death clauses in KJV “to die” and Geneva “to the death” are the kind of wording those added words would give; which Greek each translator held is reported here, not checked. Luke’s report of Festus’s answer to the priests at 25:4–5 has no sentence about custom in it; whether v16 is that answer put another way, or a further one, the text does not say.

“Face to face … a chance to make a defense” (Acts 25:16). The principle has two limbs. The accused is to have his accusers kata prosōpon, “face to face,” and to receive topon apologias, a “place” or room for a defense, “about the charge.” The phrase kata prosōpon stands in six verses of the New Testament: Luke 2:31, “before the face of all the peoples”; Acts 3:13, where Peter says the people denied Jesus “before Pilate” as the governor was deciding to release him; here; and three of Paul’s, 2 Corinthians 10:1 and 10:7 and Galatians 2:11 (not yet on these pages), the last where he opposed Cephas to his face. The two Acts uses both belong to a trial before a Roman. The same wish for the accusers to be present had been said three times already: by the tribune, who instructed Paul’s accusers “to speak against him before you” (23:30); by Felix, “I will hear you out when your accusers also arrive” (23:35); and by Paul, of the Asian Jews who “ought to be here before you and to accuse” (24:19). Apologia is the noun of Paul’s own speech on the temple steps (22:1; see apologia), and its verb is Paul’s at 24:10 and 25:8. The charge itself is enklēma, a noun that stands in two verses of the New Testament, 23:29, where Lysias writes that he found nothing in it worthy of death or bonds, and here. Festus and Lysias are the two Romans who use it. The shelf puts the “place” in several ways. Geneva “have place to defend himself” keeps the Greek noun, topos; ASV “had opportunity to make his defense,” NIV “an opportunity to defend themselves” and TLB “an opportunity to defend himself” turn it into opportunity (three of seven); NWT 1984 “a chance to speak in his defense” into a chance; KJV “licence to answer for himself” and Douay “liberty to make his answer” into permission. For the charge, KJV “crime laid against him” and Geneva “the crime” name an offense, where NWT 1984 “complaint” and NIV “the charges” name only what is alleged.

Verses 17–21 · ἀναβολὴν μηδεμίαν … ὑπενόουν … δεισιδαιμονίας … ζητήματα … Ἰησοῦ τεθνηκότος … ἔφασκεν … ζήτησιν … τοῦ Σεβαστοῦ διάγνωσιν anabolēn mēdemian … hypenooun … deisidaimonias … zētēmata … Iēsou tethnēkotos … ephasken … zētēsin … tou Sebastou diagnōsin — Acts 25:19: “religion” or “superstition” (deisidaimonia)? What Festus tells King Agrippa about Paul, a dead man called Jesus, and the “decision” of the Emperor (Acts 25:17–21)

“I made no delay” (Acts 25:17). Anabolēn mēdemian poiēsamenos is literally “making no delay.” The noun anabolē stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The verb on the same stem, anaballō, stands in one verse as well, 24:22, where Felix “put them off” and said, “When Lysias the tribune comes down, I will decide your case.” Luke does not set the governor who postponed beside the governor who did not, but the two words stand in neighboring chapters. Festus then tells again what the narrator told at 25:6, in the narrator’s own words. The phrase kathisas epi tou bēmatos, “sitting on the judgment seat,” stands in three verses: 12:21, where Herod sits to make his speech, 25:6, where it is said of Festus, and here, where Festus says it of himself; and ekeleusa achthēnai, “I ordered … to be brought,” answers the narrator’s ekeleusen … achthēnai at 25:6. On the day count the retelling agrees with the narrative: both set the hearing on the next day. The wording of that next day differs. Festus says tē hexēs, while the narrator’s own next days at 25:6 and 25:23 are tē epaurion. For the seat see bēma. The shelf renders the delay in four ways. ASV “I made no delay” and NWT 1984 “I made no delay” keep the Greek idiom; KJV “without any delay” and Douay “without any delay” and Geneva “without delay” turn it into an adverb; NIV “did not delay the case” makes the delay the court’s; and TLB “called the case the very next day” drops the word. Five of the seven keep the seat itself: NWT 1984 “judgment seat,” ASV “judgment-seat,” KJV “judgment seat,” Geneva “judgment seat” and Douay “judgment seat”; NIV “convened the court” names the act and not the furniture. The apparatus has one variant in the opening words. Westcott–Hort print synelthontōn oun enthade, “when they had come together here,” and Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont add autōn, “their,” after oun. The participle is plural either way, so the English “they” is the same.

“The evil things that I was supposing” (Acts 25:18). Oudemian aitian epheron hōn egō hypenooun ponērōn is, word for word, “they were bringing no charge of which things I was supposing, evil ones.” The verb hyponoeō, “suppose,” stands in three verses of the New Testament: here; 13:25, where John asks, “What do you suppose me to be?”; and 27:27 (not yet on these pages), where it is what the sailors surmise in the middle of the night. The noun hyponoia stands in one verse, 1 Timothy 6:4 (not yet on these pages), where the Greek has hyponoiai ponērai, with the same adjective for “evil.” Festus’s sentence says what he expected, and not what the accusers in fact said. The narrator’s own account of the hearing is different in kind. At 25:7 the Jews bring “many heavy accusations” (aitiōmata) that they could not prove, and at 25:8 Paul’s denial names the law, the temple and Caesar. Festus’s summary mentions none of the three, and Luke does not say which charges were heavy, so the two reports stand side by side without being joined. The apparatus has three variants. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print epheron, “were bringing”; Robinson–Pierpont print epepheron, the same verb with epi-, “on.” Robinson–Pierpont also reverse egō hypenooun to hypenooun egō, which is word order and no change of sense. And Robinson–Pierpont leave out ponērōn, “evil,” so that the text without the adjective reads simply “of which things I was supposing.” The shelf shows the split. Three of the seven carry a word for badness: NWT 1984 “wicked things,” ASV “evil things” and Douay “thought ill of”; NIV “crimes I had expected” names the kind of thing. KJV “such things as I supposed,” Geneva “such things as I supposed” and TLB “weren’t at all what I supposed” carry none. Five of the seven print “supposed” for the verb; the two that do not are the “expected” and “thought ill of” wordings quoted above. Whether the adjective belongs to the text is a question for the manuscripts, and the editions above divide on it three to one; which Greek text each shelf translator worked from is not checked on this page. The accusers “stood” (stathentes, from histēmi) with peri hou, “concerning whom.” Six of the seven print a standing-up wording: NWT 1984 “Taking the stand,” ASV “stood up,” KJV “stood up,” Geneva “stood up,” Douay “stood up” and NIV “got up to speak.” The narrator’s verb for the same scene at 25:7 is a different one, periestēsan, “stood around him,” with peri- on the verb and the accusative auton.

“Their own religion” (Acts 25:19). Peri tēs idias deisidaimonias, “about their own deisidaimonia.” The family of deisidaimonia stands in two verses of the New Testament: the comparative adjective at 17:22, in Paul’s first words to the Athenians, which this translation prints “very much given to the fear of divinities,” and this noun, which stands here and nowhere else. It is built from deidō, “to fear,” and daimōn; the dictionary entry sets out the range, from praise (“unusually reverent”) to blame (“over-anxious about the powers”). Luke does not say which Festus means, and a Roman governor describing another people’s worship to a king could be courteous, distant or merely vague; the Greek does not decide it. This translation prints “religion” for the noun and not the roots of 17:22. At 17:22 the speaker is Paul and the object is a whole city’s devotion, and the two roots keep both readings open. Here the word names the subject matter of the questions in an official report, and “religion” is the plain English for it; the range stays in this note. The shelf splits three, three and one, with the same versions in the same groups as the entry records at 17:22. ASV “religion,” NIV “religion” and TLB “religion” take the neutral end; KJV “superstition,” Geneva “superstitions” and Douay “superstition” take the other; and NWT 1984 “worship of the deity” prints neither word. The one version that prints neither word also changes its own rendering between the two verses: at 17:22 it has “fear of the deities,” which keeps the roots, and at 25:19 it turns the noun into “worship.” “Their own” is idias, and the word sets the questions apart as belonging to the Jews, a point Festus’s sentence makes before it mentions Jesus.

“Questions” and “inquiry” (Acts 25:19–20). Zētēmata in v19 and zētēsin in v20 are two nouns built on zēteō, “to seek.” The first, zētēma, stands in five verses, all in Acts: 15:2, 18:15, 23:29, here, and 26:3. Of the three earlier verses that concern Paul, two are spoken by Roman officials. Gallio says at 18:15, “if they are questions about a word and names and your own law, see to it yourselves; I do not wish to be a judge of these things.” The tribune’s letter says at 23:29 that he found Paul accused “about questions of their law.” Festus is the third Roman official in this book to sort the charge under that noun, and what he does with it differs from Gallio’s refusal: he says he was at a loss (v20, below). At 26:3 Paul will tell Agrippa that he knows the Jews’ ethōn te kai zētēmatōn, “customs and questions,” the same noun again. The second noun, zētēsis, stands in seven verses, counted by reading the hits, because the string also catches the different noun ekzētēsis at 1 Timothy 1:4: John 3:25, 15:2, 15:7, here, 1 Timothy 6:4, 2 Timothy 2:23 and Titus 3:9 (the letters not yet on these pages). This translation prints “debate” at 15:2 and 15:7, where the seeking is by argument. Here Festus’s trouble is how to look into the matter, and “inquiry” keeps the sense of looking. The shelf agrees with that sense when it uses a verb. NIV “how to investigate,” ASV “how to inquire” and TLB “how to decide” turn the noun into a verb. The other four use one English noun for both Greek nouns: KJV “questions,” Geneva “questions” and Douay “questions” at v19 are followed by a form of “question” at v20, and NWT 1984 “disputes” at v19 is followed by “dispute” at v20. The apparatus has one variant in v20: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print peri toutōn, “about these things,” and Robinson–Pierpont print peri toutou, “about this matter.”

“A certain Jesus who had died, whom Paul asserted to be alive” (Acts 25:19). Festus’s summary is the shortest statement of the case in the chapter. The name Iēsou stands once in Acts 25, here, with tinos, “a certain,” and the words “Christ” and “resurrection” do not stand in the chapter at all. Paul’s own phrase for the issue had been fuller: “concerning hope and resurrection of the dead I am being judged” (23:6), and again before Felix, “Concerning resurrection of the dead I am being judged before you today” (24:21; see also 24:15). Festus gives it in two verbs. Tethnēkotos is a perfect participle, “having died,” and zēn is the present infinitive, “to be alive.” The verb of claim is ephasken, the imperfect of phaskō, which stands in three verses of the New Testament: here; 24:9, where the Jews join the attack “asserting that these things were so”; and Romans 1:22. It is a verb of claim and does not itself say whether the claim is true. Luke gave it to Paul’s accusers at 24:9, and Festus now gives it to Paul. The imperfect tense allows “kept asserting.” The shelf divides on how firmly to state it. ASV “affirmed,” KJV “affirmed,” Geneva “affirmed” and Douay “affirmed” use one word, four of the seven; NWT 1984 “kept asserting” is the one that prints the imperfect; NIV “claimed” and TLB “insists” put the claim at a distance. For the dead man, NWT 1984 “was dead,” ASV “was dead,” KJV “was dead” and Geneva “was dead” are four of the seven; Douay “deceased,” NIV “dead man” and TLB “died” are the others. The wording of the report recalls two earlier verses. At Luke 24:23 the women come back saying they had seen a vision of angels “who say that he is alive,” legousin auton zēn, and the two-word string auton zēn stands in three verses: Luke 24:23, Acts 22:22, where the crowd says it is not fit that Paul “should live,” and 25:24, where Festus tells the audience that the Jews shouted that he “must not live any longer.” The same infinitive thus stands for the claim of life and for the demand for death. Acts 1:3 uses a different form of the verb for the same subject, parestēsen heauton zōnta, “presented himself alive” (1:3).

“At a loss” (Acts 25:20), and the proposal retold. Aporoumenos is the present participle of aporeō, “to be at a loss,” and it puts the confession on Festus’s lips, not in the narrator’s description. The same participle in the plural is the disciples’ state in John 13:22, “at a loss as to which of them he meant,” and Paul uses the verb of himself in Galatians 4:20 (aporoumai) and of his company in 2 Corinthians 4:8 (aporoumenoi, with the compound exaporoumenoi after it), neither yet on these pages. The shelf splits three, three and one. NWT 1984 “perplexed,” ASV “perplexed” and TLB “perplexed” are one group; KJV “doubted,” Geneva “doubted” and Douay “in a doubt” are another; and NIV “at a loss” is the one that matches this translation. “Doubted” reads, to a modern ear, as a doubt about a fact, where the Greek has a judge who does not know how to proceed. What Festus says he then asked is the question of 25:9 in reported form: whether Paul wished to go to Jerusalem and be judged there peri toutōn, “about these things.” Two things the narrator reported at 25:9 are missing from the retelling. The first is the words “before me” (ep’ emou), and the second is Festus’s motive, “wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews” (see charin katatithemi). The verbs for “wish” also differ: thelēis in Festus’s question at 25:9 and bouloito in the report, both printed “wish.” Luke does not comment on either difference, and the retelling is set beside the narrative and not joined to it. The apparatus has a spelling variant in v20: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print Hierosolyma and Robinson–Pierpont Hierousalēm, both “Jerusalem.”

“Appealed to be kept” (Acts 25:21). Epikalesamenou tērēthēnai auton: the verb of the appeal is epikaleō, the same middle verb as Paul’s Kaisara epikaloumai, “I appeal to Caesar,” at 25:11. It returns at 25:25, and at 26:32, and at 28:19 (not yet on these pages). The retelling words the appeal differently from the narrator. At 25:11 Paul’s sentence has one object, Caesar. Here he appealed tērēthēnai, “to be kept,” for the decision of the Sebastos, and Caesar is named only as the place Festus will send him. The council that Festus consulted at 25:12 does not appear in the retelling. The verb “kept” is the one Felix gave the centurion for Paul at 24:23 and Festus used of the present arrangement at 25:4. In v21 it stands twice, as tērēthēnai in Paul’s request and as tēreisthai in Festus’s order, so that the same word names what Paul asked for and what Festus granted. English can show this only if both are “kept.” NWT 1984 “appealed to be kept” and ASV “appealed to be kept” do so. KJV “reserved unto the hearing,” Geneva “reserved to the examination” and Douay “reserved unto the hearing” print “reserved” for the request and “kept” for the order, and NIV “held over” keeps one verb for both, as in “ordered him held.” TLB “appealed to Caesar” reports the appeal in the words of 25:11 and so leaves out the request to be kept.

“The decision of the Emperor” (Acts 25:21). Tēn tou Sebastou diagnōsin. The noun diagnōsis stands in one verse, this one, counted by reading the hits, since the string also catches diagnōsomai at 24:22. Its verb family stands in three verses, each in the long Caesarea case: diaginōskō at 23:15, where the plotters say the Sanhedrin will “determine more exactly” Paul’s case; 24:22, where Felix promises “I will decide your case”; and here. The first is a pretext, the second a promise that Luke does not report as kept, and the third is the name of a ruling that Festus says is to come from above him. The shelf has three words for the noun: NWT 1984 “decision,” ASV “decision” and NIV “decision”; KJV “hearing” and Douay “hearing”; and Geneva “examination.” TLB “arrange to get him to the emperor” leaves the noun out. “Hearing” and “examination” describe the proceeding; “decision” names its result, and the verb at 23:15 and 24:22 is printed with a result as well (“determine,” “decide”). Sebastos is a title and not a name. The root Sebast- stands in three verses: as a noun here and at 25:25, where Paul “appealed to the Emperor,” and as an adjective at 27:1 (not yet on these pages), in speirēs Sebastēs, the “Augustan” cohort. The Greek spelling of the Latin title, Augoustos, stands in one verse of the New Testament, Luke 2:1, Kaisaros Augoustou, “Caesar Augustus” (see Caesar Augustus, where the title is glossed “the revered one”). Josephus, read in the Greek, uses it for the title: the edict he quotes from Augustus opens Kaisar Sebastos archiereus dēmarchikēs exousias legei (Antiquities 16.162), the edicts of Claudius open Tiberios Klaudios Kaisar Sebastos Germanikos (19.280, 19.287), and he writes that Herod named a harbor Sebastos “in honor of Caesar” (17.87); the Perseus Greek of the Antiquities has no form of Augoustos. In Josephus’s Greek, then, Sebastos is the title the emperors carry in the edicts he quotes; that those edicts render the Latin Augustus is reported here, not checked. The narrative treats the two names as one appeal: the Caesar of 25:11–12 and the Sebastos of 25:21 and 25:25 are the same decision-maker, though Luke never says so in a sentence, and he names no emperor in Acts 25. This translation prints “the Emperor” for Sebastos and keeps “Caesar” for Kaisar, so that the reader meets both words as the Greek does. The shelf splits three, three and one. KJV “Augustus,” Geneva “Augustus” and Douay “Augustus” keep the Latin title; ASV “the emperor,” NIV “Emperor’s decision” and TLB “the emperor” translate it; and NWT 1984 “the August One” translates the sense of the title as a descriptive phrase.

“Send him up” (Acts 25:21). The last clause is heōs hou anapempsō auton pros Kaisara. The verb anapempō stands in five verses of the New Testament: Luke 23:7, 23:11, 23:15, here, and Philemon 12. In Luke 23 it is the verb of a prisoner passed from one ruler to another: Pilate sends Jesus to Herod because he was “under Herod’s jurisdiction” (23:7), Herod “sent him back to Pilate” (23:11), and Pilate says that Herod “sent him back to us; and look — nothing deserving of death has been done by him” (23:15). In Philemon 12 it is Onesimus, “whom I have sent back to you.” The prefix ana- can be read “up” or “back” and this translation prints “back” at Luke 23:11, Luke 23:15 and Philemon 12, “off” at Luke 23:7, and “up” here; the choice of “up” is a reading of the prefix, and the other four verses do not show which it means. The shelf prints the prefix once. NWT 1984 “send him on up to Caesar” has the “up,” while ASV “send him to Caesar,” NIV “send him to Caesar” and KJV, Geneva and Douay “send him to Caesar” use the plain verb, and TLB “get him to the emperor” changes the clause. The apparatus has the same split: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print anapempsō, “send up,” and Robinson–Pierpont print pempsō, “send.” Festus’s sentence ends on the one fact the whole retelling has led to: the case now sits with Rome, and the next lines (25:22) turn to Agrippa, who wants to hear the man himself.

Verses 22–24 · Ἐβουλόμην … μετὰ πολλῆς φαντασίας … εἰς τὸ ἀκροατήριον … χιλιάρχοις … κατʼ ἐξοχήν … ἐνέτυχόν μοι Eboulomēn … meta pollēs phantasias … eis to akroatērion … chiliarchois … kat’ exochēn … enetychon moi — Acts 25:23: what is the “audience room” Agrippa and Berenice enter “with great display,” who are the tribunes and the “men of distinction,” and what did the Jews “petition” Festus for?

“I myself was also wishing to hear the man” (Acts 25:22). Eboulomēn kai autos tou anthrōpou akousai opens with an imperfect of boulomai, “to wish, to will.” Grammars are reported to read an imperfect of wishing as a courteous or half-held wish, not a demand; that is reported here, not checked on this page. The form eboulomēn stands in three verses of the New Testament: here; 2 Corinthians 1:15, printed “I intended to come to you first”; and Philemon 13, printed “I was wanting to keep him with me.” The three verses carry three English verbs, and the one here keeps the imperfect and the plain word “wish.” The shelf mostly turns it into a “would”: KJV, Geneva and Douay “I would also hear the man” (Douay adds a comma before “myself”), NWT 1984 “would also like to hear the man,” NIV “I would like to hear this man myself” and TLB “I’d like to hear the man myself,” so six of the seven read it as a “would.” ASV “I also could wish to hear the man myself” is the one that keeps “wish.” NIV also prints “this man” where the Greek has the plain article, “the man.” Luke gives no reason for the wish. The preceding speech has just laid the case out, down to “a certain Jesus who had died, whom Paul asserted to be alive” (25:19). Another Herod in Luke’s writing wished to meet Jesus: he “had wanted to see him for a considerable time, because he had heard about him” (Luke 23:8, with thelōn), and he “kept trying to see him” before that (Luke 9:9, with ezētei). The verbs there are other ones, and the wish is to see, where Agrippa’s is to hear. Hearing is the thread. Felix, with Drusilla, “sent for Paul and heard him” (24:24, ēkousen autou), and Josephus’s Greek, read at that verse, has Drusilla as one of three daughters of Agrippa and Cypros (Antiquities 18.132, where the Perseus text gives the sisters as Berenikē, Mariammē and Drousilla and the sons as Agrippas and Drousos). That the Agrippa and Berenice of Luke are that brother and sister is the reference works’ identification, reported here and not decided on this page (see Drusilla, Agrippa II and Berenice). The verse closes on Aurion phēsin, akousē autou, “Tomorrow, he says, you will hear him,” with the same verb of hearing and no named speaker; phēsin is a present tense in a past narrative, and it is Festus who answers. Festus’s next speech opens the same way, kai phēsin ho Phēstos (25:24). When Paul begins to speak he will ask the king “to hear me” (akousai mou) at 26:3.

“With great display” (Acts 25:23). Meta pollēs phantasias turns on a noun that stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one (phantasia). It is built on the root of phainō, “to appear, to shine,” and so means a showing; that root is reported here from the lexicons, not examined on this page. The related phantasma stands in two verses, Matthew 14:26 and Mark 6:49, where the disciples who see Jesus on the sea think they see a ghost; the two words share the root, not the sense. The lexicons are reported to give the noun both a neutral sense (a display) and a critical one (a show), and the Greek does not say which Luke means. Six of the seven agree on one English word: KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NIV and TLB print “with great pomp,” and NWT 1984 “much pompous show” is the one that makes the criticism explicit. “Display” says what is put before the eyes and leaves the verdict to the reader. The setting makes its own contrast: the whole of v23 is a chain of genitive absolutes (elthontos of Agrippa and Berenice, eiselthontōn of the group, keleusantos of Festus) and its main verb is the one passive, ēchthē ho Paulos, “Paul was brought in.” The ordering and the bringing use the same two verbs, keleuō and agō, as 25:6 (ekeleusen … achthēnai) and 25:17 (ekeleusa achthēnai): at each of the three scenes Festus orders and Paul is brought. Berenice is spelled Bernikē in the Greek, and the name stands in three verses (Bernikē): here and at 25:13; the third is 26:30. Seven of the seven shelf versions print “Bernice” (one with syllable dots), and this translation prints “Berenice.” Josephus’s Greek, in the Perseus text of the Antiquities, spells the name with the extra e, Berenikē, in eighteen places, for several women and a city, among them 20.143 and 20.145, where she is Drusilla’s sister and Herod’s widow, and never as Bernikē. Why Luke’s spelling is the shorter one the Greek does not say.

“Audience room” (Acts 25:23). Akroatērion, “a place for hearing,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one (akroatērion). The Latin word is found in the Digest of a judge’s hearing room: Paulus says that an adviser, while he sits as assessor, may not handle cases in his own auditorium (1.22.5: in suum quidem auditorium). That the Greek is formed on akroaomai, “to listen to a speaker,” is reported here, not checked on this page. The English “audience” comes from Latin audire, “to hear” (also reported), so “audience room” keeps the hearing in the word, and the passage is built around it: akousai and akousē in v22, akroatērion in v23. It is not the judgment seat. At 25:6 and 25:17 Festus “sat on the judgment seat” (epi tou bēmatos; see bēma); here no seat is mentioned, and what the room is for is told by Festus himself at 25:26: an anakrisis, an examination, so that he may have something to write. The shelf names the room in six ways. NIV “audience room” matches this translation; NWT 1984 “audience chamber” and Douay “hall of audience” keep the hearing; KJV and ASV “place of hearing” spell the stem out; Geneva “Common hall” names a public room; and TLB “courtroom” is the only one of the seven that makes it a court, which the word does not say.

“Tribunes” and “men of distinction” (Acts 25:23). Sun te chiliarchois kai andrasin tois kat’ exochēn tēs poleōs, “with tribunes and the men of distinction in the city.” The Greek root chiliarch- stands in twenty-one verses of the New Testament, seventeen of them in Acts, and in sixteen of the seventeen the officer is one man, ho chiliarchos, the tribune who rescues Paul at 21:31, takes him into the barracks, and is named Lysias in the letter at 23:26. Felix’s promise “When Lysias the tribune comes down, I will decide your case” (24:22) is the last singular. This verse is the plural, and the only plural in Acts; the book records no arrival of Lysias, and these tribunes are not named or numbered (see chiliarchos). The one plural in the Gospels is at Mark 6:21, where Herod’s birthday banquet has “nobles and military commanders and the leading men of Galilee”; this translation prints “military commanders” there and “tribunes” in Acts, as the entry for the word explains, and the officers again stand among a Herod’s guests. The shelf: Douay “tribunes” keeps the Roman rank; KJV, ASV and Geneva “chief captains” keep the older English; NWT 1984 “military commanders,” NIV “high-ranking military officers” and TLB “military officers” name the function. The second group is described by a noun that stands in one verse of the New Testament, exochē, from the verb for standing out; kat’ exochēn is reported from the lexicons as an idiom for “pre-eminently,” here applied to men. “The city” is not named in the verse; the setting, 25:13, makes it Caesarea (see Caesarea). Luke does not say whether any of these men were Jews. The shelf: KJV, ASV and Douay “principal men,” NIV and TLB “prominent men,” Geneva “chief men” and NWT 1984 “men of eminence” each choose a rank word.

“All you men present with us” (Acts 25:24). Agrippa basileu kai pantes hoi symparontes hēmin andres. The participle symparontes, “present together with,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The king is addressed first, by name and then title; the vocative Agrippa basileu stands in one verse, and when Festus (25:26) and Paul (26:2, 26:19 and 26:27) turn to him again the order is basileu Agrippa, in those four verses. Luke does not say why the first address differs. The second group is andres, “men,” a word the lexicons are reported to give for male persons, where anthrōpoi would be “people”: Berenice has entered the room (v23) and is not named in the address. KJV “all men which are here present with us,” ASV “all men who are here present with us,” Geneva “all men which are present with us,” NWT 1984 “men who are present with us” and Douay “all ye men who are here present with us” keep “men,” five of the seven, while NIV “all who are present with us” and TLB “all present” widen it to everyone; whether the Greek meant her to be included, it does not say. Theōreite touton is “you see this man.” The form theōreite is the same in the indicative and in the imperative, so it can also be read as “look at this man,” and the context settles nothing; six of the seven print an indicative with a verb of seeing (KJV “ye see,” ASV “ye behold,” NIV “you see this man!”), and TLB “this is the man” drops the verb. The same form stands beside akouō at 19:26, where Demetrius says “you see and hear.”

“Petitioned me” (Acts 25:24). Enetychon moi, “they petitioned me,” is the verb entynchanō. The root stands in six verses of the New Testament, five with the simple verb, three of them here and at Romans 8:27 and 8:34, and one with the compound hyperentynchanei at Romans 8:26. The other two simple forms stand at Romans 11:2 and Hebrews 7:25 (neither yet on these pages). This translation prints “pleads” in Romans 8, where the Spirit pleads “for holy ones according to God” and Christ “pleads for us,” and the word there is the vocabulary of intercession. Here it is a plain petition to an official, and the verse is the only one of the five where the one petitioned is a man (at Romans 11:2 it is God). What the petition asked is in the participle that follows, boōntes. The shelf takes the verb six ways: NIV “has petitioned me” matches this translation; NWT 1984 “have applied to me,” ASV “made suit to me,” Geneva “have called upon me” and KJV “have dealt with me” each take a sense, and Douay “dealt with me at Jerusalem, requesting and crying out” prints two participles for the Greek’s one; TLB “whose death is demanded” drops the verb and the first person and makes it a passive. The three accounts of the approach at Jerusalem name different petitioners. Luke’s narration says that “the chief priests and the leading men of the Jews gave him information against Paul” at Jerusalem (25:2), asking a favor (25:3); Festus to Agrippa says it was “the chief priests and the elders of the Jews,” asking “for a sentence of condemnation” (25:15); and to the room here it is “the whole multitude of the Jews,” both in Jerusalem and here. The “here” is 25:7, where the Jews who had come down stood around him with accusations they could not prove; none of these narrated scenes has a multitude or a shout. Whether Festus is summing up, or simply speaking for effect, the Greek does not say.

“Shouting that he must not live any longer” (Acts 25:24). Boōntes mē dein auton zēn mēketi. The participle is from boaō, “to shout,” and in Acts the verb stands in three verses (boaō in 8:7, where unclean spirits come out “crying with a loud voice,” 17:6, where the accusers drag Jason before the politarchs “shouting,” and this verse). The nearest shouts in the book are the crowd’s at the temple, in Jerusalem: “Away with him!” (21:36) and “Away from the earth with such a man! For it is not fit that he should live!” (22:22), both said before Festus succeeded Felix (24:27). Luke does not say how the governor knew them. The wording has changed. At 22:22 the reason is ou gar kathēken auton zēn, “it is not fit that he should live”; here it is mē dein auton zēn mēketi, dei being, as the lexicons give it, the verb of necessity, with mēketi, “no longer,” added. The infinitive zēn, “to live,” is the same in both. The shelf prints one wording for it: KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay, NWT 1984 and NIV “ought not to live any longer,” six of the seven, where this translation prints “must not.” English “ought” leans toward what is proper, and “must” is closer to the necessity of dei. The verse gives no ground for the demand. The charges Festus heard at Caesarea are narrated as many heavy accusations that were not proved (25:7); the ambush of 25:3, a repeat of the plot at 23:12–21, is in Luke’s narration only, and Festus does not mention it at 25:15–16 either. His next sentence is his own verdict on the man (25:25).

The apparatus (Acts 25:22–24). The notes numbered 958–965 are eight, and seven of them are Robinson–Pierpont against the other three editions. Two are about the speakers in v22: RP adds ephē, “said,” after “Festus” (958) and Ho de, “And he,” before “Tomorrow” (959), so that both changes of speaker are marked where Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 leave the second to the present-tense phēsin. Two are in v23: RP adds tois, the article, before chiliarchois (960), so “with the tribunes,” and ousin, “who are,” after exochēn (961), so that the men are those “who are” prominent in the city. Five of the seven shelf versions print an English “the” before the officers, where the critical text has no article. Three are in v24 and are of no weight for English: RP’s pan for hapan (962), both “all”; RP’s epiboōntes, a prefixed form of the same verb of shouting, for boōntes (964), a compound that is not in the Greek text printed on these pages; and RP’s order zēn auton for auton zēn (965), a word-order difference only. One note (963) separates the editions differently: enetychon in Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and RP, enetychen in Westcott–Hort. The accents are the enclitic moi pulling an accent back, so the difference is the ending, plural -on (“they petitioned”) against singular -en (“it petitioned”), the second agreeing with the singular plēthos, “multitude.” “Petitioned” reads the same in English either way.

Verses 25–27 · κατελαβόμην μηδὲν ἄξιον θανάτου … ἐπικαλεσαμένου τὸν Σεβαστὸν … ἀσφαλές τι γράψαι τῷ κυρίῳ … ἀνακρίσεως … ἄλογον … σημᾶναι katelabomēn mēden axion thanatou … epikalesamenou ton Sebaston … asphales ti grapsai tō kyriō … anakriseōs … alogon … sēmanai — Acts 25:25–27: what does Festus mean by “nothing worthy of death,” who is the “Emperor” and the “lord,” and why can he not write his letter without Agrippa?

“I found that he had done nothing worthy of death” (Acts 25:25). Katelabomēn is the middle of katalambanō, a verb that in the active takes hold of a person or overtakes him: the darkness that may “overtake” the hearers in John 12:35, the day that overtakes the unprepared in 1 Thessalonians 5:4 (not yet on these pages). The stem katalambanō stands in three verses of Acts, 4:13, 10:34 and here, and in all three it is a taking-hold with the mind, not with the hand. These pages print three different English words for it: “perceiving” (4:13, the Sanhedrin seeing that Peter and John were unlettered), “grasping” (10:34, Peter’s “In truth I am grasping that God is not partial”) and “found” here. That lexicons list “perceive” and “find” for the middle voice is reported here, not examined on this page. “Found” is the English a judge uses of a finding, and it suits the sentence: Festus does not say what he heard, only what he took the case to be. The shelf has five of seven at “found”: ASV “I found that he had committed nothing worthy of death,” KJV “when I found,” Geneva “Yet have I found nothing worthy of death,” Douay “Yet have I found nothing” and NIV “I found he had done nothing deserving of death.” NWT 1984 “I perceived” is the sixth and is the English that matches 4:13 here; TLB “in my opinion” turns a finding into a view, which the verb does not say. The apparatus carries four notes on this one verse. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestlé–Aland 28 print the finite katelabomēn; Robinson–Pierpont prints the participle katalabomenos, “having found,” which makes the finding a subordinate clause leading to the one main verb ekrina, “I decided,” and the same text adds kai before autou (“and he himself”) and auton after pempein (“send him”). The fourth is word order only (auton thanatou against thanatou auton). The KJV sentence “But when I found that he had committed nothing worthy of death, and that he himself hath appealed” has the shape of the Robinson–Pierpont construction, one clause leaning on the next; the verse here has the two finite halves of the other three editions, “But I found … and when he himself appealed, I decided.” English needs the object “him” after “send” whether or not the Greek prints auton, so the seven versions cannot show that variant.

“Nothing worthy of death” (Acts 25:25). Mēden axion thanatou pepraxenai: “to have done nothing worthy of death.” The adjective and noun, axion thanatou, stand in this order in three verses of the New Testament: Pilate’s report on Jesus in Luke 23:15, Lysias’s letter to Felix at 23:29, and Paul’s own conditional at 25:11. Here a pronoun, auton, sits between the two words, so the string is not the same, though the sense is. The verb is the link to 25:11. Paul said ei … axion thanatou pepraxa ti, “if … I have done anything worthy of death, I do not beg off from dying” (25:11), and Festus now answers with the same verb in the perfect infinitive, pepraxenai, “to have done.” The perfect of prassō stands in four verses of the New Testament: Luke 23:15, 25:11, here and 26:26, where it is said of an event, “this has not been done in a corner.” The other three are about what a man has done, and in two of them an official finds nothing worthy of death in the prisoner before him. Lysias at 23:29 wrote “worthy of death or bonds”; Festus says “death” alone, and he is about to send a man desmion, “a prisoner” (v27; see desmios). The Greek does not say why “or bonds” has dropped out; the pairing returns, in the other order, at 26:31. One adjective, axios, has two English words on these pages: “deserving of death” at Luke 23:15 and “worthy of death” at 23:29, 25:11 and here. The Acts verses keep one word for the one phrase. On the shelf, KJV “nothing worthy of death,” ASV “nothing worthy of death” and TLB “nothing worthy of death” print “worthy”; NWT 1984 “nothing deserving of death” and NIV “nothing deserving of death” print “deserving,” and Douay “worthy of death” and Geneva “worthy of death” reorder the words but keep the adjective, so five of the seven print “worthy.”

“Appealed to the Emperor; I decided to send him” (Acts 25:25). Autou de toutou epikalesamenou ton Sebaston is literally “this one himself having appealed to the Sebastos”: autou de toutou, “this very man,” puts the weight on Paul as the one who moved the case, and the middle participle of epikaleō is the verb of 25:11 and 25:12, Kaisara epikaloumai, “I appeal to Caesar.” The title has changed. Kaisara was Paul’s word at 25:11 and Festus’s own in his answer at 25:12; Sebastos, “revered, august,” is what he says to Agrippa, here and at 25:21, “the decision of the Emperor.” The stem Sebastos stands in three verses of the New Testament: 25:21, here, and 27:1 (not yet on these pages), where it is the adjective of the “Augustan” cohort. Luke uses the Greek spelling of the Latin name once, at Luke 2:1, where the decree comes from “Caesar Augustus”; the letters Augoust- stand in one verse of the New Testament. In Josephus’s Greek Sebastos is the title the emperors carry in the edicts he quotes (Antiquities 16.162, 19.280, 19.287; see the note at 25:21), and the “revered one” is the gloss of the entry Augustus; that those edicts render the Latin Augustus is reported here, not checked. Which emperor is meant, the verses leave out; the library entry on Festus reads Josephus (Antiquities 20.182), where Nero sends Festus out as Felix’s successor. This translation prints “the Emperor” for Sebastos, as at 25:21, and keeps “Caesar” for Kaisar; the title is explained in sebastos. The shelf splits four ways. KJV “Augustus,” Geneva “Augustus” and Douay “Augustus” print the Latin name; ASV “the emperor” and NIV “the Emperor” print the office; NWT 1984 “the August One” translates the title and keeps it a title; TLB “Caesar” gives the name Paul used and drops the second title altogether. The verb ekrina is “decided”: NWT 1984 “I decided” and NIV “I decided” print it; KJV “I have determined,” ASV “I determined,” Geneva “I have determined” and Douay “I have determined” print “determined” (four of seven), and TLB “I have no alternative but to send him” changes a choice into a necessity. NIV adds “to Rome” here and again in v27; the Greek of both verses has the verb pempein and no destination.

“My lord” (Acts 25:26). Ti grapsai tō kyriō: “to write to the lord,” with the article and no possessive; “my” is supplied because the sentence is Festus’s own. The word is kyrios, here in the ordinary sense of someone with authority over a person, and the referent is the man Paul appealed to in v25, so the word is “lord” and not “Jehovah.” No name stands in the verse. Luke gives the word to a Roman governor speaking to a king; whether he means the reader to set this “lord” beside the title the congregations gave to Jesus, the verse does not say. Commentators report that the Greek-speaking provinces used kyrios as a title for emperors, a point reported here, not checked on this page. The shelf spells it three ways. KJV “unto my lord,” ASV “unto my lord,” Geneva “unto my lord” and Douay “to my lord” print the lower-case word; NWT 1984 “[my] Lord” capitalizes it and puts “my” in brackets for the word the Greek lacks; NIV “His Majesty” replaces the title with a court address, and TLB “what shall I write the emperor?” drops the title for the office.

“Nothing certain to write” (Acts 25:26). Asphales ti grapsai is “something certain to write,” and the neuter asphales stands in four verses of the New Testament: the tribune at 21:34, who “could not learn the certainty because of the uproar,” his second try at 22:30, “wishing to know the certainty,” this verse, and Philippians 3:1 (not yet on these pages), where it means “safe.” Lysias wanted the certainty and could not get it for the noise; Festus has heard the accusers (25:7) and Paul’s answer (25:8) and still has none. The English “certain” here renders asphales; at 25:14 and 25:19 the same English word renders a different Greek word, tis, “a certain man,” “a certain Jesus,” and the reader should not take them for one. The earlier letter of a Roman officer in Acts is Lysias’s to Felix (23:26–30), which gave a finding, “no accusation worthy of death or bonds,” and the instruction that the accusers speak “before you.” Festus’s letter does not yet exist, and he brings Paul out “so that when the examination has taken place I may have something to write”: schō ti grapsō, with the aorist of echō, which NWT 1984 “might get something to write” takes as “get.” Robinson–Pierpont prints the infinitive grapsai for the subjunctive grapsō (apparatus 970); the English is the same either way. The noun anakrisis, “examination,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. The verb anakrinō is the word of Pilate in Luke 23:14, of the council in 4:9, of Herod and the guards in 12:19, and of Tertullus in 24:8, where he tells the court that Felix will learn the truth “by examining him.” The noun here names that kind of inquiry, though Agrippa is not named as the judge: the appeal has already put the case in Caesar’s hands (25:12), and the hearing is for the sake of the letter. The shelf: NWT 1984 “nothing certain,” Douay “nothing certain,” KJV “no certain thing,” ASV “no certain thing” and Geneva “no certain thing” print “certain” (five of seven); NIV “nothing definite” turns the word into a specification. For the noun, NWT 1984 “judicial examination” spells out the court sense the stem carries, KJV “examination had,” ASV “examination had” and Geneva “examination had” keep the old absolute construction, Douay “examination being made” the other, and NIV “investigation” moves toward a fact-finding word; TLB “to examine him” makes Agrippa the examiner and gives the verb to the king, which the Greek does not.

“Unreasonable … to indicate the charges” (Acts 25:27). Alogon gar moi dokei pempsonta desmion mē kai tas kat’ autou aitias sēmanai: the sentence gives Festus’s reason in one stroke, and the reason is the form of a letter. Alogon is a-logos, “without a reason, an account,” and the word stands in three verses of the New Testament: here, Jude 10 and 2 Peter 2:12 (not yet on these pages), both of which have it as aloga zōa, “unreasoning animals.” Festus’s use is the mild one, “it is not reasonable,” the voice of an official who thinks a file without its charges is incomplete. KJV “unreasonable,” ASV “unreasonable,” NWT 1984 “unreasonable,” Geneva “unreasonable,” Douay “unreasonable” and NIV “unreasonable” agree (six of seven), and TLB “doesn’t seem reasonable” says the same thing the other way round. The verb sēmanai, “to indicate, to signify,” stands in six verses of the New Testament: John 12:33, 18:32 and 21:19, where the narrator says a saying of Jesus pointed to the kind of death, Acts 11:28, where Agabus “signified” a famine by the Spirit, this verse, and Revelation 1:1. NWT 1984 “signify,” KJV “signify,” ASV “signify” and Douay “signify” keep the old English verb (four of seven); NIV “specifying” and Geneva “show” print the plain verb, and NWT 2013 “indicate” agrees with the choice made in this verse. The object is tas kat’ autou aitias, “the charges against him,” the plural of aitia, which Lysias used of what he wished to find out at 23:28 and which Festus himself used at 25:18 (“they brought no charge of the evil things that I was supposing”). KJV “the crimes laid against him” names crimes where the Greek has charges, and Douay “the things laid to his charge” keeps the Greek’s neutral wording, while Geneva “the causes” has the older sense of “cause” as the ground of a suit. The sentence states a rule of procedure, that a prisoner goes with his charges, and it is the same Festus who told the accusers that it is not a Roman custom to hand a man over before the accused has met his accusers face to face (25:16). None of the three verses, 25 to 27, names Rome.

What the chapter leaves open (Acts 25:25–27). The speech ends without a verdict and without a letter. Festus has found nothing worthy of death, has sent no letter, and has asked a king to help him compose one; the Greek does not say what he expects Agrippa to supply, whether a charge, a reading of the Jewish case, or a form of words. Luke prints Lysias’s letter to Felix in full (23:26–30) and does not print Festus’s. Read through the Acts verses with the stems for writing and letters (graph-, gramma-, epistol-), the ones after 25:26 are 26:24, where Festus says Paul’s many grammata (his “learning”) are turning him to madness, and 28:21 (not yet on these pages), where the Jews of Rome say they have received no letters about Paul from Judea; neither shows Festus’s letter. What follows the speech is the examination itself at Acts 26, and the two verses that close it, 26:31 and 26:32, are Greek that can be read now: those who have withdrawn, the king, the governor, Berenice and the others who sat with them (26:30), say to one another Ouden thanatou ē desmōn axion prassei ho anthrōpos houtos, “This man is doing nothing worthy of death or bonds,” and Agrippa says to Festus Apolelysthai edynato ho anthrōpos houtos ei mē epekeklēto Kaisara, “This man could have been released if he had not appealed to Caesar.” That is read here from the archived Greek, not discussed. Paul’s own account in Rome, at 28:18 and 28:19 (not yet on these pages), says that those who “examined” him (anakrinantes, the verb of 25:26’s noun) wished to release him because there was no cause of death in him, and that he was compelled to appeal to Caesar when the Jews objected. Whether the examination of 28:18 is the hearing before Festus, the one before Agrippa, or both, the Greek does not say.
