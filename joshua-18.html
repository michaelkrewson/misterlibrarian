The seven tribes still without land are asked how long they will go on being slack about taking it. The congregation gathers at Shiloh, where the tent of meeting is settled; three men from each tribe are sent to walk the land and write it up by cities in seven portions in a book; and Joshua throws the lot for them at Shiloh before Jehovah. Benjamin's comes up first, between Judah and the house of Joseph. Its border is walked from the Jordan at Jericho west past Luz and Beth-horon to Kiriath-jearim, then back east along Judah's own northern line past the shoulder of the Jebusite and En-rogel to the Salt Sea, and its twenty-six towns follow in lists of twelve and fourteen, Jerusalem among them.
A legal/administrative chapter like 13-17, giving no date or duration of its own: the survey is a walk across the whole land and back, and the book does not say how long it took. It opens on the condition Numbers 32:22 set for the eastern tribes' release, the land 'subdued', and it moves the camp from Gilgal to Shiloh, where the remaining lots and the tent of meeting (19:51) stay. The other six lots follow in chapter 19. Same contested early/late-date question as the rest of the conquest (c. 1406 BC on the early date this project foregrounds, c. 1230-1210 BC on the late).

Verse 1 · Shiloh and the tent of meeting: a land “subdued” at last, the condition Moses set for Reuben and Gad, and a tent named for the first time in seventeen chapters

★★★ “Subdued” is the condition Moses set for the eastern tribes, and this is the verse that says it has been met. The passive verb nikhbeshah, ‘was subdued,’ stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: Numbers 32:22 and 29 (already on these pages), this verse, and 1 Chronicles 22:18 (not yet on these pages). In Numbers 32 it is the hinge of a bargain: if Reuben and Gad cross the Jordan armed with the rest, then “the land is subdued before Jehovah, and afterward you return” (32:22). Joshua 22:1–4 and 9 (not yet on these pages) is where that clause is acted on: Joshua sends the two and a half tribes back to their tents, and they leave “out of Shiloh,” the place this verse has just made the camp.

⚠ The same book still says the work is unfinished. Joshua 13:1 (already on these pages) has Jehovah tell an old Joshua that “very much of the land still remains to be possessed,” and the very next verse here counts seven tribes with nothing yet divided. “Subdued” therefore means that the war has been won, not that the settling is done, and the Hebrew puts the two statements one verse apart without comment. The TLB puts the seam inside its first sentence: ‘although seven of the tribes of Israel had not yet entered and conquered the land’.

★★ The tent of meeting has not been named for seventeen chapters. The phrase ohel mo’ed, ‘tent of meeting,’ stands in two verses of the book of Joshua: this one and 19:51 (now on these pages), where the apportioning ends “at the door of the tent of meeting.” The last verse of the Torah to use the phrase is Deuteronomy 31:14 (already on these pages), where Moses and Joshua present themselves at the tent so that Jehovah can commission Joshua; between that scene and this one, the first seventeen chapters of Joshua do not use it once.

★★ Three words of gathering in one verse, and the tent’s second word is the congregation’s root. The verb is qahal, ‘assembled,’ and the people are the whole edah, ‘congregation’; this translation keeps the two apart everywhere, as the dictionary entry records. Edah comes from the same root as mo’ed, ‘meeting,’ the second half of the tent’s name, so the people and the tent are named from one root and brought together by another verb.

★★ “Settled” is the tent’s own name turned into a verb. Vayashkinu is the causative of shakan, ‘to dwell,’ the root of mishkan, the dwelling-place — so the verse says, nearly word for word, that they made the tent of meeting dwell there. This translation prints ‘settled,’ which keeps the dwelling inside the English verb. The root comes back whenever the Bible looks at this site from the far end. Jeremiah 7:12 (not yet on these pages) has Jehovah call Shiloh “my place… where I made my name dwell at the first,” and Psalm 78:60 (not yet on these pages) says he forsook “the tabernacle of Shiloh, the tent he made to dwell among men” — both in the intensive form of the verb, not this verse’s causative. And Jeremiah 26:6 and 9 (already on these pages) show what the name had become by then: the example Jeremiah holds up of what Jehovah can do to his own house.

★★ This is the first verse of the Bible in which Shiloh is plainly a town. The only earlier verse with the name’s consonants is Genesis 49:10 (already on these pages), where the longer spelling, with a yod, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible and the word is the most disputed in Genesis. The dictionary entry gives that verse’s four readings, one of which is this place, and this translation does not choose among them. Judges 21:19 (not yet on these pages) gives the way to the town: north of Bethel, east of the highway that goes up from Bethel to Shechem, and south of Lebonah.

⚠ The shelf splits three ways on the tent’s name. The KJV reads ‘set up the tabernacle of the congregation there’, and the Geneva ‘they set up the Tabernacle of the Congregation there’; the Douay-Rheims, following the Latin, ‘the tabernacle of the testimony’; the ASV ‘set up the tent of meeting there’, the NIV ‘set up the tent of meeting there’, and the NWT 2013 ‘they set up the tent of meeting there’. The NWT 1984 alone changes the verb: ‘proceeded to locate the tent of meeting there’. For the land the NIV turns the passive into a state, ‘The country was brought under their control’, and the Geneva into a subject, ‘after the land was subject unto them’.

Verses 2–5 · Seven tribes left, and Joshua’s “how long”: the root Pharaoh used on Israel and Jehovah used in a promise, and a survey that was written, not drawn

★★ Seven is what is left over, and it closes against Joshua 14. The seven tribes of v2, plus Judah and Ephraim, make the “nine tribes” of 14:2 (already on these pages), and Manasseh’s western half is the “half-tribe” that verse adds; 14:4 explains why Joseph counts as two, Manasseh and Ephraim, and why Levi counts as none. The number then runs through these verses: shiv’ah, ‘seven,’ stands four times, at vv2, 5, 6 and 9 — seven tribes at the start, then seven portions three times — so the survey is ordered to bring back one portion for each tribe still waiting. Which portion belongs to which tribe is the one thing a written survey cannot say, and that is what the lot is for.

★★★ “Slack” is a root with a history in this book: Jehovah’s promise, Pharaoh’s charge, and now Joshua’s. Mitrappim, the reflexive participle ‘going slack,’ stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, but the root (rafah, to let drop or let go) does not. The promise form, lo yarpekha or lo arpekha, ‘he will not let go of you,’ stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, with or without the ‘forsake’ that usually follows: Deuteronomy 4:31, 31:6 and 8, Joshua 1:5, where Jehovah says it to Joshua himself (all already on these pages), and 1 Chronicles 28:20 (not yet on these pages). Inside Joshua the root turns up once more at 10:6, where the Gibeonites beg, “Do not let your hands fall slack from your servants.” And nirpim, ‘idle,’ stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, both Pharaoh’s: Exodus 5:8 and 17 (already on these pages), his charge against Israelites who ask leave to go out and sacrifice. So the verb that opened this book as Jehovah’s promise to Joshua is the verb Joshua now turns on the tribes — and the verb Pharaoh once turned on the same people for wanting to go out is turned on them here for not going in.

★★ The Hebrew says “write,” and what comes back is a list. The verb is katav five times in vv4–9, at v4, v6, twice at v8, and v9, and no verb of drawing, tracing or measuring appears in the instructions or in the report of what the men did. What the men bring is described at v9: the land “by cities,” “in seven portions,” al sefer, “in a book.” That is a document of towns, the shape of the lists that follow — Benjamin’s at vv21–28 here, Judah’s at 15:21–62 — and this translation prints the verb the Hebrew has: “write it up.” The map that many readers picture is the shelf’s, not the text’s.

★★ Verse 5 draws the picture that verse 11 fills in. Judah is to stand on his border in the south and the house of Joseph on theirs in the north; the unassigned country lies between them, and the first lot to come up (v11) falls exactly there, “between the sons of Judah and the sons of Joseph.” Joshua 17:17 (already on these pages) is where Joshua addresses Ephraim and Manasseh together as “the house of Joseph”: the two blocks that frame the seven tribes are the blocks that have already drawn their lots, and the seven are to be fitted into the gap.

⚠ The shelf finds six different renderings for “slack,” and the survey becomes a map in two versions. At v3 the KJV reads ‘How long are ye slack to go to possess the land’, the ASV ‘How long are ye slack to go in to possess the land’ and the Geneva ‘how long are you so slack to enter and possess the land’; the Douay-Rheims doubles it, ‘indolent and slack’; the NWT 1984 alone reads ‘How long are YOU going to be delinquent’, the NWT 2013 ‘How long will you put off going in to take possession’, and the NIV ‘How long will you wait before you begin to take possession’. The TLB adds a conquest the verse does not mention: ‘How long are you going to wait before clearing out the people living in the land’. At v4 the verb is where the maps come in: the NWT 1984 reads ‘map it out in accord with their inheritance’ and the NWT 2013 ‘map it out according to their inheritance’, where the ASV has ‘describe it according to their inheritance’, the KJV ‘describe it according to the inheritance of them’, the NIV ‘make a survey of the land and to write a description of it’ and the Douay-Rheims ‘go and compass the land, and mark it out’. The three men themselves are asked for in four ways: the KJV ‘Give out from among you three men for each tribe’, the ASV ‘Appoint for you three men of each tribe’, the NWT 1984 ‘Furnish for yourselves three men of each tribe’, and the NWT 2013 ‘Give me three men from each tribe’.

Verses 6–10 · “Our God” for the lot, the priesthood of Jehovah as the Levites’ inheritance, what Gad, Reuben and half of Manasseh “took,” the camp that moved from Gilgal to Shiloh, and one lot thrown with three different verbs

★ The rebuke speaks of “the God of your fathers”; the instruction speaks of “Jehovah our God.” The first is v3, the second v6, and the move from “your” to “our” puts the speaker inside the sentence that throws the lot. The Douay-Rheims alone does not keep it, reading ‘your God’ at v6, following the Latin.

★★★ A third way of saying what a landless tribe has instead. Joshua 13:14 (already on these pages) names the fire-offerings of Jehovah as Levi’s inheritance, 13:33 names Jehovah himself, and this verse names kehunnat Yhwh, “the priesthood of Jehovah”: the phrase stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The first is what the Levite receives, the second whom he has, the third what he is — and this verse sets the third directly after “no portion,” the word the lot is about to hand out to everyone else.

★★ The Hebrew says “his inheritance” of a whole tribe, and all eight English witnesses on the shelf say “their.” Nachalato, singular, follows the plural “Levites” as it does in Deuteronomy 18:2 and Joshua 13:14 (both already on these pages), where the tribe is spoken of as one man; 13:33 is the plural of the three. The KJV reads ‘is their inheritance’, the ASV ‘the priesthood of Jehovah is their inheritance’, the Geneva ‘is their inheritance’, the Douay-Rheims ‘is their inheritance’, the NWT 1984 and the NWT 2013 ‘the priesthood of Jehovah is their inheritance’, the NIV ‘is their inheritance’ after ‘priestly service’, and the TLB ‘That is their wonderful heritage’. This translation keeps the singular.

★★★ “Taken” and “given” share a verse again. ‘They have taken their inheritance’ stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible, all of them about the same two and a half tribes: Numbers 34:14 and 15, Joshua 13:8 and this one. Joshua 13:8 and this verse close alike, with Moses “the servant of Jehovah” as the giver: the tribes took, and Moses gave. The nine and a half tribes of the west are told at Numbers 34:13 that their land is theirs “by lot,” and this is the chapter where the lot is thrown. The shelf mostly flattens the verb. The NWT 1984 keeps it, ‘have taken their inheritance’, and the NWT 2013 ‘have already taken their inheritance’; the KJV, ASV and Geneva all read ‘have received their inheritance’, the NIV ‘have already received their inheritance’, the Douay-Rheims ‘have already received their possessions’, and the TLB drops the verb for ‘already have land on the east side of the Jordan’.

★★ The camp has moved from Gilgal to Shiloh, and the Hebrew phrase shows it. ‘To the camp, Gilgal’ stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible, all in this book and all already on these pages: 9:6, 10:6, 15 and 43. ‘To the camp, Shiloh’ stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: v9 here and Judges 21:12 (not yet on these pages), where four hundred young women of Jabesh-gilead are brought to the camp at Shiloh and, at 21:14, given to the surviving men of Benjamin. Joshua 14:6 (already on these pages) still finds the sons of Judah approaching Joshua “in Gilgal”; by this verse he is at Shiloh. The KJV prints the word as ‘host’, ‘to the host at Shiloh’, where the ASV has ‘unto the camp at Shiloh’, the Geneva ‘into the camp at Shiloh’, the Douay-Rheims ‘to the camp in Silo’ and the NIV ‘in the camp at Shiloh’.

★★★ One act, three verbs — and two of them are used of a lot nowhere else. At v6 Joshua says yariti, from yarah, ‘to throw’ or ‘shoot’; at vv8 and 10 the verb is hishlikh, ‘to cast’; and at v11 the lot itself comes up. Of the sixty-five verses of the Hebrew Bible that contain goral, only these three make the lot the thing thrown by yarah or hishlikh. Everywhere else lots are made to fall (hippil: Psalm 22:19; Jonah 1:7; Proverbs 1:14; Esther 3:7), cast with yadad (Joel 4:3; Obadiah 1:11; Nahum 3:10), or hurled into the lap (yutal, Proverbs 16:33). Micah 2:5 has the verb of vv8 and 10 and the noun of Joshua 17:5 and 14 — “one who casts a cord by lot in the assembly of Jehovah” — but there the cord, chevel, is what is cast; whether Micah is recalling this scene the verse does not say. All three casts are made “before Jehovah” (vv6, 8, 10), and this chapter’s casting is done by Joshua alone: Eleazar the priest, named beside him at 14:1 and 17:4 (both already on these pages) and again at 19:51 (now on these pages), is not named here.

⚠ The shelf flattens the three verbs into one, and one version turns the lot into dice. The ASV reads ‘I will cast lots for you here before Jehovah our God’ at v6, ‘I will cast lots for you here’ at v8 and ‘Joshua cast lots for them in Shiloh’ at v10; the KJV ‘that I may cast lots for you here’ at v6, and the NIV ‘I will cast lots for you in the presence’. The NWT 1984 alone varies the verb, ‘I must cast lots here for YOU’ at v6 against ‘here is where I shall draw lots for YOU’ at v8. The TLB alone changes the object, ‘I will throw the sacred dice’ — and the dictionary entry for goral describes a marked stone or token, not a die.

Verses 11–14 · Benjamin’s lot comes up first, between Judah and Joseph; its northern border is Ephraim’s southern one, and Luz is glossed “that is, Bethel”

★★★ Benjamin’s is the first lot of the seven, and it falls between the two tribes that stood either side of him in Genesis 44. The Hebrew does not say “first”; Joshua 19:1 (now on these pages) calls Simeon’s the second lot of the seven, and the NIV carries that number back to this verse, ‘The first lot came up for the tribe of Benjamin’, as the Douay-Rheims does, ‘And first came up the lot of the children of Benjamin’. Verse 5 had put Judah at the south and the house of Joseph at the north, and the lot lands in the gap. It is the arrangement of the Joseph story’s climax: Joseph’s cup is found in Benjamin’s bag (Genesis 44:12), and Judah, who had stood surety for the boy (43:9), offers to stay a slave in his place (44:33) — all already on these pages. Whether the lot-makers meant it the text does not say; the map says it anyway.

★★ The lot “comes up,” but its border still “goes out.” ‘The lot came up’ stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one and Joshua 19:10 (Zebulun’s, now on these pages). The ten tribal lots of Joshua open three ways. Two open vayehi ha-goral, ‘the lot was’: Judah’s (15:1) and Manasseh’s (17:1). Two open ‘came up’: Benjamin’s and Zebulun’s. Six open ‘went out’: Joseph’s (16:1) and, in Joshua 19 (now on these pages), Simeon’s, Issachar’s, Asher’s, Naphtali’s and Dan’s. And Benjamin’s verse keeps two of the verbs apart: the lot comes up and the border of the lot goes out, the verb chapter 15 and chapter 16 use of their borders.

★★ Benjamin’s northern border is Ephraim’s southern border, written from the other side. Joshua 16:1–5 (already on these pages) runs from the Jordan at Jericho up through the hill country to Bethel, on to Luz and Ataroth, down to Beth-horon the nether, and gives Ephraim’s own limit as “Atroth-addar, as far as Beth-horon the upper.” This chapter starts at the same Jordan, goes up the mountain westward past the wilderness of Beth-aven to Luz, goes down to Atroth-addar and passes the mountain south of Lower Beth-horon. Jericho, Luz and Bethel, Atroth-addar and Beth-horon stand in both surveys.

★★★ Luz gets its gloss here, and the gloss completes a count that Joshua 16 started. Luzah, with the directional ending, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible as a place: Genesis 35:6, Joshua 16:2 and this one. Genesis 35:6 and this verse add “that is, Bethel”; Joshua 16:2 does not, and runs “from Bethel to Luz” as two names. They are one town: Judges 1:23 (already on these pages) says outright that “the name of the city formerly was Luz,” and the border here runs along the town’s southern shoulder, with Bethel then counted among Benjamin’s own towns at v22. This translation keeps the bare ‘to Luz’ of Joshua 16 and carries the gloss at the Hebrew’s own point, hi Beit-El.

⚠ Kiriath-jearim is already Judah’s. V14 ends the western side at “Kiriath-baal — that is Kiriath-jearim, a city of the sons of Judah,” the town Judah’s own list counts (“Kiriath-baal — that is, Kiriath-jearim”) and that Judah’s border text at 15:9 calls Baalah. The border is drawn up to a Judahite town and not through it; v28 will come back to whether a Benjaminite “Kiriath” is the same one.

Verses 15–20 · Benjamin’s southern border is Judah’s northern border read backwards: twelve shared landmarks, Gilgal or Geliloth, the Stone of Bohan, the Jebusite’s shoulder, and Moses’ “between his shoulders”

★★★ Judah’s northern border and Benjamin’s southern border are one line, walked in opposite directions. Joshua 15:5–11 (already on these pages) starts at the tongue of the sea at the end of the Jordan and runs west to Kiriath-jearim; vv14–19 here start at Kiriath-jearim and run east to the same tongue and the same end of the Jordan. Twelve landmarks stand in both, in almost exactly reverse order: Kiriath-jearim, the spring of Nephtoah, the Valley of Rephaim, the Valley of the son of Hinnom, the shoulder of the Jebusite, En-rogel, En-shemesh, the Ascent of Adummim, the Stone of Bohan, Beth-hoglah, the tongue of the sea, and the end of the Jordan. Each survey also names a little the other leaves out: Judah’s the Valley of Achor, Debir and the cities of Mount Ephron, Benjamin’s the Arabah. The name Nephtoah stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, 15:9 and v15 here, one in each survey of the line.

★★★ One landmark, two names: “Gilgal” in Judah’s survey, “Geliloth” in Benjamin’s. Joshua 15:7 turns north “toward Gilgal, which faces the Ascent of Adummim”; v17 here goes “to Geliloth, which faces the Ascent of Adummim” — the same clause with the name changed. Both names come from galal, ‘to roll’, the root Joshua 5:9 (already on these pages) gives for Gilgal, “Rolling.” Geliloth stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: Joshua 13:2, where this translation prints it “regions” (“all the regions of the Philistines”), this verse, Joshua 22:10 and 11 (not yet on these pages), where the eastern tribes build an altar at “the Geliloth of the Jordan” in the land of Canaan, and Joel 4:4 (not yet on these pages). Whether the Geliloth of v17 and the Gilgal of 15:7 are one place or two the text does not decide; the identical clause that fixes them suggests one. The ASV reads ‘Geliloth, which is over against the ascent of Adummim’, the KJV ‘Geliloth, which is over against the going up of Adummim’, the NIV ‘Geliloth, which faces the Pass of Adummim’ and the Geneva ‘Geliloth, which is toward the going up unto Adummim’; the Douay-Rheims alone translates the name, ‘the hills that are over against the ascent of Adommim’.

★★★ Five of Joshua’s eight “shoulders” are in Benjamin’s border, and Moses said Benjamin lives “between his shoulders.” The word ketef, ‘shoulder,’ stands in eight verses of the book of Joshua: 15:8, 10 and 11 in Judah’s survey, and vv12, 13, 16, 18 and 19 here. Every use in the book is a border description, and five of the eight are Benjamin’s. Deuteronomy 33:12 (already on these pages) blesses Benjamin in the same noun: ‘between his shoulders’ stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, that one and 1 Samuel 17:6 (not yet on these pages), where it places Goliath’s bronze javelin. Whether the border-writer had Moses’ line in mind the text does not say; the word is the ordinary surveyor’s word for the side of a hill, and Judah’s survey uses it three times with no blessing near. The old reading of the blessing, though, puts the Temple on Benjamin’s ridge: Rashi on Deuteronomy 33:12 (checked in the English text on Sefaria) says that “on the highest spot of his (Benjamin’s) land was the Temple built,” and that Moses set Joseph’s blessing straight after Benjamin’s because Joseph “too had a Sanctuary in his territory: the Tabernacle at Shiloh was erected in his territory.” The two sanctuary-tribes are neighbors on the ground as well, Joseph in the northern block of v5, Benjamin in the gap.

★★ The line passes south of the Jebusite, so the town lies on Benjamin’s side, and the text says so at v28. V16 takes the border “to the shoulder of the Jebusite on the south”; 15:8 gives the same landmark with a gloss, “that is Jerusalem,” and v16 here leaves the gloss off and saves it for v28. The valley is named twice in v16, once as the Valley of the son of Hinnom and once as the Valley of Hinnom; the short form is the one the Greek Gehenna is built on. Two shelf versions name Jerusalem here where the Hebrew still says only “the shoulder of the Jebusite”: the TLB reads ‘crossed south of the old city of Jerusalem where the Jebusites lived’, and the NIV turns the people into a town, ‘along the southern slope of the Jebusite city’.

★★ A Reubenite’s stone stands on a Judah–Benjamin boundary, and nothing else is known of him. ‘The Stone of Bohan’ stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: 15:6 and this one. The man is a “son of Reuben,” yet he is in neither list of Reuben’s sons on these pages, Genesis 46:9 and Numbers 26:5–9, which give Hanoch, Pallu, Hezron and Carmi, and Pallu’s son Eliab with his sons. The consonants are those of bohen, ‘thumb’ or ‘big toe,’ as in Leviticus 8:23 (already on these pages), with one vowel different. All eight English witnesses on the shelf take the name as a man’s; the Douay-Rheims alone spells him differently, ‘the stone of Been the son of Ruben’.

⚠ The NIV writes “Beth Arabah” where the Hebrew says only “the Arabah.” V18’s Hebrew is mul ha-Aravah, “facing the Arabah,” with no beit in it. The NIV reads ‘the northern slope of Beth Arabah and on down into the Arabah’, which agrees with Judah’s survey (“north of Beth-arabah,” 15:6) and with the town at v22 but not with the word in this verse. The KJV keeps the Hebrew, ‘over against Arabah northward’, as do the ASV, ‘over against the Arabah northward’, the TLB, ‘along the north edge of the Arabah’, and the Geneva, ‘over against the plain Northward’.

⚠ “Tongue” stays a tongue, as in Judah’s survey. V19’s lashon yam ha-melach, ‘the tongue of the Salt Sea,’ is the word of 15:2 and 5, and the shelf reads it as an inlet: the ASV ‘the north bay of the Salt Sea’, the KJV ‘the north bay of the salt sea’, the NIV ‘the northern bay of the Dead Sea’ and the TLB ‘the north bay of the Dead Sea’. The Geneva alone reaches for a headland, ‘to the point of the salt Sea Northward’, and the Douay-Rheims drops the word.

Verses 21–28 · Benjamin’s twenty-six towns in lists of twelve and fourteen; Jerusalem claimed by Benjamin as well as Judah, Jericho under Joshua’s oath, and Beth-hoglah, which is also a daughter’s name

★★★ The totals check, but the second holds only if two pairs of names are read as four towns. Verses 21–24 name twelve towns (Jericho, Beth-hoglah, Emek-keziz, Beth-arabah, Zemaraim, Bethel, Avvim, Parah, Ophrah, Chephar-ammoni, Ophni, Geba), and vv25–28 name fourteen (Gibeon, Ramah, Beeroth, Mizpeh, Chephirah, Mozah, Rekem, Irpeel, Taralah, Zela, Ha-eleph, the Jebusite, Gibeath, Kiriath): twenty-six in all. Fourteen is reached only if Zela and Ha-eleph are two names and Gibeath and Kiriath are two; read either pair as one name and the list holds thirteen, read both pairs as one and it holds twelve. The TLB adds the two lists up for the reader, ‘These twenty-six cities’. And the Geneva splits Chephar-ammoni in two, ‘Chephar, Haammoni’, and so prints thirteen names under its own ‘twelve cities’, where the KJV keeps it as one, ‘Chepharhaammonai’.

★★★ Jerusalem is on Benjamin’s list, in Judah’s failure, and in Judah’s capture, and the book keeps all of it. Verse 28 lists “the Jebusite — that is Jerusalem” among Benjamin’s fourteen. Joshua 15:63 says the sons of Judah could not drive the Jebusites out of Jerusalem, and that the Jebusite “has dwelt with the sons of Judah in Jerusalem to this day.” Judges 1:21 says the sons of Benjamin did not drive the Jebusite out, and that he “has dwelt with the sons of Benjamin in Jerusalem to this day.” And Judges 1:8 has the sons of Judah fight against Jerusalem, capture it, strike it with the edge of the sword and send it up in fire (all already on these pages). Four verses, two tribes, one city; this translation leaves the seams showing. Bethel is counted the same way: it is on the list at v22, on Ephraim’s border at 16:1–2, and taken by “the house of Joseph” at Judges 1:22. Borders were not walls between tribes; the towns on them were claimed from both sides.

⚠ The shelf cannot decide what to call the town. The Hebrew gives a people with the definite article, “the Jebusite.” The KJV turns it into a place, ‘Jebusi, which is Jerusalem’, the Geneva ‘Jebus, (which is Jerusalem)’ and the Douay-Rheims ‘Jebus, which is Jerusalem’; the ASV keeps the people, ‘the Jebusite (the same is Jerusalem)’, the NIV adds a city, ‘the Jebusite city’, and the TLB reads ‘Jebus (or Jerusalem)’.

★★ Jericho heads the list, and Joshua’s own oath is still waiting for it. At Joshua 6:26 (already on these pages) Joshua swears that the man who rises up and rebuilds Jericho will lay its foundation “at the cost of his firstborn” and set up its gates “at the cost of his youngest son.” This verse counts Jericho among Benjamin’s towns without a word about rebuilding. 1 Kings 16:34 (not yet on these pages) reports the oath fulfilled: Hiel the Bethelite built Jericho, laying the foundation “with Abiram his firstborn” and setting up the gates “with his youngest son Segub,” “according to the word of Jehovah, which he spoke by the hand of Joshua the son of Nun.” Hiel was a man of Bethel — the town at v22 of this very list.

★★ Beth-hoglah carries the name of one of Zelophehad’s daughters. The letters Hoglah stand in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible: four are the daughter’s name (Numbers 26:33, 27:1, 36:11 and Joshua 17:3, the chapter just before this one) and three are the town (15:6, and vv19 and 21 here). Beth-hoglah is “house of Hoglah”; the text draws no line between the woman and the border village.

★★ The last name on the list is a bare “Kiriath,” and Kiriath-jearim is already spoken for. V14 calls Kiriath-jearim “a city of the sons of Judah,” and Judah’s own list counts it (15:60). V28’s Kiriath is the construct form kiryat, ‘city of,’ with nothing after it, as Gibeath before it is ‘hill of’; the Hebrew leaves the second half of each name unsaid, and the versions fill them differently. The ASV reads ‘Gibeath, and Kiriath’, the NIV ‘Gibeah and Kiriath’ and the Douay-Rheims ‘Gabaath and Cariath’, all bare; the TLB alone completes the second, ‘Gibeah, and Kiriath-jearim’. This translation prints what is written.

⚠ The first list’s third name is a valley, and the shelf cannot agree whether to translate it. Emek-keziz is “valley of Keziz.” The KJV and the Geneva translate it, ‘the valley of Keziz’; the ASV prints ‘Emek-keziz’, the NIV ‘Emek Keziz’, and the Douay-Rheims splits the difference with ‘Vale-Casis’. This translation prints the name, as the ASV and the NIV do.

Echoes paid. Joshua 17’s forecast that Benjamin’s border would run past Luz a second time, with the gloss that chapter’s own v2 left out, is confirmed at v13: Luzah stands in exactly three verses, and this is the second to add “that is, Bethel.” Numbers 32’s “subdued” (32:22, 29) is met at v1. The eastern tribes’ “taken” of Numbers 34:14–15 and Joshua 13:8 is repeated at v7, where the Levite’s inheritance gains a third formula beside Joshua 13:14 and 33. The “nine tribes and the half-tribe” of Joshua 14:2 close against vv2 and 5. Judah’s northern border (Joshua 15:5–11) is walked backward in vv14–19, and Ephraim’s southern border (Joshua 16:1–5) is read from the other side in vv12–14. Deuteronomy 33:12’s “between his shoulders” meets the five shoulders of vv12–19, and the root of “I will not let go of you” (Joshua 1:5) comes back at v3 as a charge.

Echoes paid, a book early. Judges 1:21 and 1:22 (already on these pages) already say what vv28 and 22 imply: that Jerusalem, though on Benjamin’s list, was a city Benjamin did not take, and that Bethel was taken by the house of Joseph. Jeremiah 26:6 and 9 (already on these pages) already hold up Shiloh, v1’s town, as the house of Jehovah that Jehovah can make a ruin.

Echoes planted. Joshua 19 (now on these pages) casts the other six lots, Simeon’s inside Judah’s portion, and ends at 19:51 with the apportioning finished “at the door of the tent of meeting”. Joshua 22:1–9 (not yet on these pages) sends Reuben, Gad and half of Manasseh home out of Shiloh, and 22:10–11 has them build their altar at the Geliloth of the Jordan, the name v17 uses for a landmark on Benjamin’s border. Judges 21:12–23 (not yet on these pages) brings four hundred women to the camp at Shiloh for the men of Benjamin and sends the rest to seize wives from the daughters of Shiloh dancing in the vineyards. 1 Samuel 3:3 (not yet on these pages) puts the ark at Shiloh, and 4:3–4 sends to Shiloh to fetch it out to the battle. 1 Kings 16:34 (not yet on these pages) is where Hiel of Bethel rebuilds Jericho at the cost Joshua 6:26 named. 2 Samuel 21:14 (not yet on these pages) buries Saul and Jonathan at Zela, v28’s town, in the tomb of Kish. Judges 6:11 and 24 (not yet on these pages) set Gideon’s Ophrah among the Abiezrites of Manasseh, which is not v23’s Ophrah of Benjamin. Psalm 78:60 and 67 (not yet on these pages) have Jehovah forsake the tabernacle of Shiloh and reject the tent of Joseph.

Decisions. Hebrew and English verse numbers align through the whole chapter, with no versification shift. Katav is “write” at vv4, 6, 8 and 9, and the survey is “written up” rather than “mapped”, since the Hebrew has no map and v9 says what came back. Yarah is “throw” and hishlikh “cast”, so the two verbs the Hebrew uses of the lot stay two. Edah is “congregation” and qahal “assembled”, as the dictionary keeps them; vayashkinu is “settled”, keeping the dwelling inside the verb; laqchu is “have taken”, as at Numbers 34:14–15; nachalato keeps its singular, “his inheritance”. Ketef is “shoulder” in all five of its verses, lashon “tongue” and gevul “border”, each as in Joshua 15; Atroth-addar is spelled as at 16:5. Written and read forms differ in the Hebrew column at vv12, 14 and 19, where the written verb is singular (and it was) and the read verb plural (and they were) for the border’s ends, and at v24, where the written ha-Ammoni stands beside the read ha-Ammonah (see ketiv / qere); the translation follows the reading in each. The Masoretic text marks three open sections, after vv10, 20 and 28, and no closed section.

Library. One new encyclopedia entry in both languages: Beth-hoglah (the border town whose name is also a daughter of Zelophehad’s). Eight existing encyclopedia entries extended in English: Shiloh, Benjamin, Bethel, Beth-aven, the Jebusites, Kiriath-jearim, En Rogel and Zelophehad — six of them extended in Spanish as well, and the other two, Benjamin and En Rogel, now written in Spanish for the first time. Five existing dictionary entries extended, each in both languages: goral (the opening verbs of all ten tribal lots, counted), raphah, ketef, machaneh and nachalah. No new dictionary entries. No new entries for Ataroth-addar, Geliloth, the Stone of Bohan, Emek-keziz, Zela or the other bare survey names — the book’s border chapters have left most of theirs unlinked.

⚠ A note on order: Joshua stands on these pages at chapters 1–19, and at Judges 1:8, 13, 21 and 29 out of sequence. Joshua 20–24 remain the open sequence; next in this book is Joshua 20, where six cities are set apart for the man who kills without intending it — Kedesh in Naphtali’s hill country the first of them.
