Samson goes down to Timnah, sees a Philistine woman and asks his parents to get her for him, because she is right in his eyes; the narrator says it was from Jehovah, seeking an occasion against the Philistines. In the vineyards of Timnah the spirit of Jehovah rushes on him and he tears a young lion apart; later he takes honey from its carcass and tells no one. At the seven-day wedding feast he sets thirty companions a riddle; on the seventh day they threaten his bride with fire, she presses him, he tells her, and she tells them. The spirit rushes on him again: he kills thirty men of Ashkelon to pay the wager, goes up to his father's house in anger, and his wife is given to his companion.
Within the period of the judges, with no date the chapter gives for itself; it falls inside the Philistine forty years of Judges 13:1. The early/late question applies (c. 1200 BC on the early reckoning this project foregrounds, c. 1100 BC on the late).

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–4 · Samson goes down to Timnah — “she is right in my eyes”, the uncircumcised, and “it was from Jehovah”

Going down. The chapter moves by one verb, yarad, “go down”: Samson goes down to Timnah (v. 1), goes down again with his parents (v. 5), goes down to speak with the woman (v. 7), his father goes down (v. 10), and Samson goes down to Ashkelon (v. 19). Timnah sits on Judah’s border (Joshua 15:10) and in Dan’s town list (Joshua 19:43), both now on these pages; here it belongs to the Philistines.

“She is right in my eyes.” Hi yashrah be’einai: Samson’s reason for the marriage, said twice (vv. 3, 7). It is the phrase Moses forbade, “each man whatever is right in his own eyes” (Deuteronomy 12:8, now on these pages), and the sentence the book ends on, when “each man did what was right in his own eyes” (Judges 17:6 and 21:25, both now on these pages). The KJV has “she pleaseth me well”, the NIV “She’s the right one for me”, the Douay “she hath pleased my eyes”; the translation keeps the eyes.

“The uncircumcised Philistines.” His parents ask whether there is no woman “among the daughters of your brothers, or among all my people”. Arelim, “uncircumcised”, is the insult Israel keeps for the Philistines; Samson uses it himself at 15:18, and Jonathan and Saul in 1 Samuel 14:6 and 31:4. The TLB has them ask “Why don’t you marry a Jewish girl?”, a word for the people that belongs to a much later age.

“It was from Jehovah.” The narrator tells the reader what the parents did not know: “for he was seeking an occasion against the Philistines”. The Hebrew does not say whether “he” is Jehovah or Samson. The NIV decides for God, “who was seeking an occasion to confront the Philistines”; the ASV keeps “for he sought an occasion”. The B Greek text has ekdikēsin, “vengeance”, the A text antapodoma, “repayment”. “The Philistines were ruling over Israel”: moshlim is the verb of rule Gideon refused for himself and his son (Judges 8:23, now on these pages).

Verses 5–6 · The young lion in the vineyards of Timnah — the spirit rushes on him, and a Nazirite among the vines

The vineyards. Samson goes down with his father and his mother, and “they came as far as the vineyards of Timnah”; yet when the lion comes he is alone, for he “did not tell his father or his mother”. The text leaves that seam showing. A Nazirite of God, bound to eat nothing that comes from the grapevine (Judges 13:14, now on these pages), meets his first test among the vines; the chapter makes no comment.

“The spirit of Jehovah rushed on him.” The verb is new to the book. Othniel and Jephthah had the spirit “come upon” them (Judges 3:10, 11:29), and it “clothed” Gideon (6:34), all now on these pages; on Samson it tsalach, rushes. The words “and the spirit rushed on him” stand in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: verses 6 and 19 here, 15:14 (now on these pages), and Saul among the prophets at 1 Samuel 10:10 (also now on these pages). The letters are those of tsalach, “prosper”, the word of Joseph in Egypt (Genesis 39:2, now on these pages); whether they are one word or two is debated. The KJV has “came mightily upon him”, the NWT 1984 “became operative upon him”.

“As one tears apart a young goat.” Shasa is the verb of the bird torn open for a burnt offering (Leviticus 1:17, now on these pages), and “with nothing in his hand” says he did it bare-handed. The TLB adds that he “ripped the lion’s jaws apart”; the jaws are not in the Hebrew. Josephus, in the Greek of the Antiquities (5.287, numbered 5.8.5 in Whiston), has him strangle it and throw it into a wooded place.

Verses 7–9 · Honey from the lion’s carcass — a swarm of bees, an unclean body, and a secret kept

A swarm in the body. He turns aside to see the mappelet, “the fallen thing”, and finds “a swarm of bees in the body of the lion”. The word for the swarm is edah, the word that names “all the assembly of Israel” (Exodus 12:3, now on these pages); the B Greek text has synagōgē. Both Greek texts put the bees “in the mouth of the lion”, and so does the Douay, “in the mouth of the lion”; the Hebrew says the body. Josephus has them “in the breast”.

Eating from a carcass. The law counts the carcass of any animal that walks on its paws as unclean to touch: “anyone who touches their carcasses will be unclean until evening” (Leviticus 11:27, now on these pages). Samson scrapes the honey out with his palms, eats, and gives it to his parents, “but he did not tell them that he had scraped the honey out of the body of the lion”. The chapter says nothing about uncleanness; it says what he did not tell.

Telling. Higgid, “tell”, is the chapter’s word. Samson tells his parents about the woman (v. 2), does not tell them about the lion (v. 6) or the honey (v. 9), dares the guests to tell the riddle (vv. 12–14), is pressed to tell it (vv. 15–16), tells (v. 17), and pays “those who had told the riddle” (v. 19). The NWT 1984 keeps the verb as “tell” throughout, the KJV moves between “told”, “declare” and “expound”.

Verses 10–14 · Samson’s riddle at the wedding feast — a drinking feast, thirty companions, and “out of the eater”

A drinking feast. “Samson made a feast there”: mishteh is from shatah, “drink” (dictionary), and both Greek texts call it potos, a drinking party. It lasts seven days, as Laban’s did when he “made a feast” and Jacob had to “finish this one’s bridal week” (Genesis 29:22 and 29:27, now on these pages). The chapter does not say whether the Nazirite drank. The Douay has the father make “a feast for his son Samson”.

Thirty companions. “When they saw him, they took thirty companions.” The A Greek text reads “when they feared him”, a small difference in the Hebrew letters between “see” and “fear”; Josephus, too, says the Timnites gave him thirty of their strongest young men, companions in name and guards in fact, “through fear of his strength” (Antiquities 5.289, numbered 5.8.6 in Whiston). The NWT 1984 calls them “thirty groomsmen”.

“Let me riddle you a riddle.” Achudah-na lakhem chidah: verb and noun are the same root, chidah, and the guests answer in kind, chudah chidatkha, “riddle your riddle”. The stakes are thirty sedinim, “linen wraps”, the goods of the capable woman of Proverbs 31:24 (now on these pages), and thirty chalifot begadim, “changes of clothes”. The phrase chalifot begadim stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: verses 12 and 13, and the ten changes of clothes Naaman brings to Israel (2 Kings 5:5). The shelf cannot agree on the linen: the KJV has “thirty sheets”, the Douay “thirty shirts”, the NWT 1984 “thirty undergarments”, the ASV “thirty linen garments”.

The riddle. Me-ha-okhel yatsa ma’akhal, u-me-az yatsa matok: two lines of three words, “out of the eater, something to eat; out of the strong, something sweet”, with the eater and the eaten built on one root. No guest could solve it, because only a man who had seen the lion knew what it was about. The KJV keeps “Out of the eater came forth meat”, meat in its old sense of food. Josephus gives it as “the all-devouring had produced pleasant food from itself, though itself most unpleasant” (5.290).

Verses 15–17 · The seventh day or the fourth? — “Entice your husband”, fire, and seven days of weeping

The seventh day, or the fourth. The Hebrew says the guests threatened the bride “on the seventh day”; yet in verse 17 she weeps over him “the seven days that their feast lasted”. Both Greek texts read “on the fourth day”, and two of the shelf follow them: the NIV “On the fourth day” and the NWT 1984 “on the fourth day”. The KJV and ASV keep “on the seventh day”. Readings differ; this translation prints the Masoretic “seventh” and leaves the strain where the reader can see it.

“Entice your husband … or we will burn you.” Patti, “entice”, is the word the Philistine lords will say to Delilah (Judges 16:5, now on these pages). The threat, “we will burn you and your father’s house with fire”, is the threat Ephraim made to Jephthah, “Your house we will burn down over you with fire” (Judges 12:1, now on these pages); here it will be carried out (15:6, now on these pages). “Did you call us here to dispossess us?” uses yarash, the book’s verb for possessing the land, as at Judges 11:24 (now on these pages), now in Philistine mouths about an Israelite wedding. The KJV has “to take that we have”, the ASV “to impoverish us”.

“You only hate me.” She weeps and says he hates her and does not love her, because he has not told her. He answers that he has not told his father or his mother either, and he has kept two secrets from them already. She “pressed him”, hetsiqathu, and he told her; the same verb and the same charge of not loving come back with Delilah (16:15–16, now on these pages).

Verse 18 · “Before the sun went down” — charsah, the answer as a riddle, and “if you had not plowed with my heifer”

The sun, or the bedroom. The men answer “before the sun went down”: be-terem yavo ha-charsah, with a rare word for the sun, the cheres of “the ascent of Heres” (Judges 8:13, now on these pages). The NWT 1984 has instead “before ever he could go into the interior room”, the bridal chamber, a reading with different Hebrew letters, ha-chadrah for ha-charsah. The two Greek texts disagree with each other: the B text has “before the sun rose”, the A text “before the sun set”. The translation keeps the Masoretic sun; the KJV and the ASV have “before the sun went down”.

A riddle for a riddle. They answer in his form, two questions in two lines: “What is sweeter than honey, and what is stronger than a lion?” He answers in a third: “If you had not plowed with my heifer, you would not have found out my riddle.” Charashtem, “you plowed”, lies close in sound to charsah, the sun of the same verse. The NWT 1984 has “my young cow”. Josephus has Samson answer plainly: “nothing is more treacherous than a woman” (Antiquities 5.294).

Verses 19–20 · Thirty men of Ashkelon — spoil and changes of clothes, and a wife given to the best man

Ashkelon. The spirit rushes on him again, and he goes down to Ashkelon, one of the Philistine cities that Judah took in Judges 1:18 (now on these pages) and that is Philistine again here. He kills thirty men, takes their chalitsot, what is stripped off, and gives the chalifot, the changes of clothes; the two words differ by one letter. The TLB adds that he “abandoned his wife”, which the Hebrew does not say.

Up, and a wife given away. “His anger burned, and he went up to his father’s house”: down to Ashkelon, up to home. Then “Samson’s wife became his companion’s, the one who had been his friend” — mere’a, the word of the thirty “companions” of verse 11. The NWT 1984 calls him “a groomsman”, the TLB “best man”. Josephus says she married the friend who had led the bride, despising Samson’s anger (Antiquities 5.294).

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “went down” for yarad, each time; “right in my eyes” for yashar be’einai, the book’s closing refrain; “rushed on him” for tsalach, kept apart from “came upon” and “clothed”; “tell” for higgid all through; “riddle you a riddle” for achudah chidah; “linen wraps” for sedinim, as in Proverbs 31:24; “changes of clothes” for chalifot; “dispossess” for yarash; and “the seventh day” and “the sun” as the Masoretic text has them.

Echoes paid. The Nazirite rule of Judges 13:14 meets the vineyards, and the spirit that began to stir Samson at 13:25 rushes on him. The fire Ephraim threatened at Judges 12:1 is threatened again. Moses’ warning against “whatever is right in his own eyes” at Deuteronomy 12:8 is Samson’s reason. Gideon’s refusal to rule at Judges 8:23 stands behind “the Philistines were ruling”. Laban’s seven-day feast at Genesis 29:22 sets the length of this one.

Echoes planted. The fire is carried out at Judges 15:6. The spirit rushes on Samson a third time at 15:14, and “the uncircumcised” comes back in his own mouth at 15:18; those three are now on these pages. “Entice him”, “you do not love me” and “she pressed him” return with Delilah at 16:5, 16:15 and 16:16, also now on these pages. “Each man did what was right in his own eyes” closes the book at 17:6 and 21:25, both now on these pages.

Next installment in this book: Judges 15 — Samson comes back with a young goat to find his wife given away; three hundred foxes and their torches burn the Philistine grain, the woman and her father are burned, Judah hands Samson over bound at Lehi, and the spirit rushes on him again: a fresh jawbone of a donkey, a thousand men, and a spring for a thirsty man.
