Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · "again," and nine hundred iron chariots

The cycle restarts at the death of the judge, as it did at the death of Othniel. Vayosifu, "again," is the verb Judges 3:12 (already on these pages) used of Eglon's oppression, and the phrase "now that Ehud was dead" fixes the moment. The reader is told the cause before the oppressor is named.

"Nine hundred chariots of iron." The iron chariots are the ones Judges 1:19 (already on these pages) said were the reason Judah could not drive out the people of the valley. Here they are the weapon of the oppressor, and the translation keeps the same phrase, rekhev barzel, so the echo is visible.

"Twenty years." Othniel's oppression lasted eight years and Eglon's eighteen; this one lasts twenty. The translation keeps the number as the text gives it.

Verses 4–5 · a prophetess who judges, under a palm

"A prophetess." Neviah, a woman who speaks for God, is the first thing said of Deborah, and the chapter then describes her as the one who judges. The English keeps the title and the office together, so the reader does not split them.

"The wife of Lappidoth." Eshet lappidot can be read as the wife of a man named Lappidoth, which is how the ASV and the KJV give it, or as a woman of torches, since lappidot means flames. The name Lappidoth stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, so no other passage settles it, and the translation follows the shelf's reading without treating the second as settled.

"Deborah's palm." The tree is named after her, and the reader sees her sitting under her own tree between Ramah and Bethel. The NWT 1984 keeps the same name for the tree. The people come up to her for judgment, which is the job the chapter gives her.

Verses 6–10 · the conditions, and the honour that goes elsewhere

A summons with a command in it. Deborah quotes the command of "Jehovah, the God of Israel" (Elohei Yisrael) before she makes her own offer, and the offer is the one the chapter turns on: she will go with Barak, and Sisera will be given "into the hand of a woman" (verse 9).

"Your honour will not be on the road." Tiferet, honour or glory, is the word the verse uses. Barak asks for her presence as a condition, and she tells him the glory will go to someone else. The translation says "honour," because the reader needs to hear the word the verse actually uses.

The woman is the sign. The sentence "into the hand of a woman" is the first hint in the chapter of how the battle will end, and the reader is meant to wait for it. Verse 21 gives the answer.

Verses 11–16 · Heber's tent, and the battle at the brook

A Kenite who has left his people. Judges 1:16 (already on these pages) says the Kenites came up with Judah from the City of Palms. Here Heber "had separated himself" from them, and the verse traces his line to Hobab, Moses' father-in-law. The translation keeps the detail; the reader will need it when Heber's wife acts in verse 17.

"Elon-bezaananim." The Hebrew names the place twice in the same verse, once as Elon and once as Bezaananim, with a doubled form in the text, so the place is not clear. The shelf reads it three ways: the ASV has "the oak in Zaanannim", the NWT 1984 "the big tree in Za·a·nanʹnim", and the KJV "the plain of Zaanaim." The translation keeps "Elon-bezaananim", the form in the Hebrew, and the note says the reading is uncertain.

"Threw into confusion." Vayahem is the verb for panic or confusion in battle, and Jehovah does it, not Barak. The translation keeps the God-subject so that the reader sees who wins the field.

Verses 17–22 · the tent, the milk, and the peg

"There was peace." Verse 17 gives the reason Sisera runs to Jael: a treaty stands between Jabin and Heber's house. The word is shalom. The translation says "peace," so the reader sees that the house that shelters him is the one with which his king has made peace.

The covering. Verses 18 and 19 say that Jael covered Sisera. The shelf reads the word three ways: the NWT 1984 "blanket", the KJV "mantle", and the ASV "rug." The Hebrew smikhah is a covering, and the translation says "cloak," which is the nearest English.

"A skin of milk." Nod ha-khalav is a skin bottle, and the milk is what she gives him to drink. The translation keeps the skin, so the reader sees the container before the drink.

The peg. Verse 21 is the centre of the chapter. The Hebrew yated is a tent peg, and the translation keeps "tent peg" where the KJV reads "nail" and the ASV "tent-pin". The peg goes into the temple, raqqah, and the verse says he was asleep, and worn out, and died. The woman's hand is the one the Hebrew has been pointing at since verse 9.

Verses 23–24 · God subdues, and the hand grows harder

"God subdued." Vayakhni'a Elohim is the verb for bringing low, and the subject is God, which the chapter gives in the words of the narrator and not in Deborah's speech. The translation keeps "God" so the reader sees who the subject is.

"The hand grew harder and harder." Halokh vekashah means, literally, going and growing hard. The NWT 2013 reads "came down harder and harder", and the translation keeps the sense of a pressure that increases until it finishes the work.

The chapter ends by stopping before the song. Judges 5 (already on these pages) is the victory song that follows this prose account, and the prose ends here with Jabin destroyed.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: "again" for vayosifu, the same verb Judges 3:12 uses; "iron chariots" for rekhev barzel, kept identical to Judges 1:19 so the echo is visible; "honour" for tiferet, the word the verse uses; "peace" for shalom, the same word the reader has met for the absence of war; and "tent peg" for yated, the object in the one verse that decides the battle.

Echoes paid. "Chariots of iron" from Judges 1:19 is paid as the oppressor's weapon. The Kenite line of Hobab, Moses' father-in-law, from Judges 1:16 is paid in Heber's tent. "Into the hand of a woman," announced in verse 9, is paid in verse 21. The verb vayosifu of Judges 3:12 returns as the chapter's first word.

Echoes planted. The song of Judges 5 (already on these pages) is where the battle is told again, from the side of the victors, and the reader should keep the brook Kishon in mind for it. Deborah's palm, under which she judges, is the place the chapter begins with.
