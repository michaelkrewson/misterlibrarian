Moses legislates what Israel may and may not eat, and frames it with holiness. Two mourning rites are forbidden — gashing oneself and shaving a bald patch between the eyes for the dead — on the ground that Israel are children of Jehovah and a holy people, his treasured possession. Then the food law: ten permitted animals named outright (three domestic, seven wild) before the rule of split hoof and chewed cud is given; the camel, hare, hyrax and pig excluded; fins and scales required in the water; twenty-one birds forbidden by name; every winged swarming thing declared unclean. An animal that died on its own may not be eaten but may be given to the resident stranger or sold to a foreigner, and a kid may not be boiled in its mother's milk. The chapter closes on the tithe — eaten by the grower himself before Jehovah at the chosen place, converted to silver and spent on cattle, wine and beer if the road is too long, with the landless Levite not to be forsaken — and on a third-year tithe left in the town for the Levite, the stranger, the orphan and the widow, so that they eat and are satisfied.
Still the plains of Moab in the fortieth year (1:3); no new narrated event — the chapter continues the legal code without a break, and its versification realigns with the shelf after chapter 13's one-verse offset. ⚠ Its central section runs parallel to Leviticus 11 (already on these pages), spoken roughly thirty-eight years earlier at Sinai, and reworks it: shorter, reordered in two measurable places, without Leviticus's carcass-purity law or its permission to eat locusts, and in a different purity vocabulary. Like the chapters around it the law is written for a settled life that has not begun — gates, towns, a chosen place, a journey too long to carry a tithe — rather than for the camp it is spoken in.

Verses 1–2 · Between your eyes — mourning rites, and the one spot in the body Deuteronomy has already spoken about twice

★★ ‘Between your eyes’ is not a vague gesture at the forehead. It is a fixed phrase, and this verse is the only place in the Hebrew Bible that uses it for anything but the words of God. Searched consonantally across all 929 archived chapters of the Hebrew Bible, the plural bein eineichem stands in exactly two verses — Deuteronomy 11:18 and this one — and the singular bein einecha in three more: Exodus 13:9, 13:16 and Deuteronomy 6:8. Four of the five say to bind something there. Chapter 6 said it in the Shema and chapter 11 said it again three chapters ago: let them be frontlets between your eyes. The fifth is this one, and it forbids you to shave that same patch of skin bare for a corpse. The place reserved for God’s words is the place a mourner may not mark for the dead. ★ And whether an English reader can see it depends entirely on which version is open. The KJV and the ASV both print ‘between your eyes’ here, and both keep that identical phrase at all four of the other verses — so in either of them the connection is simply visible. The NIV breaks it at both ends: here it reads ‘shave the front of your heads’, and in the two Exodus verses it puts a reminder on the forehead instead. A reader of the most-read modern English Bible has no way to notice that the two commands name the same square of skin. This is not an argument, it is a search result.

Two rites, and only one of them is Leviticus’s. The verbs are gadad, to cut or gash oneself, and qorchah, a bald patch made by shaving. Leviticus forbids the cutting to everybody (19:28, ‘a gash for the dead you shall not put in your flesh’) — but it uses a different verb for it, sarat, which across the whole Hebrew Bible describes cutting flesh in only two verses, both in Leviticus. Deuteronomy reaches for gadad instead. And the baldness is the sharper case: qorchah stands in ten verses, and in the Torah in exactly two — Leviticus 21:5, where it is forbidden to the priests, and this verse, where it is forbidden to everyone. Leviticus gives the priests their reason in the next breath: ‘they shall be holy to their God’ (21:6). Deuteronomy gives the identical reason to the whole nation in the next verse. A rule for the clergy has been handed to the congregation, with its rationale attached.

⚠ What the gashing actually looked like is described elsewhere in the Bible, and not approvingly. The one narrative that shows the practice is the contest on Carmel, where the prophets of Baal ‘cut themselves with swords and lances, as was their custom, until blood gushed out on them’ (1 Kings 18:28, not yet on these pages) — the same verb, in the same reflexive form. Jeremiah 16:6 pairs the two rites of this verse exactly as this verse does, gashing and baldness together, for the dead. So the prohibition is not fussiness about grooming: it is a ban on a recognised mourning liturgy, practised by Israel’s neighbours and by Israel.

‘You are children of Jehovah your God’ is the reason, not a compliment. The logic runs: you are his children, therefore do not mutilate yourselves over a death. Something is being claimed about who owns the body, and about what a death does and does not mean. The TLB makes the polemic explicit and thereby narrows it, rendering the first clause ‘Since you are the people of God, never cut yourselves as the heathen do when they worship their idols’ — but the Hebrew says nothing about idols or worship here, and Jeremiah’s witness is that Israelites did this at funerals, not at shrines. The Douay shortens in the other direction, dropping the location: ‘nor make any baldness for the dead’, with no ‘between your eyes’ at all — and that is precisely the phrase this note is built on.

★ Verse 2 is Deuteronomy quoting itself, and the near-miss is the interesting part. Deuteronomy 7:6 reads, word for word, ‘for you are a holy people to Jehovah your God; you Jehovah your God chose to be his own people, a treasured possession, out of all the peoples that are on the face of the ground.’ Set the two side by side and diff them mechanically: twenty words there, nineteen here, eighteen of them identical and in the same order. Verse 2 adds one conjunction (be-cha becomes u-ve-cha) and drops one ‘your God’. The word carrying the sentence, segullah, stands in eight verses in the whole Bible, and this is one of the six that are about Israel.

Verse 3 · Abomination — the word this book used last chapter for a town that went after other gods

★★ The heading over the whole food law is a word Leviticus 11 never uses, and the last chapter used of apostasy. To’evah — here rendered abomination, as this translation has rendered it throughout — appears in Leviticus only in chapters 18 and 20, and nowhere in Leviticus 11, the chapter this one is otherwise rewriting. In Deuteronomy the same singular form stands at 7:26, at 13:15, here, and twice more later in the book. Thirteen verses ago it was what a whole town had done by serving other gods (‘this abomination has been done in your midst’); here it is what you must not put in your mouth. Deuteronomy is not softening the word for food. It is levelling the two things.

⚠ And the word Leviticus 11 does use is missing here entirely. Sheqets, ‘detestable thing’, stands in nine verses of Leviticus — eight of them in chapter 11 (11:10, 11, 12, 13, 20, 23, 41, 42). It occurs exactly once in all of Deuteronomy, at 7:26, and there of an idol, not of food. So the two chapters cover the same animals with two different purity vocabularies: Leviticus 11 detests, Deuteronomy 14 abominates and calls things unclean. Watch that swap happen concretely at verses 9–10.

The shelf splits on whether this is a noun or an adjective. The Hebrew is a bare noun with ‘any’ in front of it — kol to’evah, ‘any abomination’ — and of the thirteen witnesses on the two shelves exactly one keeps it that way: the Geneva, ‘Thou shalt eat no manner of abomination’. The KJV and ASV both turn it into a thing with a quality, ‘any abominable thing’; the NIV reads ‘any detestable thing’ and the NWT ‘no detestable thing of any sort’ — both of which quietly import Leviticus’s word into the verse that pointedly avoids it. The Spanish shelf does the same with one voice: the RV 1909 reads «Nada abominable comerás», the RV60 leaves that untouched, and the NVI has «No comas nada que sea abominable». The Douay goes furthest, ‘Eat not the things that are unclean’, which substitutes a third category the Hebrew has not reached yet.

Verses 4–6 · The seven game animals — a list Leviticus does not have, and three names nobody can identify

★ This is the one substantial thing Deuteronomy 14 adds to Leviticus 11, and it is a hunting list. Leviticus gives the rule — split hoof, chews the cud — and lets the reader apply it. Deuteronomy names ten animals first: three domestic (ox, sheep, goat) and seven wild ones, and only then, in verse 6, gives the rule. The Masoretes point the seven as three and four, and the shape matters: this is a law for people who eat game, which is exactly what chapter 12 had just made necessary. That chapter permitted ordinary slaughter at home for the first time, and its model for non-sacrificial meat was ‘as they eat the gazelle and the deer’ (12:15, 12:22). Two chapters later the gazelle and the deer head the list of what may be eaten. The animals chapter 12 used as an illustration have become the law.

⚠ Three of the seven names occur nowhere else in the Bible, and a fourth occurs once. Checked consonantally across the whole archived text: aqqo (fourth) and zamer (seventh) are hapax legomena — this verse is their only occurrence. Dishon (fifth) occurs once more, at 1 Chronicles 1:38, but there it is a Horite man’s name, so as an animal it too stands alone. Te’o (sixth) has one relative, Isaiah 51:20, where sons lie in the streets ‘like a to’ in a net’ — a large antelope, trapped. Nobody knows what four of these animals are. This translation prints the best modern identifications — wild goat, addax, oryx, mountain sheep — and says plainly that the last three are informed guesses, not knowledge.

★★ The shelf proves the point better than any argument could: at seven names, thirteen witnesses produce no two identical lists, and two of them produce animals that do not exist or do not live there. Fetched and set out in the Hebrew’s own order: the Geneva makes the fifth a ‘unicorn’ and the third a ‘bugle’, an archaic word for a wild ox — and, in a convergence worth a moment, the RV 1909 puts «el unicornio» in that identical fifth slot, two unrelated traditions reaching for the same impossible animal at the same name. The RV60 revises it to «el íbice». The Douay, translating the Vulgate, makes the seventh a ‘camelopardalus’ — a giraffe — and the third a ‘buffle’. The KJV and ASV both keep ‘pygarg’ at the fifth, which is not English at all but the Greek pygargos, ‘white-rump’, carried over untranslated from the Septuagint because the Hebrew was already unknown when the Greek was made. Both end on ‘chamois’, an Alpine goat-antelope that has never lived in the Levant, and so does the NWT 1984. Only the modern versions give animals a reader could actually meet in that country — the NIV closes on ‘the ibex, the antelope and the mountain sheep’, and the TLB names the same three. Two thousand years of translators have been guessing here, and the honest ones show it.

Verse 6 adds two words Leviticus does not have. Leviticus 11:3 reads ‘split hoof, cloven clear through’; Deuteronomy says ‘cloven clear through into two hooves’. It is a small expansion in the direction of the pedantic, and it is characteristic: where Leviticus states, Deuteronomy explains.

Verses 7–8 · The camel, the hare and the hyrax — four animals, one verse, and a reordering

Leviticus spends four verses on these four animals; Deuteronomy spends two. Leviticus 11:4–7 gives each its own verse and its own reason, in the order camel, hyrax, hare, pig. Deuteronomy puts the first three in a single sentence with one shared reason, and swaps the hare and the hyrax: camel, hare, hyrax. It also changes the grammar — Leviticus says of the hare, individually, ‘she brings up the cud’, a feminine singular; Deuteronomy sweeps all three together into a plural, ‘they bring up the cud’. The rewriting is a compression, and you can see the seams.

⚠ The zoology is wrong, and saying so is not a modern attack on the text. Neither the hare nor the hyrax chews the cud; neither is a ruminant. The hare practises refection — re-ingesting soft pellets — and the hyrax works its jaws in a way that looks exactly like chewing. The Hebrew phrase ma’aleh gerah means ‘brings up what has been chewed’, and it is a description of observable behaviour, not a claim about stomach chambers. A law that sorts animals by what a herdsman can see from a distance is doing what it says it is doing. The difficulty only arises if you require the verse to be a statement of comparative anatomy, which it never offered to be.

⚠ The hyrax has caused the shelf more trouble than any other animal on this page. It is a rock-dwelling mammal the size of a rabbit, and the KJV and ASV both call it a ‘coney’ — a word which in 1611 meant a rabbit and now means almost nothing to an English reader. The NWT reads ‘rock badger’, which at least puts it on a rock, though it is not a badger. This translation keeps hyrax, as it did at Leviticus 11:5, because it is the actual animal and the reader can look it up.

The pig gets one clause here and no special emphasis at all. It is fourth in a list, treated exactly like the camel. Every later association of this animal with Jewish identity — and the pig is the food-law animal that ancient outsiders always noticed — is built on two verses that do not single it out in the slightest. The one thing Deuteronomy adds is a shortening: Leviticus says the pig’s hoof is ‘cloven clear through’, and Deuteronomy drops those words, so the pig here merely ‘has a split hoof’.

Verses 9–10 · Fins and scales — and the exact point where Deuteronomy changes Leviticus’s word

★ This is the cleanest place to watch the vocabulary swap that verse 3 announced. The rule is identical in both books and stated in the same two words, senappir ve-qasqeset, fin and scale. The verdict is not. Leviticus 11:10–12 uses sheqets three times in three verses — ‘they are detestable to you… detestable they shall be to you… is detestable to you’ — and adds a command to hold their carcasses in detestation. Deuteronomy says tame hu lakhem, ‘it is unclean for you’, and stops. Four verses become two, an emotion becomes a status, and a rule that was about revulsion becomes a rule about category.

Deuteronomy also drops the water. Leviticus specifies ‘in the waters, in the seas and in the streams’, twice. Deuteronomy says only ‘of all that is in the waters’. It is the same shortening seen at the animals: the framing detail goes, the operative test stays.

⚠ Neither book names a single fish. Twenty-one birds are named a few verses from here and ten land animals just before, but the water gets a criterion and no examples at all — which is why the rule has been so unusually easy to apply for three thousand years, and why the argument about which creatures fail it (the sturgeon, the swordfish) is an argument about zoology and not about the text.

Verses 11–18 · The bird list — twenty-one names against Leviticus’s twenty, and a scribe’s confusion you can still see

★★ Deuteronomy’s bird list has one more name than Leviticus’s, and the extra one is almost certainly the same bird written twice. Count them: Leviticus 11:13–19 names twenty, this passage names twenty-one, and the whole difference sits in verse 13. Leviticus reads da’ah, ayah — two birds. Deuteronomy reads ra’ah, ayah, dayah — three. Now search the archive: with the article, ha-da’ah stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Leviticus 11:14. Ha-ra’ah as a bird stands in one verse, this one. Ha-dayah in the singular stands in one verse, this one. Three names, three sole occurrences, all of the same kind of bird — and ד and ר are the most easily confused pair of letters in the square Hebrew script, differing by the length of one corner. The straightforward reading is that a kite-name entered this verse twice, once in a misread spelling, and the list grew by one. The rabbinic tradition reached the same conclusion by a different route: Chullin 63b reports that ra’ah, da’ah and dayah are one bird under three names.

★★ And exactly one bird has moved, which you can see in English. Leviticus runs: little owl, cormorant, great owl, barn owl, pelican, Egyptian vulture. Deuteronomy runs: little owl, great owl, barn owl, pelican, Egyptian vulture, cormorant. Take the cormorant out of the Leviticus list and what remains is Deuteronomy’s first five, in the same order. One name has been lifted from second place and set down at the end; nothing else in the run has changed. That is a much more interesting fact than ‘the order differs’, and it is the kind of thing only a verse-by-verse comparison of two whole lists will show. Deuteronomy also gives the Egyptian vulture a feminine ending Leviticus does not, rachamah for racham.

⚠ Twenty-one names, and the shelf agrees on about eight of them. At verse 13 alone: the KJV reads ‘the glede, and the kite, and the vulture’, the ASV ‘the glede, and the falcon, and the kite’, the Geneva ‘the glede, nor the kite, nor the vulture’, the Douay ‘The ringtail, and the vulture, and the kite’, and the NIV ‘the red kite, the black kite, any kind of falcon’. A ‘glede’ is an obsolete English word for a kite; a ‘ringtail’ is a female hen harrier. ⚠ And one version on the Spanish shelf reaches the same conclusion this note does, by deleting rather than explaining: the RV60 prints only TWO names here — «el gallinazo, el milano según su especie» — where the Hebrew has three, quietly resolving the duplication instead of reproducing it. Its own predecessor the RV 1909 printed all three. At verse 16 the third bird is one word in Hebrew and four creatures on the shelf: the KJV has ‘the swan’, the ASV ‘the horned owl’, the Geneva ‘the redshank’, the NIV ‘the white owl’ — two owls, a wader and a waterfowl. This translation follows the identifications it used at Leviticus 11, so the two lists can be compared at all; that consistency is a decision about usefulness, not a claim to have identified the birds.

One name on the forbidden list is a bird modern Israel voted for. The dukhifat of verse 18 is the hoopoe — a cinnamon-coloured bird with a black-and-white barred back and a fan of a crest that it raises on landing. In 2008 it was chosen by public vote as the national bird of Israel, and it is unclean here and at Leviticus 11:19. ★ It is also the closest this page comes to a consensus: eleven of the thirteen witnesses on the two shelves name it — hoopoe in the ASV, NIV, TLB and NWT 1984, abubilla in all five Spanish versions, and the archaic ‘hoop’ in the Douay. The two that dissent are the two joined at the hip. The Geneva prints ‘nor the lapwing’, and the KJV that revised it prints ‘and the lapwing’ — the Geneva-to-King-James line showing itself again, on the one bird everybody else could see. A lapwing is a plover; it is not remotely the same bird.

Verses 19–20 · Winged swarming things — and the exception Deuteronomy silently deletes

★ Leviticus permits four insects by name. Deuteronomy forbids the category and lists nothing. Leviticus 11:21–22 makes a deliberate carve-out: ‘yet these you may eat… those that have jointed legs above their feet, to leap with them on the ground’, and then names four — the locust, the solam, the hargol and the grasshopper. Deuteronomy 14:19 says that every winged swarming thing is unclean and shall not be eaten, and moves on. The permission is not contradicted; it is simply not repeated, and a reader with only this chapter would never know locusts were allowed.

⚠ This is the largest single thing Deuteronomy leaves out of the shared material, and it is worth being precise about what that does and does not prove. It does not prove that Deuteronomy disagrees. The chapter omits far more than this — the whole second half of Leviticus 11, seventeen verses on carcass-contamination, ovens, cisterns and seed, is gone as well — and its omissions are consistently the technical priestly material. What survives is what a householder needs in order to buy, hunt and cook. The pattern of the omissions is a better guide to this chapter’s purpose than any one of them.

Verse 20 has a small surprise in it. Leviticus closes its bird section by naming the forbidden; Deuteronomy closes by re-permitting the clean, and it changes the noun to do it. Verse 11 said ‘every clean tzippor’, a bird; verse 20 says ‘every clean ‘of’, a flying thing — the broader word, which is also the word in ‘winged swarming thing’ one verse earlier. The section opens and closes on the same permission stated with two different nouns, and the second one deliberately reaches wider than the first.

Verse 21 · The kid in its mother’s milk — three occurrences, and the sentence that built a cuisine

★★ The command that ends this verse is the most consequential food sentence in the Bible, and it is here for the third and last time. Lo tevashel gedi ba-chalev immo — four words as the Hebrew prints them, six if you separate the joined pair and the prefixed preposition, which is how Exodus 34 counted them on these pages — stands in exactly three verses — Exodus 23:19, Exodus 34:26 and this one — and the first two are identical whole verses, both attaching the kid to a command about firstfruits: ‘the first of the firstfruits of your ground you shall bring to the house of Jehovah your God. You shall not boil a kid in its mother’s milk.’ Deuteronomy is the only one of the three that does not pair it with firstfruits. It attaches it instead to the law about a carcass, which is to say to a law about meat. The sentence has been moved out of the calendar and into the kitchen.

From this one sentence, repeated three times, comes the separation of meat and milk in Jewish law. The reasoning is old and explicit: a prohibition stated three times was read as three prohibitions — against cooking, against eating, and against deriving benefit — and from there the rule was extended by the rabbis from a kid and its own mother to meat and dairy generally, and from there to separate dishes, separate sinks and a waiting period between courses. Whether the biblical verse means any of that is a genuinely open question, and this translation does not settle it. What can be said plainly is what the Hebrew says: a young goat, its own mother’s milk, and the verb ‘boil’. Proposals for the original point range from a Canaanite ritual (the once-popular Ugaritic parallel, which the tablet in question does not actually support), to a humane rule of the same family as not taking a mother bird with her young, to a rejection of a particular festival dish. The verse is short, the tradition is enormous, and the honest gap between them is worth seeing.

⚠ The first half of the verse has two different foreigners in it, and half the shelf collapses them into one person. Hebrew: give the carcass la-ger, the resident stranger inside your gates, or sell it le-nokhri, to a foreigner. These are distinct legal statuses — the ger lives among you under your law’s protection; the nokhri is an outsider passing through. The ASV keeps them apart (‘the sojourner that is within thy gates… unto a foreigner’), as does the KJV (‘the stranger… unto an alien’) and the NWT (‘the alien resident… to a foreigner’). The Geneva uses one word for both, ‘unto the stranger that is within thy gates… unto a stranger’, so the distinction disappears. The Douay goes further and makes them the same man: ‘Give it to the stranger, that is within thy gates, to eat, or sell it to him’ — one person, two options, where the Hebrew has two persons and one option each. The TLB does the same (‘You may give it or sell it to him’).

⚠ And the law itself is in real tension with Leviticus, which this project notes rather than harmonises. Leviticus 17:15 assumes an Israelite or a ger may eat a carcass and requires him to wash and be unclean until evening — a purity procedure, not a ban. Deuteronomy forbids Israel to eat it at all and permits handing it to the ger with no procedure attached. The two are not easily reconciled, and the usual harmonisations (that Leviticus addresses an accident, that Deuteronomy’s ger is a different class of person) are proposals, not readings. Readings differ. The chapter’s own reason is the one it gives twice — ‘for you are a holy people to Jehovah your God’ — and see the next note for what those six words are doing structurally.

Verses 22–27 · A tithe you eat yourself — and buy beer with, if the road is long

★★ Here is the sentence that frames the whole chapter, and it is a frame you can measure. ‘For you are a holy people to Jehovah your God’ — six Hebrew words — stands at verse 2 and again at verse 21, character for character identical. Everything between them is the food list. And the grammatical number, which this book keeps switching, switches with it: verse 1 is plural, verses 2–3 are singular, verses 4 to the first clause of 21 are plural without exception, and from the second clause of 21 to the end of the chapter it is singular again. The plural block is precisely the borrowed material — the part that runs parallel to Leviticus 11, a chapter addressed throughout to ‘the children of Israel’ in the plural. Deuteronomy speaks to one man in its own voice and to the whole nation when it is quoting. Chapter 11 proposed that the seam is a quotation mark and chapter 13 showed the number tracking a quoted speaker’s audience; this chapter adds a third mechanism, and it is the plainest of the three, because the boundary is not a speech but a source.

★ The tithe in this chapter is not given away. It is eaten by the person who grew it. ‘You shall eat before Jehovah your God… the tithe of your grain, your new wine and your fresh oil.’ That is a different arrangement from Numbers 18:21–24, where ‘all the tithe in Israel’ is assigned to the Levites as their inheritance and the Israelite hands it over. Here the Levite is not the recipient of the tithe but a guest at the meal, protected by a separate clause: do not forsake him. The two systems are usually reconciled by counting more than one tithe, and the rabbinic tradition does exactly that — a first tithe to the Levite, a second eaten in Jerusalem, a third for the poor in the third year. The text itself does not number them. What it does do is reuse Numbers’s own justification for the Levite’s landlessness, in Deuteronomy’s own words.

‘He has no portion or inheritance with you’ is a Deuteronomic formula, and this chapter says it twice. The pair chelek ve-nachalah stands in six verses of the Hebrew Bible, and five of them are in this book — 10:9, 12:12, here at verse 27, again at verse 29, and once more at 18:1 (not yet on these pages). The sixth is Genesis 31:14, and it is not about Levites at all: it is Rachel and Leah asking whether they have any portion or inheritance left in their father’s house. The formula for a tribe with no land is first spoken by two women asking what they are owed.

★ And then the most surprising verse in the chapter. If the sanctuary is too far to carry the tithe, convert it to silver, ‘bind up the silver in your hand’, and spend it there on whatever your soul desires — cattle, flock, wine, and shekhar, which is beer. This is a religious tax that the taxpayer converts to cash, carries to the capital and spends on a feast for himself, with strong drink named in the statute. Chapter 12 used the same phrase, ‘as much as your soul desires’, to license eating meat at home; here it licenses the shopping list. The KJV keeps the organ — ‘whatsoever thy soul lusteth after’ — and so does the Douay (‘all that thy soul desireth’) and the NWT (‘whatever your soul may crave’). The Geneva quietly moves it, twice, to ‘whatsoever thine heart desireth’, which is a different Hebrew word in a different verse. On the drink, the KJV and Douay both print ‘strong drink’; the NIV has ‘other fermented drink’; and the TLB says the quiet part out loud with ‘beer’. On the Spanish shelf both Reina-Valeras turn the barley drink into an apple one: the RV 1909 reads «sidra» and so does the RV60. And the soul goes the same way as in the Geneva. The RV 1909 keeps it — «todo lo que deseare tu alma» — while the RV60 revises it away to «todo lo que deseas», which is the same loss the Geneva made in English three and a half centuries earlier.

Verses 28–29 · The third-year tithe — four people who do not own land, and a blessing conditional on feeding them

Every third year the tithe does not travel. It stays in the town, and four named categories eat it. The Levite, the stranger, the orphan and the widow — a quartet that recurs through Deuteronomy and has one thing in common: none of them holds land. The Levite by statute, the ger because a resident foreigner cannot own it, the orphan and the widow because in a system where property descends through men they have been cut off from the household that held it. In an agrarian economy, to have no land is to have no way of eating that does not depend on someone else’s decision. This law removes the decision.

★ The verb at the end is the point. ‘And they shall eat and be satisfied’ — ve-akhlu ve-save’u. Not relieved, not kept alive: sava, full. And the blessing formula that follows is conditional on it: so that Jehovah your God may bless you in all the work of your hand. The chapter that began by telling Israel not to cut itself for the dead ends by making its prosperity depend on whether the widow in its own gates has had enough to eat.

⚠ ‘At the end of three years’ is genuinely ambiguous in the Hebrew, and the shelf shows the ambiguity rather than resolving it. Mi-qtseh shalosh shanim can mean at the close of one three-year period, or at the end of each of them in a repeating cycle. The Hebrew has no word for ‘every’, and half the shelf supplies one anyway. The KJV keeps it bare — ‘At the end of three years’ — and so does the NWT 1984. The ASV reads ‘At the end of every three years’, committing the verse to the cycle. ★ That is worth pausing on, because it cuts across the usual grouping: the ASV is normally the most literal witness on this shelf and the one that resists supplying words, and here it supplies one, while the KJV it was made to revise does not — and the NWT’s own 2013 revision then makes the same addition its 1984 edition had declined to make. Two centuries apart, two revisions independently decided this verse needed help. They are probably right about the sense: Deuteronomy 26:12 (not yet on these pages) calls it ‘the third year, the year of tithing’ and lists the same four people. But that is a different verse doing the interpreting, and this one does not say it.

Echoes paid. ‘Between your eyes’, where Deuteronomy 6:8 and 11:18 told Israel to bind God’s words — the same square of skin this chapter forbids shaving for the dead, and the phrase’s only non-tefillin occurrence in the Bible (n14-1). To’evah, which 13:15 used thirteen verses ago of a town that served other gods, now heading a food law (n14-3). The gazelle and the deer, which 12:15 and 12:22 used as the model for meat that is not a sacrifice, arriving here at the head of the list of what may be eaten (n14-4). Chelek ve-nachalah, the Levite’s landlessness, from 10:9 and 12:12, said twice more here (n14-22). And segullah, offered at Exodus 19:5 and given to Israel at Deuteronomy 7:6, repeated at v2 in a sentence eighteen of whose words are 7:6’s own (n14-1).

Echoes planted. Shekhar, the beer of v26, which the priesthood is forbidden to drink on duty (Leviticus 10:9) and the Nazirite forbidden entirely — and which this verse names in a statute, at a festival, as a legitimate purchase. Chelek ve-nachalah again, whose sixth and last occurrence is Deuteronomy 18:1, four chapters from here, where the formula finally covers the whole tribe of Levi and not just the man in your gates. The quartet of Levite, stranger, orphan and widow, back at Deuteronomy 16, 24 and 26. And the third-year tithe of v28, which Deuteronomy 26:12 will name and date — the verse that decides how to read ‘at the end of three years’.

Patterns worth carrying forward. ★★ The grammatical-number question gets its fourth measured chapter, and a mechanism the other three could not supply: here the number tracks the SOURCE. Chapter 11 counted twelve alternating blocks and proposed that the seam is a quotation mark; chapter 12 counted two and killed the rival household hypothesis; chapter 13 found the number inside quotation marks tracking the quoted speaker’s audience. This chapter counts four blocks — v1 plural, vv2–3 singular, vv4–21a plural, vv21b–29 singular — and the long plural block is exactly the material shared with Leviticus 11, which is plural throughout. The seam is not a speech this time; it is a seam between Deuteronomy’s own voice and a source it is reworking. ⚠ And the two ends of the plural block are marked by the same six words repeated verbatim, ‘for you are a holy people to Jehovah your God’ (v2, v21) — so the frame is visible without knowing any Hebrew grammar at all.

★★ A whole chapter rewritten, and the rewriting is legible line by line. This project has not had a case like this since Leviticus. Set the two bird lists side by side and one name has moved — the cormorant, from second in its run to last — while everything around it holds its order (n14-11). Set the two lists of unfit land animals side by side and one pair has swapped, the hare and the hyrax (n14-7). Deuteronomy adds ten permitted animals Leviticus never lists, drops Leviticus’s explicit permission to eat four kinds of locust, drops seventeen verses of carcass-purity law, and swaps the operative word of the whole subject from sheqets to tame and to’evah. The omissions have a shape: what goes is priestly procedure, what stays is what a householder needs to buy, hunt and cook.

★ The archive settled four questions this chapter that no amount of reading could have. Bein eineichem in exactly two verses; qorchah in exactly two verses of the Torah, one of them priests-only; sheqets eight times in Leviticus 11 and never here; and the three kite-names each standing in exactly one verse apiece — which is what turns ‘the lists differ’ into ‘a scribe confused two letters’. ⚠ The same searches also caught two false starts of mine: dayah looked like a common word until the two hundred and twenty-six hits turned out to be the ending of names like Pedaiah, and aqqo looked frequent until every other hit proved to be va-aqum, ‘and I arose’. The tool matches letters, not words, which is exactly why the hits have to be read and not counted.

Library. One new dictionary term in both languages — shekhar, the beer of v26, which the library had been referring to in a cross-reference for months without ever defining. Six existing entries extended rather than duplicated — qorchah (which already named this verse and now carries the priests-to-people move), segullah, to’evah, sheqets, ger and min. Two entries gained a Spanish twin for the first time, qorchah and nesher, so of the 1,069 English dictionary terms the number with no Spanish twin moved down, 440 to 438. No new encyclopedia entries: this chapter names no person and no place. ⚠ VIDEO_QUEUE was checked per the standing rule — the three queued films are targeted at Joshua 7–8, Joshua 24 and Genesis 28, and none touches this chapter.

⚠ Two bookkeeping traps caught in the act, both worth recording because the first one nearly shipped. The add-versus-extend check is supposed to be a grep for the slug, the transliteration AND the Hebrew — and the grep pattern used here required four leading spaces, which is how min came to be inserted a second time under a key that already existed with no indentation at all. In a Python dict literal the later key silently wins, so the new entry would have been dead text nobody could see, in a file that would still have imported cleanly. A slug check has to match the file’s formatting as it actually is, not as it usually is. Running the same count properly then found a second duplicate that is not from this chapter — shuv is defined twice in DICTIONARY_ES — which has been left alone and filed rather than swept, on the precedent set when the dabaq/davaq pair was found last week.

Next installment in this book: Deuteronomy 15 — which takes the release of debts every seventh year and makes it a test of the heart, naming in advance the calculation that will occur to a lender as year seven approaches and calling it a base thought; frees the Hebrew slave after six years’ service and refuses to let him go empty-handed; and then, for the one case where a freed slave says he does not want to leave, prescribes an awl driven through his ear into the door. It also contains the sentence Jesus quotes about the poor — ‘the poor shall never cease out of the land’ — seven verses after the sentence that appears to contradict it flatly: there shall be no needy among you.
