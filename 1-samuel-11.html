Nahash the Ammonite besieges Jabesh-gilead and demands every man's right eye; Saul, coming in from the field behind his oxen, is seized by the spirit of God, cuts a yoke of oxen in pieces and sends them through Israel, musters the people at Bezek, and scatters the Ammonite camp in the morning watch. He spares the men who had doubted him, and at Gilgal the kingship is renewed and Saul is made king before Jehovah.
Soon after the assembly at Mizpah; the Greek and Josephus put the siege about a month later (1 Samuel 11:1).

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · Nahash the Ammonite and the right eye — “cut a covenant with us”, a reproach on all Israel, and seven days to find someone to save them

Nahash at Jabesh-gilead. “Then Nahash the Ammonite came up and encamped against Jabesh-gilead.” The king’s name is spelled exactly like nachash, “serpent”. Jabesh is the town of Gilead from which not a man came to the assembly at Mizpah against Benjamin, and it paid for it with four hundred of its daughters, who became wives for the men of Benjamin (Judges 21:12–14, now on these pages); the man who will rescue it is a Benjamite from Gibeah. The Greek opens the chapter “And it came about after about a month”, the words it read at the end of chapter 10 where the Hebrew has “he was like one keeping silent” (10:27); the Douay follows the Latin, which follows the Greek: “And it came to pass about a month after this”. Josephus calls Jabesh the chief city of the Gileadites and says Nahash had already blinded the right eyes of the people beyond the Jordan whom he conquered (Antiquities 6.68–71, numbered 6.5.1 in Whiston).

“Cut a covenant with us.” The men of Jabesh offer terms before a blow is struck: kerat lanu berit, “cut a covenant with us, and we will serve you” (karat). The request stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: twice from the Gibeonites, who came to Joshua at Gilgal with old sacks and dry bread and said “we are your servants; so now, cut a covenant with us” (Joshua 9:6, 11, now on these pages), and here, where an Israelite town says it to a foreign king. The ASV has “Make a covenant with us, and we will serve thee”; the NIV “Make a treaty with us, and we will be subject to you”; the NWT 1984 “Conclude a covenant with us that we may serve you”.

The right eye. Nahash’s condition is “by gouging out every right eye of yours”: binqor, from naqar, the verb of Samson’s blinding, “the Philistines seized him and gouged out his eyes” (Judges 16:21, now on these pages), and of Dathan and Abiram’s taunt, “Will you put out the eyes of these men?” (Numbers 16:14, now on these pages). The point is said aloud: “I will lay it as a reproach on all Israel”, a cherpah. The Hebrew gives no reason for the right eye; Josephus does: with the left eye covered by the shield, a man without his right eye was useless in battle (Antiquities 6.70). The ASV has “that all your right eyes be put out”; the KJV “that I may thrust out all your right eyes”; the NWT 1984 “on the condition of boring out every right eye of YOURS”.

“No one to save us.” The elders ask, “Hold off from us seven days”, heref lanu, the verb of “let go”, so that they can “send messengers throughout all the territory of Israel; and if there is no one to save us, we will come out to you”. Ein moshia, “no one to save”, is the phrase of the curse in Deuteronomy, “you will be only oppressed and robbed, always, with no one to save you” (Deuteronomy 28:29, now on these pages), and it answers the sneer that closed the last chapter, “How will this one save us?” (10:27). “Come out” is surrender.

Seven days’ respite. The ASV has “Give us seven days’ respite … if there be none to save us, we will come out to thee”; the KJV “if there be no man to save us”; the NWT 1984 “if there is no savior of us, we must then go out to you”. Josephus says the Gileadites dared not answer either way, and asked for the seven days to call on their kinsmen (Antiquities 6.72).

Verses 4–8 · Saul and the yoke of oxen — Saul behind the oxen, the spirit of God rushes on him, the pieces sent “throughout all the territory of Israel”, and the muster at Bezek

Behind the oxen. The messengers come “to Gibeah of Saul”, the first time the town carries his name, and the people weep. “And look — Saul was coming behind the oxen from the field”: the king chosen at Mizpah is back in the field with the cattle. The Greek reads “after the morning”: ha-baqar, “the oxen”, and ha-boqer, “the morning”, are the same four letters, and the morning returns in verse 11, “in the morning watch”. Josephus says Saul came back from his farm work to find the city weeping (Antiquities 6.75, numbered 6.5.2 in Whiston). The ASV has “Saul came following the oxen out of the field”; the KJV “Saul came after the herd out of the field”; the TLB “Saul was plowing in the field”.

The spirit rushes on Saul. “And the spirit of God rushed on Saul when he heard these words”: tsalach, the verb of the sign at Gibeah of God (10:6, 10) and of the spirit on Samson, “the spirit of Jehovah rushed on him” (Judges 14:6, now on these pages). At Gibeah it brought prophecy; here it brings anger, “and his anger burned very hot”. In verses 6 and 9 the written text has be-, “in”, where the margin reads ke-, “as”; the sense is the same. The Greek reads “the spirit of the Lord”. The ASV has “the Spirit of God came mightily upon Saul”; the NIV “the Spirit of God came powerfully upon him”; the NWT 1984 “the spirit of God became operative upon Saul”, its usual rendering, which reflects that version’s view of the spirit as God’s active force.

The pieces sent through Israel. “And he took a yoke of oxen and cut them up, and sent them throughout all the territory of Israel by the hand of the messengers”: vay-nattechehu, the verb of the Levite who “cut her up, limb by limb, into twelve pieces, and sent her throughout all the territory of Israel” (Judges 19:29, now on these pages; natach). “Throughout all the territory of Israel” stands in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible, two of them in this chapter and the first of them that one in Judges. The town is the same, Gibeah of Benjamin; then the pieces called the tribes against it, and now they call the tribes to follow a man from it. This time the threat is spelled out, and it is aimed at the cattle: “Whoever does not come out after Saul and after Samuel, so shall it be done to his oxen.” “A yoke of oxen” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible; the other two count Job’s five hundred yoke (Job 1:3, now on these pages, and 42:12, not yet). Josephus has Saul hamstring his oxen rather than cut them in pieces, and threaten the same to every man who did not come armed to the Jordan the next day (Antiquities 6.77, numbered 6.5.3 in Whiston). The KJV has “a yoke of oxen, and hewed them in pieces”; the NWT 1984 “a pair of bulls and cut them in pieces”; the TLB “two oxen”.

The dread of Jehovah, as one man. “And the dread of Jehovah fell on the people, and they came out as one man.” “The dread of Jehovah” stands in seven verses of the Hebrew Bible, this one first, then three in Isaiah 2 and three in Chronicles (pachad). “As one man” stands in nine verses, and three of them are the muster against Gibeah, when “the congregation was assembled as one man” (Judges 20:1, 8, 11, now on these pages). The Greek has “cried out as one man”. The ASV has “the dread of Jehovah fell on the people, and they came out as one man”; the KJV “they came out with one consent”; the TLB “God caused the people to be afraid of Saul’s anger”, which names the cause the Hebrew leaves as “the dread of Jehovah”.

Bezek: three hundred thousand. “He mustered them at Bezek”, paqad, the verb of a census, and Israel and Judah are counted apart: “the sons of Israel were three hundred thousand, and the men of Judah thirty thousand.” Bezek is west of the Jordan, opposite Jabesh; whether it is the Bezek where Judah fought (Judges 1:4, now on these pages) is uncertain. The Greek reads “six hundred thousand” and “seventy thousand”, and Josephus seven hundred thousand besides Judah, and seventy thousand of Judah (Antiquities 6.78). The NIV has “When Saul mustered them at Bezek”; the NWT 2013 “there were 300,000 Israelites and 30,000 men of Judah”.

Verses 9–11 · Jabesh-gilead rescued — “tomorrow, by the time the sun is hot, you shall have salvation”, a double-edged surrender, and three companies in the morning watch

“You shall have salvation.” The answer goes back with the messengers: “Tomorrow, by the time the sun is hot, you shall have salvation”, teshu’ah, from the root of moshia in verse 3, and the word of Samson’s cry, “You have given this great salvation by the hand of your servant” (Judges 15:18, now on these pages). Josephus makes it sharper: Saul promises to come on the third day and to win before sunrise, so that the sun would rise on men already set free (Antiquities 6.76, numbered 6.5.2 in Whiston). The ASV has “To-morrow, by the time the sun is hot, ye shall have deliverance”; the NWT 1984 “Tomorrow salvation will take place for YOU when the sun gets hot”; the TLB “We will rescue you before tomorrow noon!”

“Tomorrow we will come out to you.” Then the men of Jabesh say to the besiegers: “Tomorrow we will come out to you, and you shall do to us all that is good in your eyes.” It is the surrender promised in verse 3, “we will come out to you”, and the Ammonites hear it as one; the reader, who has just heard the other “tomorrow”, hears it as the other. The Hebrew does not say to whom they spoke; the Greek adds “to Nahash the Ammonite”. The ASV has “To-morrow we will come out unto you”; the NWT 1984 “Tomorrow we shall come out to YOU people”; the NIV “Tomorrow we will surrender to you”, which settles for the Ammonites’ meaning.

Three companies in the morning watch. “Saul put the people in three companies”, sheloshah rashim. “Three companies” stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible, among them Gideon’s three hundred (Judges 7:16) and Abimelech’s ambush (Judges 9:43), both now on these pages. They come into the camp “in the morning watch”, be-ashmoret ha-boqer, and the phrase stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here and at the sea, “in the morning watch Jehovah looked down on the camp of Egypt” (Exodus 14:24, now on these pages). They strike Ammon “until the heat of the day”, the hour promised in verse 9, “and those who were left were scattered, and two of them were not left together”. Josephus adds a night march over the Jordan and has Nahash himself killed in the battle (Antiquities 6.79, numbered 6.5.3 in Whiston); in Samuel, David will later speak of the kindness Nahash showed him (2 Samuel 10:2). The ASV has “in the morning watch … until the heat of the day”; the NIV “during the last watch of the night”.

Verses 12–15 · The kingship renewed at Gilgal — “Shall Saul reign over us?”, “not a man shall be put to death this day”, and Saul made king before Jehovah

“Shall Saul reign over us?” The victory turns the people on the doubters: “Who is it that said, Shall Saul reign over us? Hand over the men, and we will put them to death.” The words quoted are not the ones recorded in 10:27, “How will this one save us?”, but the men are plainly the same. In the Hebrew the quotation is a question without a question word; the Greek makes it a flat refusal, “Saul shall not reign over us”. The ASV has “Who is he that said, Shall Saul reign over us?”; the NWT 1984 “‘Saul—is he to be king over us?’”; the TLB “those men who said that Saul shouldn’t be our king”.

“Not a man shall be put to death this day.” Saul refuses: “for today Jehovah has worked salvation in Israel”, the teshu’ah of verse 9, now credited to Jehovah. Josephus gives the reason in his own words: it would be wrong to stain the victory God had given with the blood of kinsmen (Antiquities 6.82). The ASV has “to-day Jehovah hath wrought deliverance in Israel”; the NWT 1984 “today Jehovah has performed salvation in Israel”; the NWT 2013 “for today Jehovah has rescued Israel”.

“Renew the kingship.” Samuel says, “Come, and let us go to Gilgal, and renew the kingship there”: u-nechadesh, from chadash, “new”, and melukhah, the kingship of 10:16 and 10:25. Saul has now been made king three times: anointed in private (10:1), taken and acclaimed at Mizpah (10:24), and made king at Gilgal. Many readers see two or three accounts of how the kingship began set side by side; “renew” is the word that joins them. The ASV has “renew the kingdom there”; the NWT 1984 “that we may there make the kingship anew”; the TLB “reconfirm Saul as our king”.

Gilgal: made king before Jehovah. “And there they made Saul king before Jehovah in Gilgal”, the camp of Joshua, where Jehovah said “Today I have rolled away the reproach of Egypt from off you” (Joshua 5:9, now on these pages): the chapter that began with Nahash’s “reproach on all Israel” ends there. It is the place where Samuel told Saul to go down and wait for him to offer “sacrifices of peace offerings” (10:8), and the offerings are made: zevachim shelamim, and the pair stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at the covenant sacrifice at Sinai (Exodus 24:5, now on these pages). The Greek says that Samuel anointed Saul there, and that it was Samuel and all Israel who rejoiced; Josephus also has Samuel anoint him a second time with the holy oil (Antiquities 6.83, numbered 6.5.4 in Whiston). In the Hebrew, “there Saul and all the men of Israel rejoiced greatly”, and Samuel is not named in the verse at all. The ASV has “there they offered sacrifices of peace-offerings before Jehovah”; the NIV “There they sacrificed fellowship offerings”; the NWT 1984 “they rendered up communion sacrifices there before Jehovah”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “cut a covenant” for karat berit, as in Joshua 9; “gouging out” for naqar, as in Judges 16:21; “reproach” for cherpah, as at Gilgal in Joshua 5:9; “no one to save” for ein moshia; “rush on” for tsalach, as in 10:6; “cut them up” and “throughout all the territory of Israel”, as in Judges 19:29; “the dread of Jehovah”; “as one man”, as in Judges 20; “salvation” for teshu’ah, as in Judges 15:18; “three companies”, as in Judges 7:16; “the morning watch”, as in Exodus 14:24; “renew the kingship”; and “sacrifices of peace offerings”, as in 10:8.

Echoes paid. The pieces sent through Israel from Gibeah in Judges 19:29 are sent again from Gibeah; Jabesh-gilead, emptied of its daughters in Judges 21, is saved by a Benjamite; the sneer of 10:27, “How will this one save us?”, is answered by “Jehovah has worked salvation”; and Saul goes down to the Gilgal of 10:8.

Echoes planted. Samuel’s speech that ties Nahash’s attack to the people’s demand for a king (1 Samuel 12:12, now on these pages), the men of Jabesh who will take down Saul’s body and bury it (31:11–13), and Hanun son of Nahash (2 Samuel 10:2).

Next in this book: 1 Samuel 12 — Samuel’s farewell at Gilgal: “Whose ox have I taken?”, the story from Egypt to Nahash retold, thunder and rain at the wheat harvest, and “do not turn aside after empty things”.
