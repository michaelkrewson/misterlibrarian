“Happy are those whose way is blameless”: the longest chapter in the Bible, an alphabet of twenty-two stanzas of eight verses, in which almost every line names God’s law, testimonies, precepts, statutes, commandments, ordinances, word or saying, and which ends “I have strayed like a lost sheep; seek your servant”.
Undated, and the psalm has no heading; its date is disputed, and many place it after the exile, when the written law stood at the center of Israel’s life. The psalm itself names no event.

אַשְׁרֵי תְמִימֵי-דָרֶךְ--    הַהֹלְכִים, בְּתוֹרַת יְהוָה.

א Felices los de camino intachable, los que andan en la ley de Jehová.

א Felices los que custodian sus testimonios, los que lo buscan con todo el corazón.

א No han hecho injusticia; han andado en sus caminos.

א Tú has mandado tus preceptos, para que se guarden con diligencia.

ג Trata bien a tu siervo, para que viva y guarde tu palabra.

ג Descubre mis ojos, para que mire las maravillas de tu ley.

ג Quebrantada está mi alma de anhelo por tus ordenanzas en todo tiempo.

ג Rueda de sobre mí el oprobio y el desprecio, porque he custodiado tus testimonios.

ד Mi alma está pegada al polvo; dame vida conforme a tu palabra.

ה Dame entendimiento, y custodiaré tu ley, y la guardaré con todo el corazón.

ה Hazme andar por la senda de tus mandamientos, porque en ella me deleito.

ז Este es mi consuelo en mi aflicción: que tu dicho me ha dado vida.

ז Los soberbios se han burlado de mí en extremo; de tu ley no me he apartado.

ז I have remembered your ordinances from of old, Jehovah, and I take comfort.note
ז Me he acordado de tus ordenanzas desde la antigüedad, Jehová, y me consuelo.

ז Una ira ardiente se ha apoderado de mí a causa de los malvados que abandonan tu ley.

ז Tus estatutos han sido mis cantos en la casa de mis peregrinaciones.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–8 · Alef — “Blessed are the undefiled in the way”: the longest chapter in the Bible, an alphabet in eights, and eight words for what God has said

The shape. Psalm 119 has 176 verses, more than any other chapter of the Bible; the next longest, Numbers 7 (already on these pages), has 89. It is an alphabet: twenty-two stanzas of eight verses, one for each letter of the Hebrew alphabet, and every verse of a stanza begins with that stanza’s letter, marked at the start of each verse. Each note on this page takes one stanza.

Eight words. Almost every verse names what God has said, and the psalm has eight words for it, kept apart here as the Hebrew keeps them: torah, “law”; edot, “testimonies”; piqqudim, “precepts”; chuqqim, “statutes”; mitsvot, “commandments”; mishpatim, “ordinances”, the word used for these laws in Deuteronomy; davar, “word”; and imrah, “saying”. Read verse by verse, “law” stands in 25 verses of the psalm, “testimonies” in 23, “precepts” in 21, “statutes” and “commandments” in 22 each, and “saying” in 19. Four verses name none of the eight (3, 37, 90 and 122); three of them speak instead of his “ways” or his “faithfulness”. Torah means instruction, a parent’s teaching as much as a court’s ruling; the Psalms on these pages render it “law”, as in “his delight is in the law of Jehovah” (Psalm 1:2, already on these pages).

Happy, blameless. “Happy are those whose way is blameless, who walk in the law of Jehovah”: the psalm opens as the Psalter does, “Happy is the man who does not walk in the counsel of the wicked” (Psalm 1:1), with ashrei and the verb “walk”; the blameless are temimim, the whole. The ASV has “Blessed are they that are perfect in the way”; the Douay prints the letter’s name in the text, “ALEPH. Blessed are the undefiled in the way”, as the Greek does. The NWT 1984 keeps a separate English word for each of the eight, “law”, “reminders”, “orders”, “regulations”, “commandments”, “judicial decisions”, “word”, “saying”. The NIV lets them overlap: its verse 2 reads “Blessed are those who keep his statutes”, with “statutes” where the Hebrew has testimonies.

Verses 9–16 · Bet — “Wherewithal shall a young man cleanse his way?”: a pure path, a saying treasured in the heart, and musing

A treasure in the heart. “How shall a young man keep his path pure? By keeping it according to your word”: yezakkeh, to make clean. “In my heart I have treasured your saying, so that I may not sin against you”: tsafanti, to hide away as treasure, and the first imrah of the psalm, set beside the davar of verse 9. It is the law’s own charge, “these words that I am commanding you today shall be upon your heart” (Deuteronomy 6:6, already on these pages), done. “On your precepts I will muse”: asichah, from siach, to talk something over with oneself, a different verb from the hagah of Psalm 1:2. The NWT 1984 has “In my heart I have treasured up your saying”; the KJV “Thy word have I hid in mine heart, that I might not sin against thee”; the Geneva “I have hid thy promise in mine heart”; the ASV “Wherewith shall a young man cleanse his way?”.

Verses 17–24 · Gimel — “Open thou mine eyes”: wonders in the law, a sojourner in the land, and reproach rolled away

A sojourner. “Uncover my eyes, that I may look at wonders out of your law”: gal, uncover, as a veil is drawn back. “I am a sojourner in the land; do not hide your commandments from me”: the ger is the resident outsider, Abraham’s word for himself at Hebron, “I am a resident alien and a settler among you” (Genesis 23:4, already on these pages). “Roll reproach and contempt off me”: gal me‘alai, from galal, to roll, the letters of verse 18’s “uncover” put to a second use. “Your testimonies too are my delight, the men of my counsel”: sha‘ashu‘ai, my delight, which comes back in verses 77, 92, 143 and 174. The ASV has “I am a sojourner in the earth”; the KJV “I am a stranger in the earth”; the NWT 1984 “I am but an alien resident in the land” and “As men of my counsel”; the Douay “For thy testimonies are my meditation”.

Verses 25–32 · Dalet — “My soul cleaveth unto the dust”: give me life, a soul that drips away, and a heart made wide

Give me life. “My soul clings to the dust; give me life according to your word”: daveqah, the verb of a man who “clings to his wife” (Genesis 2:24, already on these pages), here clinging to the ground; and chayyeni, “give me life”, the prayer the psalm makes nine times (verses 25, 37, 40, 88, 107, 149, 154, 156, 159). “My soul drips away with grief”: dalefah, to drip or leak; the Greek read “my soul slumbered”, and so the Douay has “My soul hath slumbered through heaviness”. “I will run the way of your commandments, for you make my heart wide”: tarchiv, the root of breadth, which returns in verse 45’s “wide place” and verse 96’s “exceedingly wide”. The ASV has “My soul melteth for heaviness”; the NWT 1984 “My soul has been sleepless from grief”; the KJV “when thou shalt enlarge my heart”; the NIV “for you have broadened my understanding”.

Verses 33–40 · He — “Teach me, O Jehovah, the way of thy statutes”: the verb behind torah, not to gain, and a saying made to stand

Teach me. “Teach me, Jehovah, the way of your statutes, and I will guard it to the end”: horeni, from yarah, to point the way, the root of torah itself. Most of this stanza’s verses begin with a causative verb, a request that God make something happen: make me understand, make me tread, bend, turn away, make stand. “Bend my heart to your testimonies, and not to gain”: betsa, profit, often profit taken by force. “Turn my eyes away from looking at worthlessness”: shav, the empty, the worthless. The NWT 1984 has “Instruct me, O Jehovah, in the way of your regulations” and “And not to profits”; the ASV “Incline my heart unto thy testimonies, And not to covetousness”.

Verses 41–48 · Vav — “I will walk at liberty”: every line begins with “and”, a wide place, and kings

And, and, and. Few Hebrew words begin with vav, and here every verse begins with the conjunction itself, “and”; this translation keeps the “and” at the head of each line. “And I will walk about in a wide place, for I have sought your precepts”: ba-rechavah, the breadth of verse 32 again, room to move. “And I will speak of your testimonies before kings, and I will not be put to shame”. “And I will lift my hands to your commandments”: the gesture of prayer turned toward the words. “And let your acts of steadfast love come to me”: chasadekha, the plural of chesed. The ASV has “And I shall walk at liberty”; the NWT 1984 “And I will walk about in a roomy place”; the NIV “I will walk about in freedom”.

Verses 49–56 · Zayin — “Thy statutes have been my songs in the house of my pilgrimage”: remember, comfort in affliction, and songs of a sojourner

Remember. The stanza turns on zakhar, to remember: “Remember the word to your servant” (49), “I have remembered your ordinances from of old” (52), “In the night I have remembered your name” (55). “This is my comfort in my affliction, that your saying has given me life”. “Your statutes have been my songs in the house of my sojourning”: megurai, my sojournings, Jacob’s word to Pharaoh, “The days of the years of my sojournings are a hundred and thirty years” (Genesis 47:9, already on these pages). “Burning rage has seized me”: zal‘afah, a scorching wind. The ASV has “Thy statutes have been my songs In the house of my pilgrimage”; the NWT 1984 “In the house of my alien residences”; the NIV “wherever I lodge”.

Verses 57–64 · Chet — “Jehovah is my portion”: a priest’s portion, a softened face, and thanks at midnight

My portion. “Jehovah is my portion; I have said that I will keep your words”: chelqi, my share, what Jehovah said to Aaron, “I am your portion and your inheritance among the children of Israel” (Numbers 18:20, already on these pages). “I have sought your favor with my whole heart”: chilliti fanekha, literally “I have made your face soft”, the verb of “Moses pleaded with Jehovah his God” after the golden calf (Exodus 32:11, already on these pages). “At midnight I rise to give thanks to you”. “The earth, Jehovah, is full of your steadfast love”. The NWT 1984 has “Jehovah is my share” and “I have softened your face”; the ASV “I entreated thy favor with my whole heart”, and “At midnight I will rise to give thanks unto thee”.

Verses 65–72 · Tet — “It is good for me that I have been afflicted”: good five times over, good sense, and hearts like fat

Good. Five of the eight verses begin with tov, “good” (65, 66, 68, 71, 72). “Teach me good sense and knowledge”: tuv ta‘am, the ta‘am of “Taste and see” in Psalm 34 (already on these pages). “Before I was afflicted I went astray … It was good for me that I was afflicted, so that I might learn your statutes”: ‘innah, the verb of Deuteronomy’s “to humble you, to test you” in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 8:2, already on these pages). “Their heart is as dull as fat”: tafash, gross, unfeeling; the Greek has “their heart is curdled like milk”, and the Douay follows, “Their heart is curdled like milk”. The KJV has “Their heart is as fat as grease”; the ASV “Teach me good judgment and knowledge”; the Douay “It is good for me that thou hast humbled me”.

Verses 73–80 · Yod — “Thy hands have made me and fashioned me”: made and established, faithful affliction, and a reading in the margin

Made and established. “Your hands made me and established me”: ‘asuni va-yekhonenuni, the pair of the Song of Moses, “He made you, and he established you” (Deuteronomy 32:6, already on these pages). “I know, Jehovah, that your ordinances are righteous, and that in faithfulness you have afflicted me”. In verse 79 the written text has ve-yed‘u, “and they will know your testimonies”, and the reading in the margin, followed here, is ve-yod‘ei, “and those who know your testimonies”. “Let my heart be blameless in your statutes”: tamim, the word of verse 1. The ASV has “Thy hands have made me and fashioned me”, and in verse 79 the written text, “And they shall know thy testimonies”; the NWT 1984 the margin, “Those also knowing your reminders”.

Verses 81–88 · Kaf — “I am become like a bottle in the smoke”: eyes that fail, a wineskin in the smoke, and how many days

Fail, and finish. “My soul fails with longing for your salvation … My eyes fail with longing for your saying”: kaletah, kalu, to come to an end, worn out by waiting; and verse 87 turns the same root on the enemies, “They had almost made an end of me”. “For I have become like a wineskin in the smoke”: a skin hung over a fire, blackened and shrivelled; the Greek has “like a wineskin in frost”, and the Douay “like a bottle in the frost”. “How many are the days of your servant?”: a life too short to wait much longer. The ASV has “For I am become like a wine-skin in the smoke”; the NWT 1984 “like a skin bottle in the smoke”; the KJV “like a bottle in the smoke”.

Verses 89–96 · Lamed — “For ever, O Jehovah, thy word is settled in heaven”: a word that stands, an earth that stands, and the limit of perfection

Standing. “Forever, Jehovah, your word stands firm in the heavens”: nitsav, stationed, set in place; “you set the earth firm, and it stands. By your ordinances they stand to this day, for all things are your servants”: heaven and earth are pictured as servants on duty. “If your law had not been my delight, I would have perished in my affliction”. “To all perfection I have seen an end; your commandment is exceedingly wide”: tiklah, “perfection”, is a noun found in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one, and its sense is guessed from its root, “to finish”; and “wide” is the root of verses 32 and 45, a third time. The ASV has “For ever, O Jehovah, Thy word is settled in heaven” and “But thy commandment is exceeding broad”; the NIV “but your commands are boundless”; the Douay “I have seen an end to all persecution”.

Verses 97–104 · Mem — “O how love I thy law!”: wiser than enemies, teachers and elders, and sweeter than honey

Love and sweetness. “Oh how I love your law! All the day it is my musing”: sichati, my musing, the noun of the verb in verse 15. Three comparisons follow: wiser than my enemies, more insight than all my teachers, more understanding than the elders. “How sweet your saying is to my palate, sweeter than honey to my mouth!”: nimletsu, “sweet”, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and the Greek’s “sweet” is the usual guess; the Greek also adds the honeycomb. The ASV has “Oh how love I thy law! It is my meditation all the day” and “How sweet are thy words unto my taste!”; the NWT 1984 “How smooth to my palate your sayings have been”; the NIV “I have more insight than all my teachers”.

Verses 105–112 · Nun — “Thy word is a lamp unto my feet”: a lamp for one foot, a life in the palm, and offerings of the mouth

A lamp. “Your word is a lamp to my foot and a light to my path”: ner le-ragli, a small clay lamp, enough light for the next step, and “foot” is singular in the Hebrew. “Accept, I pray, the freewill offerings of my mouth”: words offered as a sacrifice is offered. “My life is in my palm continually”: to carry one’s life in one’s palm is to risk it, as Jephthah says, “I put my life in my palm and crossed over to the children of Ammon” (Judges 12:3, already on these pages). “I have bent my heart to do your statutes, forever, to the end”: ‘eqev, the “end” of verse 33. The KJV has “Thy word is a lamp unto my feet, and a light unto my path”; the NWT 1984 “Your word is a lamp to my foot”; the ASV “My soul is continually in my hand”; the NIV “Though I constantly take my life in my hands”.

Verses 113–120 · Samekh — “I hate vain thoughts”: those of two minds, a hiding-place, dross, and flesh that bristles

Two minds. “Those who are of two minds I hate, but your law I love”: se‘afim stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and Elijah’s question on Carmel, how long the people will limp between two opinions (1 Kings 18:21). “You are my hiding-place and my shield”: the seter of “The one who sits in the hiding-place of the Most High” (Psalm 91:1, already on these pages). “Like dross you have put an end to all the wicked of the earth”: sigim, the scum skimmed off molten silver, Isaiah’s “Your silver has become dross” (Isaiah 1:22, already on these pages); the Greek reads “I counted all the sinners of the earth as transgressors”, and the Douay “I have accounted all the sinners of the earth prevaricators”. “My flesh bristles for dread of you”: the Greek “nail down my flesh”, the Douay “Pierce thou my flesh with thy fear”. The ASV has “I hate them that are of a double mind”; the KJV “I hate vain thoughts”; the NWT 1984 “The halfhearted ones I have hated”.

Verses 121–128 · Ayin — “Be surety for thy servant for good”: a guarantor, time for Jehovah to act, and gold

A surety. “Be surety for your servant for good; do not let the arrogant oppress me”: ‘arov, to stand guarantor, Judah’s pledge for Benjamin, “I myself will be surety for him” (Genesis 43:9, already on these pages). This is one of the four verses that name none of the eight words; it asks for a person, not a ruling. “It is time for Jehovah to act; they have broken your law”: heferu, to break a covenant or annul a vow. “Therefore I love your commandments more than gold, more than fine gold”. In verse 128 the Hebrew has “all precepts concerning all things”, with no “your”. The ASV has “Be surety for thy servant for good” and “It is time for Jehovah to work; For they have made void thy law”; the NWT 1984 “It is the time for Jehovah to act”; the NIV “Ensure your servant’s well-being”.

Verses 129–136 · Pe — “The entrance of thy words giveth light”: an opening, a panting mouth, a shining face, and streams of tears

An opening. “The opening of your words gives light; it gives understanding to the simple”: petach, a doorway, an opening; and the peta’im are the simple, the untaught, whom Proverbs is written for, “to give the simple shrewdness” (Proverbs 1:4, already on these pages). “I opened my mouth wide and panted, for I longed for your commandments”. “Make your face shine on your servant”: the priests’ blessing, “Jehovah make his face shine on you” (Numbers 6:25, already on these pages), asked for one person. “Streams of water run down from my eyes, because they do not keep your law”. The ASV has “The opening of thy words giveth light”; the KJV “The entrance of thy words giveth light”; the NIV “The unfolding of your words gives light”; the NWT 1984 “The very disclosure of your words gives light”; the Douay “The declaration of thy words giveth light”.

Verses 137–144 · Tsade — “Righteous art thou, O Jehovah”: righteousness five times, zeal that consumes, and a refined saying

Righteous. The stanza’s letter begins tsaddiq, righteous, and the root stands five times in it: “Righteous are you, Jehovah” (137), “in righteousness” (138), “Your righteousness is righteousness forever” (142), “Your testimonies are righteous forever” (144). “My zeal has consumed me”: tsimmetatni, a different verb from the one in “Zeal for your house will consume me” (John 2:17, already on these pages, quoting Psalm 69:10). “Your saying is thoroughly refined”: tserufah, smelted, the dross gone. “I am small and despised”. The ASV has “My zeal hath consumed me” and “Thy word is very pure”; the NWT 1984 “My ardor has made an end of me” and “Your saying is very much refined”; the NIV “My zeal wears me out”.

Verses 145–152 · Qof — “I prevented the dawning of the morning”: calling, rising before dawn, and two kinds of near

Near and near. “I have called with my whole heart … I have called to you”: qarati, twice. “I rise before the dawn and cry for help … My eyes are awake before the night watches”: qiddamti, qiddemu, to be ahead of, up before the twilight and before each watch of the night. Then the root qarav, near, twice: “Those who pursue wicked schemes draw near” (150), and “You are near, Jehovah” (151). The schemes are zimmah, from the root of zamam, to plan. The ASV has “I anticipated the dawning of the morning, and cried” and “Thou art nigh, O Jehovah; And all thy commandments are truth”; the KJV “I prevented the dawning of the morning”; the NWT 1984 “My eyes have been ahead of the night watches”.

Verses 153–160 · Resh — “The sum of thy word is truth”: see, plead my case, and the head of your word

The head of your word. “See my affliction and rescue me” (153); “See how I love your precepts” (159); “I have seen the treacherous” (158): the stanza’s letter begins re’eh, see. “Plead my case and redeem me”: rivah rivi, argue my lawsuit, and ge’aleni, the verb of the go’el. “Give me life” comes three times here (154, 156, 159). “The sum of your word is truth”: rosh, the head, can mean the sum of a count or the beginning of a thing, and the shelf divides. The ASV has “The sum of thy word is truth”; the KJV “Thy word is true from the beginning”; the Douay, with the Greek, “The beginning of thy words is truth”; the NWT 1984 “The substance of your word is truth”.

Verses 161–168 · Shin — “Seven times a day do I praise thee”: great spoil, praise seven times a day, and no stumbling block

Seven times. The letter is written alike for sin and shin, and the stanza uses both: sarim, princes (161), sas, I rejoice (162), and sheqer, falsehood (163). “I rejoice over your saying like one who finds great spoil”. “Seven times a day I praise you for your righteous ordinances”: the Rule of Benedict (chapter 16) builds the seven daytime hours of monastic prayer on this verse in its Latin, as reported, not checked here. “Great peace have those who love your law, and for them there is no stumbling block”: mikhshol, the obstacle of “nor put a stumbling block before the blind” (Leviticus 19:14, already on these pages). The ASV has “Seven times a day do I praise thee” and “Great peace have they that love thy law”; the NWT 1984 “And for them there is no stumbling block”; the KJV “and nothing shall offend them”.

Verses 169–176 · Tav — “I have gone astray like a lost sheep”: a ringing cry, praise poured out, and an ending that is still a plea

A lost sheep. “Let my ringing cry come near before you”: rinnati, a shout, of joy or of need. “Let my lips pour out praise”: tabba‘nah, to bubble up like a spring. The psalm ends neither in triumph nor in rest: “I have strayed like a lost sheep; seek your servant, for I have not forgotten your commandments”. Ta‘iti, I have strayed, is the verb of “All of us like sheep have wandered” (Isaiah 53:6, already on these pages), and the shepherd who goes “after the lost one until he finds it” is the one in Luke 15:4 (already on these pages). After 175 verses of loving the law, the last word is that the lover is still lost, and still remembers. The ASV has “I have gone astray like a lost sheep; Seek thy servant”; the NWT 1984 “I have wandered like a lost sheep” and “May my lips bubble forth praise”; the ASV “Let my lips utter praise”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: the eight words, each with its own English throughout: “law” (torah), “testimonies” (edot), “precepts” (piqqudim, as in Psalm 103:18), “statutes” (chuqqim), “commandments” (mitsvot), “ordinances” (mishpatim, as in Deuteronomy), “word” (davar) and “saying” (imrah); “keep” for shamar and “guard” for natsar; “give me life” for chayyeni, nine times; “muse” for siach; “my delight” for sha‘ashu‘ai; “wide” for the root of breadth in verses 32, 45 and 96; and every line of the vav stanza beginning with “and”.

Echoes paid. The opening of Psalm 1; the words upon the heart of Deuteronomy 6; Abraham the resident alien (Genesis 23) and Jacob’s sojournings (Genesis 47); the clinging of Genesis 2:24; Aaron’s portion (Numbers 18); Moses pleading (Exodus 32); the humbling in the wilderness (Deuteronomy 8); “He made you, and he established you” (Deuteronomy 32); Jephthah’s life in his palm (Judges 12); the hiding-place of Psalm 91; Isaiah’s dross; Judah the surety (Genesis 43); the priests’ blessing (Numbers 6); the stumbling block of Leviticus 19; the straying sheep of Isaiah 53 and the shepherd of Luke 15; and the ta‘am of Psalm 34.

Echoes planted. Elijah’s two opinions (1 Kings 18:21) and the zeal of Psalm 69:10; neither is translated here yet.
