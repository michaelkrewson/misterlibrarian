The Nazirite vow — a voluntary, ordinarily temporary separation to Jehovah marked by no wine or grape products, no haircut, no contact with the dead — followed by the priestly blessing, the three-line benediction Aaron and his sons are told to speak over Israel, closing on Jehovah's own claim: 'they shall put my name on the children of Israel, and I will bless them.'

Numbers 6 meaning — a vow anyone could take, three chapters this project has already promised it would pay off

⚠ The Nazirite vow is voluntary and, by its own terms, temporary — any Israelite, man or woman, could take it for a fixed stretch of time, marked by three restrictions: no wine or anything from the grapevine, no cutting the hair, no contact with a corpse, not even for the closest family (v7). This project's own dictionary entry on nazir already named this chapter as the place its full law would land, and three already-shipped chapters have been quietly waiting on it: Samson's own lifelong vow (Judges 13, not yet on these pages), Hannah's vow over Samuel — 'no razor shall touch his head' (1 Samuel 1:11, already on these pages) — and John the Baptist, 'marked out from the womb like a lifelong Nazirite' (Luke 1:15, already on these pages). All three are LIFELONG versions of a vow this chapter's own law treats as ordinarily temporary — the exception, not the rule, in every case it produces a named figure worth remembering.

A vow interrupted by a stranger's death, not the Nazirite's own choice

V9's scenario is deliberately outside anyone's control: someone simply drops dead nearby, ‘very suddenly,’ and the vow is broken through no fault of the person keeping it. The law does not treat this as a moral failure — there is no punishment, only a reset: shave the head, wait seven days, bring two birds on the eighth, and start the whole count over. ⚠ V12’s closing line is unusually blunt about what this costs: ‘the former days shall be void.’ However long the vow had already run, none of it counts. A Nazirite three weeks from finishing who stands too close to a sudden death starts again from zero.

Finishing the vow costs more than starting it did

The completion ritual (vv13-20) is disproportionate to everything that came before it: three separate animals — a burnt offering, a sin-offering, a peace offering — plus bread, grain, and drink offerings, for a vow whose actual daily requirements were purely negative (no wine, no haircut, no corpses). ⚠ The single strangest instruction in the chapter is v18: the Nazirite shaves the hair grown specifically for this vow and burns it in the fire under the peace-offering sacrifice — the one physical, visible mark of months or years of separation, deliberately destroyed rather than kept, buried, or given any other treatment. The hair was never the point; the vow was, and once the vow is fulfilled the hair has no further status at all.

The oldest surviving words of the Bible, worn as jewelry a thousand years before anyone had a Bible to bind

⚠ In 1979, in a burial cave at Ketef Hinnom just outside Jerusalem's Old City, archaeologist Gabriel Barkay's team found two tiny rolled silver scrolls, inscribed with this exact blessing and dated to roughly 600 BC — before the Babylonian exile, centuries before the Dead Sea Scrolls, the oldest known fragment of biblical text in existence. They were amulets, worn rather than read, unrolled only after three years of careful conservation work; they now sit in the Israel Museum. The blessing itself is built to be felt as much as read: three lines in the Hebrew, each one longer than the last (three words, then five, then seven), each one naming Jehovah as the one who acts and the worshiper as the one blessed — guard, then shine and be gracious (the same root Joseph reaches for over the brother he cannot yet claim, Genesis 43:29, already on these pages, and the root inside the name Hannah, who pledged her own son to this chapter's own Nazirite vow), then lift up his face and grant peace. V27 closes on a claim stronger than the blessing itself: Jehovah puts his own name on the people being blessed, so that the blessing is not the priests' to give — they only speak it; Jehovah is the one who actually does it.

The shelf splits on v26's own idiom for lifted attention. KJV and ASV both keep the older English ‘lift up his countenance upon thee’, a word most modern readers meet nowhere else; NIV reads ‘turn his face toward you’, trading the archaism for a plainer image; NWT keeps the literal body part, ‘lift up his face toward you’, and renders v24’s ‘keep’ as ‘safeguard,’ a stronger English verb for the same Hebrew shamar this project has already tracked as the human's own garden vocation in Eden. This translation follows NWT’s literal face over KJV/ASV’s countenance, on the same reader-test principle guiding every other rendering choice here: a word still alive in ordinary English over one that sends the reader to a dictionary before it sends them to the sense.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. Hebrew and English Numbers 6 both run 1–27 — verified against the Masoretic text, no versification shift. Six Masoretic paragraphs divide it: closed after v23, v24, v25, v26, v27; open after v21. No new dictionary entries this chapter; nazir, neder, panim, and chanan are all reused from earlier chapters (already on these pages), each entry extended with this chapter's own payoff.

Echoes paid. The nazir dictionary entry's own promise (already on these pages) that ‘the full law is in Numbers 6,’ now delivered and linked from Hannah's vow over Samuel and from John the Baptist's own lifelong dedication (both already on these pages); the chanan dictionary entry's own root (already on these pages), first flagged at Joseph's blessing over Benjamin, now doing its most famous work in the Bible's best-known benediction; the panim dictionary entry's own running count of the word's different jobs (already on these pages), gaining a new one here — Jehovah's face turned toward, not away from, the one blessed; and Jeremiah 18-19's own bare citation of this exact blessing (already on these pages), now a real link in both directions, where the withdrawal of Jehovah's face reverses this chapter's own blessing on purpose.

Echoes planted. Judges 13's account of Samson's own lifelong Nazirite vow (not yet on these pages), announced before his birth exactly as Samuel's and John's were.
