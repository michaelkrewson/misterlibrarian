🕰
Word reaches Jesus, across the Jordan where chapter 10 just left him, that his friend Lazarus is sick; he deliberately waits two more days before setting out for Bethany, arriving to find Lazarus already four days in the tomb. Martha and Mary each tell him, separately, that he would not have died had Jesus been there; Martha confesses him 'the Christ, the Son of God,' and Jesus answers her 'I am the resurrection and the life.' At the tomb itself he weeps, then commands the stone removed and cries, 'Lazarus, come out' — and the dead man walks out, still bound in his grave wrappings. The chapter closes with the Sanhedrin convening over the sign's political fallout; the high priest Caiaphas argues, unknowingly prophesying, that one man dying for the people is better than the whole nation's destruction, and from that day the authorities plot to kill Jesus, who withdraws to a town called Ephraim near the wilderness.
c. AD 30, some weeks after the withdrawal across the Jordan that closed chapter 10 (10:40) and roughly a month before the final Passover of 12:1 and 13:1 (neither yet on these pages) — the chapter itself gives no exact date, only that Jesus then stayed with his disciples at Ephraim until 'the Jews' Passover was near' (11:54-55).

Ὁ οὖν Ἰησοῦς οὐκέτι παρρησίᾳ περιεπάτει ἐν τοῖς Ἰουδαίοις, ἀλλὰ ἀπῆλθεν ἐκεῖθεν εἰς τὴν χώραν ἐγγὺς τῆς ἐρήμου, εἰς Ἐφραὶμ λεγομένην πόλιν, κἀκεῖ ἔμεινεν μετὰ τῶν μαθητῶν.
Jesus therefore no longer walked about openly among the Jewish people, but went away from there into the region near the wilderness, to a town called Ephraim, and there he stayed with the disciples.note
Por eso Jesús ya no andaba abiertamente entre los judíos, sino que se retiró de allí a la región cercana al desierto, a una ciudad llamada Efraín, y allí se quedó con los discípulos.
55
Ἦν δὲ ἐγγὺς τὸ πάσχα τῶν Ἰουδαίων, καὶ ἀνέβησαν πολλοὶ εἰς Ἱεροσόλυμα ἐκ τῆς χώρας πρὸ τοῦ πάσχα ἵνα ἁγνίσωσιν ἑαυτούς.
Now the Passover of the Jewish people was near, and many went up from the country to Jerusalem before the Passover to purify themselves.note
Estaba cerca la Pascua de los judíos, y muchos subieron del campo a Jerusalén antes de la Pascua para purificarse.
56
ἐζήτουν οὖν τὸν Ἰησοῦν καὶ ἔλεγον μετʼ ἀλλήλων ἐν τῷ ἱερῷ ἑστηκότες· Τί δοκεῖ ὑμῖν; ὅτι οὐ μὴ ἔλθῃ εἰς τὴν ἑορτήν;
They were looking for Jesus and saying to one another as they stood in the temple, ‘What do you think? That he will not come to the feast at all?’note
Buscaban a Jesús, y se decían unos a otros, de pie en el templo: «¿Qué les parece? ¿Que no vendrá a la fiesta?».
57
δεδώκεισαν δὲ οἱ ἀρχιερεῖς καὶ οἱ Φαρισαῖοι ἐντολὰς ἵνα ἐάν τις γνῷ ποῦ ἐστιν μηνύσῃ, ὅπως πιάσωσιν αὐτόν.
Now the chief priests and the Pharisees had given orders that if anyone knew where he was, he should report it, so that they might arrest him.note
Y los principales sacerdotes y los fariseos habían dado órdenes de que, si alguien sabía dónde estaba, lo denunciara, para poder arrestarlo.

Notes on this translation

Verses 1–6 · ⚠ a name that means ‘God has helped,’ a narrator who assumes a scene not yet told, and two different verbs of love used of the same three people

‘Lazarus’ transliterates the Hebrew El’azar, ‘God has helped’ — a name this chapter is about to make literal. By pure coincidence of Greek spelling it is also the name Jesus gives the beggar in his own parable of the rich man (Luke 16:20, already on these pages), the only character given a personal name in any of his parables; the two Lazaruses are otherwise unconnected — one a parable figure, this one reported as history — but readers have confused them for centuries.

⚠ Verse 2 describes an anointing this Gospel has not yet narrated, as though the reader already knows it. ‘This Mary was the one who anointed the Lord with perfume and wiped his feet with her hair’ refers forward to a scene this book itself only tells at John 12:1–8 (not yet on these pages) — the narrator writing for an audience who, by the time this Gospel was finished, already knew the anointing as a famous, settled part of the story (compare Mark 14:9: ‘wherever the good news is proclaimed... what she has done will be told in memory of her’), even though inside this chapter’s own timeline it has not happened yet. This translation leaves the forward reference exactly as the Greek has it rather than smoothing the sequence.

‘The one you love’ (v3, phileis) and ‘Jesus loved’ (v5, ēgapa) use two different Greek verbs of the identical relationship, three verses apart. John famously varies agapaō and phileō in Peter’s own threefold restoration (John 21:15–17, not yet on these pages), and readers have argued for centuries over whether the swap there carries a deliberate shift in meaning. This chapter is a useful check on that argument: the sisters’ own message uses phileis of Jesus’ love for Lazarus, the narrator’s own comment three verses later uses ēgapa, and the crowd at the tomb uses ephilei again (v36) — three uses, two verbs, one unchanging relationship, with no hierarchy of intensity visible anywhere in the pattern. This translation keeps both English verbs as ‘love’ rather than inventing two different English words to chase a distinction the text itself does not appear to be making here.

Verses 7–16 · walking in daylight, a euphemism taken literally, and a disciple who expects a funeral

‘Are there not twelve hours in the day?’ reaches back for an image this Gospel has already built two chapters running. The Jewish day was reckoned in twelve hours from sunrise to sunset regardless of season, so the saying is first of all a plain proverb about timing — there is a safe window for walking, and night is not it. But ‘light of this world’ (v9) repeats, word for word, the claim this Gospel has Jesus make of himself at John 8:12 and John 9:5 (both already on these pages), where the same darkness/stumbling logic describes not a clock but a person. Jesus answers his disciples’ literal fear of Judea’s stone-throwers with what sounds like a proverb and is also, on this Gospel’s own established terms, a claim about who he is.

⚠ ‘Lazarus our friend has fallen asleep’ stages, as an actual scene, the misunderstanding this Gospel has built into a running device. The disciples hear the ordinary sense of a common Greek euphemism for death and answer about a sickbed, not a grave — forcing the narrator to step outside the dialogue and spell out, in his own voice, what Jesus actually meant (v13). This is the identical device already used of a word taken too literally at John 3:4 (being born anōthen, ‘again’ versus ‘from above’) and John 4:11 (‘living water’ as running water versus a gift that never runs dry): a character hears the plain, everyday sense of a word and is, each time, both right about the sense and wrong about what Jesus means by it. Checked directly against the shelf, ASV and KJV both read ‘our friend Lazarus is fallen asleep’/‘sleepeth’, keeping the euphemism this translation follows; the NWT’s 1984 edition instead prints ‘has gone to rest,’ a periphrasis that loses the sleep-image entirely, before its own 2013 revision moves back to ‘has fallen asleep’ — the 1984-versus-2013 drift this project keeps meeting elsewhere, here running the opposite direction, back toward the more literal image rather than away from it.

Thomas gets one line in this chapter, and this Gospel gives it to him rather than to Peter. ‘Let us also go, that we may die with him’ (v16) is not confusion or doubt but a grim, clear-eyed willingness to follow Jesus into what Thomas reasonably expects to be a fatal trip back into Judea — the same man this Gospel will later make famous for refusing to believe without proof (John 20:24–29, not yet on these pages). Didymos, ‘Twin,’ translates his Aramaic name, Te’oma; neither Gospel says who the other twin was.

Verses 17–27 · ⚠ four days, a later rabbinic belief, and the fifth of this Gospel’s seven ‘I am’ sayings

Fifteen stadia (v18) is a little under two miles, about three kilometres — the ordinary Greek unit the narrator reaches for to place Bethany precisely, the same measure Luke uses once elsewhere of the walk to Emmaus (Luke 24:13, already on these pages).

⚠ The ‘fourth day’ that Martha states so bluntly (v39, below) is already assumed here at v17, and later rabbinic sources supply a reason the Gospel itself never states. A belief attested in writings compiled well after this Gospel — that a departed soul lingers near the body for three days, hoping to re-enter it, and departs only once decomposition becomes visible on the fourth — is often read back into this chapter's own emphasis on the FOURTH day specifically, past the point where any ordinary hope of revival would still seem possible. This translation states the tradition for what it is, a later witness rather than a contemporary one, and does not claim the Gospel itself knew or relied on it.

‘Hoi Ioudaioi’ shifts referent inside this very chapter, and this translation tracks the shift rather than flattening it to one English phrase. At v8, backward-pointing to the stoning attempt that closed chapter 10, it names the hostile authorities and is kept as ‘the Jewish leaders,’ the rendering this project has used since John 1:19. From v19 on, though, it names the ordinary Judean public — neighbors come to sit with two grieving sisters, people who will go on, many of them, to believe in Jesus by the chapter’s own end (v45) — a group this translation renders ‘the Jewish people’ rather than forcing the leadership sense onto mourners at a funeral. The distinct hostile party in this chapter’s second half is instead named explicitly, in the Greek itself, as ‘the chief priests and the Pharisees’ (vv47, 57).

‘I am the resurrection and the life’ (v25) is the fifth of this Gospel’s seven predicated ‘I am’ sayings — after the bread of life (John 6:35) and the light of the world (John 8:12), the gate and the good shepherd (John 10:7, 11, all already on these pages); the sixth and seventh, ‘the way, the truth, and the life’ and ‘the true vine,’ already stand on these pages too, at John 14:6 and John 15:1, translated ahead of this chapter in the book's own sequence. Martha’s own confession that follows, ‘you are the Christ, the Son of God, who is coming into the world’ (v27), anticipates almost word for word this Gospel’s own stated purpose for having been written at all (John 20:31, not yet on these pages) — the clearest such confession any character gives Jesus face to face anywhere in this Gospel, spoken by a woman, before the sign that is supposed to prove it has even happened.

Verses 28–32 · the identical sentence from both sisters, and a crowd that follows a mourner by mistake

Mary greets Jesus with the same sentence Martha already used (v32, compare v21), down to the word order. Both sisters, independently, reach first for the identical conditional — ‘Lord, if you had been here, my brother would not have died’ — a repetition this translation keeps rather than varying one sister’s wording for the sake of style; two people who have clearly said the same thing to each other already, waiting for him, now say it to his face.

The crowd following Mary ‘supposing that she was going to the tomb to weep there’ (v31) is a small, human detail: they follow their own assumption about her grief and stumble, by accident, into the scene they are about to witness. Nothing in the Greek suggests they know Jesus has arrived; they are consoling a mourner and end up as witnesses to a resurrection because they guessed wrong about where she was going.

Verses 33–37 · ⚠ two different Greek words for weeping, eight words apart, and a verb every shelf version softens

‘Deeply moved in spirit and troubled’ (v33) translates enebrimēsato, a verb whose ordinary force is closer to anger or stern indignation than to the gentler ‘moved’ every shelf version checked here prints, English and Spanish alike. Built on the sound of a horse’s snort, it names a stern, almost harsh warning elsewhere in the Gospels (Mark 1:43; Matthew 9:30). ASV and KJV both read ‘he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled’, keeping ‘spirit’ explicit; the NWT’s 1984 edition agrees, ‘groaned in the spirit and became troubled,’ but its own 2013 revision drops the word entirely — ‘he groaned within himself and became troubled’ — and the Spanish TNM's 2019 revision softens furthest of all, ‘se conmovió en lo más profundo de su ser y se sintió angustiado,’ with no anger, no spirit, and no indignation left anywhere in the sentence. What, exactly, Jesus is indignant AT here — death itself, the crowd’s unbelief, grief’s own helplessness in the face of what he is about to do — is a real question this chapter raises and never answers; this translation states the word’s harder edge rather than resolving which reading is right, and uses the identical verb again three verses later (v38) of Jesus ‘again deeply moved within himself’ approaching the tomb.

⚠ ‘Jesus shed tears’ (v35) is, in the Greek, a different and rarer verb than the loud weeping this same chapter has just used twice of Mary and the mourners (vv31, 33). Edakrysen is a true New Testament hapax — its one and only appearance anywhere in the Greek New Testament — built on the noun for a single tear, a quieter act than the audible klaiō used all around it. The world's shortest-famous verse by word count turns out to rest on the New Testament's single rarest verb for weeping, a distinction every shelf version flattens to the identical ‘wept’/‘shed tears,’ this translation included in its own English prose outside this note — the difference survives only in a note like this one.

Verses 38–44 · a prayer spoken for the crowd’s benefit, a name called into a grave, and a dead man who comes out still bound

Martha’s objection (v39) is the chapter’s most ordinary, least theological line, and it is exactly what grounds everything that follows. ‘By now he smells’ is blunt, practical, embarrassed realism from a woman who has already confessed Jesus ‘the Christ, the Son of God’ twelve verses earlier (v27) — belief and a sister’s plain horror at an open grave sit side by side in the same character without either one cancelling the other. The shelf splits three ways on just how blunt to let her be: KJV keeps the crude physical verb outright, ‘by this time he stinketh’; the ASV reaches for a euphemism instead, ‘by this time the body decayeth’, trading Martha’s blunt sensory complaint for an abstract process; and the NWT, in both its 1984 and 2013 editions, keeps the plain ‘he must smell’ — closer to the KJV’s bluntness than to the ASV’s abstraction, and the reading this translation follows.

The prayer at the open tomb (vv41–42) is addressed to the Father but spoken, by Jesus’ own stated reason, for the crowd standing around. ‘I knew that you always hear me, but I said this for the sake of the crowd... so that they may believe that you sent me’ makes the prayer itself a public sign rather than a private petition — Jesus states outright that the outcome was never in doubt to him, only its evidentiary value to everyone else present.

⚠ ‘Lazarus, come out’ (v43) is, as far as this translation can find, the only resurrection command in any Gospel addressed to the dead person BY NAME. Jesus raises Jairus’s daughter with ‘Child, arise’ (Luke 8:54, already on these pages) and the widow of Nain’s son with ‘Young man, I say to you, arise’ (Luke 7:14, already on these pages) — neither one named. Only here does Jesus call a specific, personal name into a grave.

Lazarus comes out of the tomb still bound hand and foot in his own grave wrappings, needing Jesus’ own separate instruction — ‘untie him, and let him go’ — before he can move freely. The miracle of life returning and the practical work of unwrapping a body are kept as two distinct actions in the Greek's own order, the second left to human hands. This chapter's own bound, wrapped dead man is worth holding against the folded, EMPTY grave-cloths this Gospel will describe at its own ending (John 20:6–7, not yet on these pages) — two resurrections, in the same book, leaving two very different kinds of evidence behind in the tomb.

Verses 45–48 · a council convened over a miracle

The Sanhedrin's fear (v48) is political, not theological, and historically plausible on its own terms: Rome had crushed provincial unrest before, and a popular movement around an acclaimed wonder-worker was exactly the kind of thing that could provoke it. ‘The Romans will come and take away both our place and our nation’ names a real and reasonable calculation, not manufactured outrage — the same practical fear that will shape the crowd's own choice between Jesus and Barabbas at the Passover this sign has now made politically urgent.

Verses 49–53 · a high priest who tells the truth for the wrong reason

Caiaphas's own argument (vv49–50) is a textbook case of a speaker saying more than he knows, and the narrator says so explicitly: ‘this he did not say on his own’ (v51). This Gospel frames an unbelieving, scheming high priest's cynical political calculation as unwitting prophecy, true in a sense its own speaker never intended — compare the narrower, older case of a hired foreign diviner made to bless the very nation he was paid to curse, against his own will, three separate times (Numbers 22–24, already on these pages): in both stories, the mouth of a man working against God's people is made to speak a truth his own intentions cannot touch.

‘To gather into one the scattered children of God’ (v52) reaches back to the image this Gospel used of its own shepherd one chapter before Lazarus died in the story's own sequence: ‘other sheep that are not of this fold... they will become one flock, one shepherd’ (John 10:16, already on these pages). The narrator's own gloss on Caiaphas's words widens a cynical political calculation about one nation into a promise about a scattered family gathered from everywhere — the identical widening this Gospel has already made once, in the shepherd's own voice.

Verses 54–57 · a town whose location nobody can confirm, and a city asking the right question for the wrong reason

⚠ ‘Ephraim’ (v54) is named only here in the whole New Testament, and no site has been identified with certainty. The likeliest candidates lie a short distance northeast of Jerusalem toward the Judean wilderness, but the Gospel supplies no further detail to confirm any one location — a real place this chapter names and then lets vanish from the record.

The crowd gathering in Jerusalem before the feast asks the one question this whole chapter has already answered for the reader, without knowing it: ‘what do you think? that he will not come to the feast at all?’ (v56). The reader already knows exactly why he might stay away — a kill order is now standing (v57) — while the crowd in the temple is still only gossiping about a no-show. The gap between what the reader has just been told and what the city itself only suspects is this chapter's own last turn of the knife before John 12 (not yet on these pages) answers the question by having him come anyway.

Patterns worth carrying forward.

⚠ Renderings chosen. Hoi Ioudaioi splits by referent within this single chapter — ‘the Jewish leaders’ at v8, matching the hostile sense carried since John 1:19, but ‘the Jewish people’ from v19 on, where the word names sympathetic mourners and neighbors rather than the authorities (the distinct hostile party in this chapter is named explicitly as ‘the chief priests and the Pharisees,’ vv47, 57). Dakryō (v35) is kept as the plain ‘shed tears’ rather than the more familiar ‘wept,’ to leave room for the dictionary note marking its distinction from klaiō. Embrimaomai (vv33, 38) keeps ‘deeply moved’ while the note states the word's harder, more indignant edge rather than silently upgrading the English past what every other shelf version prints.

Echoes paid. The ‘light of this world’ (v9) repeats John 8:12 and John 9:5 (both already on these pages). The misunderstood euphemism (vv11–13) repeats the device already used of anōthen at John 3:4 and ‘living water’ at John 4:11 (both already on these pages). ‘I am the resurrection and the life’ (v25) is the fifth of the seven predicated ‘I am’ sayings this Gospel opened at John 6's bread of life, continued at John 8's light, John 10's gate and shepherd, and already paid out of order at the sixth and seventh, John 14:6 and John 15:1. ‘Gather into one the scattered children of God’ (v52) answers the ‘other sheep... one flock, one shepherd’ promise of John 10:16. And the man born blind's own healing (John 9, already on these pages) is the exact precedent some in the crowd cite while watching this chapter's own greater sign (v37).

Echoes planted. Lazarus still bound in his own grave wrappings (v44) anticipates the folded, empty grave-cloths of Jesus' own tomb (John 20:6–7, not yet on these pages) — two resurrections in one Gospel, leaving two different kinds of evidence behind. Caiaphas's unwitting prophecy (vv49–52) is the first time this Gospel names him; he returns, presiding over Jesus' own trial, many chapters from now (18:13–14, 24, 28, not yet on these pages). And Martha's confession (v27) anticipates this Gospel's own stated purpose for existing at all (20:31, not yet on these pages), word for word.

Left open on purpose. What, precisely, Jesus is indignant at when he is ‘deeply moved in spirit’ (vv33, 38) — death, unbelief, or grief itself — a question this chapter raises and does not resolve. Whether this chapter's own insistence on the FOURTH day draws on the later rabbinic three-days tradition about a lingering soul, or simply reports what Martha happened to say — the Gospel itself supplies no citation either way. And where, exactly, ‘Ephraim’ (v54) stood — a real place named once and never again in the whole Bible, its location a matter of likelihood rather than certainty even now.

Next installment in this book: John 12, not yet translated — six days before the Passover, an anointing this very chapter already assumed the reader knew, and the triumphal entry into Jerusalem.
