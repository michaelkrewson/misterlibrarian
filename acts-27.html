When it is decided that ‘we’ should sail away to Italy, Paul and certain other prisoners are handed over to a centurion named Julius, of the Augustan cohort, and the party sets sail in a ship of Adramyttium, Aristarchus of Thessalonica with them. At Sidon Julius treats Paul with kindness and lets him go to his friends; the party sails under Cyprus, across the open sea along Cilicia and Pamphylia, to Myra of Lycia, where the centurion puts them on a ship of Alexandria sailing for Italy. After many slow days they sail under Crete, off Salmone, and with difficulty reach Fair Havens, near the city of Lasea. With the Fast already past and the voyage hazardous, Paul advises them that it will be with injury and much loss, not only of the cargo and the ship but of their lives; the centurion is more persuaded by the pilot and the shipowner, and the majority give counsel to set sail for Phoenix, a harbor of Crete looking down the southwest wind and down the northwest wind, to winter there. A south wind blows gently; soon a wind of typhoon force, called Euraquilo, beats down, the ship cannot face into it, and they run under the small island of Cauda, barely getting control of the ship’s boat, undergirding the ship and lowering the gear for fear of the Syrtis. The next day they begin to throw cargo out, on the third day they throw out the ship’s equipment with their own hands, and with neither sun nor stars for many days all hope of being saved is taken away. After a long lack of food Paul stands in the midst of them and says they ought to have obeyed him, urges them to take courage, for there will be no loss of life but only of the ship, and tells of an angel of the God whose he is and whom he serves, who stood by him that night: ‘Do not fear, Paul; you must stand before Caesar, and look, God has granted you all who are sailing with you’; they must be cast onto a certain island. On the fourteenth night, being driven across the Adria, the sailors suspect land, sound twenty fathoms and then fifteen, throw four anchors from the stern and pray for day; when the sailors seek to flee, lowering the ship’s boat under a pretext, Paul tells the centurion and the soldiers that unless these men stay in the ship they cannot be saved, and the soldiers cut the ropes of the boat and let it fall away. Before day Paul urges them all to take a share of food, for not a hair from the head of any of them will perish; he takes bread, gives thanks to God before all and begins to eat, and they all become cheerful and eat; there are two hundred seventy-six souls in the ship, and they throw the grain into the sea. At day they do not recognize the land but see a bay with a beach and plan to drive the ship onto it; they cast off the anchors, loose the ropes of the steering oars, hoist the foresail and hold for the beach, but fall into a place between two seas and run the ship aground, the bow immovable and the stern broken up by the force. The soldiers plan to kill the prisoners; the centurion, wishing to bring Paul safely through, prevents them and orders those who can swim to go first to the land, the rest on planks and on things from the ship, and so all are brought safely through to the land.
Undated by the text. The voyage follows the hearing of Acts 26 and the decision to send Paul to Italy (25:12; 26:32), and the text gives no year. It places two things on the calendar, ‘the Fast had already gone by’ (v9), which reference works tie to the autumn (reported, not checked here), and a count of fourteen nights and days at sea (v27, v33), with the starting point of the count not given. This library dates the events no more closely than the book does. Acts 28 follows on these pages.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · ἐκρίθη … ἡμᾶς … δεσμώτας … ἑκατοντάρχῃ … σπείρης Σεβαστῆς … Ἀδραμυττηνῷ ekrithē … hēmas … desmōtas … hekatontarchē … speirēs Sebastēs … Adramyttēnō — Acts 27:1–2: why does “we” return to the story, who are the “prisoners” and the centurion Julius of the “Augustan cohort,” and what is a ship “of Adramyttium”?

“It was decided that we should sail away” (Acts 27:1). Hōs de ekrithē tou apoplein hēmas eis tēn Italian: the verb is an impersonal passive, “it was decided,” and Luke does not say by whom. The decision itself was made two chapters back: Festus said “To Caesar you shall go” (25:12), and Agrippa said the man could have been released had he not appealed (26:32). The new thing is the pronoun. I searched Acts for hēmin, hēmas, hēmōn, hēmeis, and between 21:18 (“Paul went in with us to James”) and this verse every hit is inside a speech (21:23, 21:25, 22:14, 23:15, 24:4, 24:8, 25:24, 26:6–14); the narration of Caesarea and the hearings is in the third person. The narrator is back among the travelers. Luke does not name the speaker and does not say when or where the narrator joined the party, and this page does not guess. Shelf: NWT 1984 “as it was decided for us to sail away to Italy,” ASV “when it was determined that we should sail for Italy” and KJV “when it was determined that we should sail into Italy” keep the “we.” Douay “it was determined that he should sail into Italy” changes it to “he,” which is the pronoun the Greek does not have, and TLB “Arrangements were finally made to start us on our way to Rome by ship” writes “Rome” where the Greek says Italian, “Italy.”

“Prisoners” (Acts 27:1). Paulon kai tinas heterous desmōtas: “Paul and certain other prisoners.” The noun desmōtēs (not the desmōtērion, “prison”) stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 27:42, where the soldiers plan to kill them. In the rest of Acts the prisoner is desmios, at 16:25, 16:27, 23:18, 25:14, 25:27 and 28:17; the entry desmios follows that word. Luke gives the other prisoners no names, number, crime or country, and heterous, “other,” does not say whether they were of the same kind as Paul. All seven shelf versions print “prisoners.”

“A centurion named Julius, of the Augustan cohort” (Acts 27:1). Hekatontarchē onomati Ioulio speirēs Sebastēs. The noun hekatontarchēs, “centurion,” stands in thirteen verses of Acts (read from the hits), eight before this chapter (10:1, 10:22, 21:32, 22:25, 22:26, 23:17, 23:23, 24:23) and five in it (27:1, 6, 11, 31, 43); no other chapter of Acts has it more than twice. The name Julius stands in two verses here (27:1 and 27:3) and nowhere else in the New Testament as a man’s name; Ioulian at Romans 16:15 is the feminine form, “Julia” (already on these pages). The unit is speira Sebastē: speira is the “cohort” of 10:1 (“called Italian”) and 21:31, and Sebastē is the adjective of the title Festus used for the Emperor at 25:21 and 25:25; the entries speira and Sebastos follow both words. Reference works connect an “Augusta” cohort with auxiliary troops in Syria; that is reported here, not checked, and the Greek says nothing of where Julius’s unit was raised or what its duty was. Shelf: ASV “of the Augustan band” is nearest; KJV “of Augustus’ band,” Geneva “of the band of Augustus” and NWT 1984 “of the band of Au·gusʹtus” make the adjective a possessive; Douay “of the band Augusta” transliterates it. NIV “the Imperial Regiment” and TLB “a member of the imperial guard” name a different kind of unit, a regiment and a guard, and TLB also turns the centurion into “an officer.”

“A ship of Adramyttium” (Acts 27:2). Epibantes de ploiō Adramyttēnō mellonti plein eis tous kata tēn Asian topous anēchthēmen. The adjective Adramyttēnos stands in one verse of the New Testament, so it is not compared with any other use. It names the ship by its port, which reference works place on the north Aegean coast of the Roman province of Asia; that location is reported, not checked here, and the library entry Adramyttium says what is known. The verb anēchthēmen is the nautical “set sail,” the verb of the departures at 20:13, 21:1 and 21:2. The apparatus (1015–1016) has two points: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print mellonti, “that was about,” agreeing with the ship, where Robinson–Pierpont has the plural mellontes, “we who were about”; and Robinson–Pierpont lacks eis, “to.” The printed text and this translation follow the first. Shelf: NWT 1984 “a boat from Ad·ra·mytʹti·um” and NIV “a ship from Adramyttium” read the adjective as “from”; ASV “a ship of Adramyttium” and KJV “a ship of Adramyttium” keep the Greek’s vagueness; Douay “a ship of Adrumetum” spells the port differently; TLB “a boat that was scheduled to make several stops along the Turkish coast” replaces the port and the voyage with a modern country and a timetable.

“Aristarchus, a Macedonian of Thessalonica” (Acts 27:2). Ontos syn hēmin Aristarchou Makedonos Thessalonikeōs. The name stands in five verses of the New Testament: Acts 19:29 (seized at Ephesus), 20:4 (“of the Thessalonians Aristarchus and Secundus”), here, Philemon 24 and Colossians 4:10 (not yet on these pages); the entry Aristarchus gathers them. Luke does not say why he sails with Paul, whether as a companion, a servant or a fellow prisoner, and the genitive absolute “being with us” is all the Greek gives. Shelf: TLB “a Greek from Thessalonica” writes “Greek” where the Greek has Makedonos, “Macedonian,” and Geneva “of Macedonia, a Thessalonian” reverses the order of region and city.

Verses 3–6 · φιλανθρώπως … ἐπέτρεψεν … ἐπιμελείας … ὑπεπλεύσαμεν … πέλαγος … Μύρα τῆς Λυκίας … πλοῖον Ἀλεξανδρῖνον philanthrōpōs … epetrepsen … epimeleias … hypepleusamen … pelagos … Myra tēs Lykias … ploion Alexandrinon — Acts 27:3–6: what does Julius’s “kindness” allow, what does it mean to sail “under” Cyprus, and why does the centurion move the prisoners to an Alexandrian ship?

“Treating Paul with kindness” (Acts 27:3). Philanthrōpōs te ho Ioulios tō Paulō chrēsamenos epetrepsen pros tous philous poreuthenti epimeleias tychein. The adverb philanthrōpōs stands in one verse of the New Testament; the noun philanthrōpia stands in two, Acts 28:2 (the islanders’ kindness) and Titus 3:4 (not yet on these pages). The verb epetrepsen, “permitted,” is the verb of leave that opens 26:1; here the one granting it is a centurion, and what he grants is that a prisoner go to “the friends” and obtain epimeleias, “care.” The noun epimeleia stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one, and Luke does not say what the care was, who the friends were, or whether a soldier went with Paul. The nearest earlier case is 24:23, where Felix “gave orders to the centurion that Paul be kept, yet have some freedom, and that none of his own people be prevented from serving him”; the same kind of leave, from another Roman officer, now at a harbor. Shelf: NWT 1984 “permitted him to go to his friends and enjoy [their] care” and NIV “allowed him to go to his friends so they might provide for his needs” keep the care as the friends’ giving; ASV “gave him leave to go unto his friends and refresh himself” and KJV “gave him liberty to go unto his friends to refresh himself” turn the noun into Paul’s own refreshing; TLB “let him go ashore to visit with friends and receive their hospitality” adds “ashore” and “hospitality.”

“We sailed under Cyprus” (Acts 27:4). Hypepleusamen tēn Kypron dia to tous anemous einai enantious. The verb hypepleō, “sail under,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 27:7 (“we sailed under Crete”); the hypo- in it is the same “under” that this translation keeps in both verses. Reference works read the prefix as “in the shelter of,” sailing on the side of the island away from the wind; that reading is reported and not checked here, and it fits the reason Luke gives, “because the winds were contrary” (enantious). It is not how the party passed Cyprus at 21:3, where Luke says they “sighted” it and “left it on our left.” Shelf: ASV “sailed under the lee of Cyprus” and NIV “passed to the lee of Cyprus” add the sailor’s word for the sheltered side, and NWT 1984 “sailed under the [shelter of] Cyprus” brackets the same supplement; KJV “sailed under Cyprus” and Douay “sailed under Cyprus” keep the Greek bare; Geneva “sailed hard by Cyprus” says “close to”; TLB “sailed north of Cyprus between the island and the mainland” gives a compass point and a channel that the Greek does not.

“The open sea along Cilicia and Pamphylia” (Acts 27:5). To te pelagos to kata tēn Kilikian kai Pamphylian diapleusantes katēlthomen eis Myra tēs Lykias. The noun pelagos stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Matthew 18:6, where a man would be “sunk in the open sea”; it is the sea away from the shore, and this translation says “open sea” at both. Cilicia and Pamphylia are two places Acts has already named together with Paul: Pamphylia at 13:13 and 14:24, and Cilicia at 15:41 and 21:39 (“a Tarsian of Cilicia”); see the entries Cilicia and Pamphylia. The harbor, Myra, stands in one verse of the New Testament as a place, and Lycia likewise; the library entry Myra says what is known. Shelf: NWT 1984 “navigated through the open sea” and NIV “sailed across the open sea” keep pelagos; ASV “sailed across the sea which is off Cilicia and Pamphylia” and KJV “sailed over the sea of Cilicia and Pamphylia” do not; TLB “passed along the coast of the provinces” makes the open sea a coast. Douay “we came to Lystra, which is in Lycia” prints a different city: Lystra lies in Lycaonia (14:6), and no reading in the apparatus at this verse has it, so the shelf version carries a name the Greek manuscripts do not.

“A ship of Alexandria sailing to Italy” (Acts 27:6). Kakei heurōn ho hekatontarchēs ploion Alexandrinon pleon eis tēn Italian enebibasen hēmas eis auto. The adjective Alexandrinos stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and 28:11, where another “Alexandrian ship” carries the party on from the island; the entry Alexandria says what is known of the city. The centurion “found” it at Myra, as the party had “found a ship crossing over to Phoenicia” (21:2), and the verb is his: he “put us on board,” so the choice of ship is Julius’s and not the prisoners’. Why a ship from Alexandria bound for Italy lay at Myra, Luke does not say. Shelf: NWT 1984 “a boat from Alexandria that was sailing for Italy, and he made us board it” and NIV “an Alexandrian ship sailing for Italy and put us on board” both keep the destination; ASV “a ship of Alexandria sailing for Italy” and KJV “a ship of Alexandria sailing into Italy” likewise; Douay “removed us into it” is a loose verb for “put on board”; TLB “an Egyptian ship from Alexandria” adds “Egyptian,” which the adjective Alexandrinon does not say.

Verses 7–8 · βραδυπλοοῦντες … μόλις … Κνίδον … Σαλμώνην … Καλοὺς Λιμένας … Λασαία bradyploountes … molis … Knidon … Salmōnēn … Kalous Limenas … Lasaia — Acts 27:7–8: what is “with difficulty” doing twice in two verses, where are Cnidus, Salmone and Fair Havens, and how is the city spelled?

“Sailing slowly” (Acts 27:7). En hikanais de hēmerais bradyploountes kai molis genomenoi kata tēn Knidon, mē proseōntos hēmas tou anemou, hypepleusamen tēn Krētēn kata Salmōnēn. The verb bradyploeō, “sail slowly,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. So do proseaō, the wind that “did not let us” go on, and the two place names, Knidos (Cnidus) and Salmōnē. Reference works place Cnidus at the southwest tip of Asia Minor and Salmone at the east end of Crete; those placements are reported, not checked, and the entries Cnidus, Salmone and Crete say what is known. The turn is in the verb: the wind will not let them go on, so they sail hypepleusamen, “under” Crete, the verb of 27:4 that this translation renders “sailed under” both times. Crete is not new to the book: 2:11 lists “Cretans” among the hearers at Pentecost, and the name and its people stand in seven verses of the New Testament, read from the hits: Acts 2:11 and Titus 1:12 (the people), Acts 27:7, 27:12, 27:13 and 27:21, and Titus 1:5 (the island). Luke does not mention Paul preaching on the island, and this page does not connect that verse to the voyage. Shelf: NWT 1984 “after sailing on slowly quite a number of days” and ASV “when we had sailed slowly many days” keep the adverb; KJV “the wind not suffering us” and Douay “the wind not suffering us” keep the older “suffering” for “allowing”; NIV “We made slow headway for many days” turns the participle into a noun phrase; Geneva “we sailed hard by Candia” uses a later name of the island; TLB “passing the port of Salome” makes Salmone a port, where the Greek has only kata Salmōnēn, “off Salmone,” and spells it differently.

“With difficulty” (Acts 27:7–8). Molis stands twice in these two verses, as “with difficulty arriving off Cnidus” (v7) and “with difficulty coasting along it” (v8). The adverb molis stands in six verses of the New Testament, read from the hits: Acts 14:18 (where this page prints “scarcely made the crowds stop sacrificing to them”), 27:7, 27:8, 27:16, Romans 5:7 (scarcely will anyone die for a righteous person) and 1 Peter 4:18 (the righteous man is saved with difficulty), the last one not yet on these pages. Four of the six are in Acts, and three of those four are in this chapter; Luke uses the word for a crowd, and then for a ship that can barely make headway. The verb paralegomai, “coast along,” stands in two verses, here and 27:13. Shelf: NWT 1984 “coasting along it with difficulty” and ASV “with difficulty coasting along it” give the pair in Luke’s order; NIV “We moved along the coast with difficulty” keeps it; KJV “hardly passing it” and Geneva “with much ado sailed beyond it” read paralegomai as going past, where the Greek verb says “along.”

“Fair Havens” and “Lasea” (Acts 27:8). Eis topon tina kaloumenon Kalous Limenas hō egys ēn polis Lasaia. Kaloi Limenes, “Fair Havens,” is a place name that stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does Lasaia, the city; both are given only by name and by their nearness to each other, and the library entries Fair Havens and Lasea say what reference works report of them. The apparatus (1019–1020) has two points: the word order, ēn polis in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont against polis ēn in Nestle–Aland 28, and the spelling, Lasaia in Westcott–Hort, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont against Lasea in Tregelles. This translation prints Lasaia as “Lasea,” the English form six of the shelf versions use (NWT 1984 “La·seʹa,” ASV, KJV, Geneva, NIV and TLB “Lasea”). Douay “a certain place, which is called Good-havens, nigh to which was the city of Thalassa” translates the harbor’s name and prints a different city, a name that none of the four editions in the apparatus has. NIV “the town of Lasea” and TLB “the city of Lasea” differ on polis; the Greek word is the usual “city.” On the shelf’s own text, TLB prints the same sentence for verses 7 and 8, so it has no separate verse 8 to compare.

Verses 9–12 · νηστείαν … παρῄνει … ὕβρεως … ζημίας … κυβερνήτῃ … ναυκλήρῳ … Φοίνικα … λίβα … χῶρον nēsteian … parēnei … hybreōs … zēmias … kybernētē … nauklērō … Phoinika … liba … chōron — Acts 27:9–12: what is “the Fast,” what does Paul foresee in the words “injury” and “loss,” why does the centurion follow the pilot, and which way does Phoenix face?

“The Fast had already gone by” (Acts 27:9). Dia to kai tēn nēsteian ēdē parelēlythenai. The noun nēsteia, “fast,” stands in five verses of the New Testament, read from the hits: Luke 2:37 (Anna, “with fastings and prayers”), Acts 14:23, this verse, and 2 Corinthians 6:5 and 11:27 (neither on these pages). It is the only one of the five with the article, tēn nēsteian, “the Fast,” and Luke does not say what fast he means. Reference works identify it with the Day of Atonement, whose date they place in the autumn; that identification is reported, not checked here, and it rests on the article and on the season, since Luke never writes “Atonement.” The Law does not call that day a “fast”; it commands that the people “afflict your souls” on the tenth day of the seventh month (Leviticus 16:29, 23:27, Numbers 29:7), and “the Fast” would then be the name Luke gives that day, if the reference works are right. The word kai, “also,” in “the Fast also” adds the Fast to “considerable time” as a second reason the voyage was hazardous. Shelf: ASV “the Fast was now already gone by,” Geneva “the Fast was now passed” and Douay “the fast was now past” keep the noun and the article; KJV “the fast was now already past” does too. NWT 1984 “even the fast [of atonement day] had already passed by” brackets the identification, NIV “it was after the Day of Atonement” prints it without brackets, and TLB “it was late in the year” drops the fast and names a season.

“Advised” and “hazardous” (Acts 27:9). Parēnei ho Paulos: the imperfect, “was advising,” a repeated or continuing act. The verb paraineō stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 27:22, where Paul “urges” courage; in both it is Paul speaking to the whole ship. The adjective episphalēs, “hazardous,” stands in one verse. Shelf: NWT 1984 “Paul made a recommendation” and Geneva “Paul exhorted them” are the nearest; ASV and KJV “Paul admonished them” make it a warning; NIV “Paul warned them” does too; Douay “Paul comforted them” says the opposite of what follows, since what Paul says in v10 is a warning; TLB “Paul spoke to the ship’s officers about it” narrows his audience to officers, where the Greek has the pronoun “them.”

“Injury and much loss” (Acts 27:10). Theōrō hoti meta hybreōs kai pollēs zēmias ou monon tou phortiou kai tou ploiou alla kai tōn psychōn hēmōn mellein esesthai ton ploun. Two nouns carry it. Hybris stands in three verses of the New Testament as a noun, this one, 27:21 and 2 Corinthians 12:10 (not yet on these pages), and this translation prints “injury” for it at both verses here. Zēmia, “loss,” stands in four verses: 27:10, 27:21, and Philippians 3:7 and 3:8 (neither yet on these pages). Both nouns return in v21, where Paul says the ship should not have sailed “and so gained this injury and loss”; the two verses bracket the storm. The sentence names three things at risk, the cargo, the ship, and psychōn, “lives,” the plural of psychē that v22 uses in the singular for “loss of life” and v37 uses for the “souls” counted aboard; the entry psychē follows the word. Shelf: ASV “with injury and much loss, not only of the lading and the ship, but also of our lives” is the nearest; KJV “with hurt and much damage” and Geneva “with hurt, and much damage” use “hurt” and “damage” for the pair; Douay “beginneth to be with injury and much damage” adds a verb of beginning; NWT 1984 “with damage and great loss” and “our souls” keeps psychōn as “souls”; NIV “disastrous and bring great loss to ship and cargo, and to our own lives also” and TLB “perhaps shipwreck, loss of cargo, injuries, and death” recast it, and the TLB adds “shipwreck” and “death,” neither of which the Greek names.

“The pilot and the shipowner” (Acts 27:11). Ho de hekatontarchēs tō kybernētē kai tō nauklērō mallon epeitheto ē tois hypo Paulou legomenois. The nouns are kybernētēs, the one who steers, in two verses (here and Revelation 18:17, not yet on these pages), and nauklēros, the owner or charterer of the ship, in one. The centurion is “persuaded” by them rather than by Paul, and the verb comes back at 27:21 in its compound: Paul says they ought to have peitharchēsantas, “obeyed,” him, a verb that stands in four verses, Acts 5:29, 5:32, 27:21 and Titus 3:1 (not yet on these pages), and is used at 5:29 for obeying God rather than men. The apparatus (1021–1022) has two small points: Robinson–Pierpont reverses mallon epeitheto and adds the article before Paul’s name. Shelf: NWT 1984 “heeding the pilot and the shipowner” and NIV “followed the advice of the pilot and of the owner of the ship” use “pilot”; Douay “believed the pilot and the master of the ship” does too, but its second word is “master”; ASV “gave more heed to the master and to the owner of the ship” and KJV “believed the master and the owner of the ship” use “master” for the pilot; Geneva “believed rather the governor and the master of the ship” uses “governor” for the steersman; TLB “the officers in charge of the prisoners listened more to the ship’s captain and the owner” makes Julius a plural group of officers and the pilot a captain.

“Phoenix, a harbor of Crete” (Acts 27:12). Anheuthetou de tou limenos hyparchontos pros paracheimasian hoi pleiones ethento boulēn anachthēnai ekeithen ei pōs dynainto katantēsantes eis Phoinika paracheimasai, limena tēs Krētēs bleponta kata liba kai kata chōron. Four rare words: aneuthetos, “unsuitable,” and paracheimasia, “wintering,” stand in one verse each; the verb paracheimazō, “to winter,” stands in four verses: this one, Acts 28:11 (in the perfect, parakecheimakoti), 1 Corinthians 16:6 and Titus 3:12 (the last two not yet on these pages). The place name Phoinix stands in one verse as a harbor, and is not the Phoenicia (Phoinikē) of 11:19, 15:3 and 21:2. The two winds are the puzzle. Kata liba kai kata chōron is literally “down the liba and down the chōron”: the first is the southwest wind and the second the northwest wind, as reference works give them (reported, not checked here, and both words stand in this verse alone in the New Testament). “Looking down” a wind can mean facing the direction the wind blows toward, which would be northeast and southeast, or facing into it, which would be southwest and northwest; the Greek preposition does not decide. The shelf splits on exactly this. NWT 1984 “opens toward the northeast and toward the southeast” and ASV “looking northeast and south-east” read the first way; KJV “lieth toward the south west and north west,” Douay “looking towards the southwest and northwest” and NIV “facing both southwest and northwest” read it the second way; TLB “with only a northwest and southwest exposure” does too; Geneva “lieth toward the Southwest and by West, and Northwest and by West” adds two intermediate points of the compass. This translation keeps Luke’s wording, “looking down the southwest wind and down the northwest wind,” and the library entry Phoenix says which harbors reference works propose. The verb katantēsantes, “reach,” is the verb of arriving that Luke uses at 21:7 and 25:13, and of the twelve tribes “attaining” the promise at 26:7. The apparatus (1023) shows Robinson–Pierpont with kakeithen, “and from there,” for the others’ ekeithen. The majority is hoi pleiones, “the more,” and they “gave counsel,” a phrase (ethento boulēn) that stands in this verse alone; the centurion’s choice in v11 and the majority’s here point the same way. Shelf: NWT 1984 “the majority advised setting sail from there,” NIV “the majority decided that we should sail on” and ASV “the more part advised” keep the group; TLB “most of the crew advised” names a crew; Geneva “many took counsel” says “many.” KJV and Douay spell the harbor “Phenice.”

Verses 13–17 · ὑποπνεύσαντος … ἆσσον … τυφωνικὸς … Εὐρακύλων … συναρπασθέντος … ἀντοφθαλμεῖν … Καῦδα … βοηθείαις … ὑποζωννύντες … Σύρτιν … τὸ σκεῦος hypopneusantos … asson … typhōnikos … Eurakylōn … synarpasthentos … antophthalmein … Kauda … boētheiais … hypozōnnyntes … Syrtin … to skeuos — Acts 27:13–17: what is the wind called Euraquilo (or Euroclydon), what is “undergirding” the ship, and what was the “gear” they lowered?

“Supposing they had obtained their purpose” (Acts 27:13). Hypopneusantos de notou doxantes tēs protheseōs kekratēkenai arantes asson parelegonto tēn Krētēn. The verb hypopneō, “blow gently,” stands in one verse, and the pairing “south wind” with “gently” is the first turn of the weather: the wind that had been contrary (27:4, 27:7) now suits them. The noun prothesis, “purpose,” is the word Barnabas uses at 11:23 (“with purpose of heart”), and it stands in two verses of Acts, there and here. The comparative asson, “closer in,” stands in this verse alone as a comparative; the Asson that Douay prints (“when they had loosed from Asson”) reads it as the city Assos, which Acts names at 20:13 and 20:14; the Greek here is the plain comparative. The verb parelegonto, “were coasting along,” is the verb of 27:8. Shelf: ASV “sailed along Crete, close in shore” and NWT 1984 “began coasting inshore along Crete” keep the comparative; KJV “sailed close by Crete” and Douay “sailed close by Crete” do; Geneva “loosed nearer” makes the comparative a nearer anchorage; NIV “they saw their opportunity” and TLB “it looked like a perfect day for the trip” replace “supposing they had obtained their purpose” with a comment on the weather.

“A wind of typhoon force, the one called Euraquilo” (Acts 27:14). Met’ ou polu de ebalen kat’ autēs anemos typhōnikos ho kaloumenos Eurakylōn. The adjective typhōnikos stands in this verse alone; the wind is named by the same ho kaloumenos, “the one called,” that Luke uses for places and for people, and the name Eurakylōn stands in one verse of the New Testament. The apparatus (1024) has two names: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print Eurakylōn, and Robinson–Pierpont prints Euroklydōn. Reference works read the first as Euros, the east wind, joined to Latin aquilo, the north wind, which is why some English versions say “northeaster,” and the second as Euros joined to klydōn, “wave”; both derivations are reported, not checked here, and the dictionary entry Euraquilon follows the name. The shelf divides by text: KJV and Geneva “Euroclydon” follow Robinson–Pierpont; NWT 1984 “Eu·ro·aqʹui·lo,” ASV “Euraquilo” and Douay “Euroaquilo” follow the others; NIV “the Northeaster” and TLB “a “northeaster,” they called it” translate the name into a direction. “Not able to face into the wind” (Acts 27:15). Synarpasthentos de tou ploiou kai mē dynamenou antophthalmein tō anemō epidontes epherometha. The verb synarpazō, “seize,” stands in three verses of Acts: 6:12, where they “seized” Stephen, 19:29, where the crowd “seized” Gaius and Aristarchus, and here, where a wind seizes the ship; Aristarchus, seized at Ephesus with Gaius, is aboard this ship (27:2). The verb antophthalmeō is, literally, “to look in the eye,” and stands in this verse alone. The ship “gave way” (epidontes, “giving over,” the verb for handing a letter over at 15:30) and was epherometha, “borne along,” the passive of pherō that Acts uses of the wind at 2:2 (“a violent wind being carried along”) and, in this chapter, at v15 and v17 (v27 has the compound diapheromenōn). Shelf: NWT 1984 “was not able to keep its head against the wind” and ASV “could not face the wind” keep the picture of a ship turning its face; KJV “could not bear up into the wind” and Douay “could not bear up against the wind” use the sailors’ “bear up”; Geneva “could not resist the wind, we let her go” says “resist”; NIV “could not head into the wind; so we gave way to it” is close to the Greek.

“Cauda” and the “ship’s boat” (Acts 27:16). Nēsion de ti hypodramontes kaloumenon Kauda ischysamen molis perikrateis genesthai tēs skaphēs. The island's name, Kauda, stands in one verse, and the apparatus (1025) gives it as Kauda in Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 against Klaudēn in Robinson–Pierpont. The English forms divide the same way: NWT 1984 “Cauʹda,” ASV and Douay “Cauda,” NIV “Cauda” against KJV, Geneva and TLB “Clauda.” The noun skaphē, a small boat, stands in three verses, 27:16, 27:30 and 27:32, all in this chapter; this translation prints “ship’s boat” at all three. Shelf: NWT 1984 “get possession of the skiff at the stern” adds “at the stern,” and TLB “the lifeboat that was being towed behind us” adds “towed behind”; the Greek says neither. ASV “secure the boat” and NIV “make the lifeboat secure” keep the verb of control; KJV and Douay “we had much work to come by the boat” and Geneva “had much ado to get the boat” make the difficulty “work” or “ado.”

“Supports,” “undergirding,” the Syrtis and “the gear” (Acts 27:17). Hēn arantes boētheiais echrōnto hypozōnnyntes to ploion; phoboumenoi te mē eis tēn Syrtin ekpesōsin, chalasantes to skeuos, houtōs epheronto. The noun boētheia, “help,” stands in two verses, here and Hebrews 4:16 (not yet on these pages); here it is plural, “helps,” and this translation prints “supports.” The verb hypozōnnymi, “undergird,” stands in this verse alone, and so does the place name Syrtis; reference works describe a practice of passing ropes under a hull to hold it together and the Syrtis as sandbanks off the Libyan coast, both reported here and not checked. The verb chalaō, “lower,” stands in three verses of Acts: 9:25, where Saul is “lowered in a basket,” here, and 27:30, where the sailors lower the ship’s boat. The noun skeuos is the general Greek word for a vessel, utensil or piece of equipment; it names no sail and no anchor, and the shelf splits on what it is: NWT 1984 “lowered the gear” and ASV “lowered the gear” keep the general word; NIV “lowered the sea anchor” names an anchor; KJV “strake sail,” Geneva “strake sail” and TLB “lowered the topsails” name a sail; Douay “let down the sail yard” names a spar. For the undergirding, NWT 1984 “began using helps to undergird the boat” and ASV “used helps, under-girding the ship” keep Luke’s two words; NIV “passed ropes under the ship itself to hold it together” and TLB “banded the ship with ropes to strengthen the hull” supply the ropes. TLB “the quicksands of the African coast” adds “African,” and KJV “quicksands” and Douay “quicksands” translate the proper name Syrtin as a common noun.

Verses 18–20 · χειμαζομένων … ἐκβολὴν … αὐτόχειρες … σκευὴν … ἐπιφαινόντων … ἐλπὶς πᾶσα … περιῃρεῖτο cheimazomenōn … ekbolēn … autocheires … skeuēn … epiphainontōn … elpis pasa … periērheito — Acts 27:18–20: what do the three stages of throwing things overboard have in common, and what is the “hope” that is being taken away?

“They began to throw cargo out” (Acts 27:18). Sphodrōs de cheimazomenōn hēmōn tē hexēs ekbolēn epoiounto. The adverb sphodrōs, “violently,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does the noun ekbolē, “a throwing out,” here a jettisoning of cargo; the imperfect epoiounto, “were making,” says it was begun and kept up, which this translation shows with “began.” The verb cheimazō, “to be storm-tossed,” is the participle of this verse; the noun cheimōn, “storm,” is in v20. Luke does not say what the cargo was at this stage; the grain is named only at v38. Shelf: NIV “began to throw the cargo overboard” and TLB “began throwing the cargo overboard” supply “cargo” and “overboard,” neither in the Greek; ASV “began to throw the the freight overboard” (with the doubled “the” as the shelf text prints it) does the same; NWT 1984 “they began to lighten the ship,” KJV “they lightened the ship,” Geneva “they lightened the ship” and Douay “they lightened the ship” name the result, a lighter ship, and leave out the act.

“With their own hands” (Acts 27:19). Kai tē tritē autocheires tēn skeuēn tou ploiou erripsan. The adjective autocheir, “with one’s own hands,” stands in one verse of the New Testament. The noun skeuē, “equipment, tackling,” is a different word from the skeuos of v17. The apparatus (1027) shows the subject: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print erripsan, “they threw,” and Robinson–Pierpont prints errhipsamen, “we threw.” The printed text and this translation follow the first, so it is the sailors who do it “with their own hands.” Shelf: NWT 1984 “with their own hands, they threw away the tackling of the boat,” ASV “they cast out with their own hands the tackling of the ship” and Douay “they cast out with their own hands” and NIV “they threw the ship’s tackle overboard with their own hands” follow the first; KJV “we cast out with our own hands” and Geneva “we cast out with our own hands” follow the second. TLB “they threw out the tackle and anything else they could lay their hands on” puts the hands to a different use and adds “anything else.” The days are counted: “the next day” (v18), “the third” (v19), “many days” (v20), and the three throwings run from cargo (v18) to equipment (v19) to the grain at v38.

“Neither sun nor stars appearing” (Acts 27:20). Mēte de hēliou mēte astrōn epiphainontōn epi pleionas hēmeras, cheimōnos te ouk oligou epikeimenou, loipon periērheito elpis pasa tou sōzesthai hēmas. The verb epiphainō, “appear, shine upon,” stands in four verses of the New Testament: Luke 1:79 (the dawn that comes “to shine on those sitting in darkness and the shadow of death”), this verse, and Titus 2:11 and 3:4 (neither yet on these pages). In Luke 1:79 the verb is the dawn shining on people in darkness; here the sun and stars do not shine on a ship, and the hearing in Acts 26 had just spoken of light for the people and the nations (26:23). The verb periaireō, “take away,” is the verb of the hope here and of the anchors “cast off” at 27:40 and 28:13; the three verses in Acts are 27:20, 27:40 and 28:13. The noun elpis, “hope,” stands in eight verses of Acts, read from the hits: 2:26, 16:19, 23:6, 24:15, 26:6, 26:7, here, and 28:20. The hope of 23:6, 24:15 and 26:6–7 is the hope of the promise and the resurrection that Paul is judged for; this is the one verse where the hope is only that of “being saved” (tou sōzesthai) from the sea. Whether Luke means a pointed contrast, he does not say. The apparatus (1028) has Robinson–Pierpont with pasa elpis, the order reversed. Shelf: NWT 1984 “all hope of our being saved finally began to be cut off” keeps the imperfect “began”; ASV “all hope that we should be saved was now taken away” and KJV “all hope that we should be saved was then taken away” and Douay “all hope of our being saved was now taken away” give a completed event; NIV “we finally gave up all hope of being saved” makes the hope something the passengers surrender instead of something taken from them; TLB “until at last all hope was gone” leaves out the sun, the stars and the being saved.

Verses 21–26 · ἀσιτίας … σταθεὶς … ἐν μέσῳ … κερδῆσαι … εὐθυμεῖν … ἀποβολὴ ψυχῆς … παρέστη … λατρεύω … Καίσαρί σε δεῖ παραστῆναι … κεχάρισταί … ἐκπεσεῖν asitias … statheis … en mesō … kerdēsai … euthymein … apobolē psychēs … parestē … latreuō … Kaisari se dei parastēnai … kecharistai … ekpesein — Acts 27:21–26: what does Paul mean by “gained this injury and loss,” who stands by whom in the angel’s word, and what does “granted” give him?

“Standing in the midst of them” (Acts 27:21). Pollēs te asitias hyparchousēs tote statheis ho Paulos en mesō autōn eipen. The noun asitia, “lack of food,” stands in one verse of the New Testament; its adjective asitoi, “without food,” is the word of 27:33. The participle statheis, “standing,” opens a speech at 2:14 (Peter, with the eleven), at 17:22, where “Paul, standing in the middle of the Areopagus,” speaks to Athens, and here. The same participle and phrase introduce Paul’s speech to Athens and his speech to a ship. Paul begins with a rebuke. Edei men, ō andres, peitharchēsantas moi mē anagesthai apo tēs Krētēs kerdēsai te tēn hybrin tautēn kai tēn zēmian: “it was necessary, men, obeying me, not to set sail from Crete, and to gain this injury and loss.” The verb peitharcheō, “obey,” answers the “persuaded” of v11, and the two nouns are the ones of v10, hybris and zēmia. The verb kerdainō is, in Acts, found only here; it is the verb “gain” of Matthew 16:26, with “injury and loss” as its object. Reference works take it as an irony (“and so gain this injury”) or as a turn of idiom meaning “incur,” or, with the negative carried over from “not set sail,” “spare yourselves”; those readings are reported, not checked here, and the shelf divides along them. Shelf: KJV “to have gained this harm and loss,” Geneva “should ye have gained this hurt and loss” and Douay “have gained this harm and loss” keep “gained”; ASV “have gotten this injury and loss” says “gotten”; NWT 1984 “have sustained this damage and loss” says “sustained”; NIV “then you would have spared yourselves this damage and loss” and TLB “you would have avoided all this injury and loss” take the avoiding sense and supply a “would have.” TLB also writes “not left Fair Havens” where the Greek has apo tēs Krētēs, “from Crete.” This translation prints “gained” and leaves the irony to the Greek.

“Take courage” (Acts 27:22, 25). Kai ta nyn parainō hymas euthymein, apobolē gar psychēs oudemia estai ex hymōn plēn tou ploiou. The stem euthym- stands in five verses of the New Testament, read from the hits: Acts 24:10 (Paul “cheerfully” makes his defense before Felix), 27:22, 27:25, 27:36 (the whole ship “became cheerful”) and James 5:13 (not yet on these pages); three of the five are in this chapter, at the speech’s beginning, its end and the meal that follows it. The noun apobolē, “loss,” stands in two verses, here and Romans 11:15. The word psychē is used three times in the chapter, “lives” in v10, “loss of life” here, and the counted “souls” of v37; the entry psychē follows the word. Shelf: NWT 1984 “not a soul of YOU will be lost, only the boat will” keeps psychē as “soul”; ASV “there shall be no loss of life among you, but only of the ship” and KJV “no loss of any man’s life among you, but of the ship” keep the noun “loss”; NIV “not one of you will be lost; only the ship will be destroyed” and TLB “Not one of us will lose our lives, even though the ship will go down” turn the noun into a verb, and the TLB changes “you” to “us.” At v25 the call is repeated: euthymeite, andres; pisteuō gar tō theō hoti houtōs estai kath’ hon tropon lelalētai moi. The verb lelalētai, “it has been told,” is a perfect passive that stands in this verse in that form alone. NWT 1984 “I believe God that it will be exactly as it has been told me” and KJV “I believe God, that it shall be even as it was told me” keep the dative “believe God”; NIV “I have faith in God” changes it to “faith in.”

“An angel stood by me; you must stand before Caesar” (Acts 27:23–24). Parestē gar moi tautē tē nykti tou theou, hou eimi hō kai latreuō, angelos, legōn: Mē phobou, Paule; Kaisari se dei parastēnai, kai idou kecharistai soi ho theos pantas tous pleontas meta sou. One verb carries it: paristēmi is parestē, “stood by,” in v23 and parastēnai, “to stand before,” in v24, so an angel who stands by Paul is the reason Paul will stand before Caesar. The same verb is used in Acts at 23:33, where the soldiers present Paul to the governor, and at 24:13. Two earlier words of encouragement to Paul come at night, 18:9 (“Do not be afraid, but speak, and do not be silent”) and 23:11 (“Take courage! … so you must bear witness in Rome also”); both come from the Lord, this one from an angel, and mē phobou, “do not fear,” stands in Acts at 18:9 and here. The verb latreuō, “serve,” stands in five verses of Acts: 7:7, 7:42, 24:14 (“I thus serve the God of my fathers”), 26:7 (the twelve tribes, “serving earnestly night and day”) and here, so Paul describes his God the way he did to Felix and to Agrippa; the entry latreuō follows the verb. The apparatus has three points: Robinson–Pierpont puts angelos before “of the God” (1030, 1032), and Nestle–Aland 28 adds egō, “I,” to “whose I am” (1031). The verb kecharistai, “has granted,” is charizomai, which stands in four verses of Acts, read from the hits: 3:14 (a murderer “granted” to the crowd), 25:11 (Paul: “no one can give me over to them as a favor”), 25:16 (Festus: “to give over any person as a favor”) and here. Paul would not be given over as a favor to men; God gives him men, “all who are sailing with you.” The entry charizomai follows the verb. Shelf: NWT 1984 “God has freely given you all those sailing with you” and Geneva “God hath given unto thee freely, all that sail with thee” add “freely”; ASV “God hath granted thee all them that sail with thee” is nearest; KJV and Douay “God hath given thee all them that sail with thee” use “given”; NIV “God has graciously given you the lives of all who sail with you” adds “the lives of,” and TLB “God has granted your request and will save the lives of all those sailing with you” adds “your request” and “will save the lives of.” For “you must stand before Caesar,” NWT 1984 “You must stand before Caesar” and ASV “thou must stand before Caesar” keep the active verb; KJV “thou must be brought before Caesar,” Geneva “thou must be brought before Caesar” and Douay “thou must be brought before Caesar” make it a passive. NIV “You must stand trial before Caesar” adds “trial”; the Greek names Caesar and does not name Rome or a court.

“Cast onto a certain island” (Acts 27:26). Eis nēson de tina dei hēmas ekpesein. The verb ekpiptō, “fall out, be cast,” stands in four verses of Acts, read from the hits, all in this chapter: 27:17 (the fear of being cast up on the Syrtis), here, 27:29 (on rough places) and 27:32 (the ship’s boat “fall away”); the same verb is used for what the sailors fear, what Paul foretells and what the soldiers do to the boat. The island is not named; Acts 28:1 does name it. The word of the angel does not say “shipwreck”; it says dei, “it is necessary,” of both the standing before Caesar and the casting on an island. Shelf: ASV “we must be cast upon a certain island” and KJV “we must be cast upon a certain island” are nearest; NWT 1984 “we must be cast ashore on a certain island” adds “ashore”; Geneva “we must be cast into a certain Island” says “into”; NIV “we must run aground on some island” names the grounding; Douay “we must come unto a certain island” says “come”; TLB “we will be shipwrecked on an island” changes “must” to “will” and adds “shipwrecked.”

Verses 27–32 · Ἀδρίᾳ … ὑπενόουν … προσάγειν … βολίσαντες … ὀργυιὰς … τραχεῖς … ἀγκύρας … ηὔχοντο … προφάσει … ἀπέκοψαν Adria … hypenooun … prosagein … bolisantes … orguias … tracheis … ankyras … ēuchonto … prophasei … apekopsan — Acts 27:27–32: what sea is “the Adria,” what do the soundings show, are the sailors “wishing” or “praying” for day, and why does Paul say the soldiers cannot be saved unless the sailors stay?

“Driven across the Adria” (Acts 27:27). Hōs de tesserakaidekatē nyx egeneto diapheromenōn hēmōn en tō Adria, kata meson tēs nyktos hypenooun hoi nautai prosagein tina autois chōran. The name Adrias stands in one verse of the New Testament. Reference works say the ancient name covered a wider stretch of the central Mediterranean than the modern Adriatic, between Greece and Crete on one side and Italy and Sicily on the other; that range is reported, not checked here, and the Adria is a name for a sea, not a place with an entry of its own. The shelf shows the difference in its names: KJV and Douay “Adria,” ASV “the sea of Adria” and NWT 1984 “the [sea of] Aʹdri·a” keep the old name, and Geneva “the Adriatic sea,” NIV “the Adriatic Sea” and TLB “the Adriatic Sea” use the modern one. The fourteen nights are counted from a point Luke does not give; TLB “the fourteenth night of the storm” supplies “of the storm,” and the nearest named beginning is the wind of v14. The verb hyponoeō, “suspect, suppose,” stands in three verses of Acts, read from the hits: 13:25 (“What do you suppose me to be?”), 25:18 (“the evil things that I was supposing”) and here. The sailors “suspect” that prosagein tina autois chōran, “some land was approaching them,” with the land as the subject; prosagō in the transitive sense is the “bringing” of 16:20, and here it is intransitive. Shelf: Geneva “some country approached unto them” keeps the land as the subject; NWT 1984 “they were drawing near to some land,” ASV “they were drawing near to some country” and KJV “they drew near to some country” make the sailors the subject; Douay “they discovered some country” turns the verb into one of seeing.

“Twenty fathoms, fifteen fathoms” (Acts 27:28). Kai bolisantes heuron orguias eikosi, brachy de diastēsantes kai palin bolisantes heuron orguias dekapente. The verb bolizō, “take soundings,” and the noun orguia, the measure of a man’s outstretched arms, each stand in one verse of the New Testament. The sounding is twice: the first finds twenty fathoms, the second, after “a little” distance, fifteen. A shallower sea is what a ship nearing land would find; Luke says that and no more, and does not say how far apart the two soundings were. The shelf has the fathoms in NWT 1984 “twenty fathoms… fifteen fathoms,” ASV “twenty fathoms… fifteen fathoms,” and the older versions print fathoms too. Two versions convert: NIV “a hundred and twenty feet deep” and “ninety feet deep” and TLB “120 feet of water” and “only 90 feet.” The conversion is six feet to a fathom, which is a modern reckoning, not a Greek number: the Greek has one unit, the orguia, and two counts. This translation prints “fathoms.”

“Rough places,” four anchors and “praying for day” (Acts 27:29). Phoboumenoi te mē pou kata tracheis topous ekpesōmen ek prymnēs rhipsantes ankyras tessaras ēuchonto hēmeran genesthai. The adjective trachys, “rough,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Luke 3:5, where “the rough places” become smooth roads. The noun prymna, “stern,” stands in three verses, Mark 4:38, where Jesus is “in the stern, sleeping on the cushion” in another storm, this one and 27:41; and ankyra, “anchor,” stands in four, 27:30, 27:40, here and Hebrews 6:19 (not yet on these pages). The verb euchomai in its own form, apart from the compound proseuchomai, stands in Acts at two verses, 26:29 (“I would pray to God”) and here. It means both “pray” and “wish,” and the shelf divides on which: NIV “prayed for daylight” and TLB “prayed for daylight” say “prayed”; NWT 1984 “began wishing for it to become day,” ASV “wished for the day,” KJV “wished for the day” and Douay “wished for the day” say “wished”; Geneva “wished that the day were come.” This translation prints “praying” because it printed “pray” for the same verb at 26:29, and Luke does not say to whom the sailors prayed. The apparatus (1033) gives Robinson–Pierpont mēpōs eis, “lest into,” for mē pou kata, “lest somewhere onto.” Shelf for the fear: ASV “cast ashore on rocky ground” and Douay “fall upon rough places” and NWT 1984 “cast somewhere upon the rocks” and KJV “fallen upon rocks” are in order; Geneva “fallen into some rough places” keeps the word; NIV “dashed against the rocks” adds a dashing, and TLB “At this rate they knew they would soon be driven ashore” turns a fear into a knowledge.

“Under a pretext” (Acts 27:30–32). Tōn de nautōn zētountōn phygein ek tou ploiou kai chalasantōn tēn skaphēn eis tēn thalassan prophasei hōs ek prōirēs ankyras mellontōn ekteinein. The noun prophasis, “pretext,” stands in six verses of the New Testament, read from the hits: Mark 12:40 (“for a show” they make long prayers), Luke 20:47, John 15:22, this verse, Philippians 1:18 and 1 Thessalonians 2:5 (the last not yet on these pages). The noun prōira, “bow,” stands in two verses, here and v41; the verb chalaō, “lower,” is the one of v17; and the “ship’s boat” is the skaphē of v16 and v32. The apparatus (1034) reverses ankyras mellontōn to mellontōn ankyras in Robinson–Pierpont. Paul answers: Ean mē houtoi meinōsin en tō ploiō, hymeis sōthēnai ou dynasthe, “Unless these men stay in the ship, you cannot be saved.” The angel’s word was that God had granted Paul “all who are sailing with you” (v24), and Paul now says the soldiers cannot be saved unless the sailors stay: Luke sets the promise and the condition next to each other and does not explain them. The soldiers then apekopsan, “cut off,” the schoinia, “ropes”: apokoptō stands in Acts here only, and the ropes are the “cords” of John 2:15, the other verse of the New Testament with this noun. The apparatus (1035) has Robinson–Pierpont with hoi stratiōtai apekopsan, the subject first. Shelf for v30: NWT 1984 “under the pretense of intending to let down anchors from the prow,” NIV “pretending they were going to lower some anchors from the bow” and TLB “as though they were going to put out anchors from the prow” render the pretext; ASV “under color as though they would lay out anchors from the foreship,” KJV and Douay “under colour” and Geneva “under a color” use the old “color” for “pretext”; TLB “Some of the sailors planned to abandon the ship” adds “some of.” Shelf for v31: NWT 1984 “Unless these men remain in the boat, YOU cannot be saved,” ASV “Except these abide in the ship, ye cannot be saved” and NIV “Unless these men stay with the ship, you cannot be saved” keep “saved”; Geneva “ye cannot be safe” says “safe”; TLB “You will all die unless everyone stays aboard” turns “be saved” into “die” and “these men” into “everyone.”

Verses 33–38 · μεταλαβεῖν τροφῆς … προσδοκῶντες ἄσιτοι … θρὶξ … ἀπολεῖται … λαβὼν ἄρτον εὐχαρίστησεν … κλάσας … διακόσιαι ἑβδομήκοντα ἕξ … τὸν σῖτον metalabein trophēs … prosdokōntes asitoi … thrix … apoleitai … labōn arton eucharistēsen … klasas … diakosiai hebdomēkonta hex … ton siton — Acts 27:33–38: what is Paul asking when he says “take a share of food,” does “hair” perish or fall, is the bread a meal or a rite, and how many are aboard, 276 or about 76?

“To take a share of food” (Acts 27:33–34, 36, 38). Parekalei ho Paulos hapantas metalabein trophēs. The noun trophē, “food,” stands in seven verses of Acts, read from the hits (the names Trophimos and similar are not the noun): 2:46, 9:19 (“taking food, he was strengthened”), 14:17, and in this passage 27:33, 27:34, 27:36 and 27:38. The idiom metalambanō trophēs, “take a share of food,” stands in Acts at 2:46 (“they were sharing food in gladness”), 27:33 and 27:34. Shelf: KJV “have tarried and continued fasting” and Geneva “have tarried, and continued fasting” and Douay “have waited, and continued fasting” and ASV “ye wait and continue fasting” keep the pair; NWT 1984 “YOU have been on the watch” reads prosdokōntes as watching; NIV “you have been in constant suspense” reads it as anxiety; TLB “You haven’t touched food for two weeks” counts fourteen days as two weeks and leaves out the waiting. The older English “meat” in KJV and Geneva and Douay (“take meat”) means food in general; the Greek is trophē, and this translation prints “food.”

“Not a hair from the head” (Acts 27:34). Oudenos gar hymōn thrix apo tēs kephalēs apoleitai: “for not a hair from the head of any of you will perish.” The apparatus (1038–1039) shows two readings of the verb: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print apoleitai, “will perish,” and Robinson–Pierpont prints peseitai, “will fall,” with ek for apo. The second is the verb of an old oath formula: a Hebrew phrase that not a hair shall fall to the ground stands in three verses, read from the hits, 1 Kings 1:52 (“not one hair of his shall fall to the earth”), and 1 Samuel 14:45 and 2 Samuel 14:11 (neither on these pages). In Luke the word is the one the first reading uses: Luke 21:18, “not a hair of your head will perish,” and Luke 12:7, where the hairs of your head are “all numbered.” The apparatus lists both readings, and this page does not decide between them. The shelf splits the same way: KJV “there shall not an hair fall from the head of any of you” and Geneva “there shall not an hair fall from the head of any of you” print “fall”; ASV “there shall not a hair perish from the head of any of you,” NWT 1984 “not a hair of the head of one of YOU will perish” and Douay “there shall not an hair of the head of any of you perish” print “perish”; NIV “Not one of you will lose a single hair from his head” changes the verb to “lose”; TLB “For not a hair of your heads shall perish!” Paul puts it after the reason, touto gar pros tēs hymeteras sōtērias hyparchei, which this translation prints “this belongs to your salvation”: NWT 1984 “in the interest of YOUR safety,” ASV “for your safety,” KJV “for your health,” Douay “for your health’s sake,” Geneva “for your safeguard,” NIV “You need it to survive,” TLB “for your own good.” The noun sōtēria is the one of 4:12, and the entry sōtēria follows the word; the Greek does not say which of its senses, rescue from the sea or the larger one, Paul intends, and this translation prints the word the others use.

“He took bread, gave thanks to God, broke it and began to eat” (Acts 27:35). Eipas de tauta kai labōn arton eucharistēsen tō theō enōpion pantōn kai klasas ērxato esthiein. The sequence of taking, thanking and breaking bread is the sequence of Luke 22:19 (“taking bread, giving thanks, he broke it”) and, with “blessed” for “gave thanks,” of Luke 24:30; the verb klaō, “break,” stands with bread in Acts at 2:46 (“breaking bread from house to house”), 20:7, 20:11 and here, and the verb eucharisteō, “give thanks,” stands in Acts at 27:35 and 28:15. Whether this meal is the Lord’s Supper or an ordinary one is argued in reference works, and the text does not decide it: the thanks go to tō theō, “to God,” with no mention of the Lord, in the sight of all 276 (or 76), with everyone aboard in view; Luke does not say who among them were disciples, and the next verse says they all took food. The apparatus (1040) has eipas in the three editions and eipōn in Robinson–Pierpont. Shelf: NWT 1984 “took a loaf, gave thanks to God before them all and broke it and started eating” and NIV “took some bread and gave thanks to God in front of them all” keep the sequence; ASV “he gave thanks to God in the presence of all” and KJV “gave thanks to God in presence of them all” keep it; TLB “took some hardtack” names the bread as a kind of biscuit and “broke off a piece and ate it” drops “began.” At v36 euthymoi genomenoi pantes kai autoi proselabonto trophēs the cheer is the stem of v22 and v25: NWT 1984 “they all became cheerful” and ASV “Then were they all of good cheer” keep it, TLB “Suddenly everyone felt better” adds “Suddenly.”

“Two hundred seventy-six” (Acts 27:37) and the grain (v38). Ēmetha de hai pasai psychai en tō ploiō diakosiai hebdomēkonta hex. The count is in this verse alone. The apparatus (1042) shows what the archive’s Greek text carries: diakosiai, “two hundred,” in Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, and in Westcott–Hort hōs, “about,” which makes the number “about seventy-six”; the printed text and this translation follow the first, and the other reading is reported, not weighed here. The persons are counted as psychai, “souls,” as the three thousand of 2:41 and the seventy-five of 7:14 are (“seventy-five souls”). The shelf prints 276 in all seven versions, in words in ASV, KJV, Geneva and Douay (“two hundred threescore and sixteen souls”), in NWT 1984 (“two hundred and seventy-six”) and in numerals in NIV and TLB (“276 of us”). At v38, korestheis, “filled,” the verb korennymi, stands in the New Testament here and at 1 Corinthians 4:8 (not yet on these pages); koufizō, “lighten,” stands in this verse alone. Only now, after the meal, does Luke name what was thrown: ton siton, “the grain,” the cargo that v10 and v18 did not name. Shelf: NWT 1984 “throwing the wheat overboard into the sea,” ASV “throwing out the wheat into the sea,” KJV “cast out the wheat into the sea” and Douay “casting the wheat into the sea” say “wheat”; NIV “by throwing the grain into the sea” says “grain”; TLB “throwing all the wheat overboard” adds “all.” The Greek sitos is the general word for grain, and the apparatus (1043) has Robinson–Pierpont with tēs trophēs, “of the food,” for trophēs. This translation prints “grain.”

Verses 39–44 · οὐκ ἐπεγίνωσκον … κόλπον … αἰγιαλόν … ἐξῶσαι … περιελόντες … ἀρτέμωνα … διθάλασσον … ἐπέκειλαν … βουλὴ … διασῶσαι ouk epeginōskon … kolpon … aigialon … exōsai … perielontes … artemōna … dithalasson … epekeilan … boulē … diasōsai — Acts 27:39–44: what is the “bay” and the “place between two seas,” what sail did they raise, and why does the centurion stop the soldiers?

“They did not recognize the land” (Acts 27:39). Hote de hēmera egeneto, tēn gēn ouk epeginōskon, kolpon de tina katenooun echonta aigialon eis hon ebouleuonto ei dynainto exōsai to ploion. The land is unnamed and unrecognized; Acts 28:1 says the party “recognized” the island’s name. Kolpos, “bay,” is the “lap” or “bosom” of Luke 6:38, Luke 16:22, 16:23, John 1:18 and John 13:23, and a bay in this verse alone, so six verses of the New Testament carry the one noun and only this one is a coast; the entry kolpos follows the word. Aigialos, “beach,” stands in six verses: Matthew 13:2, 13:48, John 21:4, Acts 21:5 (“kneeling down on the beach, we prayed”), here and v40. The verb exōtheō, “drive,” stands in two verses, here and 7:45, where God “drove out” the nations before the fathers; the apparatus (1046) has Westcott–Hort with eksōsai, “to bring safely out,” for the others’ exōsai. The same apparatus has Robinson–Pierpont with ebouleusanto for ebouleuonto (1044) and dynaton, “possible,” for dynainto (1045). Shelf: NWT 1984 “a certain bay with a beach” and ASV “a certain bay with a beach” and NIV “a bay with a sandy beach” say “bay”; KJV “a certain creek with a shore” and Douay “a certain creek that had a shore” and Geneva “a certain creek with a bank” use the older “creek” for an inlet. For the plan, NWT 1984 “to beach the boat,” ASV “they took counsel whether they could drive the ship upon it,” NIV “they decided to run the ship aground if they could,” KJV “thrust in the ship,” and TLB “wondered whether they could get between the rocks and be driven up onto the beach” differ on whether this was a decision or a trial; TLB adds “between the rocks.”

“Casting off the anchors” and the sail (Acts 27:40). Kai tas ankyras perielontes eiōn eis tēn thalassan, hama anentes tas zeuktērias tōn pēdaliōn, kai eparantes ton artemōna tē pneousē kateichon eis ton aigialon. The verb periaireō is the verb that took the hope away at v20; here it takes the anchors off. The noun zeuktēria, “lashing,” and artemōn, a sail, stand in this verse alone; pēdalion, “steering oar,” stands in two, here and James 3:4 (not yet on these pages). What the artemōn was Luke does not say, and reference works call it a foresail; that is reported, not checked here. The shelf is split: NWT 1984 “hoisting the foresail to the wind,” ASV “hoisting up the foresail to the wind,” NIV “they hoisted the foresail to the wind” and TLB “raised the foresail” say “foresail”; KJV “hoised up the mainsail to the wind,” Geneva “hoisted up the main sail to the wind” and Douay “hoisting up the mainsail to the wind” say “mainsail.” The anchors: NWT 1984 “cutting away the anchors, they let them fall into the sea” and ASV “casting off the anchors, they left them in the sea” and NIV “Cutting loose the anchors, they left them in the sea” and TLB “Cutting off the anchors and leaving them in the sea” leave the anchors behind; KJV “they had taken up the anchors,” Geneva and Douay “taken up the anchors” bring them aboard, and then have the ship or the crew “committed” to the sea. The Greek verb is one of removing, perielontes, and eiōn is “they left,” so the anchors stay in the sea. The ropes of the rudders: ASV “loosing the bands of the rudders,” NIV “untied the ropes that held the rudders,” NWT 1984 “loosing the lashings of the rudder oars,” KJV “loosed the rudder bands.”

“A place between two seas” (Acts 27:41). Peripesontes de eis topon dithalasson epekeilan tēn naun, kai hē men prōra ereisasa emeinen asaleutos, hē de prymna elyeto hypo tēs bias. The adjective dithalassos, “with a sea on each side,” and the verb epikellō, “run aground,” each stand in one verse; the noun naus, “ship,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, where the rest of the chapter has ploion. The adjective asaleutos, “immovable,” stands in two verses, here and Hebrews 12:28 (not yet on these pages). The noun bia, “force,” stands in three verses of Acts, read from the hits: 5:26, 21:35 and here. The apparatus (1047–1048) has two points. Robinson–Pierpont reads epōkeilan for epekeilan, a spelling of the same verb. And Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont add tōn kymatōn, “of the waves,” after bias, where Westcott–Hort, whose reading the printed text follows here, has only “the force.” Four of the shelf versions have the waves: ASV “the stern began to break up by the violence of the waves,” KJV “broken with the violence of the waves,” Geneva “broken with the violence of the waves” and TLB “exposed to the violence of the waves.” Douay “the violence of the sea” and NIV “broken to pieces by the pounding of the surf” also have the sea; NWT 1984 “began to be violently broken to pieces” has only the force. The place: ASV “a place where two seas met,” KJV “a place where two seas met,” Geneva and Douay “a place where two seas met” keep the Greek picture; NWT 1984 “a shoal washed on each side by the sea” reads it as a shallow; NIV “a sandbar” and TLB “a sandbar” name a bar of sand. Luke does not say what the obstacle was.

“The soldiers’ plan” and “bring Paul safely through” (Acts 27:42–44). Tōn de stratiōtōn boulē egeneto hina tous desmōtas apokteinōsin, mē tis ekkolymbēsas diaphygē. The soldiers’ boulē, “plan, counsel,” is the second of the chapter: 27:12 had the majority’s counsel, which prevailed; this one does not. The verb ekkolymbaō, “swim out,” and diapheugō, “escape,” each stand in this verse alone. What an escape cost a guard Acts has shown twice: Herod “examined the guards and ordered them to be led away” (12:19), and the jailer “drew his sword and was about to kill himself, supposing that the prisoners had escaped” (16:27); whether Roman law fixed that penalty is reported in reference works and not checked here, and Luke gives the soldiers no reason. The centurion boulomenos diasōsai ton Paulon ekōlysen autous tou boulēmatos, “wishing to bring Paul safely through, prevented them from their plan.” The verb diasōzō, “bring safely through,” stands in five verses of Acts: 23:24 (the escort to Felix), here (v43 and v44), and 28:1 and 28:4. Kōlyō, “prevent,” stands in seven verses of Acts: 8:36, 10:47, 11:17, 16:6, 24:23 (Felix: none of his own to be “prevented” from serving Paul), here and 28:31; the centurion who had given Paul leave (v3) now prevents his own men. The order of escape is by ability: first the swimmers, then sanisin, “planks,” a noun that stands in this verse alone, and “certain things from the ship,” and kai houtōs egeneto pantas diasōthēnai epi tēn gēn, “and so it happened that all were brought safely through to the land.” The angel said God had granted Paul “all who are sailing with you” (v24) and Paul had said that there would be no loss of life (v22); this verse adds no count of the survivors. Shelf for v42: NWT 1984 “it became the determination of the soldiers to kill the prisoners,” ASV “the soldiers’ counsel was to kill the prisoners” and NIV “The soldiers planned to kill the prisoners” keep the plan; TLB “The soldiers advised their commanding officer to let them kill the prisoners” adds an appeal to an officer. For v43, NWT 1984 “desired to bring Paul safely through” keeps diasōsai, while ASV “desiring to save Paul” and NIV “wanted to spare Paul’s life” do not; Douay “forbade it to be done” names the prevention but drops the purpose; TLB “But Julius wanted to spare Paul, so he told them no” puts the centurion’s name in the sentence, where the Greek has only ho hekatontarchēs. For v44, NWT 1984 “all were brought safely to land” is nearest; ASV “they all escaped safe to the land,” KJV “they escaped all safe to land” and TLB “everyone escaped safely ashore” use “escaped,” Douay “every soul got safe to land” makes the count souls.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “set sail” for anēchthēmen at v2, v4, v12 and v21, the verb of 20:13 and 21:2; “prisoners” for desmōtas at v1 and v42, a noun the New Testament uses twice, kept apart from the desmios of 23:18; “kindness” for philanthrōpōs at v3; “under” for hypepleusamen at v4 and v7, and “with difficulty” for molis at v7, v8 and v16 against the “scarcely” of 14:18; “hazardous” for episphalēs at v9; “advised” for parēnei at v9 and “urge” for parainō at v22; “injury” and “loss” for hybris and zēmia at v10 and v21; “lives,” “loss of life” and “souls” for psychē at v10, v22 and v37; “pilot” and “shipowner” at v11; “typhoon force” for typhōnikos at v14; “ship’s boat” for skaphē at v16, v30 and v32; “supports” and “gear” at v17 and “equipment” at v19; “take courage” for euthymeō at v22 and v25; “granted” for kecharistai at v24, the verb of 3:14, printed “give over … as a favor” at 25:11 and 25:16; “praying” for ēuchonto at v29, the verb of 26:29; “salvation” for sōtēria at v34; “bring safely through” for diasōsai and diasōthēnai at v43 and v44, the verb of 23:24.

Echoes paid. The “we” of the narration — 21:18, at v1–2. Leave granted by a Roman officer — 24:23 and 26:1, at v3. Aristarchus — 19:29 and 20:4, at v2 and v15. Cyprus on the other side — 21:3, at v4. Cilicia and Pamphylia — 13:13 and 21:39, at v5. Crete — 2:11, at v7–21. The hope — 23:6, 24:15 and 26:6–7, at v20. The word of the night — 18:9 and 23:11, at v23–24. Serving God — 24:14 and 26:7, at v23. The appeal to Caesar — 25:11, 25:12 and 26:32, at v24. Being given over as a favor — 25:11 and 25:16, at v24. Standing in the midst — 17:22, at v21. Bread, thanks, breaking — Luke 22:19, 2:46 and 20:7, at v35. The count of souls — 2:41 and 7:14, at v37. What an escape costs a guard — 12:19 and 16:27, at v42. The escort to Felix — 23:24, at v43.

Echoes planted. The island, named, at 28:1. The kindness of the islanders, at 28:2. The verb of being brought safely through, at 28:1 and 28:4. Another Alexandrian ship, and wintering, at 28:11. The thanks to God, at 28:15. The prisoner, at 28:17. The hope of Israel, at 28:20. The verb of preventing, at 28:31. The verb of waiting for, at 28:6. The fasts, at 2 Corinthians 6:5 and 11:27 (not yet on these pages). The anchor, at Hebrews 6:19 (not yet on these pages).
