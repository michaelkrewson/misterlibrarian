Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–4 · Partiality in the synagogue — the man with gold rings, the poor man, and “sit there below my footstool”

“My brothers, you do not hold the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ of glory with acts of partiality, do you?” The Greek can be a question, as the text printed here punctuates it, or a command, “do not hold”; the shelf divides. Prosōpolēmpsia, “partiality,” literally receiving a face, stands in four verses; the other three say there is “no partiality” with God, as at Romans 2:11 (dictionary). “Of glory” is a bare genitive at the end of the name, read as “our glorious Lord,” as “the Lord of glory” (the title of 1 Corinthians 2:8), or as “Jesus Christ, our glory”; this page keeps the Greek order and does not choose. The KJV reads “have not the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory, with respect of persons”; the NWT 1984 reads “the faith of our Lord Jesus Christ, our glory, with acts of favoritism, are YOU?”; the NIV reads “believers in our glorious Lord Jesus Christ must not show favoritism.”

“For if a man with gold rings on his fingers and in bright clothing comes into your synagogue, and a poor man in filthy clothing also comes in …” “Synagogue” is synagōgē: James names the gathering with the word for a Jewish assembly, and later calls the community an ekklēsia, “congregation,” at 5:14 (dictionary). Chrysodaktylios, “with gold rings on his fingers,” gold-fingered, stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). “Bright” is lampros, the “bright robe” Herod puts on Jesus at Luke 23:11 (dictionary). “Filthy” is the adjective of the “filth” to be put away at James 1:21. The KJV reads “For if there come unto your assembly a man with a gold ring”; the ASV reads “For if there come into your synagogue a man with a gold ring”; the TLB reads “If a man comes into your church,” where the Greek has synagogue.

“‘You sit here in a good place,’ and to the poor man you say, ‘You stand, or sit there below my footstool,’ have you not made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts?” “Look with favor” is epiblepō, the verb of Mary’s “he has looked on the low estate of his bondslave” at Luke 1:48 (dictionary). “Footstool” is hypopodion (dictionary). The printed text has “You stand, or sit there”; Tregelles has “You stand there, or sit”; Robinson–Pierpont has “You stand there, or sit here” (the apparatus). “Made distinctions” is diakrinō, the verb of the one who “doubts” at James 1:6; it is read here as making distinctions or as being divided within yourselves. The KJV reads “Stand thou there, or sit here under my footstool”; the NIV reads “Sit on the floor by my feet”; the NWT 1984 reads “YOU have class distinctions among yourselves.”

Verses 5–7 · “Did not God choose the poor?” — rich in faith, dragged into court, and “the good name called over you”

“Did not God choose the poor in the world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom that he promised to those who love him?” “In the world” is a dative that can mean poor in the eyes of the world or poor in this world’s goods; Robinson–Pierpont has “of the world” (the apparatus). “To be” is supplied. “The kingdom” recalls “Happy the poor — for yours is the kingdom of God” at Luke 6:20, and “promised to those who love him” is the phrase of the crown of life at James 1:12. The KJV reads “Hath not God chosen the poor of this world rich in faith”; the ASV reads “did not God choose them that are poor as to the world to be rich in faith”; the NIV reads “those who are poor in the eyes of the world.”

“But you have dishonored the poor man. Is it not the rich who oppress you, and they themselves who drag you into the courts? Is it not they who blaspheme the good name that was called over you?” Katadynasteuō, “oppress,” stands in two verses; the other is all “who were being oppressed by the devil” at Acts 10:38 (dictionary). “Drag” is the verb of Paul and Silas “dragged” into the marketplace at Acts 16:19. Kritērion, “court,” stands in three verses, the other two at 1 Corinthians 6:2 and 6:4. “The good name that was called over you” is the idiom of a name spoken over what belongs to its owner, as in Amos quoted at Acts 15:17, “all the nations upon whom my name has been called”; which name James means, Christ’s or God’s, he does not say. The KJV reads “Do not they blaspheme that worthy name by the which ye are called?”; the Douay reads “Do not they blaspheme the good name that is invoked upon you?”; the NIV reads “the noble name of him to whom you belong.”

Verses 8–13 · “The royal law” — love your neighbor, guilty of all, and “mercy boasts over judgment”

“If, however, you fulfill the royal law according to the scripture, ‘You shall love your neighbor as yourself,’ you do well. But if you show partiality, you commit sin and are convicted by the law as transgressors.” The scripture is Leviticus 19:18, “you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am Jehovah,” quoted in the words of its Greek (checked), as by Jesus at Matthew 22:39 and by Paul at Romans 13:9 and Galatians 5:14. Basilikos, “royal,” stands in five verses, among them the “royal official” of John 4:46 (dictionary); “royal” is read as the king’s law, the law of the kingdom of verse 5, or the law that rules the rest. The verb prosōpolēmpteō, “show partiality,” stands in one verse. Parabatēs, “transgressor,” stands in five verses, among them Romans 2:25 and Galatians 2:18 (dictionary). The KJV reads “If ye fulfil the royal law according to the scripture”; the NWT 1984 reads “carrying out the kingly law”; the TLB reads “when you truly obey our Lord’s command,” an interpretation the Greek does not state.

⚠ “For whoever keeps the whole law but trips in one point has become guilty of all. For he who said, ‘Do not commit adultery,’ also said, ‘Do not murder.’” The verb ptaiō, “trip,” stands in four verses, among them “we all trip in many ways” at 3:2 and Romans 11:11 (dictionary). The two commandments come in the order of the Greek of Exodus, which has “You shall not commit adultery” before “You shall not murder”; the Hebrew of Exodus 20:12 (20:13–16 in English Bibles) and of Deuteronomy 5:16 (5:17–20) has murder first, and so does the Greek of Deuteronomy (all checked). Adultery comes first again at Luke 18:20 and Romans 13:9. Robinson–Pierpont has the future “you shall not” for “do not” (the apparatus). The KJV reads “and yet offend in one point, he is guilty of all”; the NWT 1984 reads “but makes a false step in one point”; the NIV reads “is guilty of breaking all of it.”

“So speak and so act as those who are to be judged by a law of liberty. For judgment is merciless to the one who has not shown mercy; mercy boasts over judgment.” “A law of liberty” is the phrase of James 1:25, “the perfect law, the law of liberty,” here without the article. Aneleos, “merciless,” stands in one verse (dictionary). The verb katakauchaomai, “boast over,” stands in three verses; Paul uses it at Romans 11:18, “do not boast over the branches” (dictionary). The KJV reads “and mercy rejoiceth against judgment”; the NWT 1984 reads “Mercy exults triumphantly over judgment”; the NIV reads “Mercy triumphs over judgment.”

Verses 14–17 · ⚠ “Faith, if it does not have works, is dead” — “Go in peace, keep warm and eat your fill”

⚠ “What good is it, my brothers, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can that faith save him?” Ophelos, “good,” profit, stands in three verses: twice in this paragraph and at 1 Corinthians 15:32, “what does it profit me?” (dictionary) The question is about faith someone “says” he has. “That faith” renders the Greek article, “the faith,” pointing back to the faith just named; the KJV reads “can faith save him?”, while the ASV reads “can that faith save him?” and the NWT 1984 reads “That faith cannot save him, can it?”

“If a brother or sister is naked and lacking daily food, and one of you says to them, ‘Go in peace, keep warm and eat your fill,’ but you do not give them what the body needs, what good is it? So also faith, if it does not have works, is dead by itself.” “Naked” can mean poorly clothed. “Lacking” is the verb of “lacking in nothing” at James 1:4 and “lacks wisdom” at 1:5. Ephēmeros, “daily,” stands in one verse (dictionary). “Go in peace” is the dismissal of Mark 5:34. The verb thermainō, “keep warm,” stands in five verses; the others are Peter “warming himself” in the high priest’s courtyard, as at Mark 14:54 (dictionary). “Eat your fill” is chortazō, “they shall be filled” at Matthew 5:6. Epitēdeios, “what is needed,” stands in one verse (dictionary). The KJV reads “Depart in peace, be ye warmed and filled”; the NWT 1984 reads “Go in peace, keep warm and well fed”; the TLB reads “Well, good-bye and God bless you; stay warm and eat hearty.”

Verses 18–20 · “Show me your faith apart from the works” — “even the demons believe, and shudder,” and faith that is idle

⚠ “But someone will say, ‘You have faith and I have works.’ Show me your faith apart from the works, and I will show you faith by my works.” The Greek has no quotation marks, and where the objector’s words end is disputed: after “I have works,” as printed here; or later, taking in the challenge; or with the two pronouns read as “one person … another.” This page punctuates as the shelf mostly does and does not settle it. Robinson–Pierpont has “from your works” for “apart from the works,” and “my faith” for “faith” (the apparatus). The Geneva reads “show me thy faith out of thy works”; the KJV reads “shew me thy faith without thy works, and I will shew thee my faith by my works”; the TLB reads “You say the way to God is by faith alone, plus nothing,” words the Greek does not have.

“You believe that God is one? You do well. Even the demons believe — and shudder. Do you want to know, you empty man, that faith apart from the works is idle?” “God is one” is the confession of Deuteronomy 6:4, “Jehovah our God, Jehovah is one,” whose Greek has “Kyrios is one” (checked), and of Mark 12:29 and Romans 3:30. The editions order the words three ways, “God is one” or “there is one God” (the apparatus). Phrissō, “shudder,” stands in one verse (dictionary). “Idle” is argē, from a-ergos, “without work,” a play on “works”; it is the “idle word” of Matthew 12:36 (dictionary). Robinson–Pierpont has “dead” for “idle” (the apparatus). The KJV reads “the devils also believe, and tremble”; the ASV reads “faith apart from works is barren”; the NWT 1984 reads “faith apart from works is inactive.”

Verses 21–26 · ⚠ “Justified by works and not by faith alone” — Abraham, Rahab, and Paul

“Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered up Isaac his son on the altar? You see that faith was working together with his works, and by the works faith was made complete.” The offering is Genesis 22:9, where Abraham “bound Isaac his son, and laid him on the altar.” “Offered up” is the verb of Hebrews 11:17, which tells the same act “by faith.” “Working together” is synergeō, as at Romans 8:28 (dictionary). “Made complete” is teleioō, the verb of the adjective “perfect” at James 1:4, “that you may be perfect and complete.” The KJV reads “Seest thou how faith wrought with his works, and by works was faith made perfect?”; the NWT 1984 reads “Was not Abraham our father declared righteous by works”; the NIV reads “considered righteous for what he did.”

“And the scripture was fulfilled that says, ‘Abraham believed God, and it was reckoned to him as righteousness,’ and he was called a friend of God.” The scripture is Genesis 15:6, “And he believed Jehovah, and he counted it to him as righteousness”: the Hebrew has the divine name, and the Greek of Genesis, like James, has “God” (checked). Paul quotes the same line at Romans 4:3 and Galatians 3:6. The phrase philos theou, “friend of God,” stands in one verse; the Hebrew calls Abraham God’s friend at Isaiah 41:8 and 2 Chronicles 20:7, where the Greek has “whom I loved” and “your beloved” (all checked). The NWT 1984 reads “Abraham put faith in Jehovah,” following the Hebrew where James has “God”; the KJV reads “and it was imputed unto him for righteousness”; the Douay reads “and it was reputed to him to justice.”

⚠ “You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone.” The phrase ek pisteōs monon, “by faith alone,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, this one. “Justified” is dikaioō (dictionary). Paul writes at Romans 3:28, “a person is justified by faith, apart from works of law,” and at Romans 4:2, “if Abraham was justified by works, he has something to boast about — but not before God”; the same Abraham and the same Genesis line serve both. How the two fit is read in more than one way. Many readers take the words in different senses: Paul’s “works of law” against James’s works of mercy, Paul’s faith that trusts against a faith someone only “says” he has, and “justified” in James as shown to be righteous. Others read James as answering a misuse of Paul’s teaching, and others as a real disagreement. Luther called James “an epistle of straw,” and the Council of Trent cited this verse in its decree on justification (both reported, not checked here). This page translates both writers with the same words, “justified,” “faith,” “works,” and does not choose. The KJV reads “and not by faith only”; the NWT 1984 reads “and not by faith alone”; the TLB reads “a man is saved by what he does, as well as by what he believes,” where the Greek has “justified.”

“And in the same way, was not Rahab the prostitute also justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out by another way? For as the body apart from spirit is dead, so also faith apart from works is dead.” Rahab is the woman of Joshua 2:1, spared at Joshua 6:25 “because she hid the messengers whom Joshua sent”; the Hebrew there has “messengers,” and the Greek of Joshua “those who spied” (checked). Hebrews 11:31 tells the same act “by faith”: “she had welcomed the spies in peace.” The verb hypodechomai, “receive” as a guest, stands in three verses; the others are Martha at Luke 10:38 and Zacchaeus at Luke 19:6 (dictionary). Westcott–Hort omit “For” in verse 26 (the apparatus). The KJV reads “Rahab the harlot justified by works”; the NIV reads “when she gave lodging to the spies”; the ASV reads “For as the body apart from the spirit is dead.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “partiality” and “show partiality,” the word of Romans 2:11; “synagogue,” not “church”; “of glory,” with its readings in the note; “filthy,” as the “filth” of 1:21; “made distinctions,” the verb of “doubts” at 1:6; “the royal law”; “trips,” as at Romans 11:11; “a law of liberty,” as at 1:25; “boasts over,” as at Romans 11:18; “lacking,” as at 1:4–5; “idle”; “justified,” “faith” and “works,” the same words as in Romans; “reckoned,” as at Romans 4:3; “made complete”; and “messengers,” as at Joshua 6:25.

Echoes paid. The doubter of James 1:6; the lowly and the rich of James 1:9; promised to those who love him from James 1:12; filth from James 1:21; the doers of James 1:22; the law of liberty of James 1:25; and faith and works, the passage James 1:22 opened.

Echoes planted. “We all trip” at 3:2, and the tongue that no one can tame in chapter 3; “friendship with the world” at 4:4, where “friend of the world” answers “friend of God”; the rich, who “drag” and “oppress” here, again at 5:1–6; and the elders of the ekklēsia at 5:14.

Next in this book: James 3, already on these pages. The tongue: “we all trip in many ways,” a bit in a horse’s mouth, a small fire and a great forest, and “the wisdom from above.”
