By the lake of Gennesaret a miraculous catch of fish that breaks the nets leads Simon, James and John to leave everything and follow Jesus. A leper is touched and cleansed; a paralyzed man let down through a roof is told 'your sins are forgiven' before he is told to get up and walk. Jesus calls a tax collector, Levi, from his booth, and eats at his house with 'tax collectors and sinners' over the Pharisees' objection, answering a question about fasting with the parables of the patched garment and the new wine.
c. AD 28-29, early in the Galilean ministry — corresponding to Mark 1:16-20 and 1:40-2:22's accounts of the same call of the fishermen, the leper, the paralytic, the call of Levi, and the fasting question, and to Matthew 4:18-22, 8:1-4 and 9:1-17.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–11 · ἀνθρώπους ἔσῃ ζωγρῶν… Σίμων Πέτρος anthrōpous esē zōgrōn… Simōn Petros — a lake, not a sea; Simon’s house already known to the reader; and a verb for catching alive, not killing

⭐⭐ “The lake of Gennesaret” — and Luke never once calls it a sea. Mark and Matthew both write “the sea of Galilee” (thalassa); Luke writes limnē, lake, here and at both later scenes on this water (8:22–23, 33). The word thalassa stands in three verses of Luke, and none of them is this lake — a millstone thrown into the sea (17:2), a tree replanted in the sea (17:6), the roaring of sea and waves at the end (21:25). A freshwater lake some thirteen miles long is, in fact, a lake and not a sea; the precision sits beside the “great fever” of 4:38, where the Hobart/Cadbury argument over Luke’s medical vocabulary was first opened, and it is exactly the same kind of small, checkable exactness — whatever its cause, it is real and consistent across the whole Gospel.

Simon’s house, already told — now the man himself. 4:38’s note observed that Luke narrates Simon’s house and his mother-in-law’s healing a full chapter before Simon is ever called, so the reader already knows the house before meeting its owner. Here is the payoff: Jesus simply gets into “one of the boats, which was Simon’s” — no introduction, because Luke’s reader has already been inside his home.

⭐ “Master” — Luke’s own word, and no one else’s. Epistata stands in exactly six verses of the New Testament, and every one of them is in Luke (5:5; 8:24, twice; 8:45; 9:33; 9:49; 17:13) — never in Matthew, Mark, or John, who reach for “Rabbi” or “Teacher” instead. Simon uses it here for the first time, before he has any reason yet to trust the carpenter’s son with a fisherman’s judgment about fish.

“I will let down the nets” — singular, though the order was plural. Jesus told “you” (plural, v4, addressed to the whole crew) to let down “your” nets; Simon answers for himself alone, chalasō, “I will let down” — the boat is his, the order is heard, and the compliance is personal before it is collective.

⭐ Two different words for “partner” in four verses, and the shelf collapses them into one. The crew in the other boat are metochoi (v7) — shareholders, the word for men who jointly hold a stake in something, a business term; James and John are koinōnoi (v10) — a word this library’s own dictionary entry already glosses from the everyday papyri as “the arrangement two men enter when they jointly own a boat.” Luke had two Greek words for the same fishing partnership and used both, one for the men who are only ever named as “the other boat,” the other reserved for the two who get names and a future. KJV and ASV render both “partners”; NWT 1984 keeps them apart, ‘partners’ for v7 and ‘sharers’ for v10.

⭐⭐⭐ “Catching people alive” — and Luke drops “fishers of men” altogether. Mark and Matthew both have Jesus promise to make the four “fishers of men” (Mark 1:17), keeping the pun on their old trade. Luke’s Jesus never says it: zōgrōn, from zōgreō, is a hunting-and-war word — to take alive rather than kill — built from zōos, “alive,” and the root of agreuō, “to hunt or catch” — a soldier’s and a hunter’s word for taking a life rather than ending one. Where Mark keeps the fishing image whole, Luke changes the second half of it: these four will not be fishers who land a catch for the market, but hunters who take their catch alive. ASV and KJV both ‘thou shalt catch men’; NWT 1984 ‘you will be catching men alive’, which alone on the shelf keeps the verb’s own force; NIV ‘you will fish for people’, folding it back into the pun Luke did not make.

⭐ “Simon Peter” — the double name arrives before Luke has ever explained it. Every verse up to this point has called him “Simon” alone; at the exact moment he falls at Jesus’s knees, the narrator writes “Simon Peter” for the first time in this Gospel — before Jesus has renamed him, which does not happen on the page until “Simon, whom he also named Peter” (6:14). Luke tells his reader the name the story has not yet earned.

⭐ The verb “take hold of” that will return at the end of this Gospel. Syllabesthai (v7), “to take hold together with, to help,” and its cognate synelabon (v9), “they had taken,” both come from syllambanō — the identical verb Gabriel used of Mary, “you will conceive” (1:31), and the one Luke will use once more when a crowd “seized” Jesus in Gethsemane (22:54, not yet on these pages). One root does the work of a womb closing on a child, a net closing on a catch, and a mob closing on a man — three kinds of being taken hold of, spaced across one Gospel.

“They left everything” — wider than the nets. Mark has the first two fishermen leave “the nets” specifically (Mark 1:18); Luke’s four leave panta, everything — the identical word he will use of Levi three verses from now (v28), and the demand Jesus will later make explicit and universal: “whoever does not renounce all that he has cannot be my disciple” (14:33, not yet on these pages).

Verses 12–16 · ἀνὴρ πλήρης λέπρας… ἀπελθὼν δεῖξον σεαυτὸν anēr plērēs lepras… apelthōn deixon seauton — a man full of leprosy, no anger and no compassion named, and a command that switches from reported speech to a direct order mid-sentence

⭐ “Full of leprosy” — only Luke says so. Mark and Matthew both simply write “a leper” (Mark 1:40; Matthew 8:2); Luke alone writes anēr plērēs lepras, a man full of it — the same instinct for a graded, almost clinical description as the “great fever” three verses into the previous chapter. Whether or not it proves a physician’s hand, it is the same kind of precision.

⭐ Three Gospels, three postures — and Luke will use his own phrase again, on another leper. Mark has the man kneeling (gonypetōn); Matthew has him bowing (prosekynei, the word this site has already tracked through Matthew’s Gospel as a loaded verb); Luke has him fall on his face (pesōn epi prosōpon). The identical phrase returns in only one other healing in this Gospel — the Samaritan among the ten lepers, who alone comes back and “fell on his face at his feet, giving thanks” (17:16, not yet on these pages). Matthew uses the same words twice, of the disciples at the transfiguration (17:6, not yet on these pages) and of Jesus himself in Gethsemane (Matthew 26:39); Luke reserves it for two lepers reaching the ground before the one man who will touch them.

⚠ No emotion at all — where Mark has a famous, disputed one. Mark’s earliest and best-attested text has Jesus “moved with anger” before he touches the leper, against the majority tradition’s softer “moved with compassion” (the crux this library discussed in full at Mark 1:40–45). Matthew has neither word; Luke has neither word either. The touch itself — the scandal of the paragraph, since contact with a leper transmitted uncleanness under the Law (Leviticus 5:3; 13:45–46, already on these pages) — survives in all three Gospels; only Mark keeps a stated feeling attached to it, and the manuscripts cannot agree on which one.

⭐⭐ The sentence changes person in the middle of itself. Parēngeilen… mēdeni eipein is reported speech, an infinitive: “he ordered him to tell no one.” The very next words, apelthōn deixon… kai prosenenke, are direct imperatives — “go, show… and offer” — addressed to the man in his own voice. Mark’s parallel is direct speech throughout (“he says to him, ‘See that you say nothing…’”, Mark 1:44); here the shift from indirect to direct happens inside Luke’s own sentence, and the King James translators plainly noticed the same seam eighteen centuries ago — “he charged him to tell no man: but go…” — and kept it with a colon rather than smoothing it into two tidy sentences, which this translation also does.

⭐ No disobedient leper, and no forced retreat. In Mark, the healed man “went out and began to proclaim it freely,” and as a result “Jesus could no longer openly enter a town” (Mark 1:45) — the crowds themselves push him out to the desolate places. Luke tells none of that: there is no report that the man disobeyed, and Jesus’s withdrawal is its own verb, ēn hypochōrōn, an imperfect — “he was in the habit of withdrawing” — a settled practice of prayer, not a retreat forced on him by a broken promise. It is the same redactional habit already flagged at 4:42 and 3:21: Luke keeps adding Jesus at prayer where his sources do not require it.

Verses 17–26 · δύναμις κυρίου ἦν εἰς τὸ ἰᾶσθαι αὐτόν… παράδοξα σήμερον dynamis kyriou ēn eis to iasthai auton… paradoxa sēmeron — Jehovah’s power for HIM to heal, a word for “paralyzed” that never appears in Mark or Matthew, and a crowd that says something no other Gospel has them say

⚠⚠ “For HIM to heal” — or “for him to heal THEM”? The earliest witnesses (𝔓75, Vaticanus, and the SBL/NA28 critical text) read eis to iasthai auton, “for him to heal” — an unusual, almost redundant construction that names Jesus himself, already the subject of the sentence, as the subject of the infinitive too. A large part of the later tradition (Tregelles, the Byzantine text) reads autous, “them,” supplying the more expected object — that his power was there to heal the sick. The harder reading is kept here: KJV prints ‘to heal them’; ASV leaves the object out entirely, ‘with him to heal’; NWT 1984 prints ‘there for him to do healing’ and NWT 2013 ‘with him to do healing’, both following the same text this translation follows.

⭐ “Jehovah’s power” — a line Mark and Matthew do not have at all. Neither of Luke’s sources opens this scene with any statement about where the power to heal was coming from (Mark 2:1–2); this sentence is Luke’s own addition, and kyriou names the God of Israel, not Jesus (the same rule already fixed at 4:8 and elsewhere): the Pharisees and teachers of the law have gathered from every quarter of the land, and the narrator tells the reader before anything happens that Jehovah’s own power is in the room.

⭐⭐ “Paralyzed” — and Luke never once uses the word Mark and Matthew both use. Paralelymenos, twice in this scene (vv18, 24), is a perfect passive participle, “one who has been enfeebled”; the adjective paralytikos, which Mark and Matthew both use nine times between them for this same word, never once appears in Luke’s Gospel. Luke’s own word returns twice more, both in Acts — the man at the Samaritan mission (8:7) and Aeneas at Lydda, “who had been bedridden eight years… paralyzed” (9:33; neither yet on these pages) — so across two books Luke keeps one word and Mark and Matthew keep a different one, cleanly, without overlap.

The roof: tiles here, mud and beams in Mark. Mark 2’s note already set out the contrast in full — Mark’s men “un-roof the roofing” and dig, describing a Galilean roof of packed earth over reeds; Luke’s men go down “through the tiles,” a Greco-Roman roof, for readers who had never stood under the other kind. The bed is Luke’s own diminutive too — klinidion, a “little bed” or stretcher — where Mark keeps the poor man’s krabattos, a mat.

“Man” — a third form of address, next to “child” and “take heart, child.” Mark has Jesus call him teknon, “child” (Mark 2:5); Matthew has “take heart, child” (Matthew 9:2); Luke has the plainer, more distant anthrōpe, “man” — a vocative with no warmth built into the word itself, though the act that follows supplies it.

⭐ “Incredible things” — a word that gives English “paradox.” Paradoxa, literally “beyond what is expected,” stands nowhere else in the New Testament. Mark’s crowd says only “we never saw anything like this” (Mark 2:12); Luke gives them a rarer, more literary word for it, alongside his own added note that the whole crowd was gripped by ekstasis, the amazement that gives English “ecstasy.”

Verses 27–32 · Λευίν… δοχὴν μεγάλην… εἰς μετάνοιαν Leuin… dochēn megalēn… eis metanoian — the tax collector Mark also calls Levi, a “reception” that is Luke’s own word, and the phrase Matthew’s earliest text does not have

Levi here, Matthew there. Mark 2’s note and Matthew 9’s note have already set out the puzzle in full: Mark and Luke both call this man Levi, only Matthew’s own Gospel calls him Matthew and only that Gospel’s list of the Twelve labels him “the tax collector” — read there as the author’s quiet signature. The encyclopedia entry, written with this very verse already in view, lays out the identification and its age without asserting it as settled.

⭐ “A great reception” — a word that stands in exactly two verses of the New Testament, both in Luke. Dochēn, from dechomai, to receive as a guest, is not the plain “reclining at table” Mark and Matthew both use (Mark 2:15; Matthew 9:10); Luke names the event itself, a formal reception, and uses the identical word only once more, in Jesus’s own teaching about whom to invite to a banquet (14:13, not yet on these pages). Levi throws exactly the kind of party Jesus will later say to give.

“Everything” again. Katalipōn panta, “leaving everything behind” — the identical word already used of the four fishermen sixteen verses earlier (v11), where Mark had only “the nets.” Two call-stories in one chapter, one Lukan word for what both men leave.

⭐ “To repentance” — secure here, missing in Mark, and the reverse of the usual direction. Matthew 9’s note already flagged that a large part of the Byzantine tradition adds “to repentance” to the end of Matthew’s parallel sentence, importing it — on the critical text’s judgment — from “this exact phrase in Luke.” This is that phrase: eis metanoian stands without any textual dispute in Luke’s own sentence, the home the borrowed words came from.

“Muttering,” not asking the disciples a question. Egongyzon, the verb the Greek Old Testament uses of Israel grumbling in the wilderness (Exodus 16:2, already on these pages) — a stronger word than Mark’s plain “they were saying” (Mark 2:16).

Verses 33–39 · ῥήξει… ὁ παλαιὸς χρηστός ἐστιν rhēxei… ho palaios chrēstos estin — a new coat torn to patch an old one, a Byzantine ending borrowed from Matthew, and “the old is nice” against “the old is better”

Cut from a NEW garment, not shrunk from unwashed cloth. Mark and Matthew both picture a patch of unshrunk cloth sewn onto an old coat, which shrinks on its first wash and tears the old garment worse (Mark 2:21). Luke’s image is a different mechanism: someone cuts a patch from a new garment to mend an old one, ruining the new coat in the act and still leaving a patch that will not match. Both parables end at the same lesson — the new destroys, rather than repairs, the old — by two different routes.

⚠ “Both are preserved” — the Byzantine text borrows Matthew’s own ending. The earliest witnesses (𝔓75, Vaticanus, Sinaiticus) end verse 38 at “new wine must be put into new wineskins”; a large part of the later tradition adds kai amphoteroi syntērountai, “and both are preserved” — Matthew’s own closing words, verbatim (Matthew 9:17). It is the same harmonizing habit already caught running the other way at Mark 2:17, where the Byzantine text borrows Luke’s “to repentance” (see the previous note). Copyists smoothed all three Synoptics toward each other from both directions. Luke’s shorter, harder ending drops straight into the next verse’s twist without a resolving cadence.

⭐⭐ “The old is NICE” — not “the old is better.” The earliest text reads the plain positive adjective chrēstos, “good, pleasant, nice”; a later tradition (behind the King James and the NIV’s own translation choice) reads the comparative chrēstoteros, “better.” ASV keeps the plain form, ‘the old is good’; KJV and NIV both read the comparative, ‘the old is better’; NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 both keep ‘the old is nice’, following the same earlier text this translation follows.

⚠ A proverb, and Luke does not say whether Jesus means it kindly. No other Gospel has this closing line at all — it is Luke’s own addition to the parable, and it can be read two ways the text itself does not settle: a neutral proverb about how people are, stated without edge; or a dry, ironic aside about the very Pharisees and disciples of John who have just been arguing for the old practice over the new one — of course they prefer what they are used to. The library states both readings and does not choose between them.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: lake for limnē, never “sea,” matching Luke’s own usage across every scene on this water; Master for Epistata, Luke’s own title, kept apart from “Teacher”/“Rabbi”; partners for both metochoi and koinōnoi, with the note explaining the two words Luke keeps apart; catching people alive for zōgrōn, not “fishers of men”; full of leprosy; paralyzed for Luke’s own paralelymenos, never Mark and Matthew’s paralytikos; Jehovah’s power for dynamis kyriou; Man for anthrōpe, distinct from “child” in Mark and Matthew; incredible things for paradoxa; a great reception for dochē, Luke’s own word, distinct from plain reclining; everything, both at the nets and at the tax booth; to repentance, secure in Luke where the same words are a later addition in Mark; and the old is nice, the plain reading against the comparative “better.”

Echoes paid. Simon’s house, named a chapter early at 4:38, is finally the house of the man himself (v3); the same verb that took hold of Mary’s child at the annunciation (syllambanō, 1:31) takes hold of the fish and, later, of Jesus himself; and the prayer Luke keeps adding where his sources do not require it (3:21; 4:42) recurs once more at v16.

Echoes planted. “Simon, whom he also named Peter” (6:14), the naming this chapter has already used twice over (v8); the bridegroom taken away (v35), read against the wedding at Cana this Gospel does not have and the passion this Gospel does; zōgreō’s promise of a catch kept alive, against the crowd that will one day cry for a different sentence; Levi’s reception (v29) and the banquet-teaching of 14:13; and “he was withdrawing and praying” (v16), a habit that will recur at Gethsemane.

Next in sequence: Luke 6 — grain plucked on the Sabbath, a man with a withered hand healed in a synagogue full of watching enemies, a night of prayer on a mountain before the Twelve are named, and the Sermon on the Plain’s blessings and woes.
