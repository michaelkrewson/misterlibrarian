The angel of Jehovah comes up from Gilgal to Bochim with the verdict the first chapter was building toward: Joshua dies at a hundred and ten, and a generation arises that does not know Jehovah. The cycle of judges begins, with deliverance, relapse and a worse corruption after each judge dies.
Undated within the chapter. It follows directly on from Judges 1 and takes the same undated window the library gives that chapter, a span the book itself does not close.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–5 · Bochim, the weepers, and the covenant that does not break

The place is named for what the people did there, not for a rock or a river. Bochim is the plural of the participle of the verb bakah, to weep, so the verse names the spot after the crying in verse 4. The old map-name for the stretch is not given; the verse only says the angel came up from Gilgal, Joshua's first camp after the crossing (Joshua 4, already on these pages), to Bochim. Gilgal is where the people's conquest began; the angel comes from there to say the conquest has a price.

"The angel of Jehovah" speaks in the first person as Jehovah. Malakh Jehovah means a messenger who carries Jehovah's words, and the speech in verses 1–3 switches to "I brought you up" without a break. The shelf reads the same way: the NWT 1984 keeps "Jehovah's angel" and gives the speech as "I proceeded to bring you up", and the ASV has "the angel of Jehovah" with "I made you to go up" (with "Jehovah" printed, as the project prints it).

"A covenant that will never break." Lo-afer briti, "I will not break my covenant," is a fixed phrase for God's own promise, and the verse is careful to say whose covenant is at stake: my covenant, which the people must not make a new covenant against. KJV and ASV both give "covenant" here; the NWT 2013 reads "I will never break my covenant with you," which is the same word-for-word sense.

"What is this you have done?" The Hebrew of verse 2 is a question, not a complaint, and the translation keeps it a question. The word mah zot, "what is this," is a question the reader is meant to answer for themselves.

Thorns in the sides. The Hebrew of verse 3 says letsiddim, "for sides," and nothing more; the English "thorns in your sides" is an idiom taken from the shelf, because the Hebrew phrase is the one Numbers uses for the surviving Canaanites: Numbers 33:55 (already on these pages) says that the survivors "shall be as thorns in your eyes, and as pricks in your sides." The reader should know the English is a supplied image. The ASV uses the same "thorns in your sides" here, and the NWT 1984 chooses "lures" for the gods instead; the Hebrew is lamoqesh, a snare or trap, and the translation keeps "snare."

Verses 6–10 · Joshua dies, and a generation arises that does not know Jehovah

The formula from the end of Joshua is repeated here, and its second half is the chapter's turn. Verse 7 says the people served Jehovah "all the days of Joshua, and all the days of the elders who outlived Joshua." That sentence is the same formula as Joshua 24:31, which is now on these pages; the archive's text there reads the same, with the people serving "all the days of Joshua and all the days of the elders that outlived Joshua." The phrase al kol yemei, "all the days of," marks the time while a living leader is still the anchor. Verse 10 is what happens when the anchor is gone.

"Another generation that did not know Jehovah." Lo yadeu et YHVH, "did not know Jehovah," is a claim about knowledge, not about belief. The Hebrew means they did not recognise what Jehovah had done, and verse 10 says so directly: they did not know the work either. The reader is meant to see that the next cycle of the book begins with a failure of memory.

"Timnath-heres." The same town is called Timnath-serah in Joshua 19:50 (already on these pages), with the consonants in the other order; the note there treats them as one place, and the shelf keeps the Hebrew spelling in its place-name. The ASV prints "Timnath-heres" in verse 9 and the KJV has "Timnathheres"; the translation follows the ASV.

"Died at a hundred and ten." The age is the same number the NWT 1984 and the KJV give. Nothing in the Hebrew invites a second reading, so none is offered.

Verses 11–15 · Baal and the Ashtaroth, and the anger that falls on a people who could not stand

The gods have two names, and the translation keeps both. Ha-baalim, "the Baals," is the plural of Baal, a title meaning lord that the Canaanites gave to many local gods. Ashtaroth is the plural of Ashtoreth, a goddess whose images stand in the Hebrew text as a plural. Verse 13 pairs the two as they were worshipped together. The KJV gives "Baal and Ashtaroth" there and the ASV "Baal and the Ashtaroth," and the NWT 1984 writes "Ashʹto·reth images", which is the same plural with the goddess's image made explicit.

"They could no longer stand." Lo-yakholu od la'amod, "they were no longer able to stand," is a military phrase. The verse describes the defeat as the point at which a people can no longer hold its ground.

"The hand of Jehovah was against them." The same image opens the chapter's Israelite judgment: yad YHVH hayta bam le-ra'ah, "the hand of Jehovah was against them for harm." The ASV says "for evil," and the translation uses "for harm," because the Hebrew word ra'ah covers both the moral and the material sense and the reader meets the disaster first.

Verses 16–19 · the judges, the relenting, and the cycle

The judges are raised up, and the people do not listen to them. Shofetim is the same word for judge the book has used since its title, and it names the men and women of the next chapters. Verse 17 turns the story: the people "did not listen even to their judges." The word zanah, "played the harlot," is the Hebrew verb for sexual infidelity, and here it is used of worship of other gods. The translation keeps the image; the NWT 1984 gives "had immoral intercourse with other gods", which is literal, and the ASV has "played the harlot."

"Jehovah relented." The verb is nicham, the same verb Genesis 6:6 (already on these pages) uses when Jehovah regrets making humanity: the archive's text there reads that it "repented the LORD that He had made man on the earth." The translation keeps the sense of a change of mind, so the reader can see that verse 18 is the same kind of statement as the Flood story. The KJV and ASV both say "repented" here, and the NWT 2013 says "was moved to pity", which softens the word toward feeling.

The cycle has three beats: deliverance, relapse, worse. Verse 19 says that when the judge died, the people turned back and "grew more corrupt than their fathers." The Hebrew verb is hishchitu, to ruin or corrupt, and the comparison is made with the fathers the book has already praised in verse 17. The reader is shown the pattern before the first judge appears in the text, which is the book's way of teaching the reader what kind of story this will be.

Verses 20–23 · the test

Jehovah stops driving out the nations, and says why. The reason given is the covenant breach of verse 20, and the consequence is verse 21: no more expulsions of the nations Joshua left. The Hebrew lo osif lehoris, "I will no longer drive out," is a decision, not a fact about the land. The NWT 2013 reads it as a plain statement, "I for my part will not drive out from before them even one of the nations"; the translation keeps the decision in the first person, as the Hebrew does.

"To test Israel by them." Lenasot bam et-Yisrael, "to test Israel by them," is the word nasah, "test." The same verb is used of the manna in Exodus 16:4 (already on these pages), where Jehovah says he will test the people "whether they will walk in My law." The reader meets the test as a purpose God has, not an accident.

"Keep the way of Jehovah." The question of verse 22 is whether Israel will keep derekh YHVH, the way of Jehovah, as the fathers did. The translation keeps "the way" rather than "the ways," because the Hebrew is singular.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: "Bochim" as the weepers' place, with the crying kept in the name; "relented" for nicham, the same verb as Genesis 6:6, so the Flood and the cycle of judges read as one kind of divine change of mind; "played the harlot" for zanah, kept as an image of worship rather than softened to "went astray"; "snare" for moqesh, a snare or trap, kept distinct from "lure"; and "thorns in your sides" supplied as an idiom, with the note saying the Hebrew is only "for sides."

Echoes paid. The formula "all the days of Joshua," which Joshua 24:31 also uses (now on these pages), is repeated in verse 7 and turned on its ending: the people serve for as long as the leader lives, and the next verse begins what comes after. The "thorns" image from Numbers 33:55 returns as the reason Jehovah keeps the nations, not a stray detail. The word nicham from the Flood is paid in verse 18.

Echoes planted. The nasah, "to test," of verse 22 is the test the reader is asked to watch for in the chapters ahead. The phrase "did not know Jehovah" of verse 10 is the one the chapter leaves open.
