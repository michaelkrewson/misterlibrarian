🕰
Women who provide for Jesus and the Twelve out of their own means are named, among them Mary Magdalene. The parable of the sower is told to a crowd and explained privately to the disciples, followed by sayings on a lamp and on hearing rightly. His mother and brothers are redefined as 'those who hear the word of God and do it.' A storm on the lake is calmed with a rebuke, and, crossing to the region of the Gerasenes, a man possessed by 'Legion' is restored to his right mind, the demons driven instead into a herd of pigs that rushes into the water. Back across the lake, a synagogue ruler named Jairus begs Jesus for his dying only daughter; on the way, a woman who had bled for twelve years touches his cloak and is healed, and word comes that the girl has died, but Jesus raises her: 'Child, arise.'
c. AD 28-29, in the Galilean ministry, directly after chapter 7 on Luke's own sequence — corresponding to Mark 4:1-25 and 4:35-5:43's accounts of the same parable, storm, demoniac and two intertwined healings, and to Matthew 8:23-34 and 9:18-26; Mark and Luke share the same 'Gerasenes' reading against Matthew's 'Gadarenes' for the demoniac's region.

y algunas mujeres que habían sido sanadas de espíritus malos y enfermedades: María, llamada Magdalena, de la que habían salido siete demonios,

«Un sembrador salió a sembrar su semilla. Y mientras sembraba, una parte cayó junto al camino, y fue pisoteada, y las aves del cielo se la comieron.

Y otra parte cayó sobre la roca, y al brotar, se secó por falta de humedad.

Esta es, pues, la parábola: la semilla es la palabra de Dios.

Y Jesús le preguntó: «¿Cuál es tu nombre?». Y él dijo: «Legión», porque muchos demonios habían entrado en él.

Y le rogaban que no les mandara ir al abismo.

Y había allí una piara numerosa de cerdos paciendo en la ladera; y le rogaron que les permitiera entrar en ellos; y se lo permitió.

Y saliendo los demonios del hombre, entraron en los cerdos, y la piara se lanzó por el despeñadero al lago, y se ahogó.

Y los que lo habían visto les contaron cómo había sido salvado el endemoniado.

Entonces toda la multitud de la región de los gerasenos le rogó que se apartara de ellos, porque estaban dominados por un gran temor; y él, entrando en la barca, se volvió.
38
ἐδεῖτο δὲ αὐτοῦ ὁ ἀνὴρ ἀφʼ οὗ ἐξεληλύθει τὰ δαιμόνια εἶναι σὺν αὐτῷ· ἀπέλυσεν δὲ αὐτὸν λέγων·
Now the man from whom the demons had gone out was begging to be with him, but he sent him away, saying,note
Y el hombre de quien habían salido los demonios le rogaba estar con él; pero él lo despidió, diciendo:
39

«Vuelve a tu casa, y cuenta todo lo que Dios ha hecho por ti». Y él se fue, proclamando por toda la ciudad todo lo que Jesús había hecho por él.

Y cuando Jesús regresó, la multitud lo recibió con gusto, porque todos lo estaban esperando.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–3 · γυναῖκές τινες… Ἰωάννα γυνὴ Χουζᾶ ἐπιτρόπου Ἡρῴδου… ἐκ τῶν ὑπαρχόντων αὐταῖς gynaikes tines… Iōanna gynē Chouza epitropou Hērōidou… ek tōn hyparchontōn autais — three named women, one of them married into Herod’s own household, who paid for a ministry from their own pockets

⭐⭐⭐ Named, and financing it. No other Gospel opens a chapter with a sentence like this one. Luke names three women — Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Susanna — and “many others,” and says plainly what they did: they diēkonoun, were providing for Jesus and the twelve, ek tōn hyparchontōn autais, “out of their own means.” Nothing here says they cooked or served at table, the usual assumption behind ‘ministered unto them’; the phrase is about money and property, not domestic labor. KJV reads ‘ministered unto him of their substance’ (following a text that names only Jesus); ASV and the NWT both correctly widen it to ‘them’, matching the earlier text followed here; the NIV spells out the sense directly, ‘helping to support them out of their own means’.

⭐⭐ Joanna, from inside Herod’s house. Her husband Chuza is epitropos, steward or business manager, of Herod Antipas — the same tetrarch who has just been introduced holding John the Baptist in prison (3:19–20) and who will ask, one chapter from now, “who is this?” about Jesus (9:9). A woman married into that household is now funding the ministry of the man Herod is hearing rumors about. Luke alone among the Gospels names her, and he names her again at the empty tomb (24:10, already on these pages) — the only person besides Mary Magdalene this chapter’s list and that later one share.

⚠⚠ “Seven demons” — stated, not explained, and never called a sin. Luke says only that seven demons had gone out of Mary; no cause is given and no shame is attached to it, unlike the unnamed “sinner” of the previous chapter, whom nothing in the text identifies with her (see 7:36’s note and the encyclopedia entry on the sixth-century sermon that first fused the two women). The TLB reads ‘from whom he had cast out demons’; the Douay reads ‘out of whom seven devils were gone forth’, and the Geneva the plainer ‘out of whom went seven devils’.

Verses 4–8 · Ἐξῆλθεν ὁ σπείρων… καρπὸν ἑκατονταπλασίονα… Ὁ ἔχων ὦτα ἀκούειν ἀκουέτω Exēlthen ho speirōn… karpon hekatontaplasiona… Ho echōn ōta akouein akouetō — the sower, in the version with only one yield

The one detail Luke drops. Matthew 13’s note calls a hundredfold an exceptional harvest against a good ancient yield of thirtyfold — and both Matthew and Mark grade the good soil three ways, ‘some a hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty’ (Matthew 13:8). Luke keeps only the top figure. Whether that is compression or a different emphasis — one standard of fruitfulness instead of three — the text does not say; the effect is a flatter, more absolute ending to the parable.

⭐ Bare instruction, no interpretation attached — yet. Jesus tells the parable itself with no comment beyond the closing call to listen (v8), exactly as in Matthew 13:9 and Mark 4:9. The explanation that follows (vv11–15) is not given to the crowd that heard the parable; it is given to the disciples alone, once the crowd is no longer in view — the very asymmetry the next note is about.

Verses 9–15 · τὰ μυστήρια τῆς βασιλείας… βλέποντες μὴ βλέπωσιν… ἐν καρδίᾳ καλῇ καὶ ἀγαθῇ ta mystēria tēs basileias… blepontes mē blepōsin… en kardia kalē kai agathē — mysteries given to some and withheld from others, and a parable that interprets its own soils

⚠ The question assumes parables obscure, not that they illustrate. Matthew 13’s note makes the same point at greater length: a modern reader expects a parable to make a hard truth easy, and Jesus’ answer confirms the opposite assumption instead of correcting it. To the disciples the mysteries of the kingdom are given; for the rest, parables are the form the teaching takes precisely so that seeing they may not see. Luke’s wording is shorter than Matthew’s at this point and closer to Mark’s.

⭐⭐ Four soils, one seed, one interpreter. The parable supplies its own reading, verse by verse, rather than leaving it to the notes: the path is a stolen word (v12), the rock a short-lived one (v13), the thorns a strangled one (v14), and the good soil the word that is kept and produces fruit with endurance — hypomonē, a word about time and pressure, not a single moment of belief. Luke alone of the three Synoptic tellings adds ‘with a noble and good heart’ (en kardia kalē kai agathē) to the fourth soil, a description neither Matthew nor Mark includes.

⚠ “Believe and be saved” — the first of four times this chapter uses the word. Sōzō stands at v12 (the word snatched away ‘so that they may not believe and be saved’), then returns at v36, v48 and v50, moving from an abstraction in a parable to three separate bodies — a demon-possessed man, a bleeding woman, a dead child — each actually saved. This translation keeps ‘saved’ at every occurrence rather than varying it with ‘healed’ or ‘made well’, so the thread stays visible in English the way it is in Greek; see the dictionary entry for where the shelf splits on it.

Verses 16–18 · λύχνον ἅψας… ἐπὶ λυχνίας… ᾧ ἔχει, δοθήσεται lychnon hapsas… epi lychnias… hō echei, dothēsetai — a lamp meant to be seen, in a chapter about a light no one can hide

⭐ The same saying, moved. Matthew places this image inside the Sermon on the Mount, of the disciples themselves (‘you are the light of the world’, Matthew 5:14–15); Luke’s own Sermon on the Plain does not include it. Here it follows directly on the parable of the sower — a saying about a lamp lighting a room, placed right after a parable about seed and soil, so that hearing rightly (v18) is the hinge joining both images: the word received is like light that cannot stay hidden once it has actually taken root.

“What he thinks he has.” The warning (v18) is not aimed at someone who has nothing; it is aimed at someone who believes he has something and does not — the same soil-logic as the seed on the rock, which springs up looking like a plant and then has no root at all.

Verses 19–21 · ἡ μήτηρ καὶ οἱ ἀδελφοὶ… οἱ τὸν λόγον τοῦ θεοῦ ἀκούοντες καὶ ποιοῦντες hē mētēr kai hoi adelphoi… hoi ton logon tou theou akouontes kai poiountes — Jesus’ own family, redefined by the very test the parable just set

⭐⭐ Luke times this scene to land right after the parable's own moral. The parable has just said that the good soil is the heart that hears the word and keeps it (v15); Jesus now defines his own family by the identical test, ‘these who hear the word of God and do it’ (v21) — akouontes kai poiountes, hearing echoed by doing, the same pairing as the parable's own vocabulary. Mark's parallel (Mark 3:31–35, already on these pages) tells the same episode with a harsher edge, opening with the family thinking Jesus ‘out of his mind’; Luke keeps only the redefinition and drops the accusation entirely, the same softening pattern already noted at 4:16–30 and 6:6–11.

⚠ Not a denial that they are his family. Jesus does not say his mother and brothers are NOT his mother and brothers; he answers a report about where they are standing with a statement about who else stands in the same place. The verse names no disrespect toward Mary, who has already been given the fullest blessing any human being receives in this Gospel (1:42, 1:48).

Verses 22–25 · Ἐπιστάτα ἐπιστάτα… Ποῦ ἡ πίστις ὑμῶν… Τίς ἄρα οὗτός ἐστιν Epistata epistata… Pou hē pistis hymōn… Tis ara houtos estin — a doubled “Master,” a question turned back on the disciples, and a question the whole chapter is really asking

⭐⭐ “Master, Master” — Luke’s own word, doubled for the only time it is. Epistata is Luke’s title, never used by Matthew, Mark or John, and this is the only one of its six appearances where it is said twice in the same breath (compare Peter’s single use three chapters earlier, 5:5, and his single use again at v45 below). The doubling reads as panic rather than formality — the same men who will use the title calmly later here repeat it as the boat is taking on water.

⭐ “Where is your faith?” answered by a question of their own. Jesus asks about faith; the disciples answer with astonishment about identity, ‘Who then is this?’ — the same question, almost word for word, that Simon the Pharisee’s guests asked in the previous chapter about a different kind of authority, ‘who is this, who even forgives sins?’ (7:49). Neither question gets answered on the page where it is asked; both wait on the reader to notice that the chapters are answering each other.

⚠ A command given to weather, not a prayer offered about it. Jesus epetimēsen, rebuked, the wind and the sea — the same verb Luke elsewhere uses to silence a demon (compare Mark 1:25 and Luke 4:35, both already on these pages). The demon met a few verses into the next scene is not rebuked but commanded to leave, parangellō (v29) — a plainer word for a stranger encounter. The KJV, Douay and Geneva all read ‘rebuked the wind’.

Verses 26–33 · τὴν χώραν τῶν Γερασηνῶν… Λεγιών, ὅτι εἰσῆλθεν δαιμόνια πολλὰ… εἰς τὴν ἄβυσσον tēn chōran tōn Gerasēnōn… Legiōn, hoti eisēlthen daimonia polla… eis tēn abysson — the same country and the same name Mark already gave this story, now told from the boat that just crossed the storm

⭐ The same disputed region-name as Mark’s telling of this story. The encyclopedia entry written for this exact textual knot at Mark 5:1 applies here without change: Gerasa sits some thirty miles from the lake, an odd home for a story that ends with a herd running downhill into the water in the same verse, and the manuscripts of all three Synoptics disagree among Gerasenes, Gadarenes and Gergesenes. This translation follows the earlier witnesses, Gerasenes, in both Gospels that tell the story this way; the KJV and the Geneva both read ‘Gadarenes’ here, following the later tradition, in agreement with their own reading of Mark.

“Do not torment me” — echoed on both sides of the lake. The demon’s plea uses basanizō, the verb Matthew’s centurion used of his servant, ‘terribly tormented’ (Matthew 8:6, already on these pages) — a man’s illness and a demon’s dread named with one word. Luke’s own centurion story (7:2) does not use it; in this Gospel the verb belongs to the demon alone.

⭐⭐ “Legion” and “the abyss” — a Roman word and a word that reappears in Revelation. A legion was some five to six thousand soldiers, the empire’s own unit of occupying force, now naming the crowd inside one man; the dictionary entry, written for this same story in Mark, covers the word's full weight in a province that had actual Roman legions garrisoned nearby. The demons’ own request — not to be sent ‘into the abyss’ — uses a word the New Testament otherwise has once in Paul (Romans 10:7, not yet on these pages) and seven times in Revelation, where the abyss is a sealed shaft opened only by permission (Revelation 9:1–2, 11; already on these pages) and, later, the place the beast comes up from (11:7, already on these pages). Whatever this confinement is, the demons already know its name and dread it more than death.

⚠ The negotiation is left strange, not smoothed. The spirits beg to stay in the region (v31), then beg to enter the pigs (v32) — granted without recorded comment — and the herd’s destruction is the immediate, unexplained result. The text does not say why the second request succeeds where the first does not, and says nothing at all about the pigs’ owners.

Verses 34–39 · πῶς ἐσώθη ὁ δαιμονισθείς… ἐδεῖτο αὐτοῦ… διηγοῦ ὅσα σοι ἐποίησεν ὁ θεός pōs esōthē ho daimonistheis… edeito autou… diēgou hosa soi epoiēsen ho theos — a whole region asks Jesus to leave, and the one man who wants to stay is sent home instead

⭐⭐ “Saved,” not merely “healed” — the second of the chapter's four uses. This translation reads esōthē (v36) as ‘saved’ rather than the more common ‘healed’ or ‘made well’, keeping it identical to v12’s ‘believe and be saved’ and anticipating vv48 and 50. Most of the shelf renders the sense rather than the word here: the KJV reads ‘healed’, the ASV ‘made whole’, and the NWT ‘made well’ — a demon-possessed man’s deliverance described with the ordinary Greek word for rescue from sin and death, not a specialized word for exorcism.

Fear of the wrong thing. The whole population asks Jesus to leave once the man is ‘clothed and in his right mind’ (v35) — the exact moment he stops being dangerous. What frightens them is not the danger they already knew, but a change in kind they cannot account for; and Jesus grants it without argument (v37), as he granted the demons theirs (v32).

⭐ The one man told to talk. The one healed person in this Gospel so far given an order about the news was told to keep it quiet (the leper, 5:14), and the next will be too (v56). Here, in Gentile territory with no synagogue audience, Jesus reverses the pattern on purpose: ‘return to your home, and tell in full what God has done for you’ (v39). The man does exactly that — and the verse's own last clause quietly swaps the subject, from what God did to what Jesus did, the same identification the whole chapter keeps circling without stating outright.

Verses 40–47 · θυγάτηρ μονογενὴς… ἥψατο τοῦ κρασπέδου τοῦ ἱματίου αὐτοῦ… δύναμιν ἐξεληλυθυῖαν ἀπʼ ἐμοῦ thygatēr monogenēs… hēpsato tou kraspedou tou himatiou autou… dynamin exelēlythyian apʼ emou — an only daughter, a fringe touched by a stranger, and a promise from two chapters back paid twice over

⭐⭐⭐ “Only” — the second of the three fathers the previous chapter promised. Luke 7’s closing note named three parents whose monogenēs child this Gospel would give: the widow at Nain's only son, already told; Jairus's only daughter, here; and a father whose boy is his only son, still to come (9:38). Jairus is a ruler of the synagogue, the establishment figure this Gospel has mostly shown in conflict with Jesus (6:6–11) — and here one falls at his feet instead.

⭐⭐ The physicians she could not afford to keep seeing. The text followed here keeps the fuller detail — ‘had spent all her living on physicians, and could not be healed by anyone’ — found in the majority of manuscripts: the KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva all keep it, as does Mark’s parallel, which adds that she had actually gotten worse under their care. Some early manuscripts, including Codex Vaticanus, omit the physicians altogether and read only ‘could not be healed by anyone’ — the shorter reading the NWT follows here. Colossians 4:14 itself calls a companion of Paul’s Luke, the beloved physician (not yet on these pages) — the New Testament’s own words, not tradition; identifying that same Luke as this Gospel’s author is the second-century tradition, attested by Irenaeus and the Muratorian Fragment. Whether that identification explains the shorter reading in some copies, or is simply coincidence, cannot be settled from the text alone — but it is the reason the theory gets proposed at all.

⭐⭐ “The fringe of his cloak” — the same word Matthew used for this identical story. Kraspedon, the tassel commanded on the four corners of a garment (Numbers 15:38–39, already on these pages), is the word Matthew also uses telling this same encounter (Matthew 9:20) — two independent Gospel accounts of the same woman touching the same detail of dress, both naming it precisely rather than settling for ‘his clothes’. She reaches for what the Law required every Israelite man to wear, not for the man himself.

“Master, the crowds are hemming you in” — the second, plainer use of Luke’s title in one scene. Peter’s objection sounds like simple math: everyone is touching Jesus, so asking ‘who touched me’ sounds absurd. Jesus insists on the distinction anyway — a crowd pressing against him and one woman touching him in faith are not the same event, even though both involve contact. Dynamis, power, going out of him ‘perceived’ rather than seen, is the same word for the miraculous force at work through Jesus that this Gospel has already used of his teaching (4:14, 4:36).

Verses 48–56 · Θυγάτηρ ἡ πίστις σου σέσωκέν σε… οὐ γὰρ ἀπέθανεν ἀλλὰ καθεύδει… Ἡ παῖς, ἔγειρε Thygatēr hē pistis sou sesōken se… ou gar apethanen alla katheudei… Hē pais, egeire — the promise from last chapter paid word for word, and the same noun that once meant a foreign slave now meaning a dying girl

⭐⭐⭐ “Daughter, your faith has saved you; go in peace” — exactly as promised. Luke 7’s closing note said this precise sentence would return ‘in the next chapter, with “Daughter” in front of it, to another woman’ — and here it is, word for word, addressed this time to a woman whose faith has just acted rather than merely been named. The sentence is a promise kept twice in two chapters: once to a woman known publicly as a sinner, once to a woman whose only fault was an illness the Law made ritually isolating (Leviticus 15:25–27, already on these pages). Both times ‘saved’ is the only sense the sentence can carry — no illness remains to be healed a second time by the same words.

⚠ “She has not died, but is sleeping” — said to mourners who were right. The professional mourners ‘laughed at him scornfully, knowing that she had died’ (v53); Luke does not correct their facts, only Jesus’s claim about what her death now means in his presence. The same sleep-language for death recurs across the New Testament (compare John 11:11–14, already on these pages, where Jesus makes the same distinction over Lazarus, and his own disciples, like these mourners, take the euphemism literally before he states plainly what he means).

⭐⭐ “Child, arise” — the word that once meant a slave, now a dying girl. Pais named the centurion’s servant two chapters ago (‘my boy’, 7:7) and will name a father’s only son one chapter from now (9:42); here, in its feminine form, it names Jairus’s daughter. One Greek noun, three different people, across three scenes, with no separate word for ‘girl’ needed. Mark’s parallel preserves the actual Aramaic Jesus spoke, Talitha koum (Mark 5:41, already on these pages); Luke gives only the Greek, egeire, the same ‘rise’ verb this translation kept fixed across last chapter’s three occurrences (7:14, 16, 22) and now extends to a fourth.

“Her spirit returned” — the detail only Luke reports. Neither Mark nor Matthew's parallel says anything about the girl’s pneuma at the moment of rising; Luke alone states that it ‘returned’, treating her death as real rather than a deep sleep misdiagnosed — the same seriousness this Gospel gave the widow’s son at Nain, where the dead man simply ‘sat up and began to speak’ with no comparable clause (7:15). The instruction to feed her, the ordinary and almost comic final note, is the same instinct that closes the calming of the storm with nothing more dramatic than a question about faith: the miracle ends, and life resumes at the level of an empty stomach.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: saved for every occurrence of sōzō in this chapter (vv12, 36, 48, 50), never varied to “healed” or “made well”; Master for Epistata, Luke’s own title (vv24, 45); torment for basanizō, linking the demon’s plea to the suffering of Matthew’s centurion’s servant (Matthew 8:6; Luke 8:28); fringe for kraspedon, matching Matthew’s word for this identical story; rise / arise for egeirō, extended from last chapter’s three occurrences to a fourth (8:54); only for monogenēs; and mysteries for mystēria, matching Matthew 13’s rendering of the same word in the same parable.

Echoes paid. Both promises made at the close of Luke 7 are kept here: monogenēs returns for Jairus’s only daughter (v42), and “your faith has saved you; go in peace,” with “Daughter” in front of it, is said to a second woman (v48). The Pharisee’s guests’ unanswered question, “who is this, who even forgives sins?” (7:49), is echoed by the disciples’ own “who then is this?” (v25) — asked of the same man, about a different kind of authority, and left just as unanswered. And basanizō, Matthew’s word for a centurion’s suffering servant (Matthew 8:6), is Luke’s word for a suffering demon (v28).

Echoes planted. Monogenēs had one appearance left — a father’s only son (9:38). Pais names that same son (9:42). The disciples’ “who then is this?” (v25) is the first of a run of identity-questions this Gospel keeps asking about Jesus: Herod asks a version of it about the rumors reaching him (9:9), and Jesus asks the Twelve directly what the crowds and what they themselves say (9:18–20) — three chapters, the same question, asked by a widening circle of people. And the sleep-language for death at v52 will recur, elsewhere in the New Testament, for the same distinction between dying and merely sleeping.

Next in sequence: Luke 9 — the Twelve sent out with nothing for the road; five loaves and two fish for a crowd of thousands; Peter’s own answer to “who do you say that I am”; and a mountain where Moses and Elijah appear beside a suddenly shining Jesus.
