From prison, Paul writes his one surviving private letter — a single page to Philemon of Colossae, asking him to take back the runaway slave Onesimus, converted in Paul's cell, 'no longer as a slave but… a beloved brother'. Onesimus carries it home alongside the letter to the Colossian church.
c. AD 60–62, from the Roman imprisonment, and written together with Colossians (the two share Timothy as co-sender and the whole company of Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke). ⚠ Unlike the Pastorals its authorship is undisputed — one of the seven letters no one questions — and its brevity and private subject are part of the reason: no one forges a note this small about one man's slave. Paul expects release and asks for a guest room to be readied (v22).

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–3 · the only private letter of Paul that survives, and how it opens

⚠ This is the shortest of Paul’s letters and the only truly PERSONAL one — a single sheet, twenty-five verses, written to one man about one man, and a small masterpiece of persuasion. And the first word tells you the strategy: not “apostle,” not “slave of Christ Jesus,” but “PRISONER of Christ Jesus” (desmios). Paul has every right to command and spends the whole letter refusing to; he opens by placing himself, in chains, below the free man he is writing to. KJV and ASV read “a prisoner of Jesus Christ”; NWT “a prisoner for the sake of Christ Jesus.” The word returns twice more (vv9, 10, 13) — the chains are never out of view.

The named household. Philemon is a well-off believer of Colossae whose house holds a congregation; Apphia “the sister” is very likely his wife, named because a runaway slave was a household matter she had a stake in; Archippus “our fellow soldier” is probably their son, and is the Archippus told to “fulfil your ministry” at Colossians 4:17. ⚠ The letter is addressed to a person but read to a church: Paul makes the whole congregation witness to a private appeal, which is itself a gentle pressure.

Verses 4–7 · praise that lays the ground

Every compliment here is doing work. Paul thanks God for Philemon’s love and faith “toward the Lord Jesus and toward all the holy ones” — and the man about to be asked a hard thing is being reminded, warmly and truthfully, that he is generous. The prayer of verse 6 is that the fellowship (koinōnia, partnership, sharing) of his faith become “effective” — that his belief translate into action — which is exactly what the letter is about to request.

“The hearts of the holy ones have been refreshed through you.” Splanchna — literally the inward parts, the guts, where the ancients felt the deepest emotions; KJV and Douay say “bowels,” NIV “hearts.” It is the key word of the letter, sounded three times (here, verse 12, verse 20), and Paul is setting a trap of the kindest sort: the man who refreshes hearts is being asked, at the end (verse 20), to refresh one more. Anapauō, “refresh,” is the rest Jesus offers the weary at Matthew 11:28.

Verses 8–11 · the appeal, the old man, and the pun on a slave’s name

⚠ “Though I could COMMAND… for love’s sake I rather APPEAL.” The hinge of the whole letter. Epitassō (command) is set aside for parakalō (appeal, urge, beseech), and the renunciation is the point: an order obeyed proves nothing, but a free man’s free choice is worth everything (verse 14 makes it explicit). ⚠ “An old man” is presbytēs — and there is a famous crux here, because the word for “ambassador,” presbeutēs, differs by one letter and was often spelled the same in this period; some read “Paul an ambassador, and now also a prisoner,” which fits his self-description elsewhere. We keep “old man,” the plain text, since the pathos of age suits the appeal — but the ambassador is standing just behind it. KJV “Paul the aged”; NIV “an old man”; Douay “whereas thou art such a one, as Paul an old man.”

⚠ “USELESS… now USEFUL” — a pun on the name. The slave is finally named in verse 10, held to the end of the sentence: Onesimus, a common slave-name meaning “useful, profitable.” Paul plays on it at once: once achrēstos (useless), now euchrēstos (useful) — the runaway has lived down to his name and now lives up to it. KJV “in time past… unprofitable, but now profitable”; Geneva “unprofitable… profitable”; NWT “useless… useful.” ⚠ There may be a second pun underneath: achrēstos and christos were pronounced almost alike, so “useless” hides “Christless.” And “whom I begot in my chains” means Paul converted him in prison — the runaway found the master’s God.

Verses 12–16 · sending him back, and the sentence that outlived slavery

“My very HEART.” Paul calls Onesimus ta ema splanchna — my own guts, my heart — and sending him back cost him: he would rather have kept him, and he says so (verse 13), but he will not make Philemon’s generosity a thing done “by compulsion” (verse 14). The whole transaction is being handed to Philemon as a free choice.

⚠ “No longer as a SLAVE but more than a slave, a beloved BROTHER.” The most consequential sentence in the letter, and perhaps in the whole New Testament on the subject. ⚠ Paul does not, in so many words, command manumission — and readers have argued about that silence for two thousand years, some hearing timidity, others hearing strategy. What he does is more corrosive to slavery than a single command would have been: he redefines the relationship out from under the institution. A slave who is now “a beloved brother, both in the flesh and in the Lord” cannot really be owned; the word “brother” dissolves the word “slave” without ever attacking it head-on. The letter was cited on both sides of the abolition debate — by defenders of slavery, because Paul sent the slave back; by abolitionists, because of what he sent him back as. The library sets the sentence down and lets it do its slow work.

“Parted for an hour, that you might have him back forever.” A delicate theology of providence — “perhaps this is why” (tacha, perhaps: Paul will not presume on God’s reasons) — and a play on time: a few months’ flight against an eternal (aiōnion) brotherhood. The runaway’s crime is being re-read as the hidden route to something permanent.

Verses 17–20 · put it on my account — the note of hand

“Receive him as you would ME.” Proslabou auton hōs eme — welcome him as you would welcome Paul himself. It is the logic of an advocate standing in for the accused: if Philemon counts Paul a koinōnos, a partner (the noun of the koinōnia of verse 6), then the partnership now covers the slave.

⚠ “Charge it to MY account… I will repay.” The letter turns to bookkeeping, and the terms are commercial: elloga, “put it on my ledger” (an accountant’s word), and apotisō, “I will pay it off” — a legal term for settling a debt. ⚠ Paul even breaks in to sign the IOU in his own hand, verse 19, the only place he does so in the middle of a letter: “I, Paul, write this with my own hand: I will repay.” It has long been read as a small living picture of what the gospel says in large — another’s debt assumed, charged to an innocent account — though the letter itself only means a runaway’s petty theft. And then, gently, the counter-claim: “not to mention that you owe me your very self” — Philemon owes Paul his own conversion, a debt against which Onesimus’s is nothing.

“Let me have BENEFIT of you.” Onaimēn — “may I profit, may I have joy” — and it is the same root as the name Onesimus: let me get some “Onesimus” out of you. The pun that opened the appeal closes it, and the request lands where verse 7 prepared it: “refresh my heart.”

Verses 21–25 · more than I ask, a guest room, and the company in the cell

⚠ “Knowing you will do even MORE than I say.” The last turn of the screw, and the most quietly loaded line in the letter. Paul has carefully not asked for Onesimus’s freedom in plain words — and here hints that he expects more than he has asked. Manumission? The return of Onesimus to serve Paul (which verse 13 all but requested)? The text leaves it open, deliberately, and readers have filled the blank both ways. What is clear is that Philemon is being trusted, publicly, to be generous.

“Prepare a guest room for me.” Paul expects release and a visit — a cheerful pressure of its own (he will come and see how it went), and a sign the letter belongs to the same imprisonment as Philippians, where he also expects to be freed. Xenia is a guest-lodging.

The same names as Colossians. Epaphras (the founder of the Colossian church, Colossians 1:7), Mark, Aristarchus, Demas and Luke all send greetings here and again at Colossians 4:10-14 — the two letters were written together and carried the same journey, Onesimus travelling home alongside Tychicus with both in the bag. ⚠ Demas, who greets warmly here, will later “desert” Paul, “having loved this present world” (2 Timothy 4:10) — a small, sad footnote read across the letters.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “prisoner” for desmios, Paul’s chosen self-title in place of “apostle”; “an old man” for presbytēs, with the “ambassador” reading flagged; the “useless / useful” pun of achrēstos / euchrēstos on the name Onesimus, and “let me have benefit” (onaimēn) that closes it; “heart” for splanchna, the inward parts, kept in all three places; “charge it to my account” and “I will repay” for the commercial elloga and apotisō; and “no longer as a slave but… a beloved brother,” left to do its own work.

Echoes paid. Timothy the co-sender and the whole company — Epaphras, Mark, Aristarchus, Demas, Luke — shared with Colossians (1:1, 1:7, 4:10-14), the twin letter Onesimus carried home; the “love and faith toward the Lord Jesus and all the holy ones” almost verbatim from Colossians 1:4; the koinōnia of faith, as in the partnership of Philippians 1:5; the splanchna Paul feels “in Christ,” as at Philippians 1:8; and the hoped-for release and visit, as at Philippians.

Echoes planted. Onesimus himself, who by one early and attractive tradition became the bishop of Ephesus a generation later — and, on a well-known guess, the man who gathered Paul’s letters into the collection that saved this one; the “beloved brother” that Colossians 4:9 will call him openly, sending him home “who is one of you”; and Demas, warm here and gone by 2 Timothy.

Philemon is new to the library — the shortest and only private letter of Paul, a single page of pure tact about a runaway slave, and the New Testament book that has weighed most in the long argument over slavery. It is one chapter entire; there is no next chapter, only the church’s two-thousand-year reading of what Paul did and did not say.
