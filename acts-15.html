The Jerusalem council: Paul and Barnabas travel up from Antioch to settle whether Gentile converts must be circumcised, a decree is drafted and carried back, and the chapter closes with Paul and Barnabas splitting over John Mark and setting out on separate journeys.
c. AD 49–50 on the most common reckoning, roughly midway through the book's own span — commonly, though not universally, identified with the private meeting Paul himself describes 'fourteen years' after his conversion (Galatians 2:1, not yet on these pages); this library reports the identification without adjudicating it, since Acts and Galatians do not obviously narrate the same meeting the same way.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–6 · τῷ ἔθει τῷ Μωϋσέως tō ethei tō Mōuseōs — the custom of Moses — the first fight the church has with itself

Every crisis Acts has recorded up to this point has come from outside the movement — arrests, a mob, a financial dispute settled by appointing seven men to wait on tables (ch. 6, not yet on these pages), a private couple's deceit. This is the first one that runs straight through the leadership itself, and it is doctrinal: ‘some men’ from Judea — unnamed here, though Galatians 2:12 (not yet on these pages) may put a face on them, ‘certain men... from James’ — insist that Gentile converts must be circumcised ‘according to the custom of Moses’ to be saved. V5 names the position's source plainly: believers ‘from the party of the Pharisees.’

⚠ The word for the resulting dispute, stasis (v2), is the same word Luke uses elsewhere in this book for a riot or an insurrection (19:40, not yet on these pages) — this was not a polite disagreement among colleagues, and the text does not pretend otherwise.

Verses 7–12 · ὁ καρδιογνώστης θεός ho kardiognōstēs theos — God, who knows the heart — a rare word cashing a promise made in chapter 1

Peter's whole argument looks backward: every point he makes has already happened, at Cornelius's household (ch. 10, not yet on these pages, though this speech tells the reader its outcome in advance). ⚠ V8's “God, who knows the heart” translates ho kardiognōstēs theos — a compound this translation has already flagged as rare enough that its only two occurrences in early literature are both in this book: the eleven men who could not see inside either candidate for the twelfth apostleship handed that decision to ‘you, Lord, who know the hearts of all’ (Acts 1:24, already on these pages); now the identical word is Peter's whole case for whether the nations belong without circumcision. The same God who alone could judge between two candidates in an empty room is the one Peter says has already judged this question too.

⚠ V10's “yoke” (zygos, already on these pages at Matthew 11:29–30) is the same picture Jesus uses there for the discipleship he offers in place of something heavier — turned here into a warning: a burden ‘neither our fathers nor we’ could bear.

V11 is built to reverse the sentence a reader expects. The obvious move would be to explain how Gentiles get saved the way Jews already are; Peter inverts it — ‘we believe that we are saved... in the same way as they also are’ — making Jewish salvation rest on the same grace, not the other way around.

Verses 13–21 · Συμεὼν ἐξηγήσατο Symeōn exēgēsato — Simeon — and an Old Testament quotation that only works in Greek

James, the Lord's brother, calls Peter by his Hebrew name, Symeōn — used nowhere else in Acts and only once more in the whole New Testament (2 Peter 1:1). A small detail, and a real one: in the mouth of the man presiding over this council, it is the most Jewish name in the room for the most Jewish possible moment.

⚠ James's scriptural proof (vv16–18) quotes Amos 9:11–12, and the argument only works in Greek. This translation has not reached Amos yet, but the Hebrew is checkable now, straight from the source: the Masoretic Text reads l'ma'an yireshu et-she'erit edom, ‘that they may possess the remnant of Edom’ (Amos 9:12). James's Greek — following the Greek Old Testament, not the Hebrew — reads hopōs an ekzētēsōsin hoi kataloipoi tōn anthrōpōn, ‘that the rest of mankind may seek out [the Lord]’ (v17). Two consonantal near-misses carry the whole difference: edom/adam (Edom / mankind) and yireshu/yidreshu (possess / seek). James's entire argument for admitting the nations without circumcision leans on a reading of Amos that the Hebrew text behind his own audience's scriptures does not carry — reported here as the documented fact it is, neither smoothed over nor resolved.

V20 states the decree for the first time: abstain from the pollution of idols, sexual immorality, what is strangled, and blood. Its second statement, nine verses later at v29, uses different words in a different order — see that note.

Verses 22–27 · οἱ ἀπόστολοι καὶ οἱ πρεσβύτεροι ἀδελφοί hoi apostoloi kai hoi presbyteroi adelphoi — the letter's own opening line, and a name that has appeared here before

⚠ V23's opening is a small, real textual fork. The text followed here reads ‘the apostles and the elder brothers’ (hoi presbyteroi adelphoi) as one phrase — two groups named, not three. The Byzantine majority text, standing behind the KJV's ‘the apostles and elders and brethren,’ inserts a second ‘and,’ splitting the elders and the brothers apart.

Judas, called Barsabbas shares his surname with the OTHER man nominated, and passed over, for Judas Iscariot's vacated apostleship at Acts 1:23 (already on these pages, Judas Iscariot's own entry) — not the same man, since that one is Joseph called Barsabbas, but the same family name surfacing twice at two hinge moments of this book, six chapters apart.

Verses 28–29 · ἀπέχεσθαι … καὶ αἵματος apechesthai … kai haimatos — the decree itself — and a manuscript tradition that rewrites it completely

This is the decree's second statement, and it is not a copy of the first. V20 lists the pollution of idols, sexual immorality, what is strangled, and blood, in that order; v29 lists food sacrificed to idols, blood, what is strangled, and sexual immorality — the same four items, reordered, and even the first item changes its Greek noun (alisgēma, ‘a pollution,’ at v20; eidōlothyton, ‘a thing sacrificed to an idol,’ at v29). This translation prints each occurrence exactly as it stands rather than harmonizing the two into one tidy list.

All eleven versions checked on this shelf keep the two orders distinct too — none smooths v20 and v29 into matching lists. At v29, KJV reads ‘meats offered to idols, and … blood, and … things strangled, and … fornication’; ASV and the NWT 1984 both keep the identical idols–blood–strangled–fornication order; and the Spanish RV60 agrees exactly (‘lo sacrificado a ídolos, de sangre, de ahogado y de fornicación’). At v20, the same four versions all print idols–fornication– strangled–blood instead — NIV included (‘food polluted by idols, from sexual immorality, from the meat of strangled animals and from blood’). The reordering is in the Greek itself, not an accident of any one translation.

⚠⚠ This is the New Testament's own inheritance of a rule already on these pages. Genesis 9:4's ‘flesh with its life still in it — its blood — you must not eat,’ tightened at Sinai into Leviticus 17:14's flat ‘you shall not eat the blood of any flesh,’ is carried here into a letter written to non-Jewish converts this very council has just voted NOT to place under the law of Moses. On the traditional reading, that is exactly the point: the blood prohibition predates Sinai, given to Noah and ‘all flesh’ nine chapters into Genesis, so a council that has just exempted Gentiles from the Mosaic law can still bind them to a rule the Mosaic law never introduced in the first place.

⚠⚠ The single most consequential manuscript disagreement in this chapter sits at these two verses. Codex Bezae (D) and the wider ‘Western’ text tradition print a genuinely different decree at both v20 and v29: they drop ‘what is strangled’ entirely and add a negative form of the Golden Rule — ‘and whatever they do not want done to themselves, they should not do to another’ — which turns a four-item list mixing food law with sexual ethics into three straightforward moral prohibitions (idolatry, bloodshed, sexual immorality) plus a general ethical maxim. Under that reading, ‘blood’ more plausibly means MURDER, not diet. The critical text followed here, and all eleven versions checked on this project's shelf, keep the four-item ritual and purity list; the Western alternative is real, ancient, and belongs to the honest record of how this verse has been read — reported, not adopted, the same rule this project applies to every other manuscript variant.

Verses 30–33 · πρὸς τοὺς ἀποστείλαντας αὐτούς pros tous aposteilantas autous — back to those who had sent them — and a verse that is not here on purpose

⚠ A verse number is deliberately missing between v33 and v35. Traditionally printed as v34, ‘But it seemed good to Silas to remain there,’ it is absent from the earliest and best manuscripts (Sinaiticus, Vaticanus, and the original hand of Bezae) and reads like a later addition written to explain why Silas is still in Antioch to be chosen again at v40 — a real question the text otherwise leaves open. This translation follows the earliest manuscripts and leaves the gap exactly where they leave it, rather than patching over the seam with an explanation the earliest witnesses do not carry.

Verses 35–41 · ἐγένετο δὲ παροξυσμός egeneto de paroxysmos — a sharp disagreement — the word behind English ‘paroxysm’

⚠ V39's ‘sharp disagreement’ translates paroxysmos — built from para- (‘alongside’) plus oxys (‘sharp’), literally a SHARPENING, and the direct ancestor of English ‘paroxysm.’ Luke reaches for the blunt word here, not a softer one: two apostles who have just won the argument of their lives together, over Gentile freedom, split from each other days later over John Mark (see that entry) — whose desertion ‘in Pamphylia’ (see that entry), an episode this translation has not reached yet, Paul treats as disqualifying and Barnabas, whether family loyalty or plain mercy, does not.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: "congregation" for ekklēsia, never "church"; "the nations" for ta ethnē, matching this translation's own use at Romans 1:5 (already on these pages) rather than switching to "the Gentiles"; "who knows the heart" for kardiognōstēs, kept as the same striking compound rather than smoothed into an adjective; and the decree's two statements (v20, v29) printed in their own different order and vocabulary rather than harmonized into one list.

Echoes paid. Who knows the heart — the dictionary's own entry on kardiognōstēs, written for chapter 1's choice of the twelfth apostle, already named this exact verse, Acts 15:8, as the word's only other appearance; that promise is paid here. The yoke (v10) — zygos, already on these pages at Matthew 11:29–30 as an offer, now turned into a warning. And Peter's own voice: the encyclopedia's entry on him already states that he "all but disappears from the book after chapter 15 as Paul takes over the narrative" — this chapter is that hinge, the last extended speech Peter gives in Acts.

Echoes planted. Barnabas and Paul's split (v39) sends Barnabas to Cyprus and out of Acts's own narrative for good, and Paul toward Silas and the whole second half of this book. Mark's desertion "in Pamphylia," named here only in passing, actually happens at Acts 13:13 (not yet on these pages) and is quietly repaired much later — Paul asks for Mark by name at 2 Timothy 4:11, "he is useful to me for ministry" (not yet on these pages), a reconciliation this chapter's own rupture will make worth noticing when that page exists. And the blood clause of vv20/29 is now linked backward to Genesis 9:4 and Leviticus 17; no later passage in Acts revisits it directly, though Paul's own letters return to food offered to idols from a markedly looser angle (1 Corinthians 8, not yet on these pages).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether James's citation of Amos 9:11–12 (vv16–18) should be read the way the Greek Old Testament reads it or the way the Hebrew does — reported with both texts quoted from the source, not adjudicated. And the Western text's rewritten three-item decree at v20/29, which drops "what is strangled" for a negative Golden Rule and reads "blood" as murder rather than diet — a real, ancient alternative to the ritual list this translation prints.

A note on order: Acts stands on these pages at chapters 1, 2, 3, and 15 — chapters 4 through 14, and 16 through 28, are not yet translated.
