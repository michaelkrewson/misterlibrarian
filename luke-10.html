Seventy-two others are sent out ahead of Jesus in pairs, with instructions like the Twelve's own sending but stricter, and towns that refuse them are warned they will fare worse than Sodom; Chorazin, Bethsaida and Capernaum are singled out by name. The seventy-two return rejoicing that even demons submit to them, and Jesus says he was watching 'Satan fall from heaven like lightning,' then thanks his Father for hiding these things from the wise and revealing them to little children. A lawyer, testing him, is answered with the double command to love God and one's neighbor, and when he asks 'who is my neighbor?' Jesus tells the parable of the good Samaritan. The chapter closes in an unnamed village, where Martha serves and her sister Mary sits at the Lord's feet.
c. AD 29, on the long journey to Jerusalem that opened at 9:51 — directly after chapter 9 on Luke's own sequence, with no full parallel in Mark's shorter itinerary; Matthew scatters the same material across several discourses (the sending in Matthew 10, the woes and the Father-Son saying in Matthew 11), and the good Samaritan and the visit to Martha and Mary are found only in Luke.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation’s choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–9 · ἑτέρους ἑβδομήκοντα δύο… ἀνὰ δύο δύο… ὡς ἄρνας ἐν μέσῳ λύκων heterous hebdomēkonta dyo… ana dyo dyo… hōs arnas en mesō lykōn — a number the manuscripts cannot agree on, sent out in an idiom the manuscripts cannot agree on either

⚠ Seventy, or seventy-two — the single most contested number in the Gospels, and it is contested twice in this chapter. The witnesses split by age as well as by count. KJV, ASV and the Byzantine majority text read ‘seventy’ at both v1 and v17, a reading Sinaiticus (4th century) also carries — but the single oldest substantial manuscript of this Gospel, Papyrus 75 (c. AD 175–225, closely allied with Vaticanus throughout Luke), reads ‘seventy-two’ (hebdomēkonta dyo) at both verses, agreeing with Vaticanus against Sinaiticus; the SBLGNT’s own base text (Westcott-Hort) and the NA28 follow that older pairing, and so does this translation. Neither number is symbolic filler: seventy echoes the seventy elders of Numbers 11:16 (already on these pages) and the seventy nations of Genesis 10 (already on these pages); seventy-two would instead echo the number of translators later legend assigns the Septuagint. No Gospel writer explains the count, and not even the two oldest complete Bibles, Vaticanus and Sinaiticus, agree with each other on which one their exemplars carried.

⚠ “Two by two” — and even the doubling itself is not secure. Ana dyo dyo, literally ‘by two, two’, repeats the numeral the way Hebrew idiom doubles a word for a distributive sense (compare Mark 6:39–40’s ‘group by group’ and ‘rows, rows’, already on these pages) — but the earlier witnesses this translation otherwise follows for the seventy-two are exactly the ones that ALSO carry the doubled form here, while other early copies simplify to the ordinary Greek distributive, a single ana dyo, ‘two apiece’, with the same plain meaning and none of the Semitic flavor. Either way the twelve sent out in the previous chapter went in a single body; this larger company is deliberately paired off.

⭐⭐ “Lambs in the midst of wolves” has no parallel in the sending of the Twelve. Luke 9’s shorter commission (already on these pages) never says who is dangerous; this one names the danger outright before naming a single instruction. Matthew keeps an almost identical image at a different sending, ‘sheep in the midst of wolves’ (Matthew 10:16, already on these pages) — Luke’s arnas, lambs, is the more vulnerable of the two words, a lamb rather than a full-grown sheep.

⚠ Stricter than the Twelve’s own packing list, one item further. Luke 9:3’s note already tracked Luke’s agreement with Matthew against Mark on staff, bag, bread and money; this sending drops sandals as well (v4) — an item neither earlier list mentions, and the opposite of Mark 6:9’s explicit permission to wear them. The larger, later mission travels lighter than the first one did.

“Greet no one along the road” (v4) is not ordinary rudeness but an urgency-idiom the Hebrew Bible already has: Elisha sends Gehazi ahead with his staff and the identical instruction, ‘if you meet any man, do not greet him’ (2 Kings 4:29, not yet on these pages) — a greeting in that world was a whole social transaction, not a nod in passing, and both errands are too urgent to spare the time.

“A son of peace” (v6) is a Hebrew-shaped idiom surfacing in Luke’s Greek — ‘son of X’ for someone characterized by X, not a literal parentage (compare ‘Sons of Thunder’, Mark 3:17, already on these pages). The peace the messengers pronounce is treated as a real, almost physical thing: it can rest on a worthy house or return, unspent, to the one who spoke it.

Verses 10–12 · τὸν κονιορτὸν… εἰς τοὺς πόδας ἀπομασσόμεθα… ἀνεκτότερον… ἢ τῇ πόλει ἐκείνῃ ton koniorton… eis tous podas apomassometha… anektoteron… ē tē polei ekeinē — the identical dust-shaking Luke 9 already gave the Twelve, now handed to a wider company against a worse verdict

⭐⭐ The dust-shaking already belongs to this Gospel. Luke 9:5 (already on these pages) gives the Twelve the same gesture in almost the same words, ‘shake off the dust from your feet as a testimony against them’; here it is spelled out in direct speech, the actual sentence the seventy-two are told to say aloud in the street rather than a stage direction about what to do. Matthew and Mark give the identical instruction to the Twelve alone (Matthew 10:14; Mark 6:11, both already on these pages) — this is the only Gospel that gives it twice, to two different missions.

⚠ Sodom, not Tyre and Sidon, is the FIRST comparison — and the harsher one waits two verses. A town that refuses these messengers is measured first against Sodom (v12), archetypal in this library’s own account for hospitality catastrophically refused (Genesis 19, already on these pages); only then, in the very next breath, against Tyre and Sidon (vv13–14, below) — a second comparison that turns out to be even less forgiving, since Matthew’s parallel measures Sodom against Capernaum specifically (Matthew 11:23–24, already on these pages) rather than against any town that merely declines a welcome.

Verses 13–16 · Οὐαί σοι, Χοραζίν… ἕως τοῦ ᾅδου καταβιβασθήσῃ… ὁ ἀθετῶν ὑμᾶς ἐμὲ ἀθετεῖ Ouai soi, Chorazin… heōs tou hadou katabibasthēsē… ho athetōn hymas eme athetei — a town about which nothing else is ever recorded, cursed by name, and a chain of rejection that reaches all the way to the sender

⭐⭐⭐ Chorazin is cursed by name and then never mentioned again in the whole Bible. Of the three Galilean towns named here, Chorazin (v13) is the only one about which the Gospels record no miracle, no visit, no scene at all — nothing beyond the bare charge that ‘most of his mighty works’ happened there (see the Chorazin encyclopedia entry). Whatever earned the woe went as unrecorded as it apparently went unrepented.

Capernaum’s own question answers itself. ‘Will you be exalted to heaven?’ (v15) is framed as a question only to be answered by its own next clause — brought down to Hades — the town that hosted more of Jesus’s Galilean ministry than any other (see the Capernaum entry) receiving the sharpest reversal of the three.

⭐⭐ A chain of four verbs links a village’s welcome all the way back to God. V16 closes the whole sending with a chain: hearing the seventy-two is hearing Christ; rejecting them is rejecting him; and rejecting him is rejecting ‘the one who sent me’ — the identical chain-of-authority language Luke 9:48 (already on these pages) already used of a child received in Jesus’s name. Atheteō, ‘reject’, is a legal and commercial word for setting aside or nullifying an agreement, stronger than a simple refusal.

Verses 17–20 · Ἐθεώρουν τὸν Σατανᾶν ὡς ἀστραπὴν… τὴν ἐξουσίαν τοῦ πατεῖν… τὰ ὀνόματα ὑμῶν ἐγγέγραπται ἐν τοῖς οὐρανοῖς Etheōroun ton Satanan hōs astrapēn… tēn exousian tou patein… ta onomata hymōn engegraptai en tois ouranois — a verb tense that keeps the vision moving, and a joy Jesus redirects before it can settle in the wrong place

⭐⭐⭐ “I was watching”, not “I saw” — the Greek tense is imperfect, not a single completed glance. Etheōroun (v18) describes an ongoing, unfolding action rather than one instant — closer to ‘I kept watching’ or ‘I was beholding’ than a simple past that could describe a single moment: KJV and ASV both read ‘I beheld’, and NIV ‘I saw’. The NWT 1984 alone keeps something of the ongoing sense, ‘I began to behold’, though its 2013 revision flattens to a plain present, ‘I see’. This translation keeps the ongoing sense the Greek tense actually carries. Whether the fall itself is a vision of a primeval event, a description of what the seventy-two’s mission is accomplishing in real time, or both at once is a question this verse does not settle by itself — see the Satan entry for the wider pattern this image belongs to.

⚠ The Byzantine text turns this into a plainer statement, without ‘holy’. The earlier witnesses this translation follows read that Jesus ‘rejoiced greatly in the Holy Spirit’ (v21, below); the later majority text instead has ‘Jesus rejoiced in spirit’, dropping both the divine title and the reference to the Spirit as a distinct person. The earlier reading is also the richer one theologically, which is itself a reason some textual critics weigh it cautiously — but it has the stronger and earlier manuscript support.

Joy redirected, not cancelled. Jesus does not tell the seventy-two their joy at v17 is wrong; he tells them where the SAME joy belongs. Power over ‘the enemy’ (v19) is real and given, but it is not the thing to celebrate — a name written in heaven (v20) is. The image of names inscribed in a heavenly register recurs across the New Testament (compare Revelation 20:15, not yet on these pages) as a settled, permanent record rather than a momentary success.

Verses 21–22 · ἠγαλλιάσατο τῷ πνεύματι τῷ ἁγίῳ… ἀπέκρυψας ταῦτα ἀπὸ σοφῶν… πάντα μοι παρεδόθη ὑπὸ τοῦ πατρός μου ēgalliasato tō pneumati tō hagiō… apekrypsas tauta apo sophōn… panta moi paredothē hypo tou patros mou — the ‘Johannine thunderbolt’ readers have named inside a Synoptic Gospel, and Luke’s own small changes to it

⭐⭐⭐ The most John-like sentence in any of the first three Gospels. V22’s mutual, exclusive claim of knowledge between Father and Son — nobody knows the Son but the Father, nobody knows the Father but the Son — reads so much like John’s Gospel that readers of the New Testament have nicknamed it ‘the Johannine thunderbolt out of a Synoptic sky’. Matthew has the identical saying, almost word for word (Matthew 11:25–27, already on these pages).

⭐⭐ Luke alone frames it with an emotion. Matthew moves straight into the prayer; Luke opens with Jesus ‘rejoicing greatly in the Holy Spirit’ (v21) before a single word of it is spoken — another instance of this Gospel’s own running habit of framing a major moment around Jesus at prayer (see Luke 9:18 and 9:28–29, both already on these pages, for the pattern).

⭐⭐ “Infants” against “the wise and understanding” inverts the culture’s own hierarchy. Nēpiois (v21) names those with no standing to claim credentialed insight at all — not humble scholars but people not yet old enough to have any learning. What is hidden from the wise is not withheld as a puzzle to be solved by cleverness; it is given to those who could not have earned it by cleverness in the first place.

⚠ “Who the Son is… who the Father is” — Luke phrases the mutual knowledge as identity, not mere acquaintance. The Greek asks who (tis) the Son and the Father each ARE, not merely that Father and Son know one another the way two people might. Matthew’s version of the same saying uses the same construction, so this is not a distinctively Lukan wording — but it is worth naming plainly rather than smoothing into a simpler ‘knows him’.

Verses 23–24 · Μακάριοι οἱ ὀφθαλμοὶ οἱ βλέποντες ἃ βλέπετε… πολλοὶ προφῆται καὶ βασιλεῖς ἠθέλησαν ἰδεῖν Makarioi hoi ophthalmoi hoi blepontes ha blepete… polloi prophētai kai basileis ēthelēsan idein — a private beatitude, and the only place in the Gospels where PROPHETS AND KINGS are named as the ones who wanted, and did not get, what the disciples now have

⭐⭐ Spoken privately, to the disciples alone. V23 repeats the same private framing this Gospel used just before Peter’s confession, ‘turning to the disciples… privately’ (compare Luke 9:18, already on these pages) — the crowd that has just watched the seventy-two return is not the audience for this particular blessing.

“Prophets and kings” — Matthew’s parallel names only prophets and righteous people. Matthew’s version of this same beatitude reads ‘many prophets and righteous people desired to see…’ (Matthew 13:17, already on these pages); Luke substitutes ‘kings’ for ‘righteous people’ — a small but real difference in who is named as having wanted, and not received, what the Twelve and the seventy-two have just been given.

Verses 25–29 · νομικός τις… τί ποιήσας ζωὴν αἰώνιον κληρονομήσω… τὸν πλησίον σου ὡς σεαυτόν nomikos tis… ti poiēsas zōēn aiōnion klēronomēsō… ton plēsion sou hōs seauton — a question Mark and Matthew both ask too, and the verse this Gospel’s own Mark note already promised forward

⭐⭐⭐ The same double commandment three Synoptics quote, and no two of them list its four terms in the same order. Mark’s version of this same Deuteronomy quotation (Mark 12:29–30, already on these pages, where its own note already tracked this exact discrepancy forward) lists heart, soul, mind, strength; Matthew’s has only three, dropping strength for mind alone (Matthew 22:37, already on these pages); Luke’s here (v27) runs heart, soul, strength, mind — the identical four Hebrew-shaped terms Mark uses, in a different order. Deuteronomy’s Hebrew has only three (heart, soul, and me’od, ‘utmost’) — and the New Testament could not settle on one Greek equivalent for the third.

⭐⭐⭐ Since this quotes Deuteronomy 6:5 directly, this translation reads “Jehovah your God”, not “the Lord your God”. The same choice was already made at Mark 12:30 and at Matthew 22:37 (both already on these pages, quoting the identical clause) — the New Testament prints the divine name wherever it quotes a text that has it, and this is one of those places.

⚠ The lawyer, not Jesus, gives the answer — and here it is the same speaker who then asks a second question to get out of it. Mark and Matthew each have a SCRIBE ask the question and Jesus supply the double commandment himself, with the scribe only repeating it back afterward (Mark 12:28–33; Matthew 22:36–40, both already on these pages). Luke reverses the roles: the lawyer answers his own question when Jesus turns it back on him (vv26–27), is told plainly ‘do this and you will live’ (v28), and only THEN, ‘wanting to justify himself’ (v29), asks the follow-up that launches the parable. The commandment is common ground across all three Synoptics; only Luke makes it something the lawyer already knew and needed pressed further.

“Test”, the same verb the wilderness used of Jesus himself. Ekpeirazōn (v25) is the identical verb the devil uses at the temptation, quoting Deuteronomy back at Jesus ‘to test’ him (Luke 4:12, already on these pages, where Jesus answers ‘you shall not put Jehovah your God to the test’). A lawyer probing Jesus with a lawful, well-formed question is framed with the same word as the wilderness’s outright temptation.

Verses 30–37 · ἱερεύς τις… Λευίτης… Σαμαρίτης δέ τις… ἐσπλαγχνίσθη… τίς… πλησίον δοκεῖ σοι γεγονέναι hiereus tis… Leuitēs… Samaritēs de tis… esplanchnisthē… tis… plēsion dokei soi gegonenai — a parable with no parallel in any other Gospel, and a question that answers the lawyer’s by turning it around

⭐⭐⭐ The parable exists nowhere else in the New Testament. Neither Matthew, Mark nor John records the good Samaritan; it is Luke’s alone, and it answers ‘who is my neighbor?’ with a story rather than a definition. ⚠ It is not set on the road a Samaritan village refused to let Jesus travel one chapter earlier (Luke 9:51–56, already on these pages) — that route ran through Samaria itself, while this one runs between Jerusalem and Jericho, well to the south and east of it, a genuinely steep, isolated descent of about a thousand meters over some twenty-seven kilometers, known in antiquity for exactly this kind of ambush. The ethnic hostility is the same; the geography is not — a hearer still smarting from one Samaritan village’s refusal is asked, one chapter later, to make a Samaritan the story’s only hero.

⭐⭐ Priest and Levite are not condemned for coldness the text ever states — only for what they DO. Neither man’s reason for passing by is given: readers have long guessed at a fear of corpse-defilement (a priest touching a possibly-dead body would be disqualified from his duties, Leviticus 21:1–3, already on these pages), but the parable itself supplies no motive at all, only the bare verb ‘passed by on the other side’ (antiparēlthen), repeated identically for both (vv31–32). The silence is deliberate: whatever their reasons, the story does not let them explain themselves.

“Moved with compassion” — a verb the Gospels mostly give to Jesus himself, and once already to a parable’s own master. Splanchnizomai (v33) stands in nine verses of the New Testament. Jesus is its narrated subject five times — moved at the sight of a crowd twice (Matthew 9:36; 14:14, not yet on these pages), a father’s convulsing boy (Mark 9:22, already on these pages), two blind men (Matthew 20:34, already on these pages), and, within this Gospel, the widow at Nain’s only son (Luke 7:13, already on these pages). But it already belongs to an ordinary character inside a parable once before this one, at Matthew’s master forgiving an unpayable debt (Matthew 18:27, already on these pages) — so the Samaritan here is not the New Testament’s first parable-character to feel it, only this Gospel’s first. Luke will give the identical verb to one more parable figure five chapters ahead, a father running to his returning son (15:20, not yet on these pages) — so that by the end of this Gospel’s own use of the word, its parables have claimed it as often as its narrator has.

⚠ An inn, not the guest room this same Gospel used for Bethlehem. Pandocheion (v34) is a genuine commercial establishment with a paid keeper — a different word entirely from katalyma, the guest room Luke uses of the crowded lodging at Bethlehem (2:7, already on these pages) and of the upper room of the Last Supper (22:11, not yet on these pages). Luke knew the word for a commercial inn and used it exactly once, here, where an actual innkeeper takes actual payment — the strongest evidence in this Gospel’s own vocabulary that Bethlehem was never called an inn by accident.

⭐ Two denarii, roughly two days’ wages, left on an open account. A denarius was a laborer’s standard daily wage (see the denarion entry, already on these pages) — the Samaritan leaves enough for perhaps a fortnight’s lodging and promises to cover whatever runs over it, an open-ended commitment rather than a fixed, limited charity.

⭐⭐⭐ The lawyer’s own question gets answered sideways, not directly. He asked ‘who is my neighbor?’ (v29) — a question about which people qualify for his obligation. Jesus’s closing question, ‘which of these three became a neighbor?’ (v36), quietly changes what the word measures: not a category of person to identify, but an action to perform. The lawyer cannot even bring himself to say ‘the Samaritan’ in his answer (v37), naming him instead by what he did — ‘the one who showed him mercy’.

Verses 38–42 · κώμην τινά… παρὰ τοὺς πόδας τοῦ Ἰησοῦ… τὴν ἀγαθὴν μερίδα ἐξελέξατο kōmēn tina… para tous podas tou Iēsou… tēn agathēn merida exelexato — an unnamed village, a title that fluctuates across the manuscripts, and a textual crux over whether one thing is needed or only a few

⚠ “A certain village” — deliberately unnamed here, though tradition supplies Bethany. Luke never names the village (v38); John’s Gospel later places Martha, Mary and their brother Lazarus at Bethany, near Jerusalem (John 11, not yet on these pages), and the identification is old and very likely correct, but it rests on that later chapter, not on anything stated here. This translation follows the text’s own reticence and leaves the village unnamed, as it is in the Greek.

⚠ Some manuscripts specify whose house it was; the earliest do not say. The text followed here reads simply that Martha ‘welcomed him’ (v38); other early copies add ‘into her house’ or ‘into her own house’, spelling out what the shorter reading only implies — that Martha, named first and clearly the household’s head, receives Jesus as her own guest.

⚠ “The Lord’s feet” — the earlier manuscripts read ‘the Lord’, where later ones read ‘Jesus’. At v39 the earlier witnesses this translation follows have Mary sitting at ‘the Lord’s’ feet; later manuscripts read ‘Jesus’s’ instead. At v41 the pattern runs the other way — the earlier text has ‘the Lord’ answering Martha, and it is the LATER manuscripts that substitute ‘Jesus’. Whichever the original wording, ‘the Lord’ is this whole chapter’s own narrating voice for Jesus: it opens the chapter, ‘the Lord appointed seventy-two others’ (v1, above), and the seventy-two themselves address him that way on their return (v17, above) — a title this Gospel increasingly reaches for once Jesus’s identity is no longer a live question in the narrative.

⭐⭐⭐ “Few things are needed — or only one” — the two oldest complete Bibles agree with each other here, and the modern critical text departs from both. Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus (both 4th century, and on opposite sides of the seventy/seventy-two split above) AGREE at v42: the earlier, longer reading is ‘few things are needed — or only one’, which is exactly what the SBLGNT’s own base text (Westcott-Hort) prints. The NA28, Tregelles and the Byzantine majority instead read the simpler, far more familiar ‘only one thing is necessary’ — the wording behind most of this shelf, from the KJV’s ‘one thing is needful’ to the ASV’s identical ‘one thing is needful’ — not because it is better attested by the earliest manuscripts, but because many editors judge the longer reading a later conflation of two separate, independently circulating shorter readings, stitched together by an early copyist. The NWT 1984 keeps the older, longer reading, ‘A few things, though, are needed, or just one’, and its 2013 revision keeps the same shape. This translation follows Vaticanus, Sinaiticus and the NWT over the modern critical majority’s preference for the shorter reading.

Martha is not rebuked for serving, but for anxiety about it. Jesus never says the serving itself is wrong — the repeated verb is merimnaō and thorybazō, ‘anxious’ and ‘troubled’ (v41), inward states, not the work itself. ‘The good portion’ Mary has chosen (v42) does not erase Martha’s hospitality from the previous verses; it names what Mary added that Martha, in that moment, could not also hold onto.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: neighbor for plēsion, carried across both the lawyer’s question and Jesus’s own closing question, so the word’s meaning visibly shifts from a category to an action (vv27, 29, 36); inn for pandocheion, its one New Testament occurrence, kept distinct from the guest room (katalyma) this Gospel used at Bethlehem; moved with compassion for splanchnizomai, given here to this Gospel’s first parable-character to feel it (v33), a role the word had already played once in Matthew before this chapter and will play once more in Luke after it; and lawyer for nomikos, kept distinct from the scribes of Mark’s and Matthew’s parallel scenes (v25).

Echoes paid. The forward pointer planted at Mark 12:28–34’s own note — that Luke 10:27 also lists all four terms of the great commandment, in yet a third order — is completed here (v27). The dust-shaking instruction Luke 9:5 already gave the Twelve (already on these pages) is handed to the wider company and, for the first time, spoken out in full rather than only narrated (vv10–11). And the private-audience framing first used at Peter’s confession (Luke 9:18) recurs for this chapter’s own beatitude (v23).

Echoes planted. “Lambs in the midst of wolves” will return, as sheep among wolves, at a different sending in Matthew’s Gospel (10:16). The Father-and-Son mutual claim of v22 has its own close Matthean twin still to come (Matthew 11:25–27). And the village left unnamed here will get its name only in a much later Gospel, at the story of Lazarus (John 11).
