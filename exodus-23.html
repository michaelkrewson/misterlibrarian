Exodus 23 — The Mister Translation

1

You shall not spread a false report; do not join hands with the wicked to be a malicious witness.note
No difundirás un informe falso; no te unirás al malvado para ser un testigo malicioso.
2
לֹא-תִהְיֶה אַחֲרֵי-רַבִּים, לְרָעֹת; וְלֹא-תַעֲנֶה עַל-רִב, לִנְטֹת אַחֲרֵי רַבִּים--לְהַטֹּת.
You shall not follow a crowd to do evil, and you shall not testify in a lawsuit so as to twist justice by siding with the crowd.note
No seguirás a la mayoría para hacer el mal, y no testificarás en un pleito de modo que tuerzas la justicia siguiendo a la mayoría.
3
וְדָל, לֹא תֶהְדַּר בְּרִיבוֹ. {ס}
And you shall not favor a poor man in his lawsuit.note
Y no favorecerás al pobre en su pleito.
4
כִּי תִפְגַּע שׁוֹר אֹיִבְךָ, אוֹ חֲמֹרוֹ--תֹּעֶה: הָשֵׁב תְּשִׁיבֶנּוּ, לוֹ. {ס}
If you come across your enemy's ox or donkey wandering off, you must return it to him.note
Si encuentras el buey o el asno de tu enemigo extraviado, deberás devolvérselo.
5
כִּי-תִרְאֶה חֲמוֹר שֹׂנַאֲךָ, רֹבֵץ תַּחַת מַשָּׂאוֹ, וְחָדַלְתָּ, מֵעֲזֹב לוֹ--עָזֹב תַּעֲזֹב, עִמּוֹ. {ס}
If you see the donkey of someone who hates you lying helpless under its load, you must not walk away from him — you must help him with it.note
Si ves el asno de quien te odia caído bajo su carga, no lo dejarás así —deberás ayudarlo con él.
6
לֹא תַטֶּה מִשְׁפַּט אֶבְיֹנְךָ, בְּרִיבוֹ.
You shall not twist the justice due your poor in his lawsuit.note
No torcerás la justicia debida a tu pobre en su pleito.

מִדְּבַר-שֶׁקֶר, תִּרְחָק; וְנָקִי וְצַדִּיק אַל-תַּהֲרֹג, כִּי לֹא-אַצְדִּיק רָשָׁע.
Keep far from a false charge, and do not kill the innocent and the righteous, for I will not acquit the guilty.note
Aléjate de una acusación falsa, y no mates al inocente ni al justo, porque yo no absolveré al culpable.
8
וְשֹׁחַד, לֹא תִקָּח: כִּי הַשֹּׁחַד יְעַוֵּר פִּקְחִים, וִיסַלֵּף דִּבְרֵי צַדִּיקִים.
You shall not accept a bribe, for a bribe blinds those who see clearly and twists the words of the righteous.note
No aceptarás soborno, porque el soborno ciega a los que ven con claridad y tuerce las palabras de los justos.
9
וְגֵר, לֹא תִלְחָץ; וְאַתֶּם, יְדַעְתֶּם אֶת-נֶפֶשׁ הַגֵּר--כִּי-גֵרִים הֱיִיתֶם, בְּאֶרֶץ מִצְרָיִם.
And you shall not oppress a foreigner; you yourselves know the heart of a foreigner, since you were foreigners in the land of Egypt.note
Y no oprimirás al extranjero; vosotros mismos conocéis el corazón del extranjero, pues extranjeros fuisteis en la tierra de Egipto.

וְשֵׁשׁ שָׁנִים, תִּזְרַע אֶת-אַרְצֶךָ; וְאָסַפְתָּ, אֶת-תְּבוּאָתָהּ.

Seis años sembrarás tu tierra y recogerás su producto,
11
וְהַשְּׁבִיעִת תִּשְׁמְטֶנָּה וּנְטַשְׁתָּהּ, וְאָכְלוּ אֶבְיֹנֵי עַמֶּךָ, וְיִתְרָם, תֹּאכַל חַיַּת הַשָּׂדֶה; כֵּן-תַּעֲשֶׂה לְכַרְמְךָ, לְזֵיתֶךָ.

pero en el séptimo la dejarás descansar sin cultivar, para que coman los pobres de tu pueblo, y lo que dejen lo coman los animales del campo. Lo mismo harás con tu viña y tu olivar.

Six days you shall do your work, but on the seventh day you shall stop, so that your ox and your donkey may rest, and your servant's son and the foreigner may be refreshed.note
Seis días harás tu trabajo, pero el séptimo día cesarás, para que descansen tu buey y tu asno, y se recuperen el hijo de tu sierva y el extranjero.
13

Tres veces al año celebrarás para mí una fiesta.

Guardarás la Fiesta de los Panes sin Levadura. Durante siete días comerás pan sin levadura, como te ordené, en el tiempo señalado del mes de Abib —pues en ese mes saliste de Egipto—. Nadie se presentará ante mí con las manos vacías.

Notes on this translation

Justice blind in both directions

The Mishpatim close with courtroom ethics, and the chapter's very first move is to rule out the crowd as a moral authority. A false report, a hand joined with the wicked, testimony bent to follow the majority (vv1–2) — the text names conformity itself as a form of corruption, not just bribery or malice. Then comes the harder, less expected turn: v3 forbids favoring a poor man in his own lawsuit. This is not the same law as v6, a few verses later, which forbids twisting justice AGAINST the poor — the two verses bracket a single principle from both sides. ⚠ This exact balance — do not favor the poor, do not defer to the great, judge your neighbor with justice — is stated almost word for word at Leviticus 19:15 (already on these pages), using the same rare verb (hadar, to show deference) for the poor man's side of the scale.

⚠ Help your enemy's animal before the law says a word about bribery

Two verses of pure kindness sit inside a law code, addressed specifically to an enemy. Return your enemy's wandering ox or donkey (v4); stop and help the very donkey of someone who hates you, if you find it collapsed under its load (v5) — not commanded toward a neighbor or a stranger first, but toward an ENEMY, before either word appears elsewhere in the chapter. The text gives no reasoning, no theological justification — only the case, stated flatly, the same casuistic form used for oxen that gore and pits that are left uncovered.

Bribery closes the judicial section on its own vivid image: a bribe does not merely tempt a corrupt judge, it blinds those who see clearly (v8) — corruption reframed as a loss of perception, not a moral failure a sharp-eyed judge could simply choose against. And the foreigner-protection law returns a third time in three chapters (v9; already given at Exodus 22:21), now with a new argument alongside the old one: not only were you foreigners in Egypt, but you KNOW the foreigner's inner life from the inside — experience offered as a form of knowledge a court can actually use. The shelf still splits along the same lines as before: KJV and ASV read ‘stranger’ / ‘sojourner,’ while NIV and NWT both modernize to ‘foreigner’ / ‘foreign resident.’

A rest that reaches the land, the ox, and the man born into someone else's house

The sabbatical year (vv10–11) extends the logic of the weekly Sabbath to the land itself — six years of sowing, then a seventh left to lie fallow, with whatever grows on its own left for the poor and for wild animals to eat. This is the first appearance of what will become the full shemitah legislation (Leviticus 25, not yet on these pages); here it is stated in a single sentence, with no enforcement mechanism and no theology attached, simply folded into the same case-law register as everything around it.

The weekly Sabbath itself (v12) is restated here with its beneficiaries named explicitly: not just the householder, but his ox, his donkey, ‘the son of your maidservant,’ and the foreigner — the same list of the vulnerable this chapter keeps returning to. ⚠ The commandment itself was already given at Exodus 20:8–11; what this verse adds is not the rule but its beneficiaries — rest, here, is framed less as worship and more as relief owed to the body, human and animal alike.

Three festivals, and the verse that built a kitchen law out of six words

This chapter gives the Torah's first calendar: three annual pilgrimage festivals requiring every male to appear before Jehovah (vv14–17) — Unleavened Bread, tied explicitly to the Exodus itself; Harvest, the firstfruits of the grain; and Ingathering, at the year's end. All three will be developed at far greater length once Leviticus 23 is reached (not yet on these pages), but the basic three-festival structure is fixed here first, and never changes.

⚠ The chapter's last clause of this section is six words in Hebrew and among the most consequential in the entire Torah: ‘you shall not boil a young goat in its mother's milk’ (v19). This exact command repeats twice more, essentially verbatim, at Exodus 34:26 and Deuteronomy 14:21 (neither yet on these pages) — a triple repetition unusual enough on its own to signal real weight. Even the verb tracks the word's long life in English: KJV reads the older ‘seethe,’ ASV and NWT both modernize to ‘boil,’ and NIV goes further still to ‘cook’ — three centuries of a single verb quietly wearing down into the plainest word available. Read narrowly, it may simply ban a specific fertility or feast-day dish attested in the wider ancient Near East. Rabbinic tradition reads it far more broadly, as the scriptural root of the entire separation of meat and dairy in later Jewish law — an entire kosher kitchen built, over centuries of interpretation, on a single clause about a young goat. This translation reports the verse plainly and does not adjudicate between the narrow and the expansive reading; the history of how one became the other is itself the whole story.

⚠ An angel who will not pardon your sin, because God's own name is inside him

The angel Jehovah promises to send ahead of Israel (v20) is one of the most theologically loaded figures in the Torah, and this chapter pushes the ambiguity further than anywhere it has appeared before. The same word already named the figure who spoke to Hagar in God's own first person at Genesis 16:7 (already on these pages, with the three standard readings discussed there: a created angel speaking on God's behalf, a direct theophany, or something the text leaves deliberately unresolved). Here the stakes are raised explicitly: this angel can refuse to pardon transgression, on his own authority, ‘for my name is in him’ (v21) — language that goes well beyond a messenger simply relaying a message. Later Jewish and Christian interpreters alike have read this figure as everything from a literal created angel to a veiled appearance of God's own presence; this translation keeps the ambiguity the Hebrew keeps, rather than resolving it in either direction.

Total rejection, and a conquest deliberately paced too slow to finish quickly

The section pivots from ethics and calendar to the coming conquest: total rejection of Canaanite worship, smashed pillars, and a real, named blessing in return — bread, water, health, no miscarriage or barrenness, a full lifespan (vv24–26). Then the means: terror sent ahead, enemies routed, and — the chapter's strangest single detail — a hornet (v28, tsirah) sent to drive out three of the listed peoples specifically. Ancient and modern readers read it every possible way: literal insects, a byword for the panic and disease that precede an advancing army, or an echo of Egyptian military campaigns into Canaan that preceded Israel's own. The shelf itself splits on even the grammatical number — KJV reads a plural, ‘hornets’; ASV keeps the Hebrew's own singular, ‘the hornet’ — and NWT abandons the insect altogether for a psychological reading, ‘the feeling of dejection,’ taking the word as a byword for demoralization rather than a literal creature. The text itself gives no gloss.

⚠ The most striking strategic detail in the whole passage is that God explicitly does NOT promise a quick conquest, and gives an ecological reason why not. Driving out the nations in a single year would leave the land desolate and let wild animals multiply out of control (v29) — so the process will run ‘little by little’ (v30), paced to Israel's own capacity to actually occupy and hold what is cleared. Total victory, deliberately withheld from being instant, for the practical benefit of the very people it is being won for. The chapter closes on borders (Red Sea to the Mediterranean, wilderness to the Euphrates, v31) and a final warning against covenants with the nations or their gods — not out of tribal exclusivity stated for its own sake, but because the text names the exact mechanism of the danger: ‘they will make you sin against me’ (v33), a snare rather than a simple prohibition.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. ‘Foreigner’ kept for ger throughout, consistent with the choice already made at Exodus 22:21. ‘Festival’ for chag and ‘pilgrimage festival’ for the fuller regel construction, since the root verb (chagag) means to circle or to keep a pilgrim-feast, not simply to celebrate. Verse numbering in this chapter follows the Hebrew exactly — unlike the previous chapter, no shift applies here; Hebrew and English Exodus 23 both run 1–33.

A note on order: Exodus stands on these pages at chapters 1–32 — the Mishpatim complete, and with them the covenant-ratification ceremony that closes the whole section, the tabernacle's furnishings, its own frame and curtains, the altar and courtyard enclosing it, and now the priesthood's own garments. Exodus 24 is where that ceremony happens; Exodus 25, Exodus 26, and Exodus 27 describe the building itself, inside, out, and around; Exodus 28 clothes the priesthood that will serve inside it. Exodus 29, the ordination ceremony, Exodus 30, the incense altar and the half-shekel census, and Exodus 31, the naming of Bezalel and Oholiab and the sabbath command that closes the whole instruction, and Exodus 32, the golden calf and Moses’ intercession, are now on these pages too. Exodus 33, Moses’ plea to see Jehovah’s glory, remains open, along with the rest of the book.
