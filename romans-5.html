The same letter, the same dictation: no narrative time passes between chapters. Having argued from Abraham that righteousness is reckoned out of faith, the letter turns to what the justified person has (peace, access, a place to stand, a ground for boasting in hope and tribulation), then to the comparison of Adam with the one man through whom the gracious gift comes: sin and death entering the world, law coming in alongside, and grace reigning through righteousness to eternal life.
Undated by the text, continuous with chapters 1 to 4; this library dates the letter no more closely than those chapters do. The only time-line the chapter itself draws is the one it names: “from Adam until Moses,” death reigning before the law came in.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · δικαιωθέντες … ἐκ πίστεως … εἰρήνην ἔχομεν … προσαγωγήν … ἑστήκαμεν … καυχώμεθα ἐπʼ ἐλπίδι dikaiōthentes … ek pisteōs … eirēnēn echomen … prosagōgēn … hestēkamen … kauchōmetha ep’ elpidi — Romans 5:1–2: “we have peace with God” or “let us have peace”? One vowel decides it, and then what “access into this grace” and boasting in hope mean

“Having been justified, then, out of faith” (Romans 5:1). Dikaiōthentes oun ek pisteōs. The chapter break is the editors’, not Paul’s: the Greek runs straight on from the last verse of chapter 4, where Jesus “was raised because of our justification.” The participle that opens this verse is the one that opens verse 9 (dikaiōthentes), so the first half of the chapter is bracketed by the same word, and the verb is dikaioō, “justify,” the verb of Romans 3:24 and 3:28. The preposition is the one of Romans 3:30, ek pisteōs, “out of faith.” What follows in the chapter is what the justified person has: peace, access, a place to stand, a ground for boasting.

⚠ “We have” or “let us have” (echomen or echōmen). The apparatus shows one letter in dispute. The printed text, echomen, is indicative, “we have peace”; Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont agree with it. Westcott–Hort and Tregelles print echōmen, subjunctive, “let us have peace.” The indicative states what justification has produced, in the same run of perfect and present verbs that follows in verse 2 (“we have obtained,” “we stand”); the subjunctive would turn the verse into an exhortation, with peace as something still to be taken up. The shelf divides on it. KJV “we have peace with God,” ASV “we have peace with God” and NIV “we have peace with God” print the indicative, as does Geneva “we have peace toward God”; Douay “let us have peace with God” and NWT 1984 “let us enjoy peace with God” print the subjunctive; TLB “we can have real peace with him” makes it a possibility, which is neither. Douay follows the Latin: the Vulgate has pacem habeamus, the subjunctive (checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org). The preposition is pros, “toward” or “with”: Geneva keeps the direction, the rest say “with.” The peace is the reverse of what verse 10 names, enmity, and of the wrath that Romans 1:18 opened with.

“Access into this grace in which we stand” (prosagōgē, Romans 5:2). Di’ hou kai tēn prosagōgēn eschēkamen tē pistei eis tēn charin tautēn en hē hestēkamen. The noun prosagōgē, “access,” stands in three verses of the New Testament (prosagōgē, searched): here, Ephesians 2:18 (“through him we both have access, in one Spirit, to the Father”) and Ephesians 3:12 (not yet on these pages). It is built on prosagō, “bring forward,” and eschēkamen is a perfect, “we have obtained and still have,” with the way in given as “through whom,” so the access is granted, not earned. The destination is a state, “this grace,” and the second perfect, hestēkamen, says that we have taken our stand in it and are standing there. Grace was the word at 3:24 (“freely by his grace”) and 4:16 (“of faith, so that it may be according to grace”); here it is a place. KJV “we have access by faith into this grace wherein we stand” and NIV “we have gained access by faith into this grace in which we now stand” keep the picture; ASV “we have had our access” and Geneva “we have had this access” put the verb in the past; NWT 1984 “we have gained our approach” chooses a court word; TLB “he has brought us into this place of highest privilege where we now stand” names God as the one who did the bringing. Douay “we have access through faith into this grace” has the same shape as the others.

“We boast in hope of the glory of God” (kauchōmetha). Chapter 3 shut boasting out (“Where, then, is the boasting? It has been shut out,” 3:27), and chapter 4 gave Abraham nothing to boast about “before God” (4:2). Here the verb comes back, and what is boasted of has changed: not a work, but a hope, and the hope is the glory that Romans 3:23 said all fall short of. The verb is kauchaomai; it stands in this chapter three times, in verses 2, 3 and 11, and every time of “we.” The shelf spreads the three verses over several English words. KJV has “rejoice” here, “glory” in verse 3 and “joy” in verse 11; ASV “we rejoice in hope of the glory of God” and Geneva “rejoice under the hope of the glory of God” keep “rejoice”; NIV “we boast in the hope of the glory of God” is the one with “boast” in this verse; Douay “glory in the hope of the glory of the sons of God” keeps “glory” and is the only version on the shelf that ends the verse with “of the sons of God” (the Vulgate reads gloriae filiorum Dei; the Greek printed here has only “the glory of God”); NWT 1984 “let us exult, based on hope of the glory of God” again takes the subjunctive; TLB “we confidently and joyfully look forward” drops the verb of boasting for an expectation. Whatever English word is chosen, it is the one Paul used against the teacher who “boasts in the law” (2:23), turned the other way: boasting that has no place for the speaker’s own record. The “glory about to be revealed to us” of Romans 8:18 is the same hope looked at from the other end.

Verses 3–5 · καυχώμεθα ἐν ταῖς θλίψεσιν … ὑπομονὴν κατεργάζεται … δοκιμήν … ἐλπὶς οὐ καταισχύνει … ἐκκέχυται kauchōmetha en tais thlipsesin … hypomonēn katergazetai … dokimēn … elpis ou kataischynei … ekkechytai — Romans 5:3–5: boasting in tribulation, the chain from endurance to hope, and why “hope does not put to shame” when God’s love is “poured out”

“We also boast in the tribulations” (Romans 5:3). Ou monon de, alla kai kauchōmetha en tais thlipsesin. The last word is thlipsis, the “tribulation” that fell “upon every soul of man who works out evil” in Romans 2:9 and is the first item on the list of things that cannot separate us from the love of Christ in Romans 8:35. Paul boasts in it as the road, not as the destination. ⚠ The apparatus shows a second reading for the verb: Westcott–Hort, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont print kauchōmetha, “we boast”; Tregelles prints the participle kauchōmenoi, “boasting,” which would hang this clause on the verb of verse 2. The ending -ōmetha is itself ambiguous: it is both the indicative, “we boast,” and the subjunctive, “let us boast,” so the same form at verse 2 could be read either way. The shelf takes both: KJV “we glory in tribulations also” and Douay “we glory also in tribulations” use the old verb; ASV “we also rejoice in our tribulations” and Geneva “we rejoice in tribulations” use “rejoice”; NIV “we also glory in our sufferings” uses “glory” here though it used “boast” in verse 2, and changes the noun; NWT 1984 “let us exult while in tribulations” is hortatory; TLB “We can rejoice, too, when we run into problems and trials” turns tribulation into a list of troubles. This translation keeps “boast” for the one Greek verb at verse 2, here and in verse 11, so that the three can be seen to be one.

“Tribulation works out endurance” (hypomonēn katergazetai). The verb is katergazomai, “work out,” the verb of the person “who works out evil” in 2:9 and of the law that “works out wrath” in 4:15. The noun is hypomonē, “endurance,” the staying-power of “those who by endurance in good work seek glory” (2:7) and of the waiting in 8:25, “we wait for it with endurance.” The same verb with the same object stands in James 1:3, to dokimion hymōn tēs pisteōs katergazetai hypomonēn, “the testing of your faith produces endurance” (the earlier chapter of this translation printed “produces”; this one prints “works out” so that katergazomai stays one word through Romans), and James goes straight on to a second link, “let endurance have its full work” (1:4). KJV “tribulation worketh patience,” Douay “tribulation worketh patience” and Geneva “tribulation bringeth forth patience” say “patience”; ASV “tribulation worketh stedfastness” and NIV “suffering produces perseverance” make it firmness; NWT 1984 “tribulation produces endurance” has the word used here. The word “patience” in the older versions reads like waiting; hypomonē is the staying under a load.

“Proven character” (dokimē, Romans 5:4). Hē de hypomonē dokimēn, hē de dokimē elpida. This is the middle link and the one the versions cannot agree on. Dokimē is the state of having been tested and found good, and the noun stands in six verses of the New Testament (dokimē, searched): here, 2 Corinthians 2:9, 8:2, 9:13 and 13:3, and Philippians 2:22 (those five not yet on these pages). Of those, 2 Corinthians 8:2 sets it beside the noun of verse 3, “a great testing of tribulation” (en pollē dokimē thlipseōs). The shelf: KJV “patience, experience; and experience, hope” and Geneva “patience experience, and experience hope” say “experience”; ASV “stedfastness, approvedness; and approvedness, hope” coins an English word for the Greek one; Douay “patience trial; and trial hope” follows the Vulgate, patientia autem probationem probatio vero spem (checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org); NIV “perseverance, character; and character, hope” says “character”; NWT 1984 “an approved condition; the approved condition, in turn, hope” also keeps the sense of passing a test; TLB “strength of character” sits inside a sentence that adds “trust God more” and a hope “strong and steady.” “Proven character” here is a character that has been through the fire and shown what it is made of; “experience” says only that something happened. The whole run is a chain, each link the product of the one before and the seed of the next, and it ends where verse 2 began: elpis, hope.

“Hope does not put to shame” (elpis ou kataischynei). The verb kataischynō is “put to shame,” the shame of expectation that fails. It stands in ten verses of the New Testament (kataischynō, searched), of which this is the first in the letter and Romans 9:33 and 10:11 (those two not yet on these pages) are the next two. The sentence sounds like the Psalms, and I report the likeness without claiming a quotation: in the Hebrew of Psalm 25:3, gam kol-qovekha lo yevoshu, “none who wait for you shall be ashamed,” and the Greek text of that psalm (checked in the Rahlfs word list) reads hoi hypomenontes se ou mē kataischynthōsin, “those who wait on you shall not be put to shame,” with the verb behind hypomonē and the verb Paul uses. A second neighbour is in Joel: the Hebrew sentence “and my people shall never be ashamed” (lo yevoshu ammi) stands in two verses, Joel 2:26 and 2:27, and the next sentence the book speaks is “I will pour out my spirit upon all flesh” (Joel 3:1 in the Hebrew numbering; Joel 2 and 3 not yet on these pages). That is the order of Romans 5:5: not put to shame, then poured out. KJV “hope maketh not ashamed,” ASV “hope putteth not to shame” and Geneva “hope maketh not ashamed” are close; Douay “hope confoundeth not” follows the Latin non confundit; NIV “hope does not put us to shame” adds “us”; NWT 1984 “the hope does not lead to disappointment” changes shame to disappointment, a smaller loss; TLB “we are able to hold our heads high” keeps the face-saving image.

“The love of God has been poured out in our hearts” (ekkechytai). The reason hope cannot fail is not that the future looks good but that something has already happened: hē agapē tou theou ekkechytai en tais kardiais hēmōn dia pneumatos hagiou tou dothentos hēmin. The perfect passive ekkechytai, “has been poured out and is still poured out,” and its participle stand in three verses of the New Testament (ekkechytai, searched): Luke 11:50, of the blood of the prophets, Acts 10:45, where the believers from the circumcision are amazed that “the gift of the Holy Spirit had been poured out even on the nations,” and this verse. The Hebrew first-person verb “I will pour out” (ashpokh) stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: Ezekiel 7:8 and Hosea 5:10 pour out wrath, and Joel 3:1 and 3:2 pour out the spirit. “The love of God” is a genitive that can mean God’s love for us or ours for him; the next three verses decide it, because it is the love God “demonstrates” toward us in verse 8. The Holy Spirit enters the argument of the letter here: this is the first verse to print the two words, with “Spirit of holiness” at Romans 1:4, and the others are 9:1, 14:17, 15:13 and 15:16 (pneumatos hagiou, searched, not yet on these pages). KJV “shed abroad in our hearts by the Holy Ghost” and ASV “shed abroad in our hearts through the Holy Spirit” and Geneva “shed abroad in our hearts by the holy Ghost” use “shed abroad”; Douay “the charity of God is poured forth in our hearts” follows caritas Dei diffusa est; NIV “God’s love has been poured out into our hearts” and NWT 1984 “the love of God has been poured out into our hearts” say “poured out”; TLB “we feel this warm love everywhere within us” adds a feeling the Greek does not name.

Verses 6–8 · ἔτι … ἀσθενῶν ἔτι κατὰ καιρὸν … ὑπὲρ ἀσεβῶν ἀπέθανεν … μόλις … ὑπὲρ τοῦ ἀγαθοῦ … συνίστησιν … ἁμαρτωλῶν eti … asthenōn eti kata kairon … hyper asebōn apethanen … molis … hyper tou agathou … synistēsin … hamartōlōn — Romans 5:6–8: why does verse 6 say “still” twice, who is the “righteous” and the “good” person, and what does God “demonstrate” by Christ dying for sinners?

“For while we were still weak, Christ, still at the right time, died for the ungodly” (Romans 5:6). Eti gar Christos ontōn hēmōn asthenōn eti kata kairon hyper asebōn apethanen. ⚠ The printed Greek has the word “still” (eti) twice, once before the participle “being weak” and once before “at the right time,” and the apparatus divides over both. At the start of the verse, Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont have Eti gar, “For still”; Westcott–Hort have Ei ge, “if indeed,” so that the verse would open as a condition rather than as a statement. At the second place, Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 have eti, and Robinson–Pierpont omit it, which gives the single “still” that most English versions print. This translation prints both, since the text it follows has both. The doubled word presses one point: the death came while we were in the condition, not after we had mended. The Latin shows yet another form: the Vulgate has ut quid enim Christus cum adhuc infirmi essemus secundum tempus pro impiis mortuus est, in which ut quid means “why” or “to what end”; neither Greek reading printed in this apparatus is a question. Douay “For why did Christ, when as yet we were weak” follows the Latin and turns the verse into a question. The rest print one “still,” or none: KJV “when we were yet without strength, in due time” and ASV “while we were yet weak, in due season” have “yet” once; Geneva “when we were yet of no strength, at his time” adds “his”; NIV “at just the right time, when we were still powerless” and TLB “Christ came at just the right time” reorder the clauses; NWT 1984 “while we were yet weak, died for ungodly men at the appointed time” keeps the order but moves the time to the end. The phrase kata kairon could attach to “died” (the right time for the death) or to “weak” (weak at that time); all seven versions named above attach it to the death.

“The ungodly” (asebōn) and the ladder of names. Four names are given in five verses for the people Christ died for: astheneis, “weak” (verse 6), asebeis, “ungodly” (verse 6), hamartōloi, “sinners” (verse 8), and echthroi, “enemies” (verse 10). Each name is harder than the one before: weakness is a condition, ungodliness a stance toward God, sin an act, enmity a war. The adjective asebēs is the word of Romans 4:5, where God is the one who “justifies the ungodly”; so the ungodly person justified there is the ungodly person who is died for here, and the noun asebeia, ungodliness, is what the wrath of Romans 1:18 was revealed against (the adjective stands in Romans only at 4:5 and here; the noun at 1:18 and 11:26, searched, 11:26 not yet on these pages). The preposition is hyper, “on behalf of,” and it is the preposition that the note to John 17:19 pointed ahead to. In the whole chapter hyper stands only in these three verses, four times (hyper, read from the Greek of the chapter): hyper asebōn (verse 6), twice in verse 7 and hyper hēmōn in verse 8. The verb apothnēskō, “die,” goes with it every time, and returns at verse 15 for the many who died in Adam.

⚠ “A righteous person” and “the good person” (Romans 5:7). Molis gar hyper dikaiou tis apothaneitai; hyper gar tou agathou tacha tis kai tolma apothanein. Paul sets two kinds of people against each other and grades the willingness to die for each: scarcely for a dikaios, perhaps, someone dares, for ho agathos. He does not define the pair, and the Greek adds a difficulty: the article stands only before “good,” and each adjective could be masculine, “a righteous man,” or neuter, “a righteous thing.” The adverb molis, “scarcely,” stands in six verses of the New Testament (molis, searched), four of them in Acts (14:18 and the three of Acts 27), and the other two are this verse and 1 Peter 4:18, where the righteous person is saved “with difficulty” (not yet on these pages). On the shelf only ASV “for the good man” and NWT 1984 “for the good [man]” show the article; KJV “for a good man,” Geneva “for a good man,” Douay “for a good man” and NIV “for a good person” do not. TLB “Even if we were good, we really wouldn’t expect anyone to die for us” changes the person who would be died for from another into us, which is not what the verse says. The Latin has vix enim pro iusto quis moritur nam pro bono forsitan quis et audeat mori, with no article either (checked at vulgate.org). This translation says “a righteous person” and “the good person” so that the article is visible, and does not decide whether the second means someone particular or only a type.

“God demonstrates his own love” (synistēsin, Romans 5:8). Synistēsin de tēn heautou agapēn eis hēmas ho theos hoti eti hamartōlōn ontōn hēmōn Christos hyper hēmōn apethanen. The verb synistēmi, literally “stand together,” is “introduce, commend, show to be.” Forms of it (synistēmi and its by-form synistanō) stand in eleven verses of the New Testament (synist-, searched): Romans 3:5, where our unrighteousness “demonstrates God’s righteousness,” this verse, and nine more: Romans 16:1, where Paul recommends Phoebe, seven verses in 2 Corinthians and one in Galatians (those nine not yet on these pages). This translation keeps “demonstrates” at both places in Romans where it is God or God’s righteousness that is shown, so that the two can be put side by side: there God’s righteousness is shown by the contrast with human wrong; here God’s love is shown by an act. The tense is a present, so it is not only that he once showed it. The words “his own” (heautou) say whose love is on trial; this settles the genitive of verse 5 in favor of God’s love for us, and the evidence is that the Son died while the ones he died for were still hamartōloi. NIV “God demonstrates his own love for us” has the verb used here; KJV “God commendeth his love toward us,” ASV “God commendeth his own love toward us” and Douay “God commendeth his charity towards us” use the old “commend,” which first meant “recommend” and now reads as praise, and the Latin has commendat; NWT 1984 “God recommends his own love to us” keeps the older sense; Geneva “God setteth out his love towards us” says “sets out,” as one lays out wares; TLB “God showed his great love for us by sending Christ to die for us” puts it in the past and adds “by sending Christ.”

Verses 9–11 · πολλῷ μᾶλλον … ἐν τῷ αἵματι … ἀπὸ τῆς ὀργῆς … ἐχθροὶ … κατηλλάγημεν … ἐν τῇ ζωῇ … τὴν καταλλαγήν pollō mallon … en tō haimati … apo tēs orgēs … echthroi … katēllagēmen … en tē zōē … tēn katallagēn — Romans 5:9–11: what does “reconciled” mean, why does the KJV say “atonement,” and how are we “saved by his life” after being reconciled by his death?

“Much more, then” (pollō oun mallon, Romans 5:9). The argument of the next three verses is the argument of the whole second half of the chapter, from the harder to the easier: if the hard thing has been done, the easy one follows. The phrase pollō mallon, “much more,” stands in this chapter four times, in verses 9, 10, 15 and 17, and nowhere else in Romans (pollō mallon, searched; verse 9 has oun, “then,” between the two words). Later Jewish teachers had a name for the form, qal va-chomer, “light and heavy” (reported, not checked in a rabbinic text). Here the heavy is first: justified, reconciled, even though we were ungodly and enemies; the light is what remains, being saved. The verb of verse 9 is again dikaioō, in the participle that opened verse 1, and the sphere is en tō haimati autou, “in his blood,” the preposition and the noun of Romans 3:25 (“through faith, in his blood”). All seven versions on the shelf print “by”: KJV “being now justified by his blood,” ASV “being now justified by his blood,” Geneva “being now justified by his blood,” Douay “being now justified by his blood,” NIV “justified by his blood,” NWT 1984 “declared righteous now by his blood” and TLB “by his blood he did all this for us.” “In” keeps the Greek preposition and does not choose between “by means of” and “within the sphere of.” The object of the salvation is apo tēs orgēs, “from the wrath,” with the article: the wrath of Romans 1:18 that is “revealed from heaven,” and the “day of wrath” that Romans 2:5 says is being stored up, and the “wrath that is coming” from which Jesus rescues us in 1 Thessalonians 1:10. ASV “from the wrath of God” and NIV “from God’s wrath” say whose; TLB “all of God’s wrath to come” also says when; the rest print a bare “from wrath.” The verb “shall be saved” is a future, which makes the salvation of this verse the one still ahead of those already justified.

“Enemies” and “reconciled” (echthroi, katēllagēmen, Romans 5:10). Ei gar echthroi ontes katēllagēmen tō theō dia tou thanatou tou huiou autou, pollō mallon katallagentes sōthēsometha en tē zōē autou. The noun echthros stands in Romans at 5:10, 11:28 and 12:20 (echthr-, searched; the related noun echthra, “hostility,” is at 8:7: “the mind of the flesh is hostility toward God”), so the word can mean those who are hostile to God, which is the natural reading beside 8:7, and the ones God is hostile to, which Romans 11:28 (“enemies for your sake,” yet “beloved” because of the fathers; not yet on these pages) permits; this verse does not say which. The verb is katallassō, “reconcile,” and the passive matters: we were reconciled to God, and no verse of this chapter says God was reconciled to us. The family (katallassō, katallagē, and the compound apokatallassō) stands in ten verses of the New Testament (katall-, searched), all in letters that bear Paul’s name: here and in verse 11, Romans 11:15, 1 Corinthians 7:11 and 2 Corinthians 5:18–20 (those five verses not yet on these pages), and the compound at Ephesians 2:16, Colossians 1:20 and 1:22. Colossians has the pair of this verse in two consecutive verses: “enemies in your mind” (1:21) and “now he has reconciled you in the body of his flesh through death” (1:22). At 1 Corinthians 7:11 the same verb is used of a wife reconciled to her husband (not yet on these pages), the one place where the two parties are two people and not God and humanity. KJV “when we were enemies, we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son,” Geneva “we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son” and Douay “we were reconciled to God by the death of his Son” say “by”; ASV “we were reconciled to God through the death of his Son,” NIV “we were reconciled to him through the death of his Son” and NWT 1984 “we became reconciled to God through the death of his Son” say “through,” the preposition of the Greek (dia); TLB “we were brought back to God by the death of his Son” renders the verb as a return. NIV’s “God’s enemies” and TLB’s “his enemies” supply a possessive that settles the question the Greek leaves open.

“Saved in his life” (en tē zōē autou). The death reconciles; the life saves. The preposition is again en, “in,” and the life is not any of the ordinary lives of the letter but the life of the one who “was raised because of our justification” (4:25) and who is “at God’s right hand, who also pleads for us” (8:34). Six versions put it as an instrument: KJV “we shall be saved by his life,” ASV “shall we be saved by his life,” Geneva “we shall be saved by his life,” Douay “shall we be saved by his life,” NIV “shall we be saved through his life” and NWT 1984 “we shall be saved by his life.” TLB “he is living within us” puts the life inside the believer, which is a sense the Greek en can bear and the verse does not state. This translation prints “in his life” so that the reader has the same open question the Greek has.

⚠ “The reconciliation” and the KJV’s “atonement” (tēn katallagēn, Romans 5:11). Ou monon de, alla kai kauchōmenoi en tō theō dia tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou, di’ hou nun tēn katallagēn elabomen. The noun is katallagē, with the article: “the reconciliation.” KJV “we have now received the atonement” and Geneva “we have now received the atonement” print the old English word, which was built from “at one” and meant bringing two parties to be at one. In the KJV this is the only verse of the New Testament that prints “atonement” (searched in a full KJV text; every other verse that prints it is in the Old Testament). In current usage “atonement” mostly points to the Day of Atonement and the covering of sin, and this translation prints “a place of atonement” at Romans 3:25 for hilastērion, so using it here would blur two different Greek words. ASV “we have now received the reconciliation,” NIV “we have now received reconciliation,” Douay “we have now received reconciliation” and NWT 1984 “we have now received the reconciliation” use the plain word, as the Vulgate does (reconciliationem accepimus); TLB “making us friends of God” keeps the sense in a paraphrase. The third and last boast of the chapter is here, and the object has moved again: first “hope of the glory” (verse 2), then the tribulations (verse 3), and now God himself, “boasting in God,” the object that 1 Corinthians 1:31 gives to the one who boasts (“boast in Jehovah”). KJV “we also joy in God,” ASV “we also rejoice in God,” NIV “we also boast in God,” Douay “also we glory in God” and NWT 1984 “we are also exulting in God” give the participle five English verbs. The phrase “through our Lord Jesus Christ” closes the section as it opened it: verse 1 ends with the words dia tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou, verse 11 has them again in the same order, and verse 21 closes the chapter with them in a different order.

Verse 12 · διὰ τοῦτο ὥσπερ … διʼ ἑνὸς ἀνθρώπου ἡ ἁμαρτία εἰς τὸν κόσμον εἰσῆλθεν … ὁ θάνατος διῆλθεν … ἐφʼ ᾧ πάντες ἥμαρτον dia touto hōsper … di’ henos anthrōpou hē hamartia eis ton kosmon eisēlthen … ho thanatos diēlthen … eph’ hō pantes hēmarton — Romans 5:12: “because all sinned” or “in whom all sinned”? The sentence Paul starts with “just as” and does not finish

“For this reason, just as…” (Romans 5:12). Dia touto hōsper di’ henos anthrōpou hē hamartia eis ton kosmon eisēlthen kai dia tēs hamartias ho thanatos. “For this reason” looks back over verses 1–11: because the reconciliation has come through one man, Christ, the comparison with another one man can now be made. ⚠ The comparison is not completed. Hōsper, “just as,” opens a sentence that needs a “so also” (houtōs kai), and the kai houtōs that does come in this verse, “and so death spread,” belongs to the first half. The sentence turns at the last two words, eph’ hō pantes hēmarton, and stops where the printed Greek has a dash. The matching “so also” comes at verse 18 (hōs… houtōs kai), and again at 19 and 21; the verses between are the interruption. This is how the shelf marks it. the KJV opens a bracket at the start of verse 13 and closes it only at the end of verse 17, with “Therefore” resuming at 18; ASV “for that all sinned:--” and NIV “because all sinned —” break off with a dash; NWT 1984 “because they had all sinned—.” prints a dash and then a period; Geneva “in whom all men have sinned” and Douay “in whom all have sinned” end with a full stop as if the sentence were whole; TLB “When Adam sinned, sin entered the entire human race” recasts the sentence and names Adam, whom Paul names only at verse 14.

“Sin entered into the world, and through sin death” (eisēlthen, diēlthen). Sin and death are subjects of verbs of motion: sin entered, death spread (diēlthen, “passed through”), and in verses 14, 17 and 21 death and sin reign (the verb is counted in the note to verses 13–14). The verb diēlthen stands in seven verses of the New Testament (diēlth-, searched), and in the six others it is used of someone making a way through a place: Acts 8:4, 10:38, 11:19, 16:6 and 20:25, and 1 Corinthians 10:1 (not yet on these pages); here death is the traveler. The world is kosmos; the one man is unnamed in this verse. KJV “death passed upon all men,” Douay “death passed upon all men,” ASV “death passed unto all men,” Geneva “death went over all men,” NIV “death came to all people” and NWT 1984 “death spread to all men” choose among “passed,” “went over,” “came to” and “spread”; this translation prints “spread,” with dia in the verb saying it went through the whole body of people. The people are anthrōpoi, which is why “all people” is printed here and at verse 18, against “one man” (henos anthrōpou) in the same verse and the next.

⚠ “Because” or “in whom” (eph’ hō). The last clause is two Greek words, a preposition epi and a relative pronoun hō in the dative, and the pair can be read as “because,” “on the ground that,” or as “in whom” or “on which.” They stand together in five verses of the New Testament (eph’ hō, searched): here, Acts 7:33, where it is the place “on which you are standing,” 2 Corinthians 5:4, Philippians 3:12 (those two not yet on these pages) and Philippians 4:10, where this translation prints “you were thinking of it all along,” the thing “about which” they were also thinking. The pair is a relative phrase and its meaning is taken from the sentence around it, and in this sentence the grammar allows the readings that have been argued. The Latin put the words into in quo omnes peccaverunt (checked in the Clementine text at vulgate.org), which can be read “in whom all sinned,” that is, in the one man; Douay “in whom all have sinned” and Geneva “in whom all men have sinned” follow it. The other five say “because” or its older twin: KJV “for that all have sinned,” ASV “for that all sinned,” NIV “because all sinned,” NWT 1984 “because they had all sinned” and TLB “for all sinned.” Two things about the Greek can be said without deciding. The verb hēmarton is the third-person plural aorist, “they sinned,” and in the New Testament the form stands in seven verses (hēmarton, searched), four of them “I sinned” (Matthew 27:4, Luke 15:18 and 15:21, Acts 25:8); the three that say “they” are all in Romans and all about everyone: 2:12 (“as many as sinned without law… as many as sinned under law”), 3:23 (“all sinned and fall short of the glory of God”) and this verse. And the aorist does not say whether the sinning was one act or many, or whether each person’s sin was that person’s own. This translation prints “because all sinned” because that is the most open of the readings, and leaves the dash to carry the break.

Verses 13–14 · ἄχρι γὰρ νόμου … ἐλλογεῖται … ἐβασίλευσεν ὁ θάνατος ἀπὸ Ἀδὰμ μέχρι Μωϋσέως … ὁμοιώματι τῆς παραβάσεως … τύπος τοῦ μέλλοντος achri gar nomou … ellogeitai … ebasileusen ho thanatos apo Adam mechri Mōuseōs … homoiōmati tēs parabaseōs … typos tou mellontos — Romans 5:13–14: how can sin be “in the world” but “not entered to an account,” why did death reign from Adam to Moses, and in what sense is Adam a “type” of the one to come?

“Until law, sin was in the world” (Romans 5:13). Achri gar nomou hamartia ēn en kosmō, hamartia de ouk ellogeitai mē ontos nomou. The first word of the explanation is gar, “for”: verses 13–14 give the ground for verse 12, and the ground is that sin and death were there before the Law, so that both cannot have begun with it. “Law” has no article, as in Romans 4:15 (“where there is no law, neither is there transgression”); the time frame here, “until law,” and in verse 14, “until Moses,” points to the law given through Moses. KJV “until the law,” ASV “until the law,” Douay “until the law” and NWT 1984 “until the Law” keep the word; Geneva “unto the time of the Law” and NIV “before the law was given” make the time explicit. The verb is ellogeō, “enter to an account”: a bookkeeper’s verb, and the only other verse of the New Testament where it stands is Philemon 18, “charge it to my account” (Philemon 18; ellog-, searched). It belongs to the ledger language of chapter 4, where logizomai, “reckon,” is the verb of eleven verses; there the entry is made by God’s decision (“the man whose sin Jehovah will never reckon,” 4:8), and here no entry is made because there is no law to measure against. KJV “sin is not imputed when there is no law,” ASV “sin is not imputed when there is no law” and Geneva “sin is not imputed, while there is no law” use “imputed”; Douay “sin was not imputed, when the law was not” puts it in the past; NIV “sin is not charged against anyone’s account where there is no law” and NWT 1984 “sin is not charged against anyone when there is no law” use “charged,” and NIV keeps the “account.” TLB “God did not in those days judge them guilty of death for breaking his laws” turns a statement about an account into a statement about God’s judging.

⚠ The two verses pull against each other, and Paul does not reconcile them in this sentence. Verse 13 says that where there is no law, sin is not entered to an account. Verse 14 says that death nonetheless reigned from Adam to Moses, even over people whose sin was unlike Adam’s. If death is the penalty entered in an account, it should not have fallen where nothing was entered; and it did. The verses leave several readings open: that death reigned because of Adam’s one act; that death reigned because the others sinned (verse 12), in a way that was real but was not the breach of a stated command; or that both are true together. The page gives the evidence and does not vote. Adam’s line was drawn in words: the command of Genesis 2:17, “from the tree of the knowledge of good and evil, you must not eat, for on the day you eat from it, you will surely die,” a line the Greek word parabasis, “crossing,” names. Genesis 5 lists ten generations from Adam to Noah, and eight of its entries close with the same sentence, wayyamot, “and he died,” which stands in eight verses of the chapter: Adam (5:5), Seth, Enosh, Kenan, Mahalalel, Jared, Methuselah and Lamech (5:8, 11, 14, 17, 20, 27 and 31). The entry for Enoch says instead that “he was not there, for God took him” (5:24), and Noah’s entry has no notice of death in this chapter. I offer the list as a place to look; Paul does not cite it.

“Death reigned” (ebasileusen ho thanatos, Romans 5:14). The verb basileuō, “reign,” stands five times in this chapter, in three verses, and each time its subject differs: death in verses 14 and 17, those who receive the abundance of grace in verse 17, and in verse 21 sin and then grace (basileu-, read from the Greek of the chapter). The same verb is printed “reigning” of Archelaus in Matthew 2:22. Death is not a state in this chapter but a ruler with a territory and a term, “from Adam until Moses,” and the chapter ends with a second ruler on the same throne. KJV “death reigned from Adam to Moses,” ASV “death reigned from Adam until Moses,” Geneva “death reigned from Adam to Moses,” Douay “death reigned from Adam unto Moses” and NIV “death reigned from the time of Adam to the time of Moses” use “reigned”; NWT 1984 “death ruled as king from Adam down to Moses” adds “as king” to make the noun inside the verb visible; TLB “So when their bodies died it was not for their own sins” has no ruler at all.

“In the likeness of Adam’s transgression” and “a type of the one who was to come.” The noun homoiōma, “likeness,” is the made thing that matches another, a copy or a form; it stands in six verses of the New Testament (homoiōma, searched): Romans 1:23 (“a likeness of an image of corruptible man”), this verse, 6:5, Romans 8:3 (“in the likeness of sinful flesh”), Philippians 2:7 (not yet on these pages) and Revelation 9:7 (“their faces were like the faces of men”). Those who “had not sinned in the likeness of Adam’s transgression” did sin (verse 12) but did not do what he did, break a command spoken to them. KJV “after the similitude of Adam’s transgression” and Douay “after the similitude of the transgression of Adam” follow the Vulgate’s in similitudinem praevaricationis Adae (checked at vulgate.org); ASV “after the likeness of Adam’s transgression” and NWT 1984 “after the likeness of the transgression by Adam” say “likeness”; Geneva “after the like manner of the transgression of Adam” says “manner”; NIV “by breaking a command, as did Adam” and TLB “had never disobeyed God’s special law against eating the forbidden fruit, as Adam had” replace the likeness with an explanation. The last clause, hos estin typos tou mellontos, calls Adam the typos of “the one about to come” (tou mellontos): a mark made by a blow, and so a model. The Latin has forma futuri. The shelf has “figure” in four: KJV “the figure of him that was to come,” ASV “a figure of him that was to come,” Geneva “the figure of him that was to come” and Douay “a figure of him who was to come”; NIV “a pattern of the one to come” says “pattern”; NWT 1984 “bears a resemblance to him that was to come” turns the noun into a verb; TLB “What a contrast between Adam and Christ” reads the likeness as a contrast, and the next three verses show that the contrast is also in the text: the chapter does not stop at the likeness (“one man, one act, all affected”) but goes on to “not as the trespass, so also the gracious gift.” This translation prints “type,” the English word typos gave rise to, and leaves the likeness and the contrast for the reader to see in verses 15–19.

Verses 15–17 · οὐχ ὡς τὸ παράπτωμα, οὕτως καὶ τὸ χάρισμα … ἡ δωρεὰ … τὸ δώρημα … κρίμα … κατάκριμα … δικαίωμα … οἱ λαμβάνοντες … βασιλεύσουσιν ouch hōs to paraptōma, houtōs kai to charisma … hē dōrea … to dōrēma … krima … katakrima … dikaiōma … hoi lambanontes … basileusousin — Romans 5:15–17: charis, charisma, dōrea, dōrēma — why does Paul use four words for “gift,” and how is the gift “not like the trespass”?

“But the gracious gift is not like the trespass” (Romans 5:15). All’ ouch hōs to paraptōma, houtōs kai to charisma. After the “just as” of verse 12 the chapter turns with a “but not,” and the next three verses are a run of differences laid over the likeness of verse 14. The difference is in amount (“much more,” verses 15 and 17), in direction (verse 16: one thing leads to condemnation, a gift from many things to a verdict of righteousness) and in what is received. The word for “trespass” is paraptōma, a false step or fall, the noun of Romans 4:25 (“handed over because of our trespasses”); it stands in seventeen verses of the New Testament (paraptōm-, searched), five of them in this chapter (verses 15 to 18 and 20), and it is the word the chapter uses to name Adam’s one act. KJV “But not as the offence, so also is the free gift,” ASV “But not as the trespass, so also is the free gift” and Douay “But not as the offence, so also the gift” keep the Greek’s abrupt shape; NIV “But the gift is not like the trespass” and NWT 1984 “But it is not with the gift as it was with the trespass” smooth it into a plain statement; Geneva “But yet the gift is not so as is the offense” adds a “yet”; TLB “And what a difference between man’s sin and God’s forgiveness!” turns the sentence into an exclamation and replaces the gift with forgiveness.

⚠ Four words for gift and grace, and none of the seven versions on the shelf keeps all four apart. In verses 15 to 17 the Greek uses charis, “grace” (three times), charisma, “gracious gift” (verses 15 and 16), dōrea, “free gift” (verses 15 and 17) and dōrēma, “gift” (verse 16, once): eight uses in three verses. They are not synonyms by accident. Charisma is the word of the spiritual gift Paul wants to share at Romans 1:11 and the “gracious gift” of 2 Corinthians 1:11, the thing given freely because of grace; dōrea is the word of the “gift of the Holy Spirit” in Acts 10:45, the giving itself; and the noun dōrēma stands in two verses of the New Testament, this one and James 1:17, “every good giving and every perfect gift” (dōrēma, searched; a verb of the same root stands in 2 Peter 1:3 and is not counted). The shelf merges them in different places. KJV and ASV use “free gift” for charisma (KJV “the free gift is of many offences”; ASV “the free gift came of many trespasses”) and plain “gift” for the other two; NWT 1984 goes the other way, “the gift resulted from many trespasses” for charisma and “free gift” for the other two; NIV “the gift followed many trespasses” and Geneva “the gift is of many offenses” use “gift” for all three; Douay “grace is of many offences” calls charisma “grace” in verse 16 (the Vulgate has gratia there); TLB “Christ freely takes away many sins” drops the nouns. This translation prints “grace,” “gracious gift,” “free gift” and “gift”, so that the reader can see Paul reaching for a fourth word and not repeating the third.

“From one… to condemnation; from many trespasses to a righteous verdict” (Romans 5:16). To men gar krima ex henos eis katakrima, to de charisma ek pollōn paraptōmatōn eis dikaiōma. Two things are paired. The krima is the judgment, and katakrima is the sentence against; the noun katakrima stands in three verses of the New Testament (katakrim-, searched): verses 16 and 18 of this chapter and Romans 8:1, “There is therefore now no condemnation for those in Christ Jesus.” Against it stands dikaiōma, which this translation prints as “righteous verdict” here, as the answer to the sentence, and as “righteous act” in verse 18, where the word stands against a single trespass. Romans uses the noun for the “righteous decree” God has laid down at 1:32, for the “requirements of the law” at 2:26 and 8:4, and, in this chapter, for the answer to the sentence and for the single act; it stands in ten verses of the New Testament (dikaiōma, read from the hits: Luke 1:6, Romans 1:32, 2:26, 5:16, 5:18, 8:4, Hebrews 9:1 and 9:10, Revelation 15:4 and 19:8). The words “from one” are ex henos, masculine or neuter, one man or one trespass, and the sentence does not choose; the “one who sinned” just before it is masculine. KJV “the judgment was by one to condemnation,” Douay “judgment indeed was by one unto condemnation” and ASV “the judgment came of one unto condemnation” leave it open; NIV “The judgment followed one sin and brought condemnation” and NWT 1984 “the judgment resulted from one trespass in condemnation” choose the trespass; TLB “Adam’s one sin brought the penalty of death to many” chooses the man and changes the sentence to a penalty of death. For the other half, KJV “unto justification,” ASV “unto justification,” NIV “brought justification” and Douay “unto justification” print “justification,” the Vulgate’s in iustificationem, which in Greek is a different noun, dikaiōsis, printed at verse 18; NWT 1984 “a declaration of righteousness” keeps the sense of a verdict.

“Those who receive… will reign in life” (hoi lambanontes, basileusousin, Romans 5:17). Verse 17 repeats verse 15’s “much more” and turns the verb of verse 14 around: death reigned through the one, and those who receive the gift will reign in life through the one. The receivers are hoi lambanontes, a present participle, “the ones who are receiving,” so the reign belongs to people who are in the act of taking what is given, and what they take is “the abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness”: righteousness here is a gift, not the wage of Romans 4:4. Their reign is in the future tense, and in life: the same thing the closing scene of Revelation says, “they will reign forever and ever” (Revelation 22:5). The subject who reigns has changed from death to persons. And the “one man, Jesus Christ” of verse 15 is a surprise in its own right: Romans calls Jesus anthrōpos here and nowhere else (searched: no other verse of the letter has the word beside his name). KJV “they which receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness shall reign in life by one, Jesus Christ” and ASV “they that receive the abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness reign in life through the one, even Jesus Christ” have the shape of the Greek; NIV “God’s abundant provision of grace and of the gift of righteousness” adds “provision”; Douay “abundance of grace, and of the gift, and of justice” has “justice” for dikaiosynē; NWT 1984 “the free gift of righteousness rule as kings in life” adds “as kings” again; TLB “all who will take God’s gift of forgiveness and acquittal are kings of life” makes the gift forgiveness and acquittal and the life a kingdom.

Verses 18–19 · ἄρα οὖν ὡς διʼ ἑνὸς παραπτώματος εἰς πάντας ἀνθρώπους … διʼ ἑνὸς δικαιώματος … δικαίωσιν ζωῆς … παρακοῆς … ἁμαρτωλοὶ κατεστάθησαν οἱ πολλοί … ὑπακοῆς … δίκαιοι κατασταθήσονται ara oun hōs di’ henos paraptōmatos eis pantas anthrōpous … di’ henos dikaiōmatos … dikaiōsin zōēs … parakoēs … hamartōloi katestathēsan hoi polloi … hypakoēs … dikaioi katastathēsontai — Romans 5:18–19: “all people” and “the many,” “one righteous act,” and what it means to be “made” sinners and “made” righteous

“So then, as… so also…” (Romans 5:18). Ara oun hōs di’ henos paraptōmatos eis pantas anthrōpous eis katakrima, houtōs kai di’ henos dikaiōmatos eis pantas anthrōpous eis dikaiōsin zōēs. This is the sentence verse 12 began: the hōs… houtōs kai, “as… so also,” that was promised and held back through verses 13–17. It has no verb. The Greek says only “as through one trespass, to all people, to condemnation; so also through one righteous act, to all people, to justification of life,” and the reader supplies the movement. This translation leaves the verb out, as the Greek does. The one grammatical hinge is the phrase eis pantas anthrōpous, “to all people,” which stands in only two verses of the New Testament (eis pantas anthrōpous, searched): verse 12, where death spread to all people, and this verse, where it occurs twice, once for condemnation and once for justification. Of the two words that end the verse, dikaiōsis is the noun of Romans 4:25 (“raised because of our justification”), found in only those two verses of the New Testament, and zōēs is a genitive: “justification of life” can mean a justification that leads to life or one that consists in life, and this translation keeps the genitive. “One righteous act” is the dikaiōma of verse 16, set against one trespass, so here it is an act, one thing done; the shelf is mixed: KJV “by the righteousness of one” and Douay “by the justice of one” print a quality, ASV “through one act of righteousness”, NIV “one righteous act” and NWT 1984 “through one act of justification” print an act, Geneva “by the justifying of one” prints an event, and TLB “Christ’s righteousness makes men right with God” prints a person and a verb. Douay “unto all men to condemnation” is verbless like the Greek; the other versions add “came,” “resulted,” “brought” or “was.”

⚠ “All people” and “the many.” The chapter uses both words of the same crowd. Death “spread to all people” (verse 12), and the next verse that says who died says hoi polloi, “the many,” died (verse 15); so in this chapter “the many” is not a smaller group than “all.” The phrase hoi polloi stands four times in two verses, 15 and 19 (hoi polloi, read from the Greek of the chapter). It echoes the servant of Isaiah, where “my servant, a righteous one, will bring righteousness to many” (Isaiah 53:11) and the same chapter says he “bore the sin of many” (53:12); the Greek of the Septuagint at 53:11 reads dikaiōsai dikaion eu douleuonta pollois (checked in the Rahlfs word list), with the verb dikaioō and the word polloi that Paul uses in verses 15 and 19. I report the likeness and do not claim a citation. What the verses do not settle is whether the second “all people” of verse 18 means every individual. Evidence on the page: the Greek has the same words twice, in the same pattern, for condemnation and for justification; verse 17 limits the reign to “those who receive” the abundance of grace; and verse 19 puts the second half in the future, “will be made righteous.” Six versions on the shelf print the phrase as “all men,” “all people” or “all,” KJV “upon all men to condemnation,” ASV “unto all men to condemnation,” Geneva “on all men to condemnation,” Douay “unto all men to condemnation,” NIV “condemnation for all people” and TLB “Adam’s sin brought punishment to all,” and the seventh, NWT 1984 “the result to men of all sorts was condemnation,” prints “men of all sorts” twice in the verse. That is a reading, not a rendering: it takes the “all” as kinds of people, not every person. The Greek words are pantas anthrōpous, and this translation prints “all people” in verses 12 and 18 and leaves the question where the Greek leaves it.

“Disobedience” and “obedience” (parakoē, hypakoē, Romans 5:19). Hōsper gar dia tēs parakoēs tou henos anthrōpou hamartōloi katestathēsan hoi polloi, houtōs kai dia tēs hypakoēs tou henos dikaioi katastathēsontai hoi polloi. The two nouns are a pair built on the same verb of hearing, akouō: parakoē, “hearing aside,” a failure to listen, and hypakoē, “hearing under,” listening and submitting. Parakoē stands in three verses of the New Testament (parako-, searched; the same letters also match verbs, so the count is read from the hits: Romans 5:19, 2 Corinthians 10:6 and Hebrews 2:2); hypakoē is the word of Romans 1:5, the “obedience of faith among all the nations” that Paul says he received an apostleship to bring about. The verb in each half is the same, kathistēmi: “were made” (aorist) and “will be made” (future). It is a verb of standing and position: Acts prints it “appointed him governor over Egypt” (Acts 7:10) and “Who appointed you ruler and judge over us?” (7:27). Whether to be “made” a sinner or righteous here means a change of nature or being set in a standing, the verse does not say. KJV “by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners,” Geneva “by one man’s disobedience many were made sinners” and Douay “by the disobedience of one man, many were made sinners” omit the article before “many”; only ASV “the many were made sinners” and NIV “the many were made sinners” keep it; NWT 1984 “many were constituted sinners” has the verb closest to the Greek’s sense of placing; TLB “Adam caused many to be sinners because he disobeyed God” makes Adam the agent of a causing and adds a reason. The Vulgate has peccatores constituti sunt multi and iusti constituentur multi (checked at vulgate.org), the same verb in both halves. This translation prints “the many” and “made” in both, so that the reader hears the two halves as a matched pair.

Verses 20–21 · νόμος δὲ παρεισῆλθεν ἵνα πλεονάσῃ τὸ παράπτωμα … ὑπερεπερίσσευσεν ἡ χάρις … ἐβασίλευσεν ἡ ἁμαρτία ἐν τῷ θανάτῳ … ἡ χάρις βασιλεύσῃ διὰ δικαιοσύνης εἰς ζωὴν αἰώνιον nomos de pareisēlthen hina pleonasē to paraptōma … hypereperisseusen hē charis … ebasileusen hē hamartia en tō thanatō … hē charis basileusē dia dikaiosynēs eis zōēn aiōnion — Romans 5:20–21: why did the law “come in alongside” so that the trespass “might increase,” and how does grace “reign through righteousness to eternal life”?

“And law came in alongside” (pareisēlthen, Romans 5:20). Nomos de pareisēlthen hina pleonasē to paraptōma. The verb is pareiserchomai, “come in alongside,” built on the verb of verse 12: sin entered (eisēlthen) and law came in beside (pareisēlthen), a second arrival, not the first. The compound stands in two verses of the New Testament (pareisēlth-, searched): here, and Galatians 2:4, where the people who pareisēlthon “to spy out our freedom” slipped in alongside (not yet on these pages). The prefix para puts the law beside a road already there, which fits the earlier verses of the chapter: law was not what began sin and death (verses 13–14), and here it is what makes the trespass larger. ASV “the law came in besides” and NWT 1984 “the Law came in beside” keep the “beside”; KJV “the law entered,” Douay “the law entered in” and Geneva “the Law entered thereupon” lose the prefix; NIV “The law was brought in” makes it passive, with someone doing the bringing, which the Greek does not say; TLB “The Ten Commandments were given” names the Ten Commandments, a restriction the Greek (nomos, “law”) does not make.

⚠ “So that the trespass might increase” (hina pleonasē). The conjunction hina usually states a purpose, “in order that,” and it can also state a result, “so that,” and the difficulty of the verse is that the English has to choose. If it is a purpose, the law came in with the aim that the trespass would increase; if it is a result, the law came in and the trespass increased as a consequence, the aim lying elsewhere. The shelf: KJV “that the offence might abound,” ASV “that the trespass might abound,” Geneva “that the offense should abound” and Douay “that sin might abound” use the old “that” and leave it open; NIV “so that the trespass might increase” chooses the sound of a result; NWT 1984 “in order that trespassing might abound” chooses the purpose; TLB “so that all could see the extent of their failure to obey God’s laws” supplies a third reading, in which the law makes the trespass visible and does not make it larger. The page prints “so that” and does not decide among them. Elsewhere Romans has said what law does: “through law comes the recognition of sin” (3:20), and “where there is no law, neither is there transgression” (4:15). The verb is pleonazō, “be more, increase,” and it stands in this verse twice, pleonasē and epleonasen, “the trespass might increase” and “sin increased,” and it comes back in the first words of the next chapter: “Shall we remain in sin, so that grace may increase?” (Romans 6:1, pleonaz-, searched). The objection of chapter 6 is built from this verse.

“Grace abounded far more” (hypereperisseusen). The verb is hyperperisseuō, in the aorist with its augment in the middle, hypereperisseusen: perisseuō, “abound,” the verb of verse 15 (“the grace of God… abounded to the many”), with hyper, “beyond,” in front of it. It is a double step: verse 15 says grace abounded, and verse 20 says that where the trespass increased it abounded beyond. In the New Testament the verb stands in two verses, here and 2 Corinthians 7:4 (hyperperiss-, searched, with a third hit at Mark 7:37 that is an adverb; 2 Corinthians 7:4 not yet on these pages). KJV “grace did much more abound” and Douay “grace did more abound” spend an adverb on the prefix; ASV “grace did abound more exceedingly” and Geneva “grace abounded much more” do the same; NIV “grace increased all the more” says “increased”, the verb of the first half of the verse, and so loses the distinction between the two Greek verbs; NWT 1984 “undeserved kindness abounded still more” keeps “abounded” for this one; TLB “the more we see God’s abounding grace forgiving us” turns it into something seen. This translation keeps “increase” for pleonazō and “abound” for perisseuō, so that the two verbs of the verse do not become one.

“Sin reigned in death… grace might reign through righteousness to eternal life” (Romans 5:21). Hina hōsper ebasileusen hē hamartia en tō thanatō, houtōs kai hē charis basileusē dia dikaiosynēs eis zōēn aiōnion dia Iēsou Christou tou kyriou hēmōn. The last verse sets the two reigns against each other, using the verb that had death for its subject in verse 14 and persons for its subject in verse 17: sin reigned, and grace might reign. The first reign is “in death,” en tō thanatō, the sphere in which sin ruled; the second is “through righteousness,” the way grace rules, to a destination, “eternal life,” the same two words as at Romans 2:7. The shelf divides over the little word en. ASV “as sin reigned in death” and NIV “sin reigned in death” keep “in”; KJV “as sin hath reigned unto death,” Geneva “as sin had reigned unto death” and Douay “as sin hath reigned to death” make death the destination, though the Latin they follow has regnavit peccatum in morte (checked at vulgate.org); NWT 1984 “sin ruled as king with death” makes death a partner; TLB “sin ruled over all men and brought them to death” makes it the result. The chapter ends on the phrase it began with: dia tou kyriou hēmōn Iēsou Christou stands at verse 1 and verse 11, and the last words of the chapter are the same phrase with the names first, “through Jesus Christ our Lord.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “boast” for kauchaomai in all three verses (vv. 2, 3 and 11), as at Romans 2:17 and 2:23, with the noun at 3:27 and 4:2; “justified” for dikaioō (v. 1, v. 9), as at 3:24, 3:28 and 4:2; “endurance” for hypomonē (vv. 3, 4), as at 2:7 and 8:25; “works out” for katergazomai (v. 3), as at 1:27, 2:9 and 4:15; “tribulation” for thlipsis (v. 3), as at 2:9 and 8:35; “wrath” for orgē (v. 9), as at 1:18, 2:5 and 4:15; “ungodly” for asebēs (v. 6), as at 4:5; “demonstrates” for synistēmi (v. 8), as at 3:5; “reconciled” for katallassō (vv. 10, 11), as the compound is “reconcile” at Ephesians 2:16 and Colossians 1:20; “trespass” for paraptōma (vv. 15–18 and 20), as at 4:25, against “transgression” for parabasis (v. 14), as at 2:23 and 4:15; “reign” for basileuō (vv. 14, 17 and 21), as at Matthew 2:22 and Revelation 22:5; “justification” for dikaiōsis (v. 18), as at 4:25; “eternal life” (v. 21), as at 2:7; “Holy Spirit” (v. 5) against “Spirit of holiness” at 1:4; “all people” for pantas anthrōpous (vv. 12, 18) against “one man” for henos anthrōpou (vv. 12, 15, 19); four English words for the four Greek words for grace and gift in vv. 15–17 (“grace,” “gracious gift” as at 2 Corinthians 1:11, “free gift,” “gift”); and “increase” for pleonazō against “abound” for perisseuō (v. 20, v. 15), as perisseuō is “abounded” at 3:7. One pair is split on purpose: dikaiōma is “righteous verdict” at verse 16 and “righteous act” at verse 18, against “righteous decree” at 1:32 and “requirements” at 2:26; the note to verses 15–17 gives the reason.

Echoes paid. Boasting shut out (3:27) and denied to Abraham “before God” (4:2) — returned in a new object at vv. 2, 3 and 11. “Raised because of our justification” (4:25) — v. 1 and v. 9. The one who justifies the ungodly (4:5) — the ungodly Christ died for, v. 6. Wrath (1:18, 2:5) — the wrath we are saved from, v. 9. “In his blood” and the demonstration of God’s righteousness (3:25, 26) — v. 9 and the demonstration of his love, v. 8. Grace (3:24, 4:16) — the place we stand, v. 2, and the reign of v. 21. Hope against hope (4:18) — vv. 2–5. Endurance and tribulation (2:7, 9) — the chain of vv. 3–4. “All sinned” (3:23, 2:12) — v. 12. Law and the recognition of sin (3:20) and no transgression without law (4:15) — vv. 13 and 20. The ledger verb (4:3–24) — ellogeō, v. 13. The spiritual gift Paul wanted to share (1:11) — charisma, vv. 15–16. “The obedience of faith” (1:5) — v. 19.

Echoes planted. “There is therefore now no condemnation” (8:1) answers the katakrima of vv. 16 and 18. Hope and endurance are taken up at 8:24–25, tribulation at 8:35, and the love of God poured out in v. 5 reaches its end at 8:39, “the love of God that is in Christ Jesus our Lord.” “The likeness” of v. 14 returns at 8:3, “in the likeness of sinful flesh.” Heirs and glory come back at 8:17 and 8:18, the “glory about to be revealed to us” being the hope of v. 2. The question the next chapter opens with is built on v. 20, “Shall we remain in sin, so that grace may increase?” (Romans 6:1), and the reign of sin comes back there at 6:12; the charisma of vv. 15–16 closes chapter 6 at 6:23, set against “wages.” Reconciliation returns at Romans 11:15 and 2 Corinthians 5:18–20 (those two not yet on these pages), and the comparison of two men at 1 Corinthians 15:21–22 and 15:45 (those two not yet on these pages).

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Whether verse 1 says “we have peace” or “let us have peace”; whether the double “still” of verse 6 is the whole text, and what “if indeed” would do to it; who the “righteous” and the “good” person are in verse 7; whether the last clause of verse 12 says “because” or “in whom,” and whether the sentence is ever completed; how verses 13 and 14 fit together; whether the second “all people” of verse 18 means every person; whether “made” sinners and righteous in verse 19 is a change of nature or of standing; and whether the hina of verse 20 is purpose or result.
