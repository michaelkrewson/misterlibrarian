📖 Acts

Πράξεις Ἀποστόλων (Praxeis Apostolōn) — 'Acts of Apostles' — and in the oldest manuscripts there is no article: not 'the acts of THE apostles' but simply 'acts of apostles.' Praxeis was an ordinary Greek book-title for an account of a hero's deeds, so the name reads like a familiar genre label. It is also inaccurate, and everyone has always known it: only two apostles get real attention (Peter, then Paul), several are never mentioned again after chapter 1, and the actor the book keeps crediting is the Holy Spirit. The title is not the author's — the book never names itself, and the manuscripts vary.

The only sequel in the Bible, and the hinge on which it turns: how a movement of about a hundred and twenty frightened people in one upstairs room in Jerusalem became, inside thirty years, a faith preached in the capital of the world.

Chapter by chapter

Acts 1The only sequel in the Bible. Luke takes up his pen a second time, addresses the same Theophilus, and opens by calling his whole Gospel merely “the first account… of all that Jesus BEGAN to do and to teach” — which leaves the obvious question of who is doing the continuing. Forty days of appearances end on a hill outside Jerusalem with the disciples asking the question they have been asking all along — “Lord, is it at this time that you are restoring the kingdom to Israel?” — and getting an answer that refuses the calendar without refusing the hope, and then hands them a map: Jerusalem, Judea and Samaria, and the end of the earth. That map turns out to be the table of contents of the twenty-seven chapters that follow. Then a cloud, and two men in white asking why they are still standing there looking up. The rest of the chapter is the church doing the only thing it can do before Pentecost: it walks back a sabbath day’s journey, climbs the stairs, and waits — a fisherman, a tax-collector who had worked for Rome and a zealot from the party that killed such men, together with the women, the brothers who had not believed, and Mary, in the last mention Scripture gives her. Then Peter stands up among “a crowd of names, about a hundred and twenty” to fill the empty twelfth chair, and the church casts lots for the last time in the Bible. ⚠ It also carries the New Testament’s most awkward parenthesis: Luke’s account of how Judas died, which does not agree with Matthew’s — printed here with both readings and no vote.Acts 2Pentecost, and the day the promise chapter 1 refused to date gets paid. ⚠ Luke is careful twice and every version blurs it once: there is no wind and no fire, there is a SOUND LIKE a violent wind and tongues AS OF fire — and glōssa means the organ, the shape a flame makes, and a language, all three live in one paragraph. The miracle is put in the hearers’ ears as much as the speakers’ mouths. ⚠ The list of nations has JUDEA in it, textually secure in every printed edition and geographically inexplicable; the old conjectures are set out and none adopted. Then Peter’s sermon, and two places where the argument is visibly built on the Greek Bible: Joel’s undated “afterward” is quoted as “IN THE LAST DAYS,” and the whole David argument turns on diaphthora, DECAY — where the Hebrew shachat is “the pit,” and the case from a rotted body does not arise. ⚠ Also “loosing the BIRTH PANGS of death,” which you cannot do, because Hebrew chevel is both a cord and a pang and the Greek took the second. And at v38 the baptism is “on the name of Jesus Christ” — the Acts pattern Matthew 28’s note named and left unresolved, now on these pages.Acts 3A man lame from birth, healed at the temple's Beautiful Gate — the first miracle in Acts worked by someone other than Jesus, and Peter's own first move is to deny credit for it. 'Silver and gold I do not have,' and a command, 'in the name of Jesus Christ the Nazarene, rise up and walk' — a phrase that genuinely divides the manuscripts, not just the translations: the oldest single witness reads bare 'walk,' while the Byzantine text and the modern critical standard both add 'rise up and.' ⚠ Peter's sermon in Solomon's Portico names Jesus with a Greek word, pais, that the whole shelf splits on translating — KJV 'Son,' everyone else 'servant,' the same fork in RV60 versus NVI. It closes on 'the times of RESTORATION of all things' — a single rare noun, apokatastasis, that Origen would later stretch into a doctrine of universal salvation the wider church never adopted. And a promise handed to a Jewish crowd in a Jewish courtyard, 'to you first,' the sequencing Paul will spend Romans repeating.Acts 4Peter and John, arrested for the first time, and a council that cannot deny the miracle, only forbid speaking of it. The Sadducees arrive because the apostles are proclaiming the one doctrine their party denies — the resurrection, ‘in Jesus’ — and by morning the Sanhedrin sits with Annas, Caiaphas and the high-priestly family. Peter, filled with the Holy Spirit as Luke 12 had promised, answers a question about a healing in the vocabulary of salvation: the verb of v9 and the noun of v12 are one root, a link no English version on the shelf keeps. ⚠ He quotes the rejected stone and changes both the Gospels’ verb and the person — ‘treated as nothing by you’ — and says there is no other name under heaven by which we must be saved. The court sees parrēsia, ‘boldness,’ in two men it can only call unlettered and ordinary, and the congregation’s prayer then asks for it, and is given it. ⚠ That prayer reads Psalm 2 as a script with Herod and Pilate for its kings, calls David and Jesus by one and the same noun (the shelf breaks that apart), and says the plan was ‘marked out beforehand.’ It closes on everything held in common, no needy person among them, and Barnabas — a Levite with a field — laying its price at the apostles’ feet in the same three verbs that open chapter 5 with a different ending.Acts 5A couple who keep back part of a price, a congregation that learns to be afraid, and the apostles in prison and out of it. ⚠ The sale that closed chapter 4 repeats in the same three verbs with a fourth added — ‘kept back for himself,’ the verb the Greek Old Testament uses of Achan — and Peter’s two questions treat a lie to the Holy Spirit and a lie to God as one; the narrator names no cause for the deaths, and the first appearance of the word ‘congregation’ in Acts comes in a sentence about fear. Signs and wonders answer the prayer of chapter 4, and Peter’s shadow is cast with the verb that once overshadowed Mary. ⚠ An angel opens the prison by night; the captain who arrests them does it ‘not with force’; ‘we cannot’ becomes ‘one must obey God rather than people’; and Gamaliel names two rebels, Theudas and Judas, in an order Josephus reverses. The apostles are flogged — the first beating in the book — and leave rejoicing that they were ‘counted worthy to be dishonored for the Name.’Acts 6A success that strains the community: the disciples are multiplying, and the Hellenists grumble — the word of Exodus 16’s wilderness — that their widows are being overlooked in the daily service. The Twelve answer with one root doing two jobs: they will not leave the word of God to serve tables, and they will devote themselves to prayer and the service of the word. ⚠ The multitude chooses seven men ‘of good testimony,’ all with Greek names, among them a proselyte of Antioch and Stephen, whose name means ‘crown’ — and hands are laid on them in a sentence that does not say whose, with the verb the Greek Old Testament uses of Joshua. The word of God keeps growing, even among priests. ⚠ Then Stephen, ‘full of grace,’ does wonders and signs in the order his own speech will use of Moses; the synagogue of the Freedmen cannot withstand the wisdom and the Spirit — a promise from Luke 21 kept — and puts up false witnesses with the charge Luke left out of Jesus’ own trial: this place, and the customs of Moses. The council stares and sees a face like an angel’s.Acts 7The longest speech in Acts, and its answer to a question about “these things”: Stephen retells Israel’s story from Abraham to Solomon without once defending himself, and the story is the defence. ⚠ He reads Scripture in its Greek translation, and the places where Acts parts from the Hebrew are the seams of the argument, reported here and not resolved: seventy-five persons where Genesis 46:27 has seventy; a tomb at Shechem ‘that Abraham bought,’ where Genesis has Jacob buying at Shechem and Abraham at Hebron; four hundred years joined to a verb from Exodus 3; and an Amos quotation that says ‘beyond Babylon’ where the Hebrew and the Greek Amos both say Damascus. The man charged with speaking against ‘this place’ tells of a God who appears outside the land — to Abraham in Mesopotamia, to Moses at a bush in the wilderness — and of the generation that pushed Moses away, made a calf and took up ‘the tent of Moloch.’ ⚠ The word the Greek Bible uses of idols, ‘made with hands,’ lands on the temple; ‘stiffnecked and uncircumcised’ turns the charge of breaking the law back on the council, which is ‘cut to the quick’ as at Acts 5:33, with no Gamaliel this time. Then a vision: the heavens opened and the Son of Man standing, where every other verse has him seated; a mob that stops its ears; the witnesses laying their garments at the feet of a young man named Saul, first named here; and a dying prayer, ‘Lord Jesus, receive my spirit,’ that sets Luke 23:46 beside it, with a prayer for his killers whose Gospel twin is itself a disputed text. ‘He fell asleep.’Acts 8The persecution that scatters the congregation out of Jerusalem: Saul, who was “approving” Stephen’s killing in the last verse of chapter 7, now “ravages” the congregation house by house, and the scattered go through the land bringing good news with one verb while Philip, one of the Seven, proclaims the Christ in Samaria with another. ⚠ A magician called ‘the Power of God that is called Great’ amazes the nation, is himself amazed, believes and is baptized, and then offers money for the authority to give the Spirit; Peter’s answer, ‘may your silver go with you to destruction,’ draws on two lines of the Greek Old Testament, and the word simony carries his name. The Spirit’s delay until Peter and John arrive is set out with the readings that differ, and no vote is cast. ⚠ Then an Ethiopian eunuch, a high official of a queen whose title, Candace, is not a name, reads Isaiah 53 in its Greek form on the road to Gaza — ‘This is desert,’ the road or the city, in a Greek that does not say — and Philip asks a question that puns on knowing and reading. The critical Greek text has no verse 37, the shelf is split on it, and the gap is left showing. Philip is snatched away by the Spirit of Jehovah, with the verb of 2 Corinthians 12 and Revelation 12, and turns up at Azotus on the way to Caesarea.Acts 9Saul, still “breathing threat and murder,” asks the high priest for letters to the synagogues of Damascus, and a light, a doubled name and “Why are you persecuting me?” stop him on the road; the words the KJV adds at vv5–6 are not in the printed Greek, and the companions’ “hearing the voice” is set against 22:9 without a vote. ⚠ Ananias, a disciple whose answer is Abraham’s “Here I am,” is sent to the street called Straight to a “chosen vessel” who must suffer; scales fall, Saul is baptized, proclaims the Son of God in the synagogues and is let down the wall in a basket, with Paul’s own account in 2 Corinthians and Galatians read beside Luke’s and not joined to it. ⚠ In Jerusalem Barnabas takes hold of him; the congregation has peace, one congregation in the Greek where the KJV has churches; and Peter, at Lydda and Joppa, heals Aeneas and raises Tabitha — mathētria, the one woman “disciple” by that word in the New Testament — with the words and gestures of the Gospels’ raisings, and stays with a tanner named Simon.Acts 10At Caesarea a centurion named Cornelius, “devout and fearing God,” whose prayers and alms “went up as a memorial before God,” is told by an angel to send to Joppa for Simon who is called Peter; the KJV and Geneva add words at vv6 and 32 and “fasting” at v30 that the printed Greek does not have. ⚠ On a roof at noon Peter sees a vessel like a linen sheet let down with every kind of creature, hears “slaughter and eat,” answers “Not at all, Lord,” and is told “What God has cleansed, you must not make common” three times; the Spirit sends him with the men “hesitating over nothing,” and all seven versions print “three men” where the Greek has only “men.” ⚠ At Caesarea Cornelius bows down and Peter refuses (“I myself also am a man”), calls it “unlawful” to join himself to one “of another tribe” without naming a law, says God is not “partial” (prosopolēmptēs), and speaks of Jesus “doing good” and of witnesses who “ate and drank with him”; the Spirit falls before he has finished, the circumcised believers are amazed, and the question is “Can anyone forbid the water?”Acts 11Peter goes up to Jerusalem and is called to account by “those of the circumcision” for going in to uncircumcised men and eating with them — a meal chapter 10 never narrates — and tells the story again “in orderly sequence,” with the housetop and the hunger gone, “wild beasts” added to the sheet, and everything “pulled up” where chapter 10 had the vessel “taken up.” ⚠ The Spirit’s own words are reported again (“making no distinction,” the verb his critics have just used of themselves), the angel’s message gains “words by which you will be saved, you and all your household,” and Peter ends on a question that changes its object: not “Can anyone forbid the water?” but “who was I, that I could hinder God?” after “the equal gift.” They quiet down, and say God gave the nations “the repentance that leads to life.” ⚠ Those scattered over Stephen reach Antioch speaking “to Jews only” — except some Cypriots and Cyrenians, who speak to “Hellenists,” a word the editions divide with “Greeks” — and “the hand of Jehovah” is with them. Barnabas, the “son of encouragement,” is sent, sees grace, and goes to Tarsus to search for Saul; for a year they teach, and the disciples are first called Christians, in a verb (chrēmatizō) the NWT renders “by divine providence.” ⚠ The chapter closes with the prophet Agabus, a famine over “the whole inhabited world,” and a gift sent, each as he had means, to the elders by the hand of Barnabas and Saul.Acts 12Herod the king lays his hands on some of the congregation, kills James the brother of John “with a sword” and, seeing that it pleases the Jews, arrests Peter in the days of the Unleavened Bread — four groups of four soldiers, a bringing-out planned for “after the Passover.” ⚠ While the congregation prays “earnestly,” an angel of Jehovah stands in the cell, strikes Peter’s side and raises him up; the chains fall, he is told “Fasten your belt” and “follow me” (the two commands of John 21:18–19), and he thinks he is seeing a vision until the iron gate opens “by itself.” At the house of Mary the mother of John called Mark the servant girl Rhoda knows his voice, will not open “for joy,” and is told “You are out of your mind”; the household says “It is his angel.” ⚠ Herod has the guards “led away,” goes down to Caesarea, and on a set day in royal apparel hears the people shout “A god’s voice, and not a man’s”; the angel of Jehovah, with the verb that raised Peter, strikes him, and he is eaten by worms. Luke’s account and Josephus’s are set side by side and not harmonized. The word of God keeps growing, and Barnabas and Saul return, having fulfilled the service, taking John called Mark.Acts 13At Antioch the prophets and teachers — Barnabas, Simeon called Niger, Lucius the Cyrenian, Manaen who was brought up with Herod the tetrarch, and Saul — are ministering and fasting when the Holy Spirit says, “Set apart, then, for me Barnabas and Saul.” ⚠ Sent out, they sail to Cyprus with John as attendant; at Paphos Saul, “who is also Paul,” stares at the magician Bar-Jesus — Elymas, who is twisting the proconsul Sergius Paulus away from the faith — calls him “son of the devil,” and the hand of Jehovah leaves him blind, seeking people to lead him by the hand as Saul himself once was; the proconsul believes, astonished at the teaching. At Perga John withdraws; at Pisidian Antioch, asked for a “word of encouragement,” Paul tells Israel’s story from the chosen fathers to David, “a man after my heart,” and to a savior, Jesus, whom the people of Jerusalem condemned and took down from the tree and whom God raised, so that he did not see decay — Psalm 2, Isaiah 55 and Psalm 16 read together. ⚠ “Through this one forgiveness of sins is being proclaimed,” with a warning from Habakkuk; the next sabbath nearly the whole city gathers, the Jews are filled with jealousy, Paul and Barnabas say “we are turning to the nations” with Isaiah’s “light of nations,” and “as many as were appointed to eternal life believed.” The two are thrown out, shake the dust off their feet and go to Iconium, and the disciples are filled with joy and Holy Spirit.Acts 14At Iconium the pair go into the synagogue again and a great number of Jews and Greeks believe; the Jews who disobeyed stir up and afflict the souls of the nations, the city is split between the Jews and “the apostles,” and when an impulse comes to treat them outrageously and stone them, they flee to Lystra and Derbe. ⚠ At Lystra a man lame from his mother’s womb, as at Acts 3, is looked at, told “Stand up on your feet, upright!” and leaps; the crowds cry out in Lycaonian that the gods have come down and call Barnabas Zeus and Paul Hermes, and a priest of “the Zeus that was before the city” brings bulls and garlands, in a Greek that does not write “temple.” Barnabas and Paul tear their garments, say “we also are humans who share your nature,” and point to a living God who did not leave himself without witness in the rains and the seasons. ⚠ Jews from Antioch and Iconium persuade the same crowds, Paul is stoned and dragged outside the city, “supposing him to be dead,” and rises up and goes back in; the two return through Lystra, Iconium and Antioch strengthening the disciples (“through many tribulations we must enter the kingdom of God”), appoint elders in each congregation with prayer and fastings, and sail home from Attalia to report all that God had done with them and that he had opened to the nations a door of faith.Acts 15The first council the congregations ever hold, called to decide whether a Gentile has to become a Jew first — and the letter it sends still carries, almost as an afterthought beside the free pass on the law of Moses, the ancient command to abstain from blood (Genesis 9:4, Leviticus 17, already on these pages), delivered whole to converts who were never under that law in the first place. ⚠ James's whole scriptural case (Amos 9:11–12) only works in the Greek Old Testament — checked here against the Hebrew itself, which reads a different word entirely. A second, less famous manuscript question sits at the decree's own two statements: the earliest Western witnesses drop 'what is strangled' and rewrite the whole thing into a version of the Golden Rule. Peter gives his last extended speech in Acts; nine verses after the council closes in triumph, Paul and Barnabas — having just won their argument together — split over one younger man's earlier failure, sharply enough that the word Luke reaches for is the direct ancestor of English 'paroxysm.'Acts 16Paul takes Timothy — a Jewish mother, a Greek father, “well spoken of” — and circumcises him “because of the Jews,” and the cities are handed “the decrees that had been decided” at Jerusalem. ⚠ The Holy Spirit hinders them from speaking the word in Asia and the Spirit of Jesus does not allow Bithynia; at Troas a Macedonian in a vision says “help us,” and the narration turns to “we.” At Philippi, “a colony,” the first people Paul speaks to are women at a river, and the Lord opens the heart of Lydia, a seller of purple from Thyatira, who urges them strongly to stay. ⚠ A servant girl with a Python spirit cries “a way of salvation” for many days until Paul orders the spirit out; her owners, seeing that the hope of their profit has “gone out,” drag Paul and Silas before the magistrates with a charge that leaves out the girl and the money. They are beaten with rods and put in the inner prison with their feet in the stocks; at midnight, singing hymns, they are the occasion of an earthquake with no angel in it, and the jailer draws his sword, hears “we are all here,” and asks “Sirs, what must I do to be saved?” ⚠ He and his household are baptized that night and a table is set; in the morning Paul will not be released secretly, because they are Romans, and the magistrates, afraid, come themselves to bring them out.Acts 17Paul and Silas pass through Amphipolis and Apollonia to Thessalonica, where for three sabbaths Paul “opens up” the Scriptures to show that the Christ had to suffer and rise, and a mob made up of marketplace men goes looking for them at Jason’s house, drags Jason before the politarchs and charges that these men have “turned the inhabited world upside down” and say “there is another king, Jesus.” ⚠ At Beroea the Jews are “more noble,” examining the Scriptures daily, until the Thessalonians arrive “shaking” the crowds. At Athens Paul, his spirit provoked by a city full of idols, argues in the marketplace with Epicureans and Stoics who call him a seed-picker and a proclaimer of “foreign divinities,” and is led to the Areopagus. ⚠ His speech there starts from an altar “to an unknown god,” says the God who made the world is not served by hands, quotes the poets (“we are also his offspring”), says the times of ignorance were overlooked, and ends with a day, a man, and a resurrection — at which some jeer, some say “we will hear you again,” and Dionysius the Areopagite and a woman named Damaris join him.Acts 18Paul goes from Athens to Corinth and finds Aquila and Priscilla, Jews lately come from Italy because Claudius had ordered all the Jews to depart from Rome, and works with them as a tentmaker; when Silas and Timothy come down from Macedonia he is “gripped by the word,” and when the Jews set themselves against him he shakes out his garments — “Your blood be on your own heads; I am clean” — and moves next door, to the house of Titius Justus, where Crispus, the ruler of the synagogue, believes the Lord with his whole household. ⚠ In a night vision the Lord says, “I have a numerous people in this city,” and Paul settles for a year and six months, until the Jews bring him before the proconsul Gallio, who says that if the dispute is “about a word and names and your own law” they may see to it themselves, drives them from the judgment seat, and lets Sosthenes be beaten in front of it, for none of it “mattered” to him. ⚠ Paul sails for Syria with Priscilla and Aquila, keiramenos at Cenchreae — “having had his head shorn,” and the Greek does not say whose — leaves them at Ephesus with “God willing,” and goes up and down through Caesarea, Antioch and the Galatian country; and Apollos, an eloquent Alexandrian “powerful in the Scriptures” who knows “only the baptism of John,” is taken to themselves by Priscilla and Aquila and has the way of God set out to him “more accurately,” then crosses to Achaia and shows from the Scriptures “that the Christ was Jesus.”Acts 19Paul comes to Ephesus while Apollos is at Corinth and asks twelve disciples, “Did you receive Holy Spirit when you believed?” — they have not even heard whether there is one, and know only John’s baptism; they are baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus, Paul lays hands on them, and they speak in tongues and prophesy. For three months he speaks boldly in the synagogue, and when some are “disobeying” and speaking evil of the Way he separates the disciples and reasons daily in the lecture hall of Tyrannus, for two years, until all who live in Asia have heard the word of the Lord; God does extraordinary acts of power, even through cloths carried from his skin. ⚠ The seven sons of Sceva, a Jewish high priest, try to name the name of Jesus over a man with an evil spirit — “Jesus I know, and Paul I know, but who are you?” — and flee naked and wounded; the name of the Lord Jesus is magnified, believers come confessing their practices, and a considerable number of those who had practiced the curious arts burn their books, fifty thousand pieces of silver, as the word of the Lord keeps growing and prevailing. ⚠ Paul resolves in the spirit to go to Jerusalem by way of Macedonia and Achaia — “I must also see Rome” — and sends Timothy and Erastus ahead; then the silversmith Demetrius, whose silver temples of Artemis bring the craftsmen “no small profit,” tells his workmen that this Paul has persuaded a considerable crowd that gods made through hands are not gods, and the city rushes into the theater with one accord crying “Great is Artemis of the Ephesians!” for about two hours. ⚠ The town clerk quiets the crowd, calls Ephesus temple keeper of the thing that fell from heaven, says that these men are neither temple-robbers nor blasphemers of “our goddess,” that court days are held and there are proconsuls, that anything further goes to the lawful assembly, and that they are in danger of a charge of insurrection — and dismisses the assembly.Acts 20After the uproar ceases Paul sends for the disciples, greets them and goes on into Macedonia and Greece, where a plot by the Jews on the eve of his sailing for Syria turns him back through Macedonia, with seven companions named; the narration returns to “we” at Philippi and Troas. ⚠ On the first day of the week, as Paul reasons on until midnight in a crowded upper room, a young man named Eutychus, sitting on the window and borne down by sleep, falls from the third floor and is picked up dead; Paul goes down, falls on him and says “Do not be thrown into an uproar,” and after the bread and the talk until daybreak they bring the boy alive. Paul sails past Ephesus, hurrying for Pentecost, and from Miletus calls the elders of the congregation, and tells them in a farewell speech that he served the Lord as a slave “with tears and trials,” shrank back from nothing profitable, is going to Jerusalem “bound in the spirit” knowing that bonds await him, and that they will see his face no more. ⚠ “Take heed… to the flock, in which the Holy Spirit placed you as overseers, to shepherd the congregation of God which he acquired through the blood of his own” — a verse whose “God,” “the Lord” and “his own” the apparatus and the shelf split on and the note sets out without a vote — and then grievous wolves from outside and twisted men from among them. He entrusts them to God and the word of his grace, reminds them that these hands served his needs, cites a saying of the Lord Jesus found in no Gospel, “It is more blessed to give than to receive,” kneels and prays, and they fall on his neck and kiss him, grieving most over the word that they will see his face no more.Acts 21Torn away from the Ephesian elders, Paul and his party make a straight run by Cos, Rhodes and Patara to Tyre, where disciples tell him “through the Spirit” not to set foot in Jerusalem, and on by Ptolemais to Caesarea and the house of Philip the evangelist, one of the Seven, whose four virgin daughters prophesy. ⚠ The prophet Agabus binds his own feet and hands with Paul’s belt and says the Jews will bind its owner at Jerusalem and hand him over to the nations; Paul answers that he is ready “not only to be bound but even to die” for the name of the Lord Jesus, and they quiet down: “The Lord’s will be done.” At Jerusalem James and the elders tell him of the tens of thousands of zealous believers who have heard that he teaches apostasy from Moses, and ask him to be purified with four men who have a vow and to pay their expenses; a note sets out what the text says and does not say of the vow, the Nazirite law of Numbers 6, and the decree they repeat. Jews from Asia then cry out that he has brought Greeks into the temple, the whole city is stirred, the doors are shut, and the tribune of the cohort runs down with soldiers, has him bound with two chains and carried up the steps to the barracks; there Paul asks the tribune, in Greek, to be allowed to speak, is taken for the Egyptian who led four thousand dagger-men into the wilderness, says he is a Tarsian, and motions to the people and addresses them in the Hebrew dialect.Acts 22From the steps of the barracks Paul addresses the crowd in the Hebrew dialect, “Men, brothers and fathers,” and they give the more quiet: a Jewish man born in Tarsus, brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel, a zealot of God who persecuted this Way to the death. ⚠ He tells the road to Damascus a second time, and the telling differs from Acts 9 — about noon, a considerable light, “I am Jesus the Nazarene,” and the men with him “beheld the light but did not hear the voice of the one speaking”; the note sets it beside “hearing the voice” at 9:7 and votes for neither. Ananias, devout and well spoken of, says “recover your sight,” and the God of our fathers has appointed him beforehand to see the Righteous One; “why do you delay? Rising, be baptized and wash away your sins, calling on his name.” In the temple, in a trance, he is told to hurry out of Jerusalem and then, “I will send you out far away to the nations” — and the crowd, listening up to this word, cries “Away from the earth with such a man!” and flings its garments and dust into the air. ⚠ The tribune has him brought into the barracks to be examined by flogging; stretched out with the straps, Paul asks the centurion whether it is lawful to flog a Roman and uncondemned, and the tribune, who bought his citizenship for a large sum, learns that Paul was born a citizen and is afraid. On the next day he releases him and sets him before the chief priests and the whole Sanhedrin.Acts 23Paul, staring at the Sanhedrin, says he has lived as a citizen before God in all good conscience — the verb whose noun, ‘citizenship,’ saved him from the lash the day before. The high priest Ananias has him struck on the mouth; Paul answers ‘God is going to strike you, you whitewashed wall,’ the only other use of kekoniamenos being Jesus’s whitewashed tombs, and then, told whom he has insulted, says he did not know he was high priest and quotes Exodus 22:28. ⚠ Seeing the council half Sadducees and half Pharisees, he cries that he is a Pharisee on trial for the hope and resurrection of the dead; the crowd splits, Luke explains that Sadducees deny resurrection, angel and spirit while Pharisees acknowledge ‘both’ (a count the Greek leaves open), and a sentence breaks off in the Greek — ‘if a spirit spoke to him, or an angel—’ — which some copyists finished with Gamaliel’s own verb, ‘let us not fight against God.’ ⚠ The tribune has the troop snatch Paul back to the barracks; that night the Lord stands by him: ‘Take courage! … so you must bear witness in Rome also.’ More than forty men bind themselves under a curse — ‘with a curse we have bound ourselves under a curse’ — to taste nothing until Paul is dead, and the plan is a request to the priests to have him brought down; Paul’s sister’s son hears of it, the tribune sends Paul by night with two hundred soldiers, seventy horsemen and two hundred dexiolaboi (a word seen once) to Felix at Caesarea, and his letter says he ‘rescued’ Paul ‘having learned that he is a Roman,’ which can be set beside what Acts 21–22 narrated. Felix asks his province, hears Cilicia, and keeps him in Herod’s praetorium until the accusers come.Acts 24After five days the high priest Ananias comes down with some elders and an orator, Tertullus, who opens with praise for Felix’s ‘peace’ and ‘reforms’ and then calls Paul a plague, a stirrer-up of dissensions throughout the inhabited world and a ringleader of ‘the party of the Nazarenes.’ ⚠ The Greek printed here has no verse 7 and only half of 6 and 8 — the tribune’s ‘great violence’ and the order that the accusers come to Felix are in the longer text some Bibles print — and the page shows the gap rather than renumbering. Paul, after the governor’s nod, says it is ‘not more than twelve days’ since he went up to worship, that he serves ‘the God of my fathers’ according to ‘the Way, which they call a party,’ with a hope in ‘a resurrection of both the righteous and the unrighteous,’ and that the Asian Jews who started it all are not here. Felix, ‘knowing more accurately the things concerning the Way,’ puts them off until Lysias comes down. Days later he comes with Drusilla; when Paul reasons about righteousness, self-control and the judgment to come, Felix becomes terrified and sends him off — while hoping for money. After two years Festus succeeds him, and Felix, wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews, leaves Paul bound.Acts 25Festus, having entered the province, goes up after three days to Jerusalem, where the chief priests and the leading men give information against Paul and ask a favor against him — that he be sent for to Jerusalem, while they make an ambush to kill him on the way; Festus answers that Paul is being kept at Caesarea. ⚠ At the hearing on the judgment seat the Jews bring ‘heavy accusations’ they cannot prove, and Paul’s denial has three members — the law, the temple, and now Caesar. Festus, ‘wishing to lay up a favor with the Jews,’ asks whether he will be judged at Jerusalem; Paul says he is standing at Caesar’s judgment seat, that he does not beg off from dying if he has done wrong, that no one can give him over to them as a favor, and ‘I appeal to Caesar.’ After talking with the council Festus answers: ‘You have appealed to Caesar; to Caesar you shall go.’ ⚠ King Agrippa and Berenice arrive, and Festus lays the case before the king: a man left a prisoner by Felix, Roman custom against giving anyone over before he meets his accusers, and questions about their own religion — the word deisidaimonia — and ‘a certain Jesus who had died, whom Paul asserted to be alive.’ Paul appealed ‘to be kept for the decision of the Emperor.’ Agrippa wishes to hear him; with great display, tribunes and men of distinction in the audience room, Festus says he found nothing worthy of death, has ‘nothing certain to write to my lord,’ and thinks it unreasonable to send a prisoner without indicating the charges.Acts 26Agrippa tells Paul he may speak for himself, and Paul, stretching out his hand, says he counts himself happy to defend himself before a king who is an expert in the customs and questions of the Jews. ⚠ The road to Damascus is told a third time, with details the first two lack: the light is ‘beyond the brightness of the sun’, all fall to the ground, and the voice speaks ‘in the Hebrew dialect’ and adds a proverb about kicking against the goads. The commission is the longest in the book — to open eyes, to turn people ‘from darkness to light’ — and Paul says he says nothing outside what the prophets and Moses spoke, ‘if the Christ was subject to suffering’. Festus shouts, ‘You are out of your mind, Paul!’ and Paul answers that he is not; Agrippa says, ‘In a little time you are persuading me to make a Christian.’ ⚠ The king, the governor and Berenice withdraw, saying the man is doing ‘nothing worthy of death or bonds’, and Agrippa tells Festus he could have been released if he had not appealed to Caesar.Acts 27Paul and certain other prisoners are handed over to a centurion named Julius of the Augustan cohort, and ‘we’ set sail in a ship of Adramyttium with Aristarchus; at Sidon Julius treats Paul with kindness, and at Myra he puts the party on a ship of Alexandria bound for Italy. ⚠ Sailing slowly under Crete, they reach Fair Havens, where Paul advises that the voyage will be with injury and much loss; the centurion is more persuaded by the pilot and the shipowner, and a gentle south wind gives way to a wind of typhoon force, Euraquilo. The ship is undergirded, the gear lowered, the cargo and then the equipment thrown out, and neither sun nor stars appear for many days. ⚠ Paul stands in the midst of them and tells of an angel of the God whose he is: ‘you must stand before Caesar, and look, God has granted you all who are sailing with you.’ On the fourteenth night the sailors take soundings and throw out four anchors; when they try to flee in the ship’s boat, Paul says the soldiers cannot be saved unless these men stay, and the soldiers cut the boat away. Paul takes bread, gives thanks to God before all and eats; there are 276 souls on board. ⚠ At day the ship is run aground in a place between two seas, the soldiers plan to kill the prisoners, and the centurion, wishing to bring Paul safely through, prevents them: all are brought to land.Acts 28Brought safely through, the party learns that the island is called Malta, and the barbarians show extraordinary kindness, kindling a fire; when Paul puts brushwood on it a viper fastens on his hand. ⚠ The islanders call him a murderer whom Justice has not allowed to live, and, when nothing happens to him, a god. Publius, the leading man of the island, hosts them; Paul heals his father of fevers and dysentery, and the rest of the sick on the island are healed. ⚠ After three months they sail in an Alexandrian ship with the Dioscuri as its emblem, by Syracuse and Rhegium to Puteoli, where brothers urge them to stay seven days; others come out to meet them at the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns, and at Rome Paul is permitted to stay by himself with the soldier who guards him. ⚠ He calls together the leading men of the Jews, says he was handed over from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans and wears this chain for the hope of Israel, and from morning until evening sets out the kingdom of God from the law of Moses and the Prophets; some are persuaded and some disbelieve, and as they leave he quotes Isaiah and says that this salvation of God has been sent to the nations. The printed text has no verse 29. ⚠ He stays on a whole two years in his own rented quarters, welcoming all who come in, proclaiming the kingdom of God with all boldness, unhindered.

About the book

Where it sits
The FIFTH book of the New Testament, standing between the Gospels and the letters — which is exactly its function, since it supplies the narrative the letters assume. In the Greek manuscript tradition it often travels not with Luke but at the head of the 'Praxapostolos,' bound with the general epistles, which is why the two halves of one work were separated early and have been read apart ever since.
Genre
HISTORICAL NARRATIVE in the Hellenistic manner — volume two of the two-part work that begins with Luke's Gospel. It has the furniture of ancient historiography: a dedication, synchronisms with named emperors and governors, speeches placed at the turning points (about a third of the book is speech), sea-voyages, trials, and a travel diary. It is also the New Testament's only book of its kind: without it the road from the empty tomb to Paul's letters would be almost entirely dark.
Author
Unnamed, and by early, wide, and unchallenged tradition LUKE — the same hand as the Third Gospel (the preface at 1:1 points back to it and to the same Theophilus). The strongest internal evidence is peculiar: from 16:10 the narrative slips, without explanation, into the first person plural — the 'we' passages — as though the writer joined the journey at Troas and is reporting what he was there for. Read one way that is an eyewitness signature; read another it is a literary convention of sea-voyage narrative. The tradition's Luke is 'the beloved physician' of Colossians 4:14, a Gentile companion of Paul.
Date written
Disputed across a range of some forty years. An EARLY date (c. AD 62–64) rests on the way the book simply stops, with Paul alive and under house arrest in Rome and no word of his death, the fall of Jerusalem, or Nero's persecution. A MIDDLE date (c. 80–85) is the majority view, tied to Luke's use of Mark and to a Gospel that seems to look back on Jerusalem's fall. A LATE date (c. 110–120) is argued from possible knowledge of Josephus and of Paul's collected letters. The book itself gives no year.
Place
Unknown. Rome, Antioch, Ephesus and Achaia have all been proposed, none decisively. What is clear is the horizon: the story ends at Rome, and Rome is where it has been pointing since 1:8.
Audience
Theophilus — a real patron, an inquirer, or a name for every reader who loves God — and beyond him a Greek-speaking readership that needed two things at once: assurance that the faith rested on evidence, and an account of how a Jewish messianic movement came to be full of Gentiles without ceasing to be the fulfilment of Israel's scriptures. A third audience is often suspected: Roman officialdom, since the book takes visible care to record that magistrate after magistrate found nothing criminal in the accused.

How it's laid out

1:1–2:47Jerusalem, waiting and then filled — the ascension, the twelfth apostle, and the day of Pentecost.
3:1–7:60The Jerusalem congregation — signs, arrests, the first shared purse, the first internal quarrel, and the first blood: Stephen.
8:1–12:25Judea and Samaria — the scattering that spreads the message, Philip and the Ethiopian, the road to Damascus, Cornelius, and Peter's escape from Herod.
13:1–20:38To the nations — three journeys out of Antioch, the Jerusalem council that decides Gentiles need not become Jews, and congregations planted across Asia and Greece.
21:1–28:31Arrest, trials, and Rome — Jerusalem, Caesarea, the appeal to Caesar, shipwreck at Malta, and two years teaching 'with all boldness, unhindered' — the last word of the book.

What it's about

  • The HOLY SPIRIT as the real protagonist — poured out, forbidding, sending, snatching away; the book is called Acts of Apostles but reads as acts of the Spirit through them.
  • WITNESS (martyria) — the whole apostolic claim is evidentiary, resting on men who saw; the same word, in this book, begins its journey toward meaning 'martyr'.
  • The WORD that spreads — Luke punctuates the book with progress reports ('the word of God kept growing'), and the plot is measured in geography, not in years.
  • GENTILES, and the cost of including them — the argument the church nearly split over, settled in chapter 15 and defended for the rest of the book.
  • GOD'S PLAN and human resistance — Luke's small relentless 'must' (dei): what happens is what had to happen, including the opposition.
  • PRAYER, and decisions made in it — the church waits, prays, fasts, and lays on hands before every hinge in the story.

The source text

Translated from the Greek of the SBL GREEK NEW TESTAMENT (SBLGNT), edited by Michael W. Holmes. ⚠ Acts is the New Testament's most textually turbulent book. Alongside the familiar Alexandrian text stands the so-called WESTERN text — represented above all by Codex Bezae (D, 5th c.) — which is roughly 8.5% LONGER, adding names, motives, itineraries and small vivid details throughout. Whether that is early tradition preserved or later expansion is a question two centuries of scholarship have not closed; a few have even argued Luke issued two editions. This translation follows the critical text and flags the notable Western readings where they fall.

Looking forward

The book's Jesus is absent and everywhere. He is taken up in the first chapter and does not return — and then acts on nearly every page: he is preached as the crucified man God raised and made 'both Lord and Christ' (2:36); he stands up at Stephen's death (7:56); he stops Saul on a road and identifies himself with the people being arrested ('why are you persecuting ME?', 9:4); his name heals, and is the thing men are forbidden to speak in. Luke's opening sentence sets the terms: the Gospel was what Jesus BEGAN to do and to teach, and this volume is the continuation — which is why the sermons in Acts are the earliest surviving Christian preaching we have, and every one of them is about a resurrection somebody claims to have seen.

Where the debates are

AUTHORSHIP and the 'we' passages — eyewitness diary, a source Luke used, or a literary convention. The DATE, on which the abrupt ending is the strongest single argument and also the most argued-over. HISTORICAL RELIABILITY, where the book's local detail (titles like politarch and proconsul, harbours, winds, the Gallio inscription at Delphi) has repeatedly checked out, while its account of the Jerusalem council is famously hard to line up with Galatians 2. The SPEECHES — verbatim record, Lukan composition in the manner of Thucydides, or something between. And the WESTERN TEXT, on which no consensus exists. This library reports the positions and their pedigrees; it does not vote.
