At Caesarea the centurion Cornelius, devout and fearing God, sees an angel in a vision and sends two servants and a soldier to Joppa for Simon who is called Peter. On the next day, about noon, Peter on a housetop sees a vessel like a great linen sheet let down with every kind of creature, hears ‘slaughter and eat’ three times, and is sent by the Spirit to go with the men; he enters Caesarea on the fourth day, is bowed to, speaks of Jesus to the gathered household, and while he is still speaking the Holy Spirit falls on all who hear; they are baptized and ask him to stay some days.
Undated by the text beyond following chapter 9: the chapter names no year and counts days only from Cornelius’s vision, with ‘on the next day’ at vv9, 23 and 24 and ‘from the fourth day’ at v30. The shelf counts that interval as four days or three (NIV). The chapter gives no date for the Italian cohort, for the visit or for the baptism. This library does not supply a year.

Verses 1–2 · ἑκατοντάρχης ἐκ σπείρης τῆς καλουμένης Ἰταλικῆς … φοβούμενος τὸν θεόν hekatontarchēs ek speirēs tēs kaloumenēs Italikēs … phoboumenos ton theon — Cornelius the centurion, Acts 10:1–2: the Italian cohort, “fearing God,” and alms from a Gentile’s hand

A centurion, in a book that has been collecting them. Hekatontarchēs is the commander of a hundred. The name Kornēlios stands in eight verses of this chapter (vv1, 3, 17, 22, 24, 25, 30 and 31) and in no other verse of the New Testament. Luke has already shown two centurions. At Capernaum, the elders say of one that “he loves our nation, and he himself built the synagogue for us” (Luke 7:5); at the cross another “glorified God, saying, ‘Certainly this man was righteous’” (Luke 23:47). Nothing in the Greek ties any of the three to another, but the profile comes round again here: a man who gives to the Jewish people and is called righteous (dikaios, v22). NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 print “army officer” for the rank, TLB “a Roman army officer,” and the KJV, ASV, Douay and NIV keep “centurion.”

“The cohort called Italian.” Speira, the word for a body of soldiers, stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Matthew 27:27 (“gathered the whole cohort against him”), Mark 15:16, John 18:3 and 18:12, here, and Acts 21:31 and 27:1. Italikēs, “Italian,” stands in this verse alone. The Greek says nothing of where the unit was raised or when it came to Caesarea, and this page supplies no date. The shelf names the unit differently: KJV “the band called the Italian band,” ASV “the band called the Italian band,” Douay “that which is called the Italian band,” NIV “what was known as the Italian Regiment,” NWT 1984 “the Italian band, as it was called,” NWT 2013 “what was called the Italian unit,” TLB “an Italian regiment.” Caesarea is the city where the previous chapters left Philip (8:40) and where the brothers sent Saul on his way to Tarsus (9:30).

“Fearing God.” Eusebēs, “devout,” is paired with phoboumenos ton theon, “fearing God,” and the pair is not left in v2. The messengers repeat it at v22 (“a righteous man and one fearing God”), Peter says it of anyone at v35 (“the one who fears him”), and the phrase comes again in Paul’s address at 13:16 and 13:26: five verses in all. Whether Luke’s participle names a recognized class of Gentiles who attached themselves to the synagogue, or only describes a man’s piety, is read both ways; the Greek gives the participle and no more, and this page does not decide. “With all his household” (syn panti tō oikō autou) comes round at 11:14, where Peter reports the angel’s words as “you and all your household”. KJV “A devout man, and one that feared God with all his house,” Douay “A religious man, and fearing God with all his house,” NIV “devout and God-fearing,” TLB “a godly man, deeply reverent.”

Alms, paid. “Doing many alms for the people” is eleēmosynai, the word of Acts 3:2, where a lame man is carried to the gate to ask for it, and of 9:36, where Tabitha is “full of good works and alms that she did.” Here it is a centurion who gives them, and the noun stands in three verses of this chapter: vv2, 4 and 31. “The people” is laos, which in Peter’s speech at vv41–42 is Israel; the alms go to the Jewish people, as the messengers’ “the whole nation of the Jews” at v22 says again. KJV “gave much alms to the people,” ASV “gave much alms to the people,” Douay “giving much alms to the people,” NWT 1984 “made many gifts of mercy to the people,” NIV “gave generously to those in need,” TLB “gave generously to charity.” The NWT keeps the word’s own eleos, “mercy,” inside it; the NIV and TLB drop the people it names.

Verses 3–8 · ἄγγελον τοῦ θεοῦ … αἱ προσευχαί σου καὶ αἱ ἐλεημοσύναι σου ἀνέβησαν εἰς μνημόσυνον ἔμπροσθεν τοῦ θεοῦ angelon tou theou … hai proseuchai sou kai hai eleēmosynai sou anebēsan eis mnēmosynon emprosthen tou theou — Cornelius’s vision, Acts 10:3–8: prayers and alms “went up as a memorial,” and “Simon who is called Peter”

“In a vision, plainly.” En horamati stands in three verses of Acts: 9:10 and 9:12, where it is Ananias’s vision and Saul’s, and here, here, where it is a Gentile’s. Phanerōs, “plainly,” says the angel was seen, not dreamed at the edge of sleep. The hour is the ninth, the hour at which Peter and John were going up to the temple for prayer (Acts 3:1); Cornelius says it again at v30 (“praying the ninth hour”). The text says nothing about where he learned the hour. KJV “He saw in a vision evidently about the ninth hour of the day,” ASV “openly,” Douay “manifestly,” NWT 1984 “saw plainly in a vision.”

“Gazing… terrified.” Atenisas is the verb of the disciples “gazing intently into heaven” (Acts 1:10), of Peter “gazing at” the lame man (3:4), of the council “staring” at Stephen (6:15) and of Stephen “staring into heaven” (7:55); it stands in ten verses of Acts, and this is the sixth. Emphobos, “terrified,” stands in five verses of the New Testament: the women at the tomb and the disciples to whom Jesus appears (Luke 24:5, 24:37), here, Felix at Acts 24:25 and the rest of the city after the earthquake, “the rest became terrified” (Revelation 11:13). “What is it, Lord?” is Ti estin, kyrie? The speaker is an angel, and kyrie can be “sir” to a stranger or “Lord” to God; this translation prints “Lord,” the word Peter uses to the voice at v14, and leaves the range to this note. TLB “What do you want, sir?” KJV, ASV, NIV and NWT “Lord.”

“Went up as a memorial.” Mnēmosynon, “a memorial,” stands in three verses of the New Testament. The other two are the woman who anoints Jesus: “what she did will also be told, in memory of her” (Matthew 26:13; Mark 14:9). In all three the thing remembered is something given, and v31 says it with the verb: “your prayer was heard and your alms were remembered.” The prayers and alms “went up” (anebēsan) “before God” (emprosthen tou theou), and at v33 the whole house puts itself “before God” (enōpion tou theou) to listen. KJV “come up for a memorial before God,” ASV “gone up for a memorial before God,” Douay “ascended for a memorial in the sight of God,” Geneva “come up into remembrance before God,” NWT 1984 “ascended as a remembrance before God,” NIV “come up as a memorial offering before God,” TLB “have not gone unnoticed by God.” The NIV adds “offering,” which the Greek does not have; the TLB drops the noun altogether.

“Simon who is called Peter,” and a tanner by the sea. Epikaleitai, “is called,” is the verb of “those who call on your name” at 9:14 and 9:21; here, in the passive, it gives Peter his second name, and the formula returns at vv18 and 32. Byrseus, “tanner,” completes the three verses of the book that name him (9:43, here and v32), and the house is “by the sea,” para thalassan. At v6 the KJV reads “he shall tell thee what thou oughtest to do,” the Geneva “he shall tell thee what thou oughtest to do,” and the Douay “He will tell thee what thou must do”; the printed Greek has no such clause, and the nearest thing to it is Peter’s own report at 11:14, “who will speak words to you”. The servants of v7 are oiketai, household servants, and the soldier is “devout” as Cornelius is; “in constant attendance” is proskarterountōn, the participle of the congregation’s devotion at 1:14, 2:42 and 2:46, and of “we will devote ourselves to prayer” at 6:4; it stands in six verses of Acts, and here it is a soldier’s duty to his officer. KJV “of them that waited on him continually,” NWT 1984 “from among those who were in constant attendance upon him,” NIV “one of his attendants,” Douay “a soldier who feared the Lord, of them that were under him,” TLB “one of his personal bodyguard.”

Verses 9–16 · ἔκστασις … σκεῦός τι ὡς ὀθόνην μεγάλην … θῦσον καὶ φάγε … Ἃ ὁ θεὸς ἐκαθάρισεν σὺ μὴ κοίνου ekstasis … skeuos ti hōs othonēn megalēn … thyson kai phage … ha ho theos ekatharisen sy mē koinou — Peter’s vision on the roof at Joppa, Acts 10:9–16: the sheet, “slaughter and eat,” “common and unclean,” and “what God has cleansed”

Noon, a roof, a trance. The two scenes are stitched by the road: Cornelius’s men are still walking (v9) on the day after his vision, and Peter goes up to pray at the sixth hour, which is noon. Prospeinos, “very hungry,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. Ekstasis, “trance,” is the noun of existēmi, “to be amazed,” and the chapter will use the verb at v45 for the believers who watch the Spirit fall; the noun stands in seven verses of the New Testament, in Acts at 3:10, here, 11:5 and 22:17. KJV, ASV, NIV, NWT and TLB “trance,” Douay “an ecstasy of mind.”

“A certain vessel.” Skeuos is the word the Lord used of Saul, “a chosen vessel” (Acts 9:15); here, and at v16 and 11:5, it names what comes down. The text does not connect the two uses. Othonē, “linen sheet,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 11:5. It comes down “by four corners,” in Greek tessarsin archais, the plural of archē, “beginning,” here in the sense of the ends of a cloth, and the same phrase stands at 11:5. The shelf splits on the noun: KJV “a certain vessel descending unto him, as it had been a great sheet knit at the four corners,” ASV “a certain vessel descending, as it were a great sheet,” Douay “a certain vessel descending, as it were a great linen sheet,” NWT 1984 “some sort of vessel descending like a great linen sheet,” while the NIV “something like a large sheet” and the TLB “a great canvas sheet” leave skeuos out. In v12 the list is by kind and not by clean and unclean, and the Greek does not say which animals were on the sheet; the TLB adds “forbidden to the Jews for food,” and the KJV and Geneva add “wild beasts,” which the printed Greek has not.

“Slaughter and eat.” Thyson kai phage uses thyō, the verb of the fattened calf (Luke 15:23), of the Passover that “had to be sacrificed” (Luke 22:7) and of the sacrifice at Lystra (Acts 14:13 and 14:18); it is a different verb from anaireō, “kill,” which this translation keeps for putting a person to death. NWT 1984 “slaughter and eat,” KJV, ASV, NIV and Douay “kill and eat.” Peter answers, Mēdamōs, kyrie, “Not at all, Lord”: the two words are in one breath, a refusal and a title, and the pair of words stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 11:8. His reason is oudepote ephagon pan koinon kai akatharton, “never have I eaten anything common and unclean.” ASV “common and unclean,” Douay “common and unclean,” KJV “common or unclean,” Geneva “polluted, or unclean,” NIV “impure or unclean,” NWT 1984 “defiled and unclean,” TLB “forbidden by our Jewish laws.” The TLB’s reason is its own; Peter says what he has not eaten, and does not here name a law (koinos).

“What God has cleansed.” Ha ho theos ekatharisen sy mē koinou has an aorist, “has cleansed,” and a present imperative with mē, “you must not make common” or “stop making common.” The verb is koinoō, the verb of Matthew 15:11 (“what goes into the mouth… defiles” in this translation), and the verb of cleansing is katharizō, which is also Mark’s aside at Mark 7:19 (“Thus he declared all foods clean”) and the verb of Acts 15:9, “cleansing their hearts by faith”: three verses, three different objects. The shelf: KJV “What God hath cleansed, that call not thou common,” Douay “That which God hath cleansed, do not thou call common,” NIV “Do not call anything impure that God has made clean,” NWT 1984 “You stop calling defiled the things God has cleansed,” NWT 2013 “Stop calling defiled the things God has cleansed,” TLB “Don’t contradict God! If he says something is kosher, then it is,” which drops both verbs. Whether the vision speaks of foods, of people, or of both is read differently across the traditions, and this page does not vote; it prints what the text itself says. Peter’s own application, at v28, is to persons, and v34–35 widens it to nations; the debate over eating that follows is at 11:3. “Three times” (epi tris) closes the scene, and the vessel goes back to heaven.

Verses 17–23 · διηπόρει … ἄνδρες ζητοῦντές σε … μηδὲν διακρινόμενος … ἐχρηματίσθη … ἐξένισεν diēporei … andres zētountes se … mēden diakrinomenos … echrēmatisthē … exenisen — the men at the gate, Acts 10:17–23: “at a loss,” “hesitating over nothing,” “divinely instructed,” and Peter as host

“At a loss.” Diēporei is the verb of the Pentecost crowd, “astonished and at a loss” (Acts 2:12), of the priests who “were perplexed” at the empty prison (5:24) and of Herod over Jesus (Luke 9:7); it stands in four verses of the New Testament. In each, people stand before something they cannot classify; here it is Peter, and the thing is his own vision (horama). The knock arrives while he is still turning it over: the men “stood at the gate” (pylōn, a gateway).

“Men” — not “three men.” The Spirit’s words at v19 are Idou, andres zētountes se, “Look — men are seeking you.” The printed Greek marks a variant at this word and has no number; v7 supplies the count, two servants and a soldier. All seven versions on the shelf print “three”: KJV “Behold, three men seek thee,” ASV “Behold, three men seek thee,” Douay “Behold three men seek thee,” Geneva “Behold, three men seek thee,” NWT 1984 “Three men are seeking you,” TLB “Three men have come to see you,” and the NIV adds a name as well, “Simon, three men are looking for you,” where the Greek has no vocative. The number is not wrong; it is not in the text printed here. That the Spirit speaks to Peter in words is as at 8:29, where “the Spirit said to Philip.” The NWT 1984 prints “the spirit” here and “holy spirit” at vv38, 44, 45 and 47, in line with its reading of pneuma as God’s active force (a doctrinal rendering, flagged and not argued); the Greek prints pneuma the same way in every verse, and the capital letter, or its absence, is each version’s own choice.

“Hesitating over nothing.” Mēden diakrinomenos: diakrinō is “to separate, to distinguish,” and in the middle “to waver, to hesitate” (Matthew 21:21: “if you have faith and do not doubt”). In Acts it stands in four verses: here; 11:2, where the circumcised “were contending with him”; 11:12, where Peter reports the same words from the Spirit as “making no distinction”; and 15:9, “he made no distinction between us and them.” (The printed Greek marks a variant at 11:12.) So the instruction can be heard two ways: “do not waver” and “do not discriminate,” and the Greek does not choose; this translation prints the first and leaves the second to this note. KJV “doubting nothing,” ASV “nothing doubting,” Douay “doubting nothing,” Geneva “doubt nothing,” NIV “Do not hesitate to go with them,” NWT 1984 “not doubting at all,” TLB “All is well,” which drops the verb.

“Well spoken of” and “divinely instructed.” Martyroumenos, “being borne witness to,” is the participle of the Seven, “seven men of good testimony” (Acts 6:3), and in Acts the passive stands of four people whom others vouch for: Cornelius here, Timothy at 16:2 (in the imperfect) and Ananias at 22:12, besides the Seven. Echrēmatisthē is chrēmatizō, the verb of a message from heaven: the magi and Joseph are “warned in a dream” (Matthew 2:12, 2:22) and it “had been disclosed” to Simeon (Luke 2:26); and at 11:26 the same verb is what happens when the disciples at Antioch are “called Christians.” KJV “was warned from God by an holy angel,” ASV “was warned of God by a holy angel,” Geneva “was warned from heaven by an holy Angel,” Douay “received an answer of an holy angel,” NWT 1984 “was given divine instructions by a holy angel,” TLB “an angel had instructed him.”

Peter becomes host. Xenizō runs through the chapter: Peter “is a guest” with Simon at vv6, 18 and 32, and at v23, in exenisen, he “hosted” Cornelius’s men. The guest of a tanner has become, for a night, host to a centurion’s servants and soldier, before the chapter’s last verse has the Gentiles asking him to stay with them. (Hebrews 13:2, not yet on these pages, uses the participle of this very verb of those who “entertained angels.”) “Some of the brothers from Joppa” go along; Peter will count them at 11:12 as “these six brothers”. NWT 1984 “he invited them in and entertained them,” KJV “called he them in, and lodged them,” NIV “Peter invited the men into the house to be his guests,” TLB “lodged them overnight.”

Verses 24–29 · προσεκύνησεν … κἀγὼ αὐτὸς ἄνθρωπός εἰμι … ἀθέμιτόν … κολλᾶσθαι ἢ προσέρχεσθαι ἀλλοφύλῳ prosekynēsen … kagō autos anthrōpos eimi … athemiton … kollasthai ē proserchesthai allophylō — Cornelius at Peter’s feet, Acts 10:24–29: “bowed down,” “I also am a man,” and “unlawful… one of another tribe”

“Bowed down.” The household is already gathered, “relatives and close friends,” and prosekynēsen is the verb of bowing down: in Acts it stands in four verses, 7:43 (the figures “that you made to worship them”), 8:27 and 24:11 (going up to Jerusalem “to worship”), and here, the only one of the four whose object is a man. The verb covers homage and worship and the Greek does not choose; Peter’s answer is the evidence. He “raised him up” (ēgeiren, as he raised the lame man at 3:7) and said kagō autos anthrōpos eimi, “I myself also am a man.” The refusal has two echoes, one before and one after: the angel who stops John from bowing, “See that you do not! I am a fellow slave of yours” (Revelation 22:9), and Paul and Barnabas at Lystra, kai hēmeis homoiopatheis esmen hymin anthrōpoi (14:15). KJV, ASV and Geneva “worshipped him,” Douay “adored,” NWT 1984 “did obeisance to him,” NIV “fell at his feet in reverence,” TLB “fell to the floor before him in worship.” At v26 the NIV adds “only” (“I am only a man myself”) and the TLB “I’m not a god!”; the Greek says neither.

“Unlawful.” Athemiton stands in two verses of the New Testament: here and 1 Peter 4:3 (“lawless idolatries,” not yet on these pages). It is not anomos, “lawless,” and the word nomos, “law,” is not in the verse. Peter says what “you yourselves understand,” hymeis epistasthe, and does not name a command, and this page supplies none. The shelf supplies one in places: NIV “it is against our law for a Jew to associate with or visit a Gentile,” TLB “it is against the Jewish laws for me to come into a Gentile home like this,” and the Douay “how abominable it is”; KJV “an unlawful thing,” ASV “unlawful,” NWT 1984 “unlawful.” “To join himself” is kollasthai, the verb of the disciples who would not join the apostles at 5:13, of the Spirit’s “join yourself to this chariot” at 8:29 and of Saul at 9:26; here it comes round again, and it stands once more at 17:34.

“One of another tribe.” Allophylō is allos, “other,” and phylē, “tribe”; the word stands in this verse alone in the New Testament. It is reported, and not checked on these pages, that the Greek Old Testament uses it of the Philistines. Five editions print “another nation,” and so blur two Greek words, since ethnos, “nation,” stands in this same chapter for “the nation of the Jews” (v22), “every nation” (v35) and “the nations” (v45): KJV “one of another nation,” ASV “one of another nation,” Douay “one of another nation,” Geneva “one of another nation,” NWT 2013 “a man of another nation”; the others differ: NWT 1984 “a man of another race,” NIV “a Gentile.” Peter then applies the vision to a person: God showed him “to call no anthrōpos common or unclean,” where the sheet held animals and the voice spoke of food. “Without objection” is anantirrētōs, an adverb in this verse alone; its adjective is at 19:36.

Verses 30–33 · ἀπὸ τετάρτης ἡμέρας μέχρι ταύτης τῆς ὥρας … ἀνὴρ ἔστη ἐνώπιόν μου ἐν ἐσθῆτι λαμπρᾷ … ὑπὸ τοῦ κυρίου apo tetartēs hēmeras mechri tautēs tēs hōras … anēr estē enōpion mou en esthēti lampra … hypo tou kyriou — Cornelius retells his vision, Acts 10:30–33: “from the fourth day,” no word of fasting, and “commanded you by the Lord”

“From the fourth day.” Apo tetartēs hēmeras counts from the vision itself. The chapter names the days: the vision (day one); “on the next day” the men near Joppa (v9); “on the next day” Peter sets out (v23); “on the next day” he enters Caesarea (v24), the fourth. The shelf counts differently: NIV “Three days ago I was in my house praying at this hour,” KJV, ASV, Geneva, Douay and TLB “Four days ago,” NWT 1984 “Four days ago counting from this hour.” Inclusive and exclusive counting give four and three for the same interval, and the Greek says “the fourth day.”

No word of fasting. Cornelius’s own sentence is ēmēn tēn enatēn proseuchomenos en tō oikō mou, “I was praying the ninth hour in my house”; the printed Greek marks a variant at this clause and has no word for fasting. Two versions print it: KJV “I was fasting until this hour; and at the ninth hour I prayed in my house,” Geneva “I fasted, and at the ninth hour I prayed in mine house.” The others keep to the prayer: ASV “I was keeping the ninth hour of prayer in my house,” Douay “I was praying in my house, at the ninth hour,” NWT 1984 “I was praying in my house at the ninth hour.” In v3 the visitor is “an angel of God” and the messengers say “a holy angel” (v22); in Cornelius’s own telling he is anēr, “a man,” “in bright clothing” (en esthēti lampra), as the two at the tomb are “men” in dazzling clothing (Luke 24:4; v23 calls them a vision of angels) and the two at the ascension are “men” in white (Acts 1:10). This page prints “a man” and does not make him an angel by editing.

What the retelling leaves out, and what it keeps. The angel’s words come back almost verbatim (compare vv4–6 with vv31–32), with “your alms were remembered” (emnēsthēsan) for the “memorial.” At v32 the KJV and Geneva add “who, when he cometh, shall speak unto thee”; the printed Greek ends at “by the sea.” The last words differ in a way the shelf shows: Cornelius says the commands are “by the kyrios” (the printed Greek marks a variant here), and KJV “commanded thee of God,” Geneva “commanded thee of God,” ASV “commanded thee of the Lord,” Douay “commanded thee by the Lord,” NIV “everything the Lord has commanded you to tell us,” NWT 1984 and NWT 2013 “commanded by Jehovah to say.” Peter has been commanded by the voice he called kyrie (v14) and by the Spirit (v19), and the verse does not say which Cornelius means, so this translation prints “Lord” and notes that the NWT’s “Jehovah” is a reading of the same kyrios. “You did well to come” is kalōs epoiēsas paragenomenos, and the room, by its own account, has come “before God” (enōpion tou theou), the phrase that carried the memorial at v4.

Verses 34–36 · οὐκ ἔστιν προσωπολήμπτης ὁ θεός … δεκτὸς αὐτῷ ἐστιν … οὗτός ἐστιν πάντων κύριος ouk estin prosōpolēmptēs ho theos … dektos autō estin … houtos estin pantōn kyrios — Peter’s opening words, Acts 10:34–36: “God is not partial,” “welcome to him,” and the sentence that loses its verb

“In truth I am grasping.” Ep’ alētheias katalambanomai is a present middle, “I am taking hold,” of something he has just come to understand; the verb is the one for the council “perceiving” that Peter and John were unlettered (Acts 4:13). KJV “Of a truth I perceive,” Douay “In very deed I perceive,” NWT 1984 “For a certainty I perceive,” NWT 2013 “Now I truly understand,” NIV “I now realize how true it is,” TLB “I see very clearly.”

“Not partial.” Prosopolēmptēs, built from prosōpon, “face,” and lambanō, “take,” stands in this verse alone. The family stands in seven verses of the New Testament: this one; prosopolēmpsia at Romans 2:11 (“no partiality with God”), Ephesians 6:9, Colossians 3:25 and James 2:1; the verb prosopolēmpteō at James 2:9; and the adverb aprosōpolēmptōs at 1 Peter 1:17 (“judges impartially”). Ephesians 6:9, Colossians 3:25, James 2:1 and James 2:9 are not yet on these pages. The compound matches a Hebrew idiom, “to lift the face.” Deuteronomy 10:17 says of Jehovah that he “does not lift the face and does not take a bribe”; that negated phrase, lo yissa panim, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, there and Deuteronomy 28:50 (“that will not lift the face of an old man”). Leviticus 19:15 has lo tissa penei dal, literally “you shall not lift the face of the poor,” which this translation prints as “you shall not favor the poor.” KJV “God is no respecter of persons,” Douay “God is not a respecter of persons,” NWT 1984 “God is not partial,” NIV “God does not show favoritism,” TLB “the Jews are not God’s only favorites,” which turns a statement about God into a statement about the Jews.

“Welcome to him.” Dektos is the word of “Jehovah’s year of welcome” (Luke 4:19) and “no prophet is welcome in his hometown” (4:24); in Luke–Acts it stands in these two verses and this one. “In every nation” (en panti ethnei) is the first time Peter says it; at Pentecost the promise was “for all those far off” (Acts 2:39), and the Greek there does not say “nations.” Whether v35 says that sincere piety anywhere is welcome to God, or says who stands among those he welcomes before the message of v43 reaches them, is read both ways, and this page does not decide. KJV “is accepted with him,” ASV “is acceptable to him,” NWT 1984 “is acceptable to him,” NIV “accepts from every nation the one who fears him,” TLB “acceptable to him.”

The sentence that loses its verb. V36 begins with an accusative, ton logon hon apesteilen, “the word that he sent,” and no verb governs it; v37 starts afresh with hymeis oidate, “you yourselves know.” The shelf rescues the grammar in different ways: KJV “The word which God sent unto the children of Israel,” Douay “God sent the word to the children of Israel,” Geneva “Ye know the word which God hath sent,” NWT 1984 “He sent out the word to the sons of Israel,” NIV “You know the message God sent to the people of Israel,” TLB “I’m sure you have heard about the Good News for the people of Israel.” This translation prints the fragment with its dash and lets v37’s “you know” run into it. “This one is Lord of all” (houtos estin pantōn kyrios) has Jesus Christ as its referent, so “Lord” stays; the phrase pantōn kyrios stands in one verse of the New Testament, here; the same two words in the other order, kyrios pantōn, stand in two (searched): Romans 10:12 (now on these pages) and Galatians 4:1 (not yet on these pages). NWT 1984 prints “Lord of all [others],” adding a bracketed word that the Greek does not have; the NWT 2013 reads “Lord of all.”

Verses 37–38 · τὸ γενόμενον ῥῆμα … ἔχρισεν αὐτὸν ὁ θεὸς πνεύματι ἁγίῳ … εὐεργετῶν … καταδυναστευομένους to genomenon rhēma … echrisen auton ho theos pneumati hagiō … euergetōn … katadynasteuomenous — Acts 10:37–38: “the thing that happened,” “anointed with Holy Spirit and power,” “doing good,” and “oppressed by the devil”

“The thing that happened.” Rhēma is “word” and “thing,” and to genomenon rhēma is, in Greek, “the word that happened”: it is the shepherds’ “this thing that has happened” (Luke 2:15, to rhēma touto to gegonos). This translation prints “the thing that happened” at both. KJV “That word, I say, ye know, which was published throughout all Judaea,” Douay “You know the word which hath been published through all Judea,” NWT 1984 “YOU know the subject that was talked about,” NIV “You know what has happened throughout the province of Judea,” TLB “This message has spread all through Judea.” The participle that follows, arxamenos, “beginning,” is masculine and nominative, and does not agree with the neuter rhēma; it hangs. “The baptism that John proclaimed” uses ekēryxen, kēryssō, the verb of the apostles’ charge at v42, and Galilee stands as a region in three verses of the book: 9:31, here and 13:31.

“Anointed.” Echrisen is chriō: “the Spirit of Jehovah is upon me, because he has anointed me” (Luke 4:18), “your holy servant Jesus, whom you anointed” (Acts 4:27), here, and Hebrews 1:9. Peter’s sentence uses the verb of Jesus’s reading at Nazareth and of the congregation’s prayer, and adds “Holy Spirit and power.” The next verbs are two of the rarest in the chapter. Euergeteō, “doing good,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament; its noun euergetēs, “benefactor,” is the title kings and rulers collect (Luke 22:25), and Jesus answers, “But not so with you” (22:26); the court of Acts 4:9 was examining a “good deed.” Katadynasteuō, “to oppress,” stands in two verses, here and James 2:6, said of the rich (not yet on these pages); it is built on dynastēs, the noun of the Ethiopian court official at 8:27. KJV, ASV and Geneva “oppressed of the devil,” Douay “oppressed by the devil,” NWT 1984 “oppressed by the Devil,” NIV “under the power of the devil,” TLB “possessed by demons,” which names a different condition from the one the Greek names.

“Because God was with him.” Hoti ho theos ēn met’ autou is said of Joseph in Stephen’s speech, “and God was with him” (Acts 7:9), as Jehovah was with Joseph in Genesis 39:2, 3 and 21, and of Jesus by Nicodemus, “unless God is with him” (John 3:2). Peter’s sentence puts Jesus where the Joseph story put Joseph, without saying so. KJV “for God was with him,” NWT 1984 “because God was with him.”

Verses 39–41 · κρεμάσαντες ἐπὶ ξύλου … ἐμφανῆ γενέσθαι … μάρτυσι τοῖς προκεχειροτονημένοις … συνεφάγομεν καὶ συνεπίομεν kremasantes epi xylou … emphanē genesthai … martysi tois prokecheirotonēmenois … synephagomen kai synepiomen — witnesses, Acts 10:39–41: “hanging him on a tree,” “visible,” “chosen beforehand,” and “ate with him and drank with him”

“On a tree.” Xylon, “wood,” names the cross in five verses of the New Testament: Acts 5:30, here, 13:29, Galatians 3:13 and 1 Peter 2:24 (the last two not yet on these pages). The wording is that of Deuteronomy 21:22–23, “you hang him on a tree” and “one who is hanged is a curse of God.” The Gospels’ word for the cross is stauros (Mark 15:21), and it is not the word here. KJV, ASV, Douay and Geneva “tree,” NWT 1984 “stake,” NIV “cross,” TLB “cross.” The killing is aneilan, a third-person plural with no named subject, and Peter’s “we are witnesses” stands in the same sentence: the speaker is a Jew speaking to a Gentile household about what was done in “the country of the Jews and in Jerusalem.”

“To become visible.” Emphanē genesthai: emphanēs stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Romans 10:20, where Paul gives Isaiah the words emphanēs egenomēn tois eme mē eperōtōsin (now on these pages). The risen one is given to be seen “not to all the people but to witnesses.” KJV “shewed him openly,” Geneva “caused that he was showed openly,” ASV “gave him to be made manifest,” Douay “gave him to be made manifest,” NWT 1984 “granted him to become manifest,” NIV “caused him to be seen.”

“Chosen beforehand.” Prokecheirotonēmenois is the one verb in the New Testament with a pro- in front of this root; the root cheirotoneō stands in Acts 14:23 (elders appointed “in each congregation”) and in 2 Corinthians 8:19 (not yet on these pages), and keeps the picture of a hand raised in choosing, here with God as the one choosing. KJV “witnesses chosen before of God,” Douay “witnesses preordained by God,” NIV “witnesses whom God had already chosen,” NWT 1984 “witnesses appointed beforehand by God,” TLB “certain witnesses God had selected beforehand.”

“Ate with him and drank with him.” Synephagomen kai synepiomen: synepiomen, “drank with,” stands in this verse alone in the New Testament, and synephagomen, “ate with,” is a form of synesthiō, the verb of Luke 15:2 (“This man welcomes sinners and eats with them”) and of 11:3, where the circumcised say to Peter “You went in to uncircumcised men and ate with them.” One verb is the apostles’ claim about the risen Jesus and the charge against Peter. In Galatians 2:12 Paul says that before certain men came from James, Peter meta tōn ethnōn synēsthien (not yet on these pages). The meals behind v41 are in Luke 24:41–43 (a piece of broiled fish, “he took it and ate it before them”), John 21:13 and Acts 1:4, where synalizomenos is the crux. KJV “who did eat and drink with him,” NWT 1984 “who ate and drank with him.”

Verses 42–43 · ὁ ὡρισμένος ὑπὸ τοῦ θεοῦ κριτὴς ζώντων καὶ νεκρῶν … πάντες οἱ προφῆται μαρτυροῦσιν ho hōrismenos hypo tou theou kritēs zōntōn kai nekrōn … pantes hoi prophētai martyrousin — Acts 10:42–43: “judge of living and dead” and “all the prophets bear witness”

“Determined by God as judge.” Ho hōrismenos is the participle of horizō, the verb of “the determined plan” at Acts 2:23 and of “a man whom he determined” at Paul’s Athens address (17:31); this translation keeps “determined” here and at 2:23. Kritēs zōntōn kai nekrōn, “judge of living and dead,” is the commission the apostles carry: “to proclaim to the people and to testify solemnly” (kēryxai, diamartyrasthai, the second the verb of 8:25). KJV “ordained of God to be the Judge of quick and dead,” ASV “ordained of God to be the Judge of the living and the dead,” Douay “appointed by God, to be judge of the living and of the dead,” NIV “appointed as judge of the living and the dead,” NWT 1984 “decreed by God to be judge of the living and the dead,” TLB “ordained of God to be the Judge of all.”

“All the prophets bear witness.” Toutō pantes hoi prophētai martyrousin names no prophet and quotes no verse. Peter has said it before in two forms: “what God announced beforehand by the mouth of all the prophets” (3:18) and “all the prophets who have spoken, from Samuel” (3:24), and the risen Jesus did it before him, “beginning from Moses and from all the prophets” (Luke 24:27). Aphesin hamartiōn, “forgiveness of sins,” is the phrase of 2:38 and 5:31, where it is given with repentance; here the verb is labein, “to receive,” and the condition is “everyone who believes in him.” V44 breaks in before Peter has finished. KJV “remission of sins,” ASV “remission of sins,” Douay “remission of sins,” NIV “forgiveness of sins through his name,” NWT 1984 “forgiveness of sins through his name,” TLB “will have their sins forgiven through his name.”

Verses 44–46 · ἐπέπεσε τὸ πνεῦμα τὸ ἅγιον … ἐξέστησαν οἱ ἐκ περιτομῆς πιστοὶ … ἡ δωρεὰ τοῦ ἁγίου πνεύματος ἐκκέχυται … λαλούντων γλώσσαις καὶ μεγαλυνόντων τὸν θεόν epepese to pneuma to hagion … exestēsan hoi ek peritomēs pistoi … hē dōrea tou hagiou pneumatos ekkechytai … lalountōn glōssais kai megalynontōn ton theon — the Spirit falls, Acts 10:44–46: “while he was still speaking,” the amazed believers, “the gift… poured out” and tongues

“Fell upon.” Epepese is the verb of 8:16, where the Spirit “had not yet fallen on any of them,” and of 11:15, where Peter says hōsper kai eph’ hēmas en archē; the Spirit is the subject of this verb in these three verses only. “While Peter was still speaking” (eti lalountos) breaks the speech off, and there are no hands and no water: at 8:17 the Spirit is received when hands are laid, at 9:17 Ananias lays hands that Saul may be “filled with Holy Spirit” and baptism follows, and at 2:38 the gift follows baptism. Acts follows more than one order, and this page does not supply one.

“Amazed.” Exestēsan is the verb of the crowd at Pentecost (2:7, 2:12) and of Samaria around Simon the magician (8:9); the noun of the same root is the trance of v10. The amazed are hoi ek peritomēs pistoi, “the believers from the circumcision,” and the same hoi ek peritomēs are the ones who contend with Peter at 11:2. KJV “they of the circumcision which believed were astonished,” Douay “the faithful of the circumcision… were astonished,” NIV “The circumcised believers… were astonished,” NWT 1984 “the faithful ones that had come with Peter who were of those circumcised were amazed,” TLB “The Jews who came with Peter were amazed.” “The nations” (ta ethnē) is the word of the Lord’s own sentence about Saul, “before nations and kings” (9:15).

“The gift… poured out.” Dōrea is “the gift of the Holy Spirit” at 2:38, “the gift of God” that Simon thought he could buy (8:20), here, and 11:17; it stands in those four verses of Acts. Ekkechytai, “has been poured out,” is the verb of Joel at Pentecost, “I will pour out from my Spirit on all flesh” (2:17), in the perfect tense. KJV “was poured out the gift of the Holy Ghost,” NWT 1984 “people of the nations,” NIV “poured out even on Gentiles,” TLB “given to Gentiles too.”

“Tongues” and “magnifying God.” Glōssais is the word of 2:4, and megalynontōn ton theon, “magnifying God,” has the root of ta megaleia tou theou, “the great things of God,” that the crowd heard at 2:11; the verb stands in five verses of Luke–Acts, among them Mary’s “My soul magnifies Jehovah” (Luke 1:46). The proof offered is what Peter’s companions heard, ēkouon gar autōn. NWT 1984 “speaking with tongues and magnifying God,” NWT 2013 “speaking in foreign languages and magnifying God,” NIV “speaking in tongues and praising God,” KJV “speak with tongues, and magnify God.” Peter “answered” (apekrithē), though he has not been asked a question: the verb answers what has happened.

Verses 47–48 · Μήτι τὸ ὕδωρ δύναται κωλῦσαί τις … ἐν τῷ ὀνόματι Ἰησοῦ Χριστοῦ βαπτισθῆναι Mēti to hydōr dynatai kōlysai tis … en tō onomati Iēsou Christou baptisthēnai — Acts 10:47–48: “Can anyone forbid the water?”, “in the name of Jesus Christ” and “stay some days”

“Can anyone forbid the water?” The question is built with mēti, the particle that expects the answer no. It answers the eunuch’s “What prevents me from being baptized?” (8:36), with the same verb, kōlyō; the verb stands in the three baptism questions of Acts, 8:36, here and 11:17 (egō tis ēmēn dynatos kōlysai ton theon), and it is Jesus’s verb of “do not hinder them” for the children (Luke 18:16). “Just as we also did” (hōs kai hēmeis) is Peter’s equality, and it comes back at 11:15 and 11:17 and at 15:8, “giving them the Holy Spirit just as he did to us,” where Peter recalls this scene: “God made a choice among you, that by my mouth the nations should hear the word” (15:7). KJV “Can any man forbid water,” ASV “Can any man forbid the water,” NWT 1984 “Can anyone forbid water,” NWT 2013 “Can anyone deny water,” NIV “stand in the way of their being baptized with water,” which adds “Surely” and “with water” and splits the sentence in two, TLB “Can anyone object to my baptizing them.”

“In the name of Jesus Christ.” Three prepositions carry the name in three baptism formulas: epi tō onomati at 2:38, eis to onoma at 8:16 and en tō onomati here; the chapter does not choose among them. The printed Greek marks a variant at the name, and the shelf splits: KJV “in the name of the Lord,” Geneva “in the Name of the Lord,” Douay “in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ,” ASV “in the name of Jesus Christ,” NWT 1984 “in the name of Jesus Christ,” NIV “in the name of Jesus Christ.” The verb is a passive, baptisthēnai, and the Greek does not say who baptized; TLB “So he did, baptizing them,” and has Cornelius beg Peter to stay, where the Greek says only “they asked.”

“To stay some days.” Epimeinai hēmeras tinas, “to stay on some days,” follows Peter’s stay of “considerable days” with the tanner (9:43): a man who has been a guest and a host (v23) is asked to stay under a Gentile roof. The chapter does not say what was eaten there; the accusation is at 11:3. KJV “tarry certain days,” ASV “tarry certain days,” Douay “tarry with them some days,” NWT 1984 “remain for some days.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “centurion” for hekatontarchēs at v1, as at Luke 7:2; “cohort” for speira at v1, as at Matthew 27:27; “fearing God” for phoboumenos ton theon at vv2 and 22, and “the one who fears him” at v35; “alms” for eleēmosynē at vv2, 4 and 31, as at 3:2 and 9:36; “memorial” for mnēmosynon at v4, against “in memory” at Matthew 26:13 and Mark 14:9; “tanner” for byrseus at vv6 and 32, as at 9:43; “constant attendance” for proskarterountōn at v7, where 1:14 and 6:4 have “devoting themselves”; “trance” for ekstasis at v10; “vessel” for skeuos at vv11 and 16, as at 9:15; “slaughter” for thyō at v13, kept apart from “killed” for aneilan at v39; “common” for koinos at vv14 and 28 and “make common” for koinoō at v15; “cleansed” for katharizō at v15, as at 15:9; “at a loss” for diaporeō at v17, as at 2:12; “hesitating” for diakrinomai at v20; “well spoken of” for martyroumenos at v22, as “of good testimony” at 6:3; “hosted” and “a guest” for xenizō at vv6, 18, 23 and 32; “bowed down” for proskyneō at v25, as at Matthew 2:2; “join himself” for kollaomai at v28, as at 5:13, 8:29 and 9:26; “tribe” for allophylos at v28, kept apart from “nation” for ethnos at vv22, 35 and 45; “unlawful” for athemitos at v28; “partial” for prosopolēmptēs at v34; “welcome” for dektos at v35, as at Luke 4:19 and 4:24; “thing” for rhēma at v37, as at Luke 2:15; “proclaim” for kēryssō at vv37 and 42, as at 8:5; “anointed” for chriō at v38, as at 4:27; “tree” for xylon at v39, as at 5:30; “determined” for horizō at v42, as at 2:23; “fell upon” for epipiptō at v44, as at 8:16; “amazed” for existēmi at v45, as at 9:21; “nations” for ethnē at v45, as at 9:15; “forbid” for kōlyō at v47, against “prevents” at 8:36; and “Lord” for kyrios at vv4, 14, 33 and 36, where no verse of the chapter has the Hebrew Bible’s God as its referent: the speaker of v4 is an angel, the voice of v14 is the one Peter answers, v33 is open, and v36 is Jesus Christ.

The Spirit speaking — Acts 8:29, at v19. No distinction — Acts 15:9, at v20. Of good testimony — Acts 6:3, at v22. Joining himself — Acts 5:13, 8:29 and 9:26, at v28. The good deed — Acts 4:9 and Luke 22:25, at v38. “God was with him” — Acts 7:9, Genesis 39:2 and John 3:2, at v38. The tree — Acts 5:30 and Deuteronomy 21:22–23, at v39.
