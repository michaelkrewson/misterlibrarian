Furious that his converts are being talked into circumcision by rival missionaries, Paul opens the one letter with no thanksgiving — insisting his gospel came straight from a revelation and not from Jerusalem, with a travelogue designed to prove how little contact he had had with the human apostles.
⚠ DISPUTED, and the dispute is the same one as 'which Galatia'. On the SOUTH-GALATIAN reading (the Roman province, the towns of the first journey) the letter can be c. AD 48 — possibly Paul's EARLIEST, written before the Jerusalem council of Acts 15. On the NORTH-GALATIAN reading (the ethnic Celtic highlands) it belongs to the mid-50s, near Romans, whose argument it anticipates in rougher form. This library reports both and does not choose.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–5 · the angriest opening Paul ever wrote

⚠ Read the salutation for what it leaves out. Every other letter of Paul begins with warmth — “grace to you,” then a paragraph of thanks for the readers (even the fractious Corinthians got one). This letter has no thanksgiving at all. He writes “grace and peace,” and then, where the thanks should stand, drops straight into “I am astonished…” (verse 6). The omission is the loudest thing in the chapter: a first-century reader, expecting the standard courtesy, would feel the cold the instant it did not come.

“An apostle — not from men nor through a man.” The whole letter is compressed into the first six words. Paul’s opponents in Galatia evidently argued that his authority was second-hand — derived from the Jerusalem apostles, and therefore subordinate to them — so before he says anything else he denies it twice, with two different prepositions: his commission is not from a human source (apo) and did not come through a human agent (dia). It came “through Jesus Christ and God the Father who raised him from the dead.” The rest of the chapter is the evidence for that one sentence.

⚠ “To rescue us out of the present evil age.” Ek tou aiōnos tou enestōtos ponērou — and this is startling in a first line. Paul does not describe salvation here as forgiveness or as heaven-after-death but as a rescue operation, an extraction from a world-order that is actively evil and still running (enestōs, “present, standing now”). The word aiōn means an age or era, the whole temper of a time — not the physical world (kosmos) but its ruling spirit. The gospel is a jailbreak from the present. KJV “deliver us from this present evil world”; ASV “out of this present evil world”; NWT “the present wicked system of things,” which catches the sense of an era with a character even as it modernises the phrase.

Verses 6–9 · a different gospel that is no gospel, and a curse said twice

⚠ “A DIFFERENT gospel — which is not ANOTHER.” Two Greek words for “other,” and the distinction is the whole point. Heteron (different in kind) and allo (another of the same kind): the Galatians are chasing a heteron euangelion, a gospel of a different species — and Paul immediately says it is not an allo, not a second real gospel alongside his, because there is only one and this is a counterfeit. English cannot easily carry two words that both come out “other,” so the note has to do the work. KJV blurs it (“another gospel: which is not another”); NIV gets it (“a different gospel — which is really no gospel at all”).

“Turn the gospel inside out.” Metastrepsai — to twist around, reverse, turn into its opposite. The agitators are not adding a footnote; they are inverting the thing. Who they were is the great background question of the letter: Jewish-Christian missionaries — the “circumcision party,” often called the Judaizers — who followed Paul into his congregations and taught that Gentile converts must be circumcised and keep the law of Moses to be fully saved. Paul’s entire reply is that adding the law to Christ does not improve the gospel, it destroys it.

⚠ “Let him be accursed.” Anathema — a thing devoted to God for destruction, the Greek Old Testament’s word for what is put under the ban and handed over to be annihilated (the cherem of Jericho). It is a terrible word, and Paul says it twice, in consecutive verses, and includes himself and an angel from heaven in its scope: not even the messenger, not even the founder, is above the message. This is where English “anathema” comes from, and it is worth knowing it began as a curse of total destruction, not a mere strong disapproval. KJV and ASV “let him be accursed”; Douay “let him be anathema,” keeping the raw word; NWT “let him be accursed.”

Verse 10 · the hinge

⚠ A single verse turns the accusation back on the accusers. Having just pronounced a double curse, Paul asks: does that sound like a man trying to win a popularity contest? The charge against him seems to have been that he was a crowd-pleaser — a flatterer who dropped the law’s hard demands to make conversion easy for Gentiles. He answers by pointing at the curse he has just uttered: no people-pleaser talks like this. Anthrōpous peithō — “do I persuade men?” can also mean “do I seek to conciliate men?” And the sting is in the last clause: “if I were still pleasing men, I would not be a slave of Christ.” The two masters are mutually exclusive; you cannot serve the applause and the Lord at once. KJV “do I now persuade men, or God?”; NIV “Am I now trying to win the approval of human beings, or of God?”

Verses 11–12 · the thesis of the autobiography

“Not according to man… through a revelation.” Here is the claim the next chapter and a half will defend with dates and travel logs: Paul’s gospel did not reach him down a human chain of teaching. He did not get it from Jerusalem, from Peter, from any human instructor — he got it di’ apokalypseōs, through an unveiling, of Jesus Christ. The genitive is deliberately ambiguous: a revelation whose content is Jesus Christ, or a revelation given by Jesus Christ — and both are true, which is why Paul does not resolve it.

⚠ Apokalypsis. The same word this library met at the head of the last book of the Bible — an un-covering, the drawing-back of a veil. Paul uses it here of his own Damascus-road experience, which Acts narrates three times as an event on the ground; Paul, from inside, calls it an apocalypse. The point of insisting on it is not mysticism but independence: a truth handed to him directly cannot be revised by a committee in Jerusalem. KJV “by the revelation of Jesus Christ”; NWT “through a revelation by Jesus Christ,” choosing the “given-by” sense the note leaves open.

Verses 13–14 · the persecutor, in his own words

⚠ “My former way of life in Judaism.” Ioudaismos — and this is one of the earliest appearances of the word anywhere. It was coined in the Maccabean literature (2 Maccabees) to name the fierce loyalty to the ancestral law that resisted Greek assimilation, and it carries that militant edge here: not “the Jewish religion” as a neutral label but zealotry for the tradition, the thing Paul excelled at. He is describing, precisely, the mindset his opponents in Galatia now have — which is why he tells the story: I was more you than you are, and I left it.

“Beyond all measure I was persecuting… and trying to destroy it.” The two verbs are brutal and deliberate. Ediōkon is the imperfect — “kept on persecuting,” a sustained campaign, not a moment’s zeal — and eporthoun, “was ravaging,” is a word used of an army sacking a city. KJV “beyond measure I persecuted the church of God, and wasted it”; NWT “I kept intensely persecuting the congregation of God and devastating it.” Acts shows him at Stephen’s stoning and “breathing threats and murder” on the road to Damascus; he does not soften any of it. That the destroyer became the builder is the evidence: a change like that is not something a man teaches himself.

“Zealous for the traditions of my fathers.” Zēlōtēs, zealot — not necessarily the political party, but the temperature; and paradosis, “tradition,” the handed-down oral law the Pharisees prized. Paul the Pharisee had been a specialist in exactly the thing his opponents are now selling to the Galatians.

Verses 15–17 · called before birth, and the road he did NOT take

⚠ “Set me apart from my mother’s womb.” Aphorisas — “set apart,” from the same root as Pharisee (the “separated ones”), which is a dry irony in the mouth of a former Pharisee, and the same word he opens Romans with. But the phrasing is lifted straight from the prophets’ call-narratives: Jeremiah “before you were formed in the womb I knew you… I appointed you” (Jeremiah 1:5) and the Servant of Isaiah “called me from the womb” (Isaiah 49:1). Paul is quietly placing his own call in the prophetic line — not a convert who volunteered, but a man commissioned before he existed.

“To reveal his Son IN me.” Not merely to me, but en emoi, in me — the revelation was also a transformation; the persecutor became the place the Son was displayed.

⚠ “I did not consult with flesh and blood… but went away into Arabia.” The point of the itinerary is what is missing from it. After the Damascus-road revelation Paul did not go up to Jerusalem to be validated by the apostles — he went the opposite direction, into Arabia (the Nabataean kingdom to the south and east, whose capital was Petra). Every step in this travelogue is chosen to prove one thing: he owed his gospel to no human authority, because for years he was nowhere near the human authorities. What he did in Arabia he never says; the silence is not evasion but argument. KJV “I conferred not with flesh and blood.”

Verses 18–20 · a fifteen-day visit, and an oath

⚠ “After three years… to get to know Cephas.” The dates are the argument. Three years after his call — not three weeks — he finally went to Jerusalem, and even then only to visit Cephas (Peter’s Aramaic name), for fifteen days, and saw no other apostle but one. The verb historēsai is telling: it means to visit in order to get acquainted, to make someone’s personal acquaintance (it is the root of “history,” inquiry) — a social call, not a training course. KJV “to see Peter”; ASV “to visit Cephas”; NWT “to visit Cephas” — all softer than the Greek’s “get personally acquainted with.” A man does not learn a gospel from a two-week houseguest visit three years into preaching it. The whole point is that Paul’s message was already fixed and independent before he had exchanged a serious word with the Jerusalem leadership.

⚠ “James the Lord’s brother.” James — not one of the Twelve (both of those were dead or elsewhere), but Jesus’ own brother, who had not believed during the ministry (John 7:5, met in the Acts 1 note) and who by this point led the Jerusalem congregation. Paul calls him an apostle here in the broad sense, and the phrase “the Lord’s brother” is one of the plainest New Testament statements that Jesus had siblings — a fact later tradition would work hard to explain away.

The oath. “Look, before God — I am not lying.” Paul swears to the accuracy of the itinerary, which tells you the itinerary was disputed: his opponents were evidently telling a different version of his early movements, one in which he had been trained and sent by Jerusalem. The oath is a man staking his integrity on a chronology.

Verses 21–24 · a face they never saw, and God glorified in a rumor

“Unknown by face to the congregations of Judea.” Still the same argument, closing it: the very churches nearest the apostles had never even met Paul. He could not have been their pupil; they knew him only as a rumor. His gospel and theirs matched not because he copied it but because both came from the same source.

⚠ The most human sentence in the chapter. What the Judean churches actually said about him — and Paul quotes it, in their voice — is: “the one who once persecuted us is now announcing the faith he once tried to destroy.” It is the whole gospel in a rumor: the ravager become the herald. KJV “He which persecuted us in times past now preacheth the faith which once he destroyed”; ASV “now preacheth the faith of which he once made havoc.” And the response was not applause for Paul but worship of God — “they were glorifying God because of me.” After twenty verses of self-defense, the chapter ends by pointing away from himself entirely. The persecuted were praising God for the persecutor, which is the only kind of vindication Paul ever actually wanted.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: the missing thanksgiving preserved by not manufacturing one — the letter is meant to feel cold at the top; “rescue us out of the present evil age” for aiōn, an era with a character, not the physical world; “a different gospel — which is not another” keeping the heteron/allo distinction the note explains; “turn inside out” for metastrepsai; “let him be accursed” for anathema, a thing devoted to destruction, said twice; “slave” for doulos, as in Romans; “Judaism” for Ioudaismos, the militant loyalty the word originally named; “set me apart from my mother’s womb” keeping the prophetic-call echo; and “to get to know Cephas” for historēsai, a social visit and not a course of instruction.

Echoes paid. Jeremiah 1:5 and Isaiah 49:1’s call “from the womb” standing under Paul’s own aphorisas ek koilias; aphorizō, “set apart,” carried over from the opening of Romans 1; anathema as the Greek Old Testament’s cherem, the ban of total destruction; James “the Lord’s brother” picking up the unbelieving brothers who were nevertheless in the room at Acts 1:14; and apokalypsis, the un-veiling, the same word that titles Revelation.

Echoes planted. The one gospel against every “different” one, which chapters 3–4 will defend from Abraham and the law; “not through man”, the independence claim that the second Jerusalem visit (chapter 2) and the confrontation with Peter at Antioch will test in public; flesh (sarx), introduced here as “flesh and blood” and about to become the letter’s great antagonist to the Spirit (5:16–25); and the present evil age, the world-order the crucified Christ has broken open, which the whole argument for freedom rests on.

Next in this book: Galatians 2 — the second Jerusalem visit fourteen years later and the agreement that Gentiles need not be circumcised; Titus, a Greek, deliberately not compelled; the “false brothers secretly brought in to spy out our freedom”; the pillars — James, Cephas and John — giving Paul the right hand of fellowship; and the great public collision at Antioch, where Paul “opposed Cephas to his face, because he stood condemned” — ending in the sentence that is the seed of the whole letter: “a person is not justified by works of the law but through faith in Jesus Christ.”
