Proverbs of Solomon on a good name, the rich and the poor, training a youth, borrowing and lending, the generous eye and the sluggard's lion; then, at verse 17, the opening of “the words of the wise”, a collection with close parallels in the Egyptian Instruction of Amenemope.
Verses 1–16 belong to the collection headed “The proverbs of Solomon” (reigned c. 970–931 BC); verses 17–29 open a separate collection of “the words of the wise”, whose parallels with an Egyptian work of roughly 1300–1075 BC suggest an older source. The book keeps no calendar of its own.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–2 · “A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches” — a name with no adjective, and the rich and the poor who meet

A name. “A name is to be chosen rather than great riches, good favor rather than silver and gold”: the Hebrew says only shem, “a name”; the word “good” belongs to the second line, chen tov, “good favor”. A name here is what others say about a person, the thing Babel grasped at, “let us make a name for ourselves” (Genesis 11:4, now on these pages). Nivchar, “to be chosen”, is from bachar, to choose, and chen is the favor Noah “found in the eyes of Jehovah” (Genesis 6:8). The NWT 1984 alone keeps the name bare, “A name is to be chosen rather than abundant riches”; the KJV has “A good name is rather to be chosen than great riches”; the Douay “A good name is better than great riches”. The Greek, like the KJV, gives the name its adjective, onoma kalon.

The rich and the poor meet. “The rich and the poor meet; the maker of them all is Jehovah”: nifgashu, they meet each other, on the road or in the market, and neither can pretend the other was made by someone else. Rash, “poor”, is the first of three words for poverty in this chapter: rash (vv2, 7) and dal (vv9, 16, 22) are both “poor” here, and ani (v22) is “the afflicted”, the word of the written text at Proverbs 3:34 (now on these pages). The same pairing returns with a different ending, “the poor and the oppressor meet; Jehovah gives light to the eyes of both” (Proverbs 29:13). The ASV has “The rich and the poor meet together: Jehovah is the maker of them all”; the NWT 1984 “The rich one and the one of little means have met each other”; the Douay “the maker of them both”, with the Greek, which reads amphoterous, “both”.

Verses 3–5 · “A prudent man foreseeth the evil” — the shrewd and the simple, the heels of humility, and thorns and traps

The shrewd and the simple. “The shrewd sees evil and hides himself, but the simple pass on and pay the penalty”: arum, “shrewd”, is the serpent’s adjective, “more crafty than any wild animal” (Genesis 3:1, now on these pages), and the root of ormah, the “shrewdness” chapter 1 offers the simple (Proverbs 1:4). Here the quality keeps its owner alive: he sees the trouble coming. The simple, peta’im, walk on into it, and ne’enashu, “pay the penalty”, is the verb of a fine: “the one responsible shall surely be fined” (Exodus 21:22, now on these pages). The written text has va-yissater, read ve-nistar, “and hides himself”. The saying stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and almost word for word at 27:12 (Proverbs 27:12). The KJV has “A prudent man foreseeth the evil, and hideth himself”; the NWT 1984 “Shrewd is the one that has seen the calamity and proceeds to conceal himself”; the Douay “the simple passed on, and suffered loss”.

On the heels of humility. “On the heels of humility, the fear of Jehovah, come riches and honor and life”: ekev is the heel, and then whatever follows on the heel of something, as in “on the heel of your listening to these ordinances” (Deuteronomy 7:12, now on these pages); Jacob’s name comes from the same root, the brother born grasping a heel (Genesis 25:26). The Hebrew sets two nouns side by side with no “and”, anavah, humility, and yir’at YHVH, the fear of Jehovah, and readings differ: either the fear of Jehovah is itself what humility leads to, or the two are a pair whose reward is riches, honor and life. The ASV takes them as a pair, “The reward of humility and the fear of Jehovah Is riches, and honor, and life”; the NWT 1984 does too, marking its added word, “The result of humility [and] the fear of Jehovah is riches and glory and life”; the NIV makes them one thing, with riches and honor and life as “its wages”. Anavah is from the root of anav, the humble to whom Jehovah “gives grace” (3:34); the Greek has “wisdom” instead, genea sophias, “the offspring of wisdom”. “Riches and honor” are what wisdom held in her left hand (3:16).

Thorns and traps. “Thorns and traps are in the way of the crooked; whoever guards his soul keeps far from them”: again two nouns with no “and”, tsinnim pachim. Iqqesh, “crooked”, is the twisted one, the word of Moses’ song, “a generation crooked and warped” (Deuteronomy 32:5, now on these pages). The ASV has “Thorns and snares are in the way of the perverse: He that keepeth his soul shall be far from them”; the NWT 1984 “Thorns [and] traps are in the way of the crooked one”; the Douay, from the Latin, “Arms and swords are in the way of the perverse”. The Greek has “thistles and snares in crooked ways”.

Verse 6 · “Train up a child in the way he should go” — chanakh, “his way”, and a verse the Greek does not have

Train up. “Train up a youth according to his way”: chanokh, from chanakh, the verb of a new house and a new temple. “What man is there who has built a new house and has not dedicated it?” (Deuteronomy 20:5, now on these pages); Solomon and all Israel “dedicated the house of Jehovah” (1 Kings 8:63). The verb stands in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: Deuteronomy 20:5, 1 Kings 8:63, its parallel in 2 Chronicles 7:5, and here. Its noun is chanukkah, the “dedication-offering of the altar” (Numbers 7:10), the word that gave the festival of Hanukkah its name. So the training is a beginning: a young life put to its first use, as a house is opened or an altar first used. The KJV and ASV have “Train up a child in the way he should go”; the NWT 1984 “Train up a boy according to the way for him”; the Geneva “Teach a child in the trade of his way”; the NIV “Start children off”, which keeps the beginning.

His way. Al pi darko is “according to the mouth of his way”, and the Hebrew does not say whose way is meant. Readings differ. The familiar “the way he should go” supplies the words “should go”, so the way is the right one, set by the parent. Others read the way as the young person’s own, his bent or his capacity, which the NWT 1984’s “the way for him” comes close to. And some take the verse as a warning in the shape of a promise: give a youth his own way, and he will never leave it. This translation prints “his way” and leaves the question where the Hebrew leaves it. Na’ar, “youth”, covers a wide span of age: Joseph at seventeen “was a lad” (Genesis 37:2, now on these pages). “Even when he grows old”, yazqin, is the verb of the elder, zaqen.

A verse the Greek does not have. The Greek translation of Proverbs, in Rahlfs’ edition, passes from verse 5 straight to verse 7; the most quoted verse of the chapter is not in it. The Douay, following the Latin, opens “It is a proverb: A young man according to his way, even when he is old he will not depart from it”, with no verb of training at all. The TLB has “Teach a child to choose the right path”.

Verses 7–9 · “The borrower is servant to the lender” — one verb for both sides of the loan, sowing and reaping, and the good eye

The borrower and the lender. “The rich rules over the poor, and the borrower is servant to the man who lends”: loveh and malveh are one verb, lavah, used for both sides of the loan, so the proverb is built from a single root. Deuteronomy promises Israel the lender’s side, “you will lend to many nations, and you yourself will not borrow” (Deuteronomy 28:12, now on these pages). Eved, “servant”, is also the word for a slave. The KJV has “the borrower is servant to the lender”; the NIV “the borrower is slave to the lender”; the NWT 1984 “servant to the man doing the lending”.

Sowing and reaping. “Whoever sows injustice will reap trouble, and the rod of his fury will come to an end”: avlah, injustice, is a form of avel, and aven, trouble, is also wickedness and nothingness. Paul’s “whatever a person sows, that he will also reap” (Galatians 6:7, now on these pages) has the same field. “The rod of his fury” is the shevet he beat others with, and it gives out. The written text has yiqtsor, read yiqtsar, the same verb. The ASV has “He that soweth iniquity shall reap calamity; And the rod of his wrath shall fail”; the KJV “shall reap vanity”; the NWT 1984 “will reap what is hurtful”.

The cheerful giver. After this verse the Greek adds a line the Hebrew does not have: andra hilaron kai dotēn eulogei ho theos, “God blesses a cheerful man and a giver”. Paul’s “God loves a cheerful giver” (2 Corinthians 9:7, now on these pages) is that line, with “blesses” turned to “loves”. Rahlfs prints it as verse 8a.

The good eye. “Whoever has a good eye will be blessed, for he has given of his bread to the poor”: tov ayin, “good of eye”, stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Its opposite, ra ayin, “evil of eye”, is the stingy man of 23:6 and 28:22 (Proverbs 23:6), and the law warns against the same eye, “and your eye be evil toward your needy brother and you give him nothing” (Deuteronomy 15:9, now on these pages). In Hebrew generosity is a way of looking. The ASV has “He that hath a bountiful eye shall be blessed”; the Geneva “He that hath a good eye, he shall be blessed”; the NWT 1984 “He that is kindly in eye”. The Douay adds another line found in the Greek: “He that maketh presents shall purchase victory and honour: but he carrieth away the souls of the receivers.”

Verses 10–12 · “Cast out the scorner” — contention leaves with him, the king’s friend, and the words Jehovah twists

Drive out the scoffer. “Drive out the scoffer, and contention goes out, and lawsuits and disgrace cease”: the lets is the scoffer of 1:22 and 3:34, the hardest of the fools, and when he leaves the quarrel leaves with him. Din is a legal claim, the word of “between claim and claim” (Deuteronomy 17:8, now on these pages); qalon, “disgrace”, is the last word of 3:35. The Greek puts the scene in a council, synedrion: “for when he sits in the council he dishonors everyone”. The KJV has “Cast out the scorner, and contention shall go out”; the NWT 1984 “Drive away the ridiculer, that contention may go out and that legal contest and dishonor may cease”.

The king’s friend. “Whoever loves purity of heart, with grace on his lips — the king is his friend”: the Hebrew runs three phrases together without joining them, and every translation has to decide how they connect. The written text has tehor, read tehar, “purity”. The ASV has “He that loveth pureness of heart, For the grace of his lips the king will be his friend”; the NWT 1984 shows the break with a dash, “The one loving purity of heart—for the charm of his lips the king will be his companion”. The Greek makes the lover kyrios, God, and adds that the blameless are acceptable to him.

He twists the words. “The eyes of Jehovah guard knowledge, but he twists the words of the treacherous”: va-ysallef divrei boged. These are the verb and the object of the law against bribes, which “twists the words of the righteous” (Exodus 23:8; Deuteronomy 16:19, both now on these pages). “And twists the words of” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, those two and this one: the bribe twists the words of the righteous, and Jehovah twists the words of the traitor. Natsar, “guard”, is the verb of “those who guard his testimonies” (Psalm 119:2, now on these pages). The ASV has “The eyes of Jehovah preserve him that hath knowledge; But he overthroweth the words of the treacherous man”; the NWT 1984 “but he subverts the words of the treacherous one”.

Verses 13–16 · “There is a lion without” — the sluggard’s excuse, the deep pit, folly bound in the heart, and the rod of discipline

A lion outside. “The sluggard says, ‘There is a lion outside! In the middle of the squares I will be murdered!’”: eratseach is from ratsach, the verb of the commandment “You shall not murder” (Exodus 20:13, printed there in verse 12, which follows the Hebrew numbering). The word belongs to a crime, not to an animal; the excuse imagines the lion as a criminal waiting in the public square. The sluggard says nearly the same thing again at 26:13 (Proverbs 26:13). The NWT 1984 has “In the midst of the public squares I shall be murdered!”; the KJV “I shall be slain in the streets”; the TLB “The lazy man is full of excuses”.

A deep pit. “The mouth of strange women is a deep pit; the one Jehovah has denounced will fall into it”: shuchah amuqqah, “a deep pit”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and “a prostitute is a deep pit” (Proverbs 23:27). Zarot, “strange women”, is “strange” in the old sense of zarah, belonging to someone else, the word of the “strange incense” (Exodus 30:9, now on these pages). Ze’um YHVH is from za’am, Balaam’s verb, “how shall I denounce whom Jehovah has not denounced?” (Numbers 23:8, now on these pages); this form stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. The ASV has “The mouth of strange women is a deep pit: He that is abhorred of Jehovah shall fall therein”; the NWT 1984 “The one denounced by Jehovah will fall into it”; the NIV “an adulterous woman”. The Greek has “the mouth of a lawless man”.

Folly bound in the heart. “Folly is bound up in the heart of a youth; the rod of discipline will drive it far from him”: ivvelet, “folly”, is the noun of the evil, the fool who despises “wisdom and discipline” (1:7). Qeshurah, “bound up”, is the verb of 3:3, “bind them around your neck”: the father binds kindness and faithfulness on the outside, and folly is already bound on the inside. Judah uses the same word of his father and the boy Benjamin: “his own soul is bound up with the boy’s soul” (Genesis 44:30, now on these pages). Musar is “discipline”, as everywhere in this book (3:11), and the rod, shevet, is a real stick, the rod of Exodus 21:20, and also the sceptre of a ruler (Genesis 49:10, both now on these pages).

The rod and its readers. This verse, with 13:24, “whoever spares his rod hates his son” (Proverbs 13:24), has been quoted for centuries to defend the beating of children. The familiar line “spare the rod and spoil the child” is not a verse of the Bible; it is from Samuel Butler’s poem Hudibras (1663). Readers differ on how far the rod here is literal: some take it as the plain instruction it appears to be, and others as a figure for correction and authority in general, the sense the same word carries when it means a sceptre. The KJV has “Foolishness is bound in the heart of a child; but the rod of correction shall drive it far from him”; the NWT 1984 “the rod of discipline is what will remove it far from him”; the TLB “punishment will drive it out of him”.

Only to want. “He who oppresses the poor to make more for himself, and he who gives to the rich, come only to want”: the Hebrew lo, “for him”, can point to the oppressor or to the poor man, and the verse has been read both ways; most translations make it two men who end in the same place. The ASV has “He that oppresseth the poor to increase his gain, And he that giveth to the rich, shall come only to want”; the NIV “both come to poverty”; the Douay “shall himself give to one that is richer, and shall be in need”.

Verses 17–21 · “The words of the wise” and the Instruction of Amenemope — a new collection, “noble things”, and thirty sayings

The words of the wise. “Incline your ear and hear the words of the wise, and set your heart to my knowledge”: divrei chakhamim, the phrase of the book’s opening, “the words of the wise and their riddles” (1:6). Here a new collection begins. The sayings of verses 1–16 belong to the collection headed “The proverbs of Solomon” at 10:1, and a teacher now speaks in his own voice, “my knowledge”, until the heading “These also are of the wise” at 24:23 (Proverbs 24:23). The ASV has “Incline thine ear, and hear the words of the wise, And apply thy heart unto my knowledge”; the KJV “Bow down thine ear”.

In your belly. “For it is pleasant if you keep them in your belly, if they are fixed together on your lips”: be-vitnekha, “in your belly”, the body’s inner store, and then the lips, where the words come out ready. “So that your trust may be in Jehovah, I have made them known to you today — yes, you”: the teaching has a stated purpose, and it is trust, mivtach, from the verb of “Trust in Jehovah with all your heart” (3:5). The NWT 1984 has “For it is pleasant that you should keep them in your belly”; the ASV “if thou keep them within thee”; the Geneva “if thou keep them in thy belly”.

Noble things, formerly, three times, or thirty. “Have I not written for you noble things of counsel and knowledge”: one word, and four readings. The written text has shilshom, “formerly”, from the idiom “yesterday and the day before”, as when Ruth came “to a people that you did not know before” (Ruth 2:11, now on these pages). The reading tradition has shalishim, the word for officers, “with officers over all of them” (Exodus 14:7) and “his chosen captains” (Exodus 15:4, both now on these pages), taken here as “noble” or “excellent things”. The Greek has trissōs, “three times”. And many modern versions read the same consonants as sheloshim, “thirty”, a change of vowels supported by the Egyptian text described below. This translation follows the reading tradition, as at 3:34. The KJV and ASV have “excellent things”; the NWT 1984, with the written text, “Have I not written you heretofore”; the TLB “In the past”; the Geneva “three times”; the Douay “three manner of ways”; the NIV “thirty sayings”.

The Instruction of Amenemope. Since Adolf Erman’s study of 1924, scholars have compared the collection that begins here with the Instruction of Amenemope, an Egyptian teaching in thirty chapters that closes, as commonly translated, “Look to these thirty chapters”. The closest parallels fall within Proverbs 22:17–23:11: hearing the words and keeping them within (vv17–18), bringing back an answer to the one who sent you (v21), not robbing the poor (v22), not keeping company with the hot-tempered (v24), not moving the boundary markers of the fields (v28 and 23:10), and the skilled scribe who stands before kings (v29). Most scholars date the Egyptian work earlier, on the evidence of its copies, and readings differ on how directly the Hebrew depends on it. The parallels are reported here from the published translations, not checked against the papyrus.

Sayings of truth. “To make you know the certainty of sayings of truth, so that you bring back sayings of truth to those who send you”: qosht, “certainty”, is a rare word, close to the Aramaic of Daniel, where the king says “Truly your God is the God of gods” (Daniel 2:47, now on these pages). The picture is a messenger’s: the pupil will be sent, and must bring back a true report. The ASV has “To make thee know the certainty of the words of truth, That thou mayest carry back words of truth to them that send thee”; the NWT 1984 “to show you the truthfulness of true sayings”.

Verses 22–25 · “Rob not the poor, because he is poor” — Jehovah pleads their case, the verb of “Will a man rob God?”, and the master of anger

In the gate. “Do not rob the poor because he is poor, and do not crush the afflicted in the gate”: gazal, “rob”, is the verb of the law, “You shall not oppress your neighbor or rob him” (Leviticus 19:13, now on these pages). “Because he is poor” names the temptation: the poor man is robbed because he cannot stop it. The gate is where the court sat, where Boaz “went up to the gate and sat down there” to settle Ruth’s case (Ruth 4:1, now on these pages), so the crushing in view is done by law. The ASV has “Rob not the poor, because he is poor; Neither oppress the afflicted in the gate”; the NWT 1984 “do not crush the afflicted one in the gate”; the NIV “do not crush the needy in court”.

Jehovah pleads their case. “For Jehovah will plead their case and despoil of life those who despoil them”: yariv rivam, “he will argue their lawsuit”, so the poor who lost in the gate gain an advocate. Qava, “despoil”, is a rare verb: it stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and twice in Malachi, the verse the familiar versions render “Will a man rob God?” (Malachi 3:8–9). The ASV has “For Jehovah will plead their cause, And despoil of life those that despoil them”; the KJV “and spoil the soul of those that spoiled them”; the NWT 1984 “he will certainly rob of soul those robbing them”. The Greek reads the second line differently, “and you will rescue your soul unharmed”.

The master of anger. “Do not befriend a master of anger, and with a man of rages do not go, lest you get used to his paths and take a snare for your soul”: ba’al af, “master of anger”, is the idiom of Joseph’s brothers, “this master of dreams” (Genesis 37:19, now on these pages), a man defined by the thing he has. The phrase stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and so does ish chemot, “a man of rages”. Te’elaf, “get used to”, is the verb of becoming familiar with a thing until it is second nature; the anger is caught like a habit. The moqesh is a snare, the word of Judges 2:3. The ASV has “Make no friendship with a man that is given to anger; And with a wrathful man thou shalt not go”; the NWT 1984 “that you may not get familiar with his paths”; the Douay “and take scandal to thy soul”, the Latin scandalum, a snare.

Verses 26–29 · “Remove not the ancient landmark” — surety, the bed taken from under you, the boundary your fathers made, and the skilled man before kings

Striking hands. “Do not be among those who strike hands, among those who stand surety for loans”: toq’ei khaf, “those who strike the palm”, the handshake that sealed a guarantee, and orvim, from arav, to stand surety, Judah’s word for the pledge he made for Benjamin, “I myself will be surety for him” (Genesis 43:9, now on these pages). The warning returns at 6:1 and 17:18 (Proverbs 6:1). The ASV has “Be thou not one of them that strike hands, Or of them that are sureties for debts”; the NWT 1984 “among those who go security for loans”.

Your bed from under you. “If you have nothing to pay, why should he take your bed from under you?”: the law protected a borrower’s cloak, because it was also his blanket, “In what else shall he sleep?” (Exodus 22:27; compare Deuteronomy 24:13, both now on these pages). The proverb warns the guarantor that his own bed is not so protected. The KJV has “why should he take away thy bed from under thee?”; the Douay “that he should take the covering from thy bed?”; the Greek “they will take the bedding from under your ribs”.

An ancient boundary. “Do not move back an ancient boundary, which your fathers made”: tasseg, “move back”, is the verb of the law, “You shall not move back your neighbour’s boundary, which the first ones bounded” (Deuteronomy 19:14), and of the curse, “Cursed is he who moves back his neighbour’s boundary” (Deuteronomy 27:17, both now on these pages). Gevul olam, “an ancient boundary”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible, here and at 23:10, which drops “which your fathers made” and adds “do not enter the fields of the fatherless” (Proverbs 23:10). Where Deuteronomy says “the first ones”, the proverb says “your fathers”: the marker is an inheritance. See gevul. The KJV has “Remove not the ancient landmark, which thy fathers have set”; the NWT 1984 “Do not move back a boundary of long ago, which your forefathers have made”; the Douay “Pass not beyond the ancient bounds”.

Skilled in his work. “Do you see a man skilled in his work? Before kings he will take his stand; he will not take his stand before obscure men”: mahir, “skilled”, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, here, Ezra “a skilled scribe in the law of Moses” (Ezra 7:6), and “the pen of a skilled scribe” (Psalm 45:2), so it is a scribe’s word. Yityatsev, “take his stand”, is to station oneself, as attendants stand before a king; chashukkim, “obscure men”, is from the root of choshekh, darkness, men who stay out of sight. The ASV has “Seest thou a man diligent in his business? he shall stand before kings; He shall not stand before mean men”; the NWT 1984 “he will not station himself before commonplace men”; the NIV “officials of low rank”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “a name” for shem with no added “good” (v1); “shrewd” for arum, as 1:4 has “shrewdness”; “the simple” for peta’im; “the scoffer” for lets, as in 1:22 and 3:34; “discipline” for musar; “folly” for ivvelet; “poor” for rash and dal and “the afflicted” for ani; “strange women” for zarot, in the old sense; “on the heels of” for ekev, as in Deuteronomy 7:12; “twists” for sillef, as in Exodus 23:8; “stand surety” for arav, as in Genesis 43:9; “move back” and “boundary”, as in Deuteronomy 19:14; “master of anger”, as “master of dreams” in Genesis 37:19; and “noble things” for the read text shalishim, with the other readings in the note.

Echoes paid. The serpent’s arum and the shrewdness of 1:4; “the words of the wise” of 1:6; the scoffers of 1:22 and 3:34; the binding of 3:3; chanukkah of Numbers 7; the boundary laws of Deuteronomy 19:14 and 27:17; the twisting bribe of Exodus 23:8; Judah’s surety of Genesis 43:9; the pledged cloak of Exodus 22:27; the sowing and reaping of Galatians 6:7; and the Greek line behind 2 Corinthians 9:7.

Echoes planted. The second ancient boundary (Proverbs 23:10); the shrewd man’s saying repeated (Proverbs 27:12); the lion again (Proverbs 26:13); the deep pit again (Proverbs 23:27); the rod (Proverbs 13:24); qava in Malachi 3:8; the skilled scribe of Ezra 7:6; and the end of “the words of the wise” at Proverbs 24:23. None is translated here yet.
