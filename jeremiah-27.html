Jeremiah wears a wooden yoke in the temple and sends its pieces to five allied kings, telling all of them and Zedekiah to submit to Babylon; the false prophets promising a quick return of the temple vessels are named liars.
⚠ v1 dates this to 'the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim,' but vv3, 12 and 20 all address Zedekiah and events of his reign — a well-known scribal duplication of 26:1's opening formula. The envoys' visit (v3) is usually placed c. 594 BC, the year of a known anti-Babylonian coalition among these same five nations.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–4 · מוֹסֵרוֹת וּמֹטוֹת moserot u-motot — straps and bars, and a king's name the chapter itself seems to contradict

⚠ This is one of the oldest and best-known textual cruxes in Jeremiah, and this translation prints the Hebrew as it stands rather than silently repairing it. Verse 1 dates the chapter to “the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim” — word for word the same opening formula as 26:1 — but verse 3 sends the yoke-message to envoys visiting “Zedekiah king of Judah,” and verse 12 has Jeremiah deliver it to Zedekiah in person. Jehoiakim and Zedekiah are different kings, roughly a decade apart, and the chapter cannot coherently be about both. The Septuagint does not have this verse at all. Nearly every modern version prints “Zedekiah” here — NIV, ASV footnote, and most critical editions — on the reasoning that a copyist's eye caught the identical phrase that opens chapter 26 and repeated it by mistake. KJV and NWT both keep “Jehoiakim” in the text with no comment, which is what the Masoretic manuscripts actually read and what this translation follows, flagging the difficulty here rather than adjusting the number.

Street theater, not metaphor. “Straps and bars” (moserot u-motot) is an actual yoke, the wooden crossbar and leather fittings used to harness draft animals, and Jeremiah is told to wear one in public and then mail its component pieces — by the hand of real foreign envoys already in Jerusalem — to five kings who have no reason yet to think Babylon is the coming power. This is the same acted-out-prophecy genre as Isaiah walking naked and barefoot for three years (Isaiah 20) and Ezekiel lying on his side for over a year (Ezekiel 4): the sign is not an illustration of the message, it IS the message, delivered to the body before it reaches the ear.

The five nations — Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, Sidon — are the same regional bloc already handed a cup of wrath at 25:21-22. Whatever anti-Babylonian coalition these envoys were in Jerusalem to discuss, Jeremiah's answer to all five kings at once is the same word he already gave his own.

Verses 5–11 · נְבוּכַדְנֶאצַּר … עַבְדִּי Nevukhadnetsar … avdi — Nebuchadnezzar, my servant, and the argument from creation itself

“My servant” is not a slip repeated by accident. The identical designation for Nebuchadnezzar already appeared at 25:9; here it is grounded in an argument, not just asserted. Verses 5-6 run from the largest possible claim (I made the earth, the human, the beast, by my own power) straight down to the smallest political fact of 594 BC (and I have now given it to Nebuchadnezzar) — the same God who assigned humanity dominion over the animals at creation is now, on the same authority, assigning a foreign king dominion over humanity. The chapter does not treat this as a contradiction of Genesis 1:26-28; it treats it as the same prerogative exercised a second time, on new terms.

⚠ And the terms have their own built-in expiration. Verse 7's “until the time of his own land comes” is the same clock already set running at 25:12: Babylon's dominion is real, total, and temporary in the same sentence. Submission is never framed as permanent defeat — only as the one option that has an end date attached to it.

A five-word wall of refusal. Verse 9 stacks prophets, diviners, dreams, soothsayers, sorcerers — five distinct Hebrew categories of people claiming access to hidden knowledge, named together only to be dismissed together. This translation keeps them as five separate words rather than collapsing them into a general “fortune-tellers,” because the chapter's whole point is that the message is uniform across every channel Judah might have trusted: whichever one you ask, the answer promising no exile is a lie.

Verses 12–15 · הָבִיאוּ אֶת-צַוְּארֵיכֶם havi'u et-tsavre'khem — bring your necks, spoken to the very king the sign was performed for

The object becomes the sentence. Verse 12's “bring your necks under the yoke” uses the identical yoke-vocabulary Jeremiah is physically wearing while he says it (v2). This is not a new metaphor invented for the king; it is the acted sign, now translated back into the plainest possible command, delivered to the one man in the room who could actually decide to obey it.

⚠ The offer is life, stated flatly, with the alternative just as flat. “Why should you die…?” (v13) is not rhetorical despair; it repeats, almost word for word, the sword-famine-pestilence sentence already pronounced generally at 25:11 and specifically on this city's own prophets at 26:8-9. The choice this chapter offers Zedekiah is the same choice Jeremiah 21 already put in front of the whole city: stay and die, or leave and live — with survival now depending on political submission rather than military escape.

Verses 16–22 · כְּלֵי בֵית-יְהוָה kelei beit-YHVH — the vessels, and a prophecy this library can already trace from both ends

⚠ This is the middle chapter of a three-part story already spanning this whole site. The FIRST deportation (605 BC) already took “some of the vessels of the house of God” to Babylon — Daniel 1:2, already on these pages. This chapter, four years before Jerusalem's final fall, insists that everything the false prophets promise is coming back — “shortly,” they say — is instead going the OTHER direction: the pillars, the great bronze Sea, the stands, and every vessel still left in the temple after 597. The THIRD chapter of the story is also already shipped: Ezra 1:7 has Cyrus “bring out the vessels of the house of Jehovah” from that same treasure house and count them back to Jerusalem, decades later. A reader can follow one set of temple furniture from capture to captivity to homecoming across three different books of this translation, in the order the history actually happened rather than the order the books were written.

“Until the day I attend to them” (paqad, v22) is the same verb this project has already flagged for its double edge — to attend to can mean to punish or to visit with favor, and here, for the first time in these chapters, it lands unambiguously on the favorable side: a homecoming with a date attached, even though the date is not given.

Jeconiah (v20) is named a third time on these pages (22:24; 24:1) — the deportation that took him is now the fixed point every later prophecy about the vessels measures itself against: taken then, still-remaining now, gone eventually. A ketiv/qere in the Hebrew spells his name two ways in one verse-tradition, a minor spelling variant this translation follows the read form for, as elsewhere.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “Jehoiakim” (v1) printed as the Masoretic text has it, with the Zedekiah difficulty flagged rather than silently corrected; “my servant” (v6) for Nebuchadnezzar, matching 25:9 exactly; the five distinct diviner-words of v9 kept apart rather than collapsed into one general term; and “vessels” (kelim) kept as the single word threading through this chapter, Daniel 1:2, and Ezra 1:7 alike.

Echoes paid. “My servant” (v6) and the land's own coming end (v7), both already stated at 25:9, 12; the sword, famine, and pestilence sentence (v13), already pronounced at 25:11 and delivered specifically to this city's prophets at 26:8-9; and Edom, Moab, Ammon, Tyre, and Sidon (v3), the exact five nations already handed a cup at 25:21-22.

Echoes planted. The vessels (vv16-22), now traceable from Daniel 1:2 through this chapter to Ezra 1:7 — a single object's whole biblical biography, told across three books in the order it actually happened. And Hananiah, the prophet who will physically break a wooden yoke off Jeremiah's own neck and be dead within the year, waiting one chapter ahead.

This library already carries Jeremiah 29 and 31, both translated out of order; the sequential thread is still open here. Next in sequence: Jeremiah 28 — the prophet Hananiah breaks Jeremiah's wooden yoke in the temple and promises the vessels back within two years, and Jeremiah answers by putting on a yoke of iron.
