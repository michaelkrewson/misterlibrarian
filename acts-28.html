The party comes ashore, and ‘we’ recognize that the island is called Malta. The islanders, whom Luke calls barbarians, show extraordinary kindness, kindling a fire against the rain and the cold; when Paul lays a quantity of brushwood on it a viper comes out because of the heat and fastens on his hand, the islanders say that a murderer, though brought safely through from the sea, is not allowed by Justice to live, and when nothing happens to him they change their minds and say he is a god. Publius, the leading man of the island, hosts them for three days; Paul, praying and laying his hands on him, heals the father of Publius of fevers and dysentery, and the rest of the island’s sick come and are healed, and the islanders honor the party with many honors and put on board what it needs. After three months they set sail in an Alexandrian ship with the Dioscuri as its emblem, put in at Syracuse for three days, cast off to Rhegium, and on the second day after a south wind rises reach Puteoli, where they find brothers and are urged to stay seven days. Brothers from Rome come out to meet them as far as the Forum of Appius and Three Taverns, and Paul gives thanks to God and takes courage. At Rome he is permitted to stay by himself with the soldier who guards him. After three days he calls together the leading men of the Jews and tells them that, having done nothing against the people or the ancestral customs, he was handed over as a prisoner from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans, that those who examined him wished to release him because there was no ground for death in him, that he appealed to Caesar when the Jews spoke against it, and that he wears this chain for the hope of Israel; they say they have received no letters about him and that this party is spoken against everywhere. A day is appointed, more come to his lodging, and from morning until evening he sets out the kingdom of God and persuades them about Jesus from the law of Moses and the Prophets; some are persuaded and some disbelieve, and as they leave out of harmony he quotes Isaiah 6:9–10 and says that this salvation of God has been sent to the nations, who will also listen. He stays on a whole two years in his own rented quarters, welcoming all who come in, proclaiming the kingdom of God and teaching the things about the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, unhindered.
Undated by the text. The chapter opens where 27:44 ends, at the landing on the island, and counts three months at the island (v11), seven days at Puteoli (v14), three days before the Jews are called together (v17) and two whole years at the end (v30); the book gives no calendar year and no season for any of it, and this library dates the events no more closely than the book does. The book ends at the close of the two years and does not say what came after them.

Translator’s Notes — verse by verse

Each note explains this translation's choice and compares the seven versions on the shelf. Quotations from copyrighted versions (NIV, TLB, NWT) are kept to short phrases for comparison; KJV, Geneva, Douay-Rheims and ASV are public domain.

Verses 1–2 · διασωθέντες … ἐπέγνωμεν … Μελίτη … βάρβαροι … φιλανθρωπίαν … ἅψαντες diasōthentes … epegnōmen … Melitē … barbaroi … philanthrōpian … hapsantes — Acts 28:1–2: what is the island called, who are the “barbarians,” and why is their kindness “extraordinary”?

“Having been brought safely through” (Acts 28:1). Kai diasōthentes tote epegnōmen hoti Melitē hē nēsos kaleitai. The participle is the passive of diasōzō, the verb of 27:43–44, where the centurion wants to “bring Paul safely through” and all are “brought safely through to the land.” The verb stands in eight verses of the New Testament (read from the hits): Matthew 14:36 (“as many as touched it were healed”), Luke 7:3 (“pull his slave through”), Acts 23:24 (bring Paul “safely through to Felix”), 27:43, 27:44, here, 28:4 and 1 Peter 3:20 (not yet on these pages). Five of the eight are in Acts, and the last two are on this island. The same participle, in the singular, opens Josephus’s own arrival after a sinking: diasōtheis d’ eis tēn Dikaiarcheian, hēn Potiolous Italoi kalousin, “having been brought safely through to Dicaearchia, which the Italians call Puteoli” (Josephus, Life 16, in Niese’s Greek). Shelf: NWT 1984 “when we had made it to safety,” ASV “when we were escaped” and Douay “when we had escaped” keep the “we”; NIV “Once safely on shore” drops the participle’s subject, and TLB “We soon learned that we were on the island of Malta” leaves the rescue out of the sentence altogether.

“We then recognized” (Acts 28:1). Tote epegnōmen: the verb is epiginōskō, “to recognize, to come to know,” the verb of 27:39, where “they did not recognize the land.” Luke is not saying the party was told the name by a native, and the Greek does not say who told them; it says only that the unrecognized land becomes a named one. The apparatus (1050–1051) has two points. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print epegnōmen, “we recognized,” where Robinson–Pierpont has epegnōsan, “they recognized,” which is why the KJV reads “when they were escaped, then they knew” and Geneva “when they were come safe, then they knew,” with “they” twice. And the name is Melitē in Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont, Melitēnē in Westcott–Hort. This translation prints Malta, the modern name of the island generally taken to be meant; the Greek name is Melitē, which the KJV, ASV and Douay spell “Melita.” NIV, NWT 1984, TLB and Geneva print “Malta.” An island of the same Greek name in the Adriatic, now Mljet, has been proposed instead, first (as the Wikipedia article on Mljet records) in the tenth century by the emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus, on the strength of the “Adria” of 27:27. Diodorus Siculus, for his part, lists three islands lying out in the sea south of Sicily and puts first “the one called Melitē,” about eight hundred stadia from Syracuse and “a colony of the Phoenicians” (Library of History 5.12, Greek fetched), which is the Malta of the Sicilian sea, and the library entry Malta says what is known. Josephus’s own sinking “in the middle of the Adria” (kata meson ton Adrian, Life 15) ends with a ship of Cyrene carrying him to Puteoli, on the west coast of Italy, so for Josephus the Adria was a sea on the way from Judea to Puteoli, not only the gulf that now bears the name. Luke gives no distance and no bearing, and the Greek does not say which island this is beyond its name.

“Barbarians” (Acts 28:2). Hoi barbaroi. The adjective stands in five verses of the New Testament (read from the hits): here and 28:4, Romans 1:14 (“To Greeks and to barbarians”), 1 Corinthians 14:11 and Colossians 3:11 (neither yet on these pages). In Acts it is used only on this island, and it is Luke’s word for people who do not speak Greek, not a verdict on how they behave; the entry barbaros follows the word from its first use on these pages, in Romans 1:14. Diodorus calls the island a colony of the Phoenicians (5.12), and Loredana Cappelletti’s study of the island’s Greek and Latin inscriptions finds that Melita, while fully inside the Roman municipal order, kept “linguistic and institutional” features of its Punic and Sicilian past (Gerión, 2024, 11–31; the quoted words are translated from her Italian summary). That makes “barbarians” to a Greek-speaking narrator plausible in the plain sense, though Luke does not say what language the islanders spoke. Shelf: KJV “barbarous people” at this verse (and “barbarians” at 28:4), ASV “barbarians,” Geneva “Barbarians” and Douay “barbarians” keep the Greek word; NWT 1984 “foreign-speaking people” keeps its sense; NIV “The islanders” and TLB “The people of the island” do not keep the word at all.

“Extraordinary kindness” (Acts 28:2). Pareichon ou tēn tychousan philanthrōpian hēmin: “they were offering us not the ordinary kindness.” The participle tychousa, “ordinary” (literally “the one that happens by”), stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and 19:11, where God does “extraordinary acts of power” through Paul’s hands (dynameis ou tas tychousas); this translation prints “extraordinary” at both. The noun philanthrōpia, “kindness” (love of human beings), stands in two verses, here and Titus 3:4 (not yet on these pages); the adverb philanthrōpōs stands in one, 27:3, where Julius treats Paul “with kindness.” The entry philanthrōpia follows the family. The kindness is described in four actions: they hapsantes pyran, “kindled a fire”; proselabonto pantas hēmas, “took us all in” (the middle voice of proslambanō, “to take to oneself,” the verb of 18:26 where Priscilla and Aquila “took him to themselves”); and the reasons given are dia ton hyeton ton ephestōta, “because of the rain that had set in,” and dia to psychos, “because of the cold.” The Greek says pantas hēmas, “all of us,” and does not give the number; 27:37 had given it as 276. Verses 3–6 · φρυγάνων … ἔχιδνα … καθῆψε … θηρίον … φονεύς … ἡ δίκη … ἄτοπον … θεόν phryganōn … echidna … kathēpse … thērion … phoneus … hē dikē … atopon … theon — Acts 28:3–6: what is the viper on Paul’s hand, who is “Justice,” and why does a murderer become a god?

“A quantity of brushwood” and “a viper” (Acts 28:3). Systrepsantos de tou Paulou phryganōn ti plēthos kai epithentos epi tēn pyran, echidna apo tēs thermēs exelthousa kathēpse tēs cheiros autou. The noun phrygana, “dry sticks, brushwood,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and so does the verb kathaptō, “to fasten onto”; neither is compared with anything else. Echidna, “viper,” stands in five verses (read from the hits), and in the other four it is only the insult gennēmata echidnōn, “offspring of vipers” (Matthew 3:7, 12:34, 23:33 and Luke 3:7); this is the one place in the New Testament where it is a snake. The cause Luke gives is that it came out apo tēs thermēs, “from the heat” of the fire, which the Greek says and does not elaborate. There is a pair here that English cannot show. The verb for kindling the fire in the last verse, hapsantes (28:2), and the verb for the viper “fastening on,” kathēpse, are the same root hapt-; the entry echidna follows the animal, and haptomai follows the root. The hand that is bitten here is the hand that lays itself on the sick at 28:8. Shelf: ASV “fastened on his hand,” KJV “fastened on his hand” and Douay “fastened on his hand” keep the verb; NWT 1984 “fastened itself on his hand” and NIV “fastened itself on his hand” make it reflexive; Geneva “leaped on his hand” reads a different action; and TLB “a poisonous snake” has no “viper.” NIV “driven out by the heat” and TLB “driven out by the heat” add a verb of force that the Greek does not have.

“The beast” (Acts 28:4–5). Kremamenon to thērion ek tēs cheiros autou. Luke calls the viper to thērion twice, here and at 28:5, where Paul shakes it into the fire; the word is the one for a wild animal (Mark 1:13, “with the wild beasts”), and the entry thērion follows it. The participle kremamenon, “hanging,” is the verb of 5:30 and 10:39, where Peter says the rulers killed Jesus by “hanging” him on a tree; in Acts the verb stands in three verses (read from the hits). Luke draws nothing from the repetition, and this note does not either. The snake is not one of the “snakes” of Mark 16:18 or Luke 10:19: those verses use ophis, and this scene uses echidna and thērion. Paul does not pick the viper up or tread on it; he shakes it off (apotinaxas) and takes no harm. Shelf: KJV “venomous beast” and Douay “the beast” keep thērion; ASV “venomous creature” and NWT 1984 “venomous creature” choose “creature”; NIV “the snake” and TLB “the snake” name the animal; and Geneva “the worm” uses the old word for a serpent.

“A murderer” and “Justice” (Acts 28:4). Pantōs phoneus estin ho anthrōpos houtos hon diasōthenta ek tēs thalassēs hē dikē zēn ouk eiasen. The noun phoneus, “murderer,” stands in seven verses of the New Testament: Matthew 22:7, Acts 3:14 (“asked for a murderer to be granted to you”), 7:52 (“traitors and murderers”), here, 1 Peter 4:15 (not yet on these pages), Revelation 21:8 and 22:15, so in Acts it is said of the man the crowd asked for instead of the Holy and Righteous One, of Stephen’s hearers, and here of Paul, by people who do not know him. The participle diasōthenta is the one of 28:1, so the islanders use Luke’s own word for the rescue as the premise of their charge: he was brought safely through the sea, and still is not to live. Hē dikē, with the article, is the person Dikē, “Justice”; in Hesiod she is a goddess, who writes hē de te parthenos esti Dikē, Dios ekgegauia, “the maiden is Dikē, born of Zeus” (Works and Days 256). The noun stands in three verses of the New Testament, here, 2 Thessalonians 1:9 and Jude 7, and in the other two it is a penalty, not a person; the entry dikē follows it. The verse reports what the islanders said, in their terms, and Luke does not say that they were right or that Justice is a being; the Greek gives them pantōs, “certainly,” and the verb eiasen, “did not allow,” is the one Acts uses of God, who “let all the nations go in their own ways” (14:16), and of the Spirit of Jesus, who “did not allow them” to go into Bithynia (16:7). Shelf: NIV “the goddess Justice has not allowed him to live” adds “goddess” to explain the capital; ASV “Justice hath not suffered to live” keeps the capital; Geneva “Vengeance hath not suffered to live” also capitalizes a different word; KJV “vengeance suffereth not to live” and Douay “vengeance doth not suffer him to live” do not; NWT 1984 “vindictive justice did not permit him to keep on living” and TLB “justice will not permit him to live” make it an abstraction.

“Nothing out of place” (Acts 28:6). Mēden atopon eis auton ginomenon. The adjective atopos stands in four verses of the New Testament: Luke 23:41, where one of the two men crucified with him says Jesus “has done nothing out of place”; Acts 25:5, where Festus tells the Jews to accuse Paul “if there is anything out of place in the man”; here; and 2 Thessalonians 3:2 (not yet on these pages). The word Luke uses for the absence of fault in Jesus and the absence of a charge against Paul is used here of the absence of harm, and the Greek does not say that the islanders are using it that way on purpose. The verb pimprasthai, “to swell up,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, and aphnō, “suddenly,” stands in three, Acts 2:2 (the sound from heaven), 16:26 (the earthquake at Philippi) and here (the death that does not come). The verb prosdokaō, “to expect,” occurs twice in this verse (prosedokōn, prosdokōntōn), and the repetition is the point: they expect, and go on expecting, and nothing comes. KJV “looked when he should have swollen” and “after they had looked a great while” and ASV “expected that he would have swollen” and “were long in expectation” keep the repetition; most of the shelf does not. Shelf: NWT 1984 “beheld nothing hurtful happen to him” and NIV “nothing unusual happen to him” give “out of place” a sense of harm or of the strange; ASV “nothing amiss” is nearest; Geneva “no inconvenience” and TLB “no harm came to him” are further off.

“They changed their minds and began saying he was a god” (Acts 28:6). Metabalomenoi elegon auton einai theon. The verb metaballō, “to turn about,” stands in one verse of the New Testament, so it is not compared with any other use; the imperfect elegon is “began saying,” and theon has no article, so it is “a god,” not “the god.” Two readings of the apparatus (1058–1059) are small: Robinson–Pierpont has the present participle metaballomenoi, “changing their minds,” for the printed aorist, and puts theon before auton einai. The reversal runs from “murderer” to “god” in the space of two verses, and it has company in Acts: at Lystra the crowds say “The gods, made like humans, have come down to us!” and call Paul Hermes (14:11–12), Cornelius falls at Peter’s feet and Peter answers “I myself also am a man” (10:26), and at Caesarea the people shout “A god’s voice, and not a man’s!” at Herod (12:22). In those three the scene turns at once: Barnabas and Paul tear their garments (14:14), Peter raises Cornelius (10:26), and an angel of Jehovah strikes Herod “because he did not give the glory to God” (12:23). Here the Greek gives no word from Paul, and this translation adds none. Shelf: Geneva “said, That he was a God” capitalizes the noun; KJV “said that he was a god,” ASV “said that he was a god” and Douay “they said, that he was a god” do not; NWT 1984 “began saying he was a god” keeps the imperfect; NIV “said he was a god” and TLB “decided he was a god” do not, and TLB “decided” turns a reaction into a conclusion.

Verses 7–10 · πρώτῳ τῆς νήσου … Ποπλίῳ … φιλοφρόνως … πυρετοῖς … δυσεντερίῳ … ἰάσατο … ἐθεραπεύοντο … τιμαῖς ἐτίμησαν prōtō tēs nēsou … Popliō … philophronōs … pyretois … dysenteriō … iasato … etherapeuonto … timais etimēsan — Acts 28:7–10: who is Publius, what are the fevers and the dysentery, and what are “many honors”?

“The leading man of the island” (Acts 28:7). En de tois peri ton topon ekeinon hyperchen chōria tō prōtō tēs nēsou onomati Popliō. The noun chōrion, plural chōria, “land, field,” is the word of the field bought with the wages of unrighteousness (1:18), of the “fields or houses” sold for the common fund (4:34) and of the land Ananias sold (5:8). The name Poplios is the Latin Publius, and the New Testament has it only in these two verses, 28:7 and 28:8; the entry Publius gathers what is said. Luke gives him one title, ho prōtos tēs nēsou, “the first of the island.” The adjective prōtos with a genitive is Luke’s way of saying “leading man” at 13:50 (the leading men of Pisidian Antioch), 25:2 (the leading men of the Jews) and 28:17 (the leading men of the Jews in Rome), and this translation prints “leading man” at all four. Two inscriptions from Malta carry a title of the same shape. A Greek one, IG XIV 601, honors Lucius Castricius Prudens, a Roman knight, as prōtos Melitaiōn, “first of the Maltese” (Cappelletti notes that Busuttil dates it to the reign of Tiberius), and a Latin one, CIL X 7495, of the second century AD and found at Mdina in 1747, calls a benefactor municipi Melitensium primus omnium, “first of all of the municipality of the Maltese.” In Cappelletti’s study the two titles seem to correspond, and modern scholars divide on what they were, a supreme local magistrate under the provincial governor (Weiss, Warnecke, Tajra and Zammit, as she lists them) or an honorary title (Suhl) (Gerión, 2024, 11–31). Luke’s phrase differs in one word, “first of the island” for “first of the Maltese,” and the Greek does not say whether Publius was a Roman official, a native noble or a landowner with a title. The verb anadexamenos, “receiving,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Hebrews 11:17 (“the man who had received the promises”), and the adverb philophronōs, “in a friendly way,” stands in one. Exenisen, “hosted,” is the host’s verb xenizō of 10:6, 10:18, 10:23, 10:32 and 21:16, six verses with this one, all of lodging. Hēmas, “us,” does not say who: the Greek names no one and gives no number, and 28:11 will say that the party stayed three months. The apparatus (1060) is word order: Westcott–Hort has hēmeras treis where Tregelles, Nestle–Aland 28 and Robinson–Pierpont print treis hēmeras. Shelf: KJV “the chief man of the island,” Geneva “the chief man of the Isle” and Douay “the chief man of the island” keep a plain “chief”; NWT 1984 “the principal man of the island” is the same; NIV “the chief official of the island” and TLB “the governor of the island” supply an office the Greek does not name.

“Fevers and dysentery” (Acts 28:8). Ton patera tou Popliou pyretois kai dysenteriō synechomenon katakeisthai. The noun pyretos stands in six verses of the New Testament (Matthew 8:15, Mark 1:31, Luke 4:38, 4:39, John 4:52 and here), and only here is it plural, “fevers.” Dysenteria stands in one verse, so it is not compared with any other use. The participle synechomenon, “gripped,” is the participle of Luke 4:38, where Simon’s mother-in-law “was gripped by a high fever,” so it is the verb for a fever in both of Luke’s books. The sequence in the sickroom is four Greek participles and a verb: eiselthōn kai proseuxamenos epitheis tas cheiras autō iasato auton, “going in, and praying, and laying his hands on him, he healed him.” Prayer and the laying on of hands stand together in the same pair at 6:6 and 13:3, and epitheis tas cheiras is also the act of Ananias, who lays his hands on Saul so that he may recover sight (9:17). The verb iaomai, “to heal,” stands in four verses of Acts: 9:34 (“Jesus Christ heals you”), 10:38, here, and 28:27, where the last words of the quotation from Isaiah are “and I would heal them.” Shelf: NIV “suffering from fever and dysentery,” TLB “ill with fever and dysentery,” NWT 1984 “distressed with fever and dysentery” and ASV “of fever and dysentery” print a singular “fever” where the Greek has the plural; KJV “of a fever and of a bloody flux” and Douay “of a fever, and of a bloody flux” do the same and call the dysentery by its old name; Geneva “of the fever, and of a bloody flix” adds the article. All seven English versions on the shelf print the singular. NIV “after prayer, placed his hands on him and healed him” and TLB “laying his hands on him, healed him” keep the sequence in fewer words.

“Were being healed” (Acts 28:9). Prosērchonto kai etherapeuonto: the two imperfects are “kept coming” and “were being healed,” and the verb is not iaomai but therapeuō, the verb of 4:14 (“the man who had been healed”), 5:16 (“they were all healed”) and 8:7 (“were healed”). It stands in five verses of Acts, and in the fifth, 17:25, it means “is served.” This translation prints “healed” for both verbs, so the two cannot be told apart in English; the Greek has one for the one man and one for the many. Hoi loipoi, “the rest,” is all the others on the island who “had sicknesses” (asthenias); Luke gives no number and no names, and does not say what Paul said or did for them. The apparatus (1062–1063) is small: Robinson–Pierpont has oun for de and puts “on the island” after “sicknesses.” Shelf: NIV “the rest of the sick on the island came and were cured” and ASV “the rest also that had diseases in the island came, and were cured” print “cured”; KJV “others also, which had diseases in the island, came, and were healed,” Geneva “were healed” and Douay “were healed” print “healed”; NWT 1984 “began to come to him and be cured” gets the imperfect; TLB “Then all the other sick people in the island came and were cured” adds “all.”

“Honored us with many honors” (Acts 28:10). Hoi kai pollais timais etimēsan hēmas kai anagomenois epethento ta pros tas chreias. The noun and the verb are from one root, timē and timaō, and this translation keeps the pairing, “honored us with many honors.” The noun is not only “honor”: in Acts it is the proceeds laid at the apostles’ feet (4:34), the price kept back (5:2), the price of Abraham’s tomb (7:16) and the price of the burned books (19:19), and this is its one use there for honor. Whether the islanders’ honors included payment for the healings is a guess from the word’s other meaning, and the Greek does not say. The second half is the practical one: anagomenois, “as we set sail,” is the verb of 27:2 and 27:12 in the participle, and ta pros tas chreias is “the things for our needs.” The noun chreia stands in five verses of Acts: 2:45 and 4:35 (“as anyone had need”), 6:3 (the “need” the seven are set over), 20:34 (“these hands ministered to my needs and to those who were with me”) and here. The last is Paul’s own phrase at Miletus, now said of him. Shelf splits on timais: KJV “honoured us with many honours,” Douay “honoured us with many honours” and ASV “honored us with many honors” keep the pairing; Geneva “did us great honor” and NIV “honored us in many ways” do not; NWT 1984 “honored us with many gifts” and TLB “showered with gifts” read the noun as gifts. For the supplies: ASV “put on board such things as we needed,” NWT 1984 “loaded us with things for our needs,” KJV “laded us with such things as were necessary” and Geneva “laded us with things necessary” keep the loading; NIV “furnished us with the supplies we needed” and TLB “put on board all sorts of things we would need for the trip” add “supplies” or “trip.”

Verses 11–13 · Ἀλεξανδρίνῳ … παρασήμῳ Διοσκούροις … Συρακούσας … περιελόντες … Ῥήγιον … δευτεραῖοι … Ποτιόλους Alexandrinō … parasēmō Dioskourois … Syrakousas … perielontes … Rhēgion … deuteraioi … Potiolous — Acts 28:11–13: what is the ship with the “Dioscuri” on it, and what does the apparatus do to the course from Syracuse to Puteoli?

“An Alexandrian ship that had wintered” (Acts 28:11). Meta de treis mēnas anēchthēmen en ploiō parakecheimakoti en tē nēsō Alexandrinō. The adjective Alexandrinos stands in two verses of the New Testament, and both are of ships: 27:6, where the centurion found one at Myra to carry them to Italy, and here, where a second one waits out the winter at the island. The man from Alexandria is a different word, Alexandreus (18:24). The perfect participle parakecheimakoti, “having wintered,” is the verb of 27:12; it stands in four verses of the New Testament, that one, this one, 1 Corinthians 16:6 and Titus 3:12 (the last two not yet on these pages). Anēchthēmen, “we set sail,” is the verb of 27:2, 27:4 and 27:12. The Greek does not say that this ship carried grain, or that it belonged to the fleet that fed Rome; Josephus has Agrippa say that Alexandria sends Rome, besides money, grain for four months of the year (siton mēnōn tessarōn, War 2.386, Greek fetched), and Seneca confirms that Puteoli was where Alexandrian ships came in: he writes that “Alexandrian ships” (Alexandrinae naves) had suddenly appeared, and that “the whole crowd of Puteoli stands on the piers” (omnis in pilis Puteolorum turba consistit) to see them in (Letters 77.1, Latin fetched). The entry Alexandria says what is known of the city. Apparatus (1065): Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print anēchthēmen; Robinson–Pierpont has ēchthēmen, “we were led away,” the simple form of the same verb. Shelf: KJV “a ship of Alexandria, which had wintered in the isle,” Douay “a ship of Alexandria, that had wintered in the island” and Geneva “a ship of Alexandria, which had wintered in the Isle” read the adjective as “of”; NIV “an Alexandrian ship” keeps the adjective; TLB “It was three months after the shipwreck before we set sail again” adds the shipwreck as the clock.

“With the Dioscuri as its emblem” (Acts 28:11). Parasēmō Dioskourois. Both words stand in one verse of the New Testament, so neither is compared with another use. Parasēmos is a ship’s identifying mark, and the dative Dioskourois is “the Dioscuri,” literally the “boys of Zeus” (Dios kouroi), the twin sons of Zeus and Leda, Castor and Polydeuces (Latin Pollux). They were the patrons of sailors. The Homeric Hymn to the Dioscuri says that Leda bore them “as saviors of men on earth and of swift-going ships” (sōtēras … epichthoniōn anthrōpōn ōkyporōn te neōn, Hymn 33.6–7, Greek fetched) and that when storms rage on the sea, men on the ships call on them with white lambs at the stern, and they appear suddenly and still the winds (33.7–16). Luke records the emblem and says nothing about it: the ship that carries Paul on from the island has the saviors of ships on its bow, three months after Paul told the ship’s company that an angel of the God he serves had granted him all who sailed with him (27:23–24). The library entry The Dioscuri gathers the names. Shelf: NWT 1984 “Sons of Zeus” translates the name; KJV “whose sign was Castor and Pollux” and Geneva “whose badge was Castor and Pollux” give the Latin names; ASV “whose sign was The Twin Brothers” and TLB “The Twin Brothers of Alexandria” give a title, and the TLB makes it the ship’s name. The last two change the count or the kind: NIV “the figurehead of the twin gods Castor and Pollux” adds “gods,” and Douay “whose sign was the Castors” names only one of the two.

Syracuse, Rhegium, Puteoli (Acts 28:12–13). Kai katachthentes eis Syrakousas epemeinamen hēmeras treis, hothen perielontes katēntēsamen eis Rhēgion. Kai meta mian hēmeran epigenomenou notou deuteraioi ēlthomen eis Potiolous. Each of the three place names stands in one verse of the New Testament (Syrakousai, Rhēgion, Potioloi), and the libraries have entries for Syracuse, Rhegium and Puteoli. Josephus gives the Greek name of the last: he reached Italy after a sinking in the Adria in which about six hundred swam all night and about eighty were taken up by a ship of Cyrene, and writes that, brought safely through, he came “to Dicaearchia, which the Italians call Puteoli” (eis tēn Dikaiarcheian, hēn Potiolous Italoi kalousin, Life 16, Niese’s Greek), where a friend, the Jewish actor Aliturus, introduced him to Poppaea; he had gone, he says, to free priests whom Felix had bound and sent to Rome to answer to Caesar (Life 13–16). The course in the middle verse depends on one letter. The verb perielontes is the participle of periaireō, “to take away from around,” and in Acts it stands in three verses, 27:20 (hope “taken away”), 27:40 (the sailors “casting off the anchors”) and here, where it is “casting off.” Westcott–Hort and Nestle–Aland 28 print that; Tregelles and Robinson–Pierpont print perielthontes, from perierchomai, “having gone around” (apparatus 1066), which is a course that makes a circuit. The shelf has the second: KJV “we fetched a compass,” Geneva “we set a compass,” ASV “we made a circuit,” NWT 1984 “we went around,” TLB “we circled around” and Douay “compassing by the shore” all read the circuit; only NIV “we set sail” reads the casting off. The last words of the verse are also a question of counting. Meta mian hēmeran, “after one day,” is when the south wind rises, and deuteraioi, “second-day men,” which stands in one verse, is when they arrive; so they reach Puteoli on the second day after leaving Rhegium, with a day between. KJV “we came the next day to Puteoli” and NIV “on the following day we reached Puteoli” make it one day; ASV “on the second day we came to Puteoli,” Douay “we came the second day to Puteoli” and Geneva “we came the second day to Puteoli” keep the number. The Greek gives no distances and no hours.

Verses 14–16 · ἀδελφοὺς … ἀπάντησιν … Ἀππίου Φόρου … Τριῶν Ταβερνῶν … θάρσος … ἐπετράπη … καθʼ ἑαυτόν … στρατιώτῃ adelphous … apantēsin … Appiou Phorou … Triōn Tabernōn … tharsos … epetrapē … kath’ heauton … stratiōtē — Acts 28:14–16: who are the brothers at Puteoli and on the road, what does “to meet us” mean, and what does the longer text of verse 16 add?

“We found brothers” (Acts 28:14). Hou heurontes adelphous parekalēthēmen par’ autois epimeinai hēmeras hepta; kai houtōs eis tēn Rhōmēn ēlthamen. Luke says nothing of how the brothers came to be at Puteoli, and nothing of who they were; adelphoi is Luke’s usual word for fellow believers (for example 9:30 and 21:17, where “the brothers received us gladly”), and the Greek does not add “of Rome” or “of the Way.” The party has stayed seven days twice before, at Troas (20:6) and at Tyre with the disciples (21:4), and parekalēthēmen, “we were urged,” is the passive of parakaleō; the invitation is the hosts’. The Greek does not say who allowed a prisoner’s party to stay a week, and the centurion Julius, last named at 27:43, is not mentioned in this chapter. The closing clause, kai houtōs eis tēn Rhōmēn ēlthamen, “and so we came to Rome,” is said before the road is described: the verse announces the arrival that verses 15–16 then walk toward. The verb is the aorist ēlthamen, “we came,” not “we went toward.” Shelf: ASV “so we came to Rome” and NIV “And so we came to Rome” keep the arrival; KJV “so we went toward Rome,” Geneva “so we went toward Rome” and NWT 1984 “in this way we came toward Rome” make it a direction; Douay “so we went to Rome” makes it a going; TLB “Then we went on to Rome” drops the “so.” NIV “invited us to spend a week with them” reads the seven days as a week and NWT 1984 “entreated to remain with them seven days” keeps the number.

“To meet us” (Acts 28:15). Kakeithen hoi adelphoi akousantes ta peri hēmōn ēlthan eis apantēsin hēmin achri Appiou Phorou kai Triōn Tabernōn. The noun apantēsis stands in three verses of the New Testament, always in the phrase eis apantēsin, “to meet”: Matthew 25:6, where the cry at midnight is “Come out to meet him”; here; and 1 Thessalonians 4:17 (not yet on these pages). The entry apantēsis follows it. The phrase is what a city does for a person it honors: in Josephus the people of Ascalon, after Jonathan’s victory at Azotus, proelthon eis apantēsin autō, “came out to meet him,” bringing him gifts of hospitality and honoring him (Antiquities 13.101, Niese’s Greek). Luke says only that the brothers “came to meet us”; he does not say they were a delegation, and the number is not given. The two places are Appiou Phoron, the Forum of Appius, and Triōn Tabernōn, the Three Taverns, and each stands in one verse of the New Testament. The Antonine Itinerary gives the stages of the road: sixteen Roman miles from Rome to Aricia, seventeen from Aricia to Three Taverns and ten from Three Taverns to the Forum of Appius (Itinerarium Antonini 107.3–4, as given in the Tabula Peutingeriana database of the University of Eichstätt and in Smith’s Bible Dictionary), so Three Taverns stands thirty-three and the Forum forty-three Roman miles from Rome; the Eichstätt database equates the Forum with modern Borgo Faiti, and Pleiades gives points for the two places on the Via Appia (Forum 422927, Three Taverns 423106). The library entries The Forum of Appius and The Three Taverns say what is known. Cicero names both in one letter to Atticus: he writes it “from the Forum of Appius, at the fourth hour” (ab Appi Foro hora quarta) and says he had sent another “a little before from Three Taverns” (dederam aliam paulo ante a Tribus Tabernis, Letters to Atticus 2.10, Latin fetched), so the two stations were one stage apart on one road. The Greek does not say that the brothers came in one party or two, or that Paul saw them at one place or at both. Paul’s response is two participles and a verb, hous idōn … eucharistēsas tō theō elabe tharsos, “seeing them, giving thanks to God, took courage.” The noun tharsos, “courage,” stands in one verse; its verb stands in seven (read from the hits), every time as the command “Take courage!” (Matthew 9:2, 9:22, 14:27, Mark 6:50, 10:49, John 16:33, and Acts 23:11), and the last is the Lord’s word to Paul in Jerusalem, “so you must bear witness in Rome also.” The apparatus (1068) has Robinson–Pierpont with exēlthon for ēlthan, “went out” for “came.” Shelf: NIV “they traveled as far as the Forum of Appius and the Three Taverns to meet us” keeps both names; ASV “The Market of Appius and The Three Taverns,” NWT 1984 “the Marketplace of” and Geneva “the Market of Appius, and at the three taverns” translate Phoron; KJV “Appii forum, and The three taverns” and Douay “Appii Forum, and the Three Taverns” keep the Latin; TLB “the Forum on the Appian Way. Others joined us at The Three Taverns” splits the brothers into two groups and adds the road. For the courage: Geneva “waxed bold” and NIV “was encouraged” are further from tharsos than KJV “took courage” and Douay “took courage.”

“Permitted to stay by himself” (Acts 28:16). Hote de eisēlthomen eis Rhōmēn, epetrapē tō Paulō menein kath’ heauton syn tō phylassonti auton stratiōtē. The verb epetrapē, “was permitted,” is the verb of 26:1, where Agrippa says Paul is “permitted” to speak, and of 27:3, where Julius “permitted” him to visit his friends. Kath’ heauton is “by himself, on his own,” and the Greek does not say whether that means in a house, a room or a camp, or where; it says one soldier guards him. Paul names a chain at 28:20, and the Greek does not say that it was fastened to the soldier; Acts has shown custody of two kinds before, Peter “bound with two chains” between two soldiers (12:6) and the freedom Felix ordered for Paul (24:23). The apparatus (1069–1070) has two points. Robinson–Pierpont has ēlthomen for eisēlthomen, “we came” for “we entered,” and a long reading, ho hekatontarchos paredōken tous desmious tō stratopedarchē, tō de Paulō epetrapē, “the centurion handed over the prisoners to the stratopedarch, but Paul was permitted.” The printed text and this translation omit it. The word stratopedarchēs is not in the archived Greek of the New Testament at all, and the commentary the word has drawn is divided: it has been read as the praetorian prefect, the commander of the camp and garrison of the Praetorian Guard, or as the lesser praefectus peregrinorum or princeps castrorum (Tajra 2010, 41–43), while inscriptions use the word to translate praefectus castrorum, the camp prefect (Applebaum 1989, 61–63); both are cited as the Wikipedia article on stratopedarches gives them. Luke does not say what became of the other prisoners of 27:1, and the long reading is a reading that does. Shelf: KJV “the centurion delivered the prisoners to the captain of the guard” and Geneva “the Centurion delivered the prisoners to the general Captain” print the long reading; Douay “Paul was suffered to dwell by himself, with a soldier that kept him” does not, so the Douay agrees with the printed text here against the KJV. ASV “Paul was suffered to abide by himself with the soldier that guarded him,” NWT 1984 “Paul was permitted to stay by himself with the soldier guarding him” and NIV “Paul was allowed to live by himself, with a soldier to guard him” keep the single soldier; TLB “Paul was permitted to live wherever he wanted to, though guarded by a soldier” adds a freedom of place the Greek does not give.

Verses 17–22 · πρώτους … παρεδόθην … ἀνακρίναντες … αἰτίαν θανάτου … ἀντιλεγόντων … Καίσαρα … ἅλυσιν … ἀδελφῶν … αἱρέσεως prōtous … paredothēn … anakrinantes … aitian thanatou … antilegontōn … Kaisara … halysin … adelphōn … haireseōs — Acts 28:17–22: how does Paul describe how he came to Rome, and what do the Jews of Rome say they know?

“He called together the leading men of the Jews” (Acts 28:17). Egeneto de meta hēmeras treis synkalesasthai auton tous ontas tōn Ioudaiōn prōtous. In city after city Paul has begun with the Jews at the synagogue: 13:14, 17:2, 18:4, 19:8, and at Pisidian Antioch he said “It was necessary that the word of God be spoken to you first” (13:46). Here he cannot go to a synagogue, and the Greek has him send for them, three days after arriving. The phrase tous prōtous tōn Ioudaiōn is the one the Jerusalem accusers were called at 25:2, “the leading men of the Jews,” and it is the same adjective, prōtos, as the “leading man” Publius at 28:7; the Greek does not say that these are the same kind of men, or how many came. The speech begins with Egō, “I,” before the address: Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print Egō, andres adelphoi and Robinson–Pierpont prints Andres adelphoi, egō (apparatus 1072), so the emphasis falls on the speaker. This translation prints “Men, brothers” as at 22:1, 23:1 and 13:15. Shelf: NIV “called together the local Jewish leaders” and TLB “called together the local Jewish leaders” add “local”; KJV “called the chief of the Jews together” and Geneva “calleth the chief of the Jews together” keep a plain “chief.”

“I was handed over” (Acts 28:17). Desmios ex Hierosolymōn paredothēn eis tas cheiras tōn Rhōmaiōn: “as a prisoner from Jerusalem I was handed over into the hands of the Romans.” The verb is paradidōmi, the verb Peter used of the people who “handed over” Jesus (Acts 3:13), and the verb of Agabus’s sign: “thus will the Jews bind him in Jerusalem and hand him over into the hands of the nations” (21:11). Paul’s sentence has Agabus’s words with “Romans” for “nations.” Luke’s own account of the day is different: a mob drags Paul from the temple and the tribune takes him from it and has him bound (21:33), and the tribune writes that he “rescued” a man “seized by the Jews and about to be killed by them” (23:27). No verse of the narration says the Jews gave Paul to the Romans; here Paul says it, in one clause, for the outcome. Shelf: NIV “I was arrested in Jerusalem and handed over to the Romans” and TLB “I was arrested by the Jews in Jerusalem and handed over to the Roman government” supply an arrest and, in the TLB, the arresting party; KJV “yet was I delivered prisoner from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans” and ASV “yet was delivered prisoner from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans” keep the Greek’s order; NWT 1984 “I was delivered over as a prisoner from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans” is the same. The entry paradidōmi follows the verb, and desmios the noun for the prisoner.

“No ground for death” (Acts 28:18). Hoitines anakrinantes me eboulonto apolysai dia to mēdemian aitian thanatou hyparchein en emoi. The Greek phrase mēdemian aitian thanatou, “no ground for death,” is word for word the phrase of 13:28, where those who live in Jerusalem and their rulers, “finding no ground for death,” ask Pilate to have Jesus killed; a third verse has the neuter form aition, Luke 23:22, where Pilate says “I have found in him no guilt deserving of death.” Paul says of himself what Acts says of Jesus. The participle anakrinantes, “examining,” stands in five verses of Acts: 4:9, 12:19 (Herod examines the guards), 17:11 (the Bereans examine the Scriptures), 24:8 and here; the entry anakrinō follows it from Luke 23:14, where Pilate says he has “examined” Jesus. “They wished to release me” is a summary, not a quotation, and the officers who say as much in the narration are the tribune (23:29, “no accusation worthy of death or bonds”), Festus (25:25, “I found that he had done nothing worthy of death”) and Agrippa (26:32, “could have been released”); Festus also asked Paul whether he would go up to Jerusalem to be tried (25:9), which this verse leaves out. Shelf: KJV “would have let me go, because there was no cause of death in me,” Geneva “would have let me go, because there was no cause of death in me” and Douay “would have released me, for that there was no cause of death in me” keep the imperfect’s “would”; NWT 1984 “were desirous of releasing me, as there was no cause for death in me” and ASV “desired to set me at liberty, because there was no cause of death in me” keep the wish; NIV “not guilty of any crime deserving death” and TLB “no cause for the death sentence demanded by the Jewish leaders” make it a verdict, and the TLB adds who demanded it.

“Spoke against,” “appeal,” “my nation” (Acts 28:19). Antilegontōn de tōn Ioudaiōn ēnankasthēn epikalesasthai Kaisara, ouch hōs tou ethnous mou echōn ti katēgorein. The verb antilegō, “to speak against,” stands in three verses of Acts, 13:45 (where this translation prints “contradicting”), here and 28:22, where the Jews of Rome say of this party that “everywhere it is spoken against”; its first appearance in Luke’s books is Simeon’s “a sign that is spoken against” (Luke 2:34). The verb ēnankasthēn, “I was compelled,” is the verb of 26:11, where Paul, before he was a believer, “tried to force” the believers to blaspheme, and it stands in Acts only in these two verses. The appeal stands in six verses (25:11, 12, 21, 25, 26:32 and here), and the new thing is the qualification, ouch hōs … echōn ti katēgorein, “not as though I had anything to accuse my nation of.” The noun ethnos is “nation” of Israel here, as at 24:17, and it is the word of “the nations” (tois ethnesin) that the chapter will end on at 28:28; the entry ethnos follows it. Shelf: KJV “when the Jews spake against it” and ASV “when the Jews spake against it” keep the verb; Douay “the Jews contradicting it” and Geneva “when the Jews spake contrary” render it differently; NWT 1984 “when the Jews kept speaking against it” adds the imperfect; NIV “The Jews objected” and TLB “when the Jews protested the decision” weaken it, and the TLB adds “the decision.” For the qualification: NIV “I certainly did not intend to bring any charge against my own people” adds “certainly” and “intend,” and TLB “with no malice against them” replaces an accusation with a feeling.

“This chain” and “the hope of Israel” (Acts 28:20). Dia tautēn oun tēn aitian parekalesa hymas idein kai proslalēsai, heneken gar tēs elpidos tou Israēl tēn halysin tautēn perikeimai. The noun aitia comes back from the last verse but one: no aitia of death in him, and so this aitia for calling them in. The verb parekalesa is the one that meant “we were urged” at 28:14; here it is “I called … in,” and the Greek does not say whether he asked to see them or whether he means that they should see him, which is why the shelf splits: KJV “called for you, to see you,” Geneva “called for you to see you” and NIV “I have asked to see you” put the seeing on his side; ASV “to see and to speak with me” puts it on theirs; Douay “I desired to see you” and NWT 1984 “entreated to see and speak to” leave the object open; TLB “I asked you to come here today so we could get acquainted” makes it an introduction. The noun halysis, “chain,” stands in four verses of Acts: 12:6 and 12:7 (Peter’s), 21:33 (“bound with two chains”) and here; elsewhere in the New Testament it stands at Mark 5:3, 5:4, Luke 8:29, 2 Timothy 1:16, Ephesians 6:20 and Revelation 20:1 (these last two not yet on these pages), and the entry halysis follows it. The chain is on the speaker, perikeimai, “lies around me,” and it is for tēs elpidos tou Israēl, “the hope of Israel” — the hope for which he said he was judged at 23:6, accused at 26:6–7 and which he confessed at 24:15. TLB “it is because I believe the Messiah has come that I am bound” replaces the phrase with a statement of belief that the Greek does not make here.

“We neither received letters,” “the brothers,” “this party” (Acts 28:21–22). Hēmeis oute grammata peri sou edexametha apo tēs Ioudaias, oute paragenomenos tis tōn adelphōn apēngeilen ē elalēsen ti peri sou ponēron. Grammata are letters in the sense of correspondence, a different sense from the schooling of John 7:15. In this verse tōn adelphōn, “of the brothers,” are Jews who have come from Judea, the same word as the Christian “brothers” of 28:14 and 15, and the Greek keeps it. The reply is that nothing, good or bad, has reached them, and that they want to hear it from Paul: axioumen de par’ sou akousai ha phroneis, “we think it right to hear from you what you think.” Then peri men gar tēs haireseōs tautēs: the noun hairesis stands in six verses of Acts (5:17, 15:5, 24:5, 24:14, 26:5 and here), and this translation prints “party” at all six, as the entry hairesis follows. The shelf prints “sect” here in six of seven versions (NIV “this sect,” KJV “this sect,” ASV “this sect,” Geneva “this sect,” Douay “this sect” and NWT 1984 “this sect”) and, in the seventh, TLB “these Christians,” which names the people where the Greek names the party. The last clause, pantachou antilegetai, “everywhere it is spoken against,” echoes the charge of the Asian Jews in the temple, that Paul “teaches everyone everywhere against the people and the law and this place” (21:28); the adverb stands in four verses of Acts (17:30, 21:28, 24:3 and here). What the Jews of Rome know and do not know sits beside 18:2, where Claudius had ordered all the Jews to leave Rome, and the Greek does not reconcile the two. Apparatus (1073–1074): Robinson–Pierpont has katēgorēsai for katēgorein at 28:19 and puts estin hēmin for hēmin estin at 28:22, neither of which changes the sense. KJV “neither any of the brethren that came shewed or spake any harm of thee” and ASV “nor did any of the brethren come hither and report or speak any harm of thee” keep the brothers; NIV “none of our people who have come from there” replaces them.

Verses 23–24 · ξενίαν … ἐξετίθετο … διαμαρτυρόμενος … πείθων … ἐπείθοντο … ἠπίστουν xenian … exetitheto … diamartyromenos … peithōn … epeithonto … ēpistoun — Acts 28:23–24: what does Paul do from morning until evening in his lodging, and why does the Greek say some “were persuaded” and others “were disbelieving”?

“At his lodging” (Acts 28:23). Taxamenoi de autō hēmeran ēkon pros auton eis tēn xenian pleiones. The noun xenia, “lodging, guest quarters,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, here and Philemon 22, where Paul, a prisoner, asks, “prepare a guest room for me.” Whether the lodging of this verse is the same place as the “rented quarters” of 28:30 is not said, and neither is where in Rome it was; the verb of hosting from the same root, xenizō, was Publius’s at 28:7. The comparative pleiones is “more” than came the first time, and no number is given. The apparatus (1075) has the archive’s Greek printing ēkon, “had come,” the Robinson–Pierpont reading, where Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print ēlthon, “came”; both are “came,” and this translation prints it so. The next note (1076) has Robinson–Pierpont adding ta before peri tou Iēsou, so that it reads “the things about Jesus,” the phrase of 28:31; the printed text does not have it. Shelf: KJV “there came many to him into his lodging,” Geneva “there came many unto him into his lodging” and Douay “there came very many to him unto his lodgings” give the lodging; NWT 1984 “came in greater numbers to him in his lodging place” and NIV “came in even larger numbers to the place where he was staying” keep the comparative; TLB “large numbers came to his house” makes it a house and drops the comparison. NIV “They arranged to meet Paul on a certain day” and TLB “a time was set” have the day appointed by someone; the Greek has the Jews appoint it, taxamenoi autō, “having appointed him a day.”

“Setting out the matter, testifying solemnly” (Acts 28:23). Hois exetitheto diamartyromenos tēn basileian tou theou peithōn te autous peri tou Iēsou apo te tou nomou Mōuseōs kai tōn prophētōn apo prōi heōs hesperas. The verb exetitheto is ektithēmi in the sense of setting out an account, the verb of Peter at 11:4, who “set it out to them in orderly sequence,” and of Priscilla and Aquila at 18:26, who “set out to him the way of God more accurately.” The participle diamartyromenos, “testifying solemnly,” is from a verb that stands in nine verses of Acts: 2:40, 8:25, 10:42, 18:5, 20:21, 20:23, 20:24, 23:11 and here. The earlier one in Paul’s own story is the Lord’s word to him at night in Jerusalem, “as you have solemnly testified to the things about me in Jerusalem, so you must bear witness in Rome also,” and this is the scene in Rome. The entry diamartyromai follows the verb. “The kingdom of God” stands in six verses of Acts as that exact phrase (1:3, 8:12, 14:22, 19:8, here and 28:31), and the first and last of the six are the book’s opening, where the risen Jesus speaks of it for forty days, and its closing line. The verb peithō, “to persuade,” is Paul’s verb at Corinth (18:4) and Ephesus (19:8, “persuading about the kingdom of God”), in Demetrius’s complaint that “this Paul has persuaded and turned away a considerable crowd” (19:26) and in Agrippa’s “you are persuading me” (26:28). The material is apo te tou nomou Mōuseōs kai tōn prophētōn, “from both the law of Moses and the Prophets,” and the same pair is the whole of Paul’s case at 24:14 (“the law and the things written in the Prophets”) and 26:22 (“nothing outside of what both the prophets and Moses spoke of”), and the Lord’s way of reading on the road to Emmaus, “beginning from Moses and from all the prophets” (Luke 24:27). The length is apo prōi heōs hesperas, “from morning until evening.” In Hebrew the phrase min-haboqer ad-ha’arev stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, Exodus 18:13, where Moses sits to judge the people “from the morning until the evening,” and in 18:16 he says he makes known “the statutes of God and his laws”; Luke does not mark it as an allusion, and this note does not claim one. Shelf: NWT 1984 “by bearing thorough witness concerning the kingdom of God” and NIV “He witnessed to them from morning till evening” give the verb a witness; KJV “expounded and testified the kingdom of God,” Geneva “he expounded, testifying the kingdom of God” and Douay “he expounded, testifying the kingdom of God” keep the pair; TLB “He told them about the Kingdom of God and taught them about Jesus from the Scriptures—from the five books of Moses and the books of prophecy” turns the law of Moses into “the five books of Moses” and the Prophets into “the books of prophecy,” and ends “He began lecturing in the morning and went on into the evening!” NIV “he tried to persuade them about Jesus” adds “tried,” which is not in the participle.

“Some were being persuaded … others were disbelieving” (Acts 28:24). Kai hoi men epeithonto tois legomenois hoi de ēpistoun. The two imperfects are the verb of the last verse’s peithōn in its passive and the opposite verb apisteō, “to disbelieve,” which stands in Acts only here; in Luke it is the apostles’ response to the women at the tomb, “they did not believe them” (Luke 24:11), and at 24:41 they “still could not believe it for joy.” The Greek is a balance, hoi men … hoi de, with no number on either side, and the imperfect does not say they were finished: they were being persuaded and were disbelieving. Shelf: NIV “Some were convinced by what he said” and Geneva “some were [persuaded] with the things which were spoken” keep the passive of peithō; KJV “some believed the things which were spoken, and some believed not,” ASV “some believed the things which were spoken, and some disbelieved” and Douay “some believed the things that were said; but some believed not” use “believed” for the first and break the link with “persuading” in the verse before; NWT 1984 “some began to believe the things said; others would not believe” also uses “believe” and turns the second imperfect into a refusal; TLB “Some believed and some didn’t” is the shortest.

Verses 25–29 · ἀσύμφωνοι … ῥῆμα ἓν … Ἠσαΐου … ἐπαχύνθη … ἐκάμμυσαν … ἰάσομαι … ἔθνεσιν … σωτήριον … ἀκούσονται asymphōnoi … rhēma hen … Ēsaiou … epachynthē … ekammysan … iasomai … ethnesin … sōtērion … akousontai — Acts 28:25–29: what does Paul quote from Isaiah, what does “sent to the nations” say and not say, and why is there no verse 29?

“Out of harmony … one word … to your fathers” (Acts 28:25). Asymphōnoi de ontes pros allēlous apelyonto, eipontos tou Paulou rhēma hen hoti Kalōs to pneuma to hagion elalēsen dia Ēsaiou tou prophētou pros tous pateras hymōn. The adjective asymphōnos, “out of harmony,” stands in one verse of the New Testament; its verb symphōneō is the “agreed” of 5:9. The verb apelyonto, “they were departing,” is the middle or passive of apolyō, the verb of 28:18, where the officers “wished to release” Paul; the entry apolyō follows it. The Greek says rhēma hen, “one word,” and not “final”; the entry rhēma follows the noun. That the Holy Spirit speaks “through” a prophet is Luke’s way of saying it at 1:16 and 4:25, and Isaiah is the prophet Philip found the Ethiopian reading (8:28, 8:30). The apparatus (1077–1078) has two points. Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28 print hymōn, “your fathers,” the phrase Stephen used when he said the fathers persecuted the prophets (7:52); Robinson–Pierpont has hēmōn, “our fathers.” And the participle legōn, “saying,” is masculine after the neuter “Spirit,” where Robinson–Pierpont has the neuter legon. Shelf: KJV “unto our fathers,” Geneva “unto our fathers” and Douay “to our fathers” follow the Robinson–Pierpont reading; ASV “unto your fathers,” NWT 1984 “to YOUR forefathers” and NIV “to your ancestors” follow the printed text; TLB “The Holy Spirit was right when he said through Isaiah the prophet” leaves the fathers out. The spelling splits too: KJV “Esaias” and Douay “Isaias” against “Isaiah.” NIV “this final statement” and TLB “this final word from Paul” add “final”; Douay “Paul speaking this one word” and ASV “Paul had spoken one word” keep the number.

The quotation (Acts 28:26–27). Poreuthēti pros ton laon touton kai eipon: Akoē akousete kai ou mē synēte, kai blepontes blepsete kai ou mē idēte; epachynthē gar hē kardia tou laou toutou, kai tois ōsin bareōs ēkousan, kai tous ophthalmous autōn ekammysan; mēpote idōsin tois ophthalmois kai tois ōsin akousōsin kai tē kardia synōsin kai epistrepsōsin, kai iasomai autous. This passage of Isaiah (Isaiah 6:9–10; Isaiah 6 is not yet on these pages) is quoted in five places in the New Testament, all of them on these pages: Matthew 13:14–15, Mark 4:12, Luke 8:10, John 12:40 and here. Mark and Luke give only a few clauses, and John’s Greek is a different rendering, Tetyphlōken autōn tous ophthalmous kai epōrōsen autōn tēn kardian, “He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart,” in which God acts. Acts 28:26–27 is the same Greek as Matthew 13:14–15 from Akoē akousete to iasomai autous, word for word (compared in the archived Greek), and this translation prints them the same. Rahlfs’s Septuagint of the passage (fetched) has the same text with two differences: it opens poreuthēti kai eipon tō laō toutō, “Go and say to this people,” where Acts has pros ton laon touton kai eipon, “Go to this people and say,” and it reads tois ōsin autōn barōs ēkousan, “with their ears they heard heavily,” where Acts and Matthew have no autōn. The Hebrew of the Masoretic text (from the archive) is not the same sentence. It is a string of commands to the prophet: shim’u shamo’a ve-al-taviynu, “Hear indeed, but do not understand,” and hashmen lev-ha’am hazzeh … hakhbed … hasha’, “Make the heart of this people fat, make its ears heavy, and shut its eyes,” and it ends ve-rafa lo, in the third person, “and healing be for him.” The Greek makes the people the one acting or acted upon (epachynthē, “has grown thick,” and ekammysan, “they closed,” both of which stand only in Matthew 13:15 and here) and ends in the first person, iasomai autous, “and I would heal them,” the verb of 28:8. The conjunction mēpote is “lest.” Shelf: NIV “You will be ever hearing but never understanding” and KJV “Hearing ye shall hear, and shall not understand” carry the doubled verb differently; ASV “By hearing ye shall hear, and shall in no wise understand” and Geneva “By hearing ye shall hear, and shall not understand” keep the dative; Douay “With the ear you shall hear” keeps the noun; NWT 1984 “By hearing, YOU will hear but by no means understand” also keeps it. TLB “Say to the Jews” writes “the Jews” where the Greek has “this people,” and its “turn to me to heal you” changes the person of the last clause. For the heart: KJV “is waxed gross,” Douay “is grown gross” and Geneva “is waxed fat” keep the thickening; NIV “has become calloused” and NWT 1984 “has grown unreceptive” interpret it. NWT 1984 “that they should never see with their eyes” turns “lest” into a purpose, where NIV “Otherwise they might see with their eyes” and Douay “lest perhaps they should see with their eyes” keep the warning.

“Sent to the nations” (Acts 28:28). Gnōston oun estō hymin hoti tois ethnesin apestalē touto to sōtērion tou theou; autoi kai akousontai. The neuter noun sōtērion stands in four verses of the New Testament, and in Luke’s two books three times: Simeon’s “my eyes have seen your salvation” (Luke 2:30), “all flesh shall see the salvation of God” (Luke 3:6) and here; the fourth is Ephesians 6:17 (not yet on these pages). The last is a line of Isaiah in the Greek: in Rahlfs’s Septuagint Isaiah 40:5 reads kai opsetai pasa sarx to sōtērion tou theou, which Luke 3:4–6 quotes, where the Hebrew reads only that “all flesh together shall see it” (Isaiah 40:5). And in Hebrew Isaiah 52:10 (not yet on these pages) puts the same two things side by side: “in the eyes of all the nations,” and “the salvation of our God” (yeshu’at eloheinu), which Luke does not quote here and this note does not claim he is quoting. The entry sōtērion follows the noun. This is the third time Paul turns to the nations after resistance or division among the Jews of a city: at Pisidian Antioch, “we are turning to the nations” (13:46), at Corinth, “From now on I will go to the nations” (18:6) and here. At Corinth the next verse has him go into the house of Titius Justus next to the synagogue (18:7), and here the next verses have him welcoming all who come in. The final clause is autoi kai akousontai, “they also will listen”: the verb is akouō, the verb of the Isaiah quotation, where the people “will indeed hear (akoē akousete) and never understand,” and the nations will listen, with no object and no result given. The apparatus (1079–1080) has Westcott–Hort with hymin esto for esto hymin, and Robinson–Pierpont lacks touto, “this,” so that it reads “the salvation of God”; the KJV “the salvation of God is sent unto the Gentiles” follows it, and the ASV “this salvation of God is sent unto the Gentiles” does not. The Greek says ethnē, which this translation prints “nations” (Luke 18:32 prints “Gentiles”); NWT 1984 “has been sent out to the nations” has it, and the NIV, KJV, Geneva and Douay print “Gentiles.” TLB “this salvation from God is available to the Gentiles too, and they will accept it” adds “too” and turns “will listen” into “will accept.” NWT 1984 “this, the means by which God saves” glosses the noun.

What these verses have been made to say. Verses 26–28 have been read as God’s closing of the door on the Jews. John Kilgallen, surveying the commentators, quotes Weiser that most exegetes take Luke to say that the Jews who do not believe in Jesus have put themselves outside Israel, and names Haenchen and Conzelmann among those who read the passage so (Biblica 90, 2009, 176–187; Weiser’s German is translated here). A 2025 study says the passage has “traditionally been interpreted as a divine pronouncement of judgment on Jewish unbelief” and has consequently functioned as a text supporting anti-Jewish interpretations (Kim Suyeon, Journal of Biblical and Ancient Studies; translated here from the Korean), and Joseph Tyson, writing on Luke–Acts and anti-Judaism, says that knowledge of “the horrifying, if unintended, consequences of anti-Jewish expressions in ancient literature, including sacred literature, should encourage us to avoid and condemn those responses that support negative attitudes toward and actions against Jews and Judaism” (Bible Interp, University of Arizona, 2010). The text will not carry the reading. Tyson himself notes that Paul returns to speak in synagogues after the first two announcements but “not after the one in Acts 28:28, which essentially ends the book,” and the Greek of 28:30 puts no limit on the “all” whom he welcomes. The quotation is addressed in Isaiah to the people of his own day, and Matthew 13:14–15 has Jesus apply it to his hearers, in the parables, long before Rome. The same scene has some of the Jews “being persuaded” (28:24). The sentence that follows the quotation is about where the salvation goes (“sent to the nations”), not about where it stops, and the verses after it have Paul welcoming pantas, “all,” who came in (28:30). At Jerusalem James and the elders had told him “how many tens of thousands there are among the Jews of those who have believed” (21:20). And Paul himself, in his letter to Rome, asks in Greek mē apōsato ho theos ton laon autou?, “Did God not push away his people?” and answers mē genoito, “May it never be” (Romans 11:1, not yet on these pages), and says ametamelēta gar ta charismata kai hē klēsis tou theou, “for the gifts and the calling of God are not taken back” (Romans 11:29, not yet on these pages); the two renderings here are this note’s own, from the archived Greek.

There is no verse 29 (Acts 28:29). The apparatus (1081) is long here. The printed text, with Westcott–Hort, Tregelles and Nestle–Aland 28, ends verse 28 at akousontai and goes on to Enemeinen de dietian holēn, “And he stayed on a whole two years.” Robinson–Pierpont adds a verse after akousontai: kai tauta autou eipontos apēlthon hoi Ioudaioi, pollēn echontes en heautois syzētēsin, “And when he had said these things, the Jews departed, having much debate among themselves.” This translation does not print it, and leaves the number 29 empty with the gap panel above. Four of the seven English versions on the shelf print the verse, the KJV, ASV, Geneva and Douay; the NIV and the TLB do not print it, and the NWT 1984 leaves a dash in its place. On the Spanish shelf the RV 1909 and RV60 print it, the NVI does not, and both TNM editions leave the dash. With the verse, the scene closes on the Jews’ debate; without it, the narration goes from “they also will listen” to the two years.

Verses 30–31 · διετίαν ὅλην … μισθώματι … ἀπεδέχετο … κηρύσσων … διδάσκων … παρρησίας … ἀκωλύτως dietian holēn … misthōmati … apedecheto … kēryssōn … didaskōn … parrēsias … akōlytōs — Acts 28:30–31: what are the “two whole years,” the “rented quarters,” and why does the book end on the word “unhindered”?

“A whole two years” and “rented quarters” (Acts 28:30). Enemeinen de dietian holēn en idiō misthōmati, kai apedecheto pantas tous eisporeuomenous pros auton. The noun dietia, “a period of two years,” stands in two verses of the New Testament, 24:27, where Felix is succeeded by Festus when “two years had been completed” and leaves Paul bound, and here, so the book has two two-year periods of custody, one in Caesarea and one in Rome. The verb enemeinen, “stayed on,” is emmenō, the verb of 14:22, where the disciples are urged “to continue in the faith”; the apparatus (1082–1083) has Robinson–Pierpont with the plain emeinen, “remained,” and with ho Paulos added after de. In the printed text, then, the name Paul does not stand in verses 26–31; the last time it is written is 28:25, and the subject of the last two verses is the same “he.” The noun misthōma stands in one verse of the New Testament, so it is not compared with any other use. It comes from the root of misthos, “wages, hire,” and the Greek is en idiō misthōmati, “in his own hired thing.” Thayer’s lexicon gives the noun two senses, “the price for which anything is either let or hired” and “that which is either let or hired for a price, as a house, dwelling, lodging,” and puts Acts 28:30 under the second, which is the reading rented quarters; some modern translations add “at his own expense,” which expands the Greek and is not a second meaning of the noun. The Greek does not say whether the quarters were a house, an apartment or a room, or whether they are the “lodging” of 28:23. The verb apedecheto, “was welcoming,” is apodechomai, which stands in four verses of Acts: 2:41 (those who “received” the word), 18:27, 24:3 (“we welcome it”) and here, in the imperfect, “was welcoming,” for the whole period. The object is pantas tous eisporeuomenous pros auton, “all who came in to him”; the Greek has no “Jews” or “Gentiles” to limit the “all,” and it follows 28:28 directly. Shelf: NIV “in his own rented house,” TLB “in his rented house,” KJV “in his own hired house,” Geneva “in an house hired for himself” and NWT 1984 “in his own hired house” make it a house, five of the seven; ASV “in his own hired dwelling” and Douay “in his own hired lodging” do not. NIV “welcomed all who came to see him” narrows the verb to visits; NWT 1984 “he would kindly receive all those who came in to him” keeps the coming in.

“Proclaiming … teaching” (Acts 28:31). Kēryssōn tēn basileian tou theou kai didaskōn ta peri tou kyriou Iēsou Christou meta pasēs parrēsias akōlytōs. The participle kēryssōn is the verb of 8:5 and 20:25, where Paul told the elders of Ephesus that he had gone about “proclaiming the kingdom”; the entry kēryssō follows it. “The kingdom of God” is the first and the last of the six verses of Acts that have the phrase (1:3, 8:12, 14:22, 19:8, 28:23 and here), and ta peri, “the things concerning,” was the way the risen Jesus’s forty days were summed up at 1:3 (“speaking of the things concerning the kingdom of God”), and the way Apollos is described at 18:25 (“the things about Jesus”). The three words kyrios Iēsous Christos in a row stand in Acts in two verses (read from the hits), 11:17 and here, and the last verse of the book is the second. The noun parrēsia, “boldness,” and its verb parrhēsiazomai together stand in twelve verses of Acts (2:29, 4:13, 4:29, 4:31, 9:27, 9:28, 13:46, 14:3, 18:26, 19:8, 26:26 and here), and the phrase “with all boldness” is the church’s prayer at 4:29, “grant to your slaves to speak your word with all boldness”; the book ends with the phrase said of Paul. The entry parrhēsiazomai follows the verb. The last word, akōlytōs, “unhindered,” stands in one verse; its verb kōlyō stands in seven verses of Acts, and the seven can be read in a row: 8:36 (“What prevents me from being baptized?”), 10:47 (“Can anyone forbid the water”), 11:17 (“who was I, that I could hinder God?”), 16:6 (“having been hindered by the Holy Spirit from speaking the word in Asia”), 24:23 (“that none of his own people be prevented from serving him”), 27:43 (the centurion “prevented them from their plan”) and here, in the adverb. The entry akōlytos follows the adverb and kōlyō the verb. Paul had said of his coming to Rome that he “often intended to come to you — and was prevented until now” (Romans 1:13), with the same verb in the passive. The Greek does not say what came after the two years, and this page does not use the reports of later writers; the book ends here. Shelf: NIV “with all boldness and without hindrance” and NWT 1984 “with the greatest freeness of speech, without hindrance” keep the adverb as a phrase; KJV “with all confidence, no man forbidding him,” ASV “with all boldness, none forbidding him” and TLB “and no one tried to stop him” make it a clause, with a different verb; Geneva “with all boldness of speech, without let” and Douay “with all confidence, without prohibition” use the old nouns.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact / distinct: “brought safely through” for diasōthentes at v1 and v4, the verb of 27:43–44; “recognized” for epegnōmen at v1 against the “did not recognize” of 27:39; “barbarians” for barbaroi at v2 and v4; “extraordinary” for tychousan at v2, as at 19:11; “kindness” for philanthrōpian at v2, the noun of the adverb at 27:3; “kindled” and “fastened on” for hapsantes and kathēpse at v2 and v3; “beast” for thērion at v4 and v5; “Justice” for hē dikē at v4; “out of place” for atopon at v6, as at Luke 23:41 and Acts 25:5; “leading man” for prōtos at v7 and v17; “gripped” for synechomenon at v8, as at Luke 4:38; “healed” for both iasato at v8 and etherapeuonto at v9; “honored … honors” for timais etimēsan at v10; “casting off” for perielontes at v13, as at 27:40; “meet” for apantēsin at v15; “permitted” for epetrapē at v16; “handed over” for paredothēn at v17; “no ground for death” for mēdemian aitian thanatou at v18, the words of 13:28; “spoke against” and “spoken against” for antilegō at v19 and v22; “chain” for halysin at v20; “party” for hairesis at v22; “testifying solemnly” for diamartyromenos and “set out” for exetitheto at v23; “nations” for ethnesin at v28, against “nation” for ethnous at v19; “salvation” for sōtērion at v28; “rented” for misthōmati at v30; “boldness” for parrēsias and “unhindered” for akōlytōs at v31. The Isaiah quotation of v26–27 is printed word for word as Matthew 13:14–15 prints it.

⚠ And the places the library reports rather than decides. Which island Melitē of v1 is, Malta or the Adriatic island. Who the “us” of v7 is, and what the title “first of the island” names. Whether the “many honors” of v10 included payment. Whether the ship of v11 belonged to the grain fleet of Alexandria. Whether the course at v13 was a circuit or a casting off. Whether the long text of v16 belongs, and what officer the stratopedarchēs was. Whether “from Jerusalem into the hands of the Romans” at v17 describes events the narration tells otherwise. Whether the “rented quarters” of v30 were a house, an apartment or a room.

The book is finished. Acts is now complete on these pages, twenty-eight chapters from the risen Jesus “speaking of the things concerning the kingdom of God” (1:3) to Paul in Rome “teaching the things concerning the Lord Jesus Christ with all boldness, unhindered” (28:31); and with Luke’s Gospel, whose twenty-four chapters are also all here, the two volumes of one author now stand whole. The promise of 1:8 is “both in Jerusalem and in all Judea and Samaria and to the end of the earth,” and the book ends in Rome without saying that it has reached the end of the earth, or what became of Paul after two years. The Greek of the last verse ends on a word that stands nowhere else in the New Testament, and this translation has tried, from the first chapter, to leave every such place on the page.
