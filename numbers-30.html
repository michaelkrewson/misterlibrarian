A single law of vows: who may override a woman's vow (a father, only while she is unmarried and in his house; a husband, once she is his), who may not (a widow or divorced woman, and never a man his own), and what happens when a husband waits too long to decide. The chapter that Numbers 29's own closing note promised.
⚠ Undated within the chapter, legislative rather than narrative, continuous with the offering calendar just given. ⚠ Hebrew and the shelf disagree at the chapter's own OPENING (not its close, as in the two chapters before it): this translation follows the Masoretic 17 verses, agreeing with the Douay-Rheims and Geneva 1599 that this chapter opens with the sentence the rest of the shelf attaches to Numbers 29 instead. ⭐ Only two Masoretic paragraph breaks in the whole chapter, after v1 and after v17 — the entire law runs as one unbroken paragraph.

Numbers 30:1-2 — The chapter the shelf cannot agree where to start

⚠ Last chapter’s closing note promised this sentence, and here it is. Numbers 29:39’s note flagged a real seam: the Masoretic text runs this exact sentence — ‘Moses told the children of Israel according to all that Jehovah commanded Moses’ — as the FIRST verse of a new chapter, under its own heading, Parashat Mattot. Checked against all thirteen witnesses on this project’s shelf, the split is not the two-versus-eleven a reader might expect from ‘the Masoretic text versus everyone else’. Only Douay-Rheims and Geneva 1599 agree with the Masoretic division, opening here with this sentence as their own v1 and running seventeen verses in total. The other eleven — KJV, ASV, NWT (both 1984 and its 2013 revision), NIV, TLB, TNM (1987 and 2019), RV 1909, RV60, and NVI — all attach it to the END of Numbers 29 instead, and open here at v2’s own words, ‘Moses spoke to the heads of the tribes’, running sixteen. The Vulgate tradition and the Hebrew agree; the whole rest of the shelf, English and Spanish alike, agrees with each other against both.

⭐ The grammar itself explains why the seam is genuinely disputed and not merely an old typo perpetuated. This sentence names no addressee and states no new content — ‘according to ALL that Jehovah commanded’ is a summary, not an instruction — so it reads equally well as the CLOSE of the two-chapter offering calendar Numbers 28 began (everything Jehovah had just commanded, transmitted) or as the OPEN of a new one (a heading announcing that what follows, too, is commanded). This translation follows the Masoretic paragraph break, which places it with the new chapter that begins the section on vows — but the ambiguity is real, not invented for this note.

⭐ V2’s own address is rare. ‘The heads of the tribes’ — rashei ha-matot — is the exact phrase, letter for letter, of only three verses in the whole Hebrew Bible: this one, and 1 Kings 8:1 / 2 Chronicles 5:2 (both not yet on these pages), the two parallel accounts of Solomon assembling ‘the heads of the tribes’ to bring the ark up into the newly built temple. A private law about a father’s or husband’s power over one woman’s vow is addressed, by name, to the same convened body that later stands for the dedication of Jehovah’s house.

Numbers 30:3-6 — A man’s word cannot be unbound; a father can unbind his daughter’s

⚠ V3’s Hebrew doubles its own noun. Le’esor issar, ‘to bind a bond’, is a cognate accusative — the verb and the noun sharing one root, asar, to bind — and issar in that exact construction occurs in only this one verse of the whole Hebrew Bible. This translation keeps the doubling rather than smoothing it to ‘an oath of abstinence’ (NWT’s own precise but non-cognate rendering) or the shelf’s more common ‘bind his soul with a bond’ (KJV, ASV, Geneva all keep ‘bond’, though none preserve the verb-noun echo as such), because the whole chapter’s legal vocabulary turns on this one root doing double duty as both the ACT and the THING done.

⭐ V3’s own rule has no exception in it anywhere. A man’s vow binds; nothing in this chapter, or anywhere else in the Torah, gives any person the power to release him from it. That absoluteness is what makes Judges 11:35-36 (not yet on these pages) legible: Jephthah, having vowed rashly and seen his own daughter come out to meet him, tears his clothes and says ‘I have opened my mouth to Jehovah, and I cannot go back’ — restating this exact verse’s own principle, from the one man in the whole Hebrew Bible for whom it costs everything.

⚠ Only starting at v4 does an override become possible, and only for a daughter still in her father’s house. Heni’a, the verb this translation renders ‘overrides’, is the shelf’s most divided single word in this chapter: KJV, ASV and Geneva all ‘disallow’; NWT and NIV ‘forbid’; Douay ‘gainsay’ (the same word Douay uses three verses later for a wholly different Hebrew verb, v9’s hafer); TLB ‘refuses to let her’; RV 1909 and RV60 «vedar»; NVI «no lo aprueba». None of the thirteen use ‘override’. This translation departs from the whole shelf on purpose: the same root recurs, in a wholly different setting, just two chapters from now — at Numbers 32:7 (not yet on these pages), where the tribes of Reuben and Gad are accused of using the identical verb to ‘discourage the heart of the children of Israel’ from entering the land at all. One father restraining one daughter’s resolve; one accusation restraining a whole nation’s. Keeping one English word for both lets a reader see the second use land on the first.

⭐⭐ And v6 raises a question its own grammar does not answer. When the father overrides her, the verse does not simply say the vow is void — it adds ‘and Jehovah will forgive her’, using salach, the verb this project has met only once before, at the pardon of the whole nation after the spies (Numbers 14:20, already on these pages). Why does a woman whose vow was overruled by someone else need forgiving at all? Jewish tradition (Mishnah Nedarim 3:1 and its discussion) reads it as covering the period BEFORE the override — she may unknowingly have acted as though bound, between the moment she vowed and the moment her father spoke. This chapter itself supplies a second, purely textual answer at its own close (see the note at v16 below): whatever the vow’s unresolved weight is, this chapter never simply erases it. Either it is forgiven, here, or it is carried — by someone.

Numbers 30:7-9 — A vow carried into marriage gets a second verb, and six more verses

⭐⭐ The father’s law (vv3-6) uses exactly one verb of override, once, in four verses. The husband’s law (vv7-16) uses two, and the second one — hafer, ‘to annul, to break’ — appears six times in ten verses (vv9, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16). V9 stacks both in one sentence: the husband first overrides her (yani’a, the same root as v6) and THEN annuls (hefer) the vow itself — two acts where the father’s case named one. Hafer is a stronger verb than heni’a elsewhere in the Torah — it is what breaks a covenant, not merely a resolve — and this translation keeps that distinction (‘overrides’ for heni’a, ‘annuls’ for hafer) rather than collapsing both into one English word the way Douay does with ‘gainsay’ at both v6 and v13. Most of the shelf keeps SOME distinction here — ASV ‘disallow’ against ‘make void’, NWT ‘forbidden’ against ‘annulled’ — so this is a case where the shelf and this translation largely agree, and Douay is the outlier.

⚠ V7’s own phrase for what a woman may carry into marriage is wider than a formal vow: ‘her vows’ AND ‘a rash utterance of her lips’ — mivta sefateha, language that in Leviticus 5:4 (already on these pages) covers a careless oath sworn without even meaning to bind anything. A husband inherits authority over both the deliberate and the careless kind, the moment either becomes his wife’s.

Numbers 30:10 — The one woman in this chapter no man can override

⭐ One verse, and it says the opposite of everything around it. A widow’s or a divorced woman’s vow ‘shall stand against her’ — the identical unconditional force v3 gives a man’s own word, and neither a father nor a husband is even mentioned, because in this chapter’s own terms neither exists over her. This translation states the fact and does not editorialize past it: the one case in the whole chapter where a woman’s vow receives a man’s full legal weight is the one case where no man stands legally between her and Jehovah.

Numbers 30:11-16 — Silence ratifies, and a vow’s unresolved weight moves to whoever waited too long

⚠ V15 states the rule vv5, 8 and 12 all assume: silence is not neutrality. A husband who ‘says nothing at all… from day to day’ has, by that silence, actively CONFIRMED every vow — the same verb, heqim, ‘he has raised up’, that v14 gives him as his other legal option alongside annulling. There is no third, passive option in this law; not deciding is itself a decision.

⭐⭐ Which is what makes v16 the chapter’s sharpest turn. Three times already — vv6, 9, 13 — an override or annulment made ON THE DAY the man hears closes with the identical formula: ‘and Jehovah will forgive her’. V16 covers a DIFFERENT case: a husband who has already, by his own silence, confirmed the vow (v15), and only LATER — after the window this law repeatedly ties to ‘the day he hears’ has already closed — tries to annul it anyway. That verse does not say Jehovah will forgive her. It says ve-nasa et-avonah, ‘he shall bear her guilt’. The doubled infinitive construction — hafer yafer, ‘if he TRULY annuls’, the same emphatic doubling this law uses three times (here, v13, and v15’s ‘if he is TRULY silent’) — marks each of this chapter’s hinge points, the moments a man must genuinely act rather than drift. On time, an overridden vow’s unresolved weight is forgiven, and it is hers to be forgiven of. Late, it does not vanish either — it moves, by name, onto him.

⚠ The shelf splits on how heavy a word to give avon here: KJV, ASV and Douay all ‘iniquity’; NIV softens it to ‘the consequences of her wrongdoing’; TNM «el error de ella», her MISTAKE, softer still; RV60 «el pecado de ella», her SIN, the strongest Spanish reading on the shelf. This translation keeps ‘guilt’ — heavier than a mistake, plainer than iniquity — because the verse’s whole point is that a real legal liability has changed hands, not merely that an inconvenience has.

Numbers 30:17 — A closing formula that forgets the one case it just made an exception of

⚠ The chapter’s own summary names two relationships, not the three it legislated. ‘Between a man and his wife… between a father and his daughter’ brackets vv3-9 and vv11-16 exactly — but says nothing of v10’s widow and divorced woman, the one case this chapter carved out as answering to neither. This translation reports the omission rather than silently patching it into the summary; the law and its own closing formula simply do not cover the identical ground.

⭐ This is one of three places in the Torah that legislate a vow, each from a different angle, and none of the three repeats the others. This chapter answers WHO may void one. Leviticus 27 (already on these pages) answers what one COSTS to redeem for money, should the vower change course entirely. Deuteronomy 23:21-23 (not yet on these pages) answers how URGENTLY, once made, a vow must be kept. Three books, three questions, one institution.

Notes on this translation

Decisions. ⚠ Hebrew and the whole English/Spanish shelf disagree at the chapter’s own opening, not its close — the note at v1 above lays out the 2-versus-11 split; this translation follows the Masoretic 17 verses, agreeing with Douay-Rheims and Geneva 1599 against the rest of the shelf. Heni’a is rendered ‘overrides’ throughout — a departure from all thirteen witnesses, made to keep visible its recurrence at Numbers 32:7 (not yet on these pages). Hafer is kept distinct as ‘annuls’. Issar is kept as ‘a bond’, preserving v3’s cognate-accusative doubling over NWT’s more explicit but non-cognate ‘vow of abstinence’. ⭐ Only two Masoretic paragraph breaks in the whole chapter — after v1 and after v17 — so the entire law of vv2-17, sixteen verses covering four distinct cases, runs as one unbroken scribal paragraph, unlike Numbers 28–29’s many internal breaks.

Library. One new English dictionary entry: issar, the bond this whole chapter turns on. One existing entry paid off: tenu’ah, whose Numbers 14 entry promised this exact chapter and verse (30:6) three chapters ago — that link now resolves instead of reading ‘not yet on these pages’.

Echoes paid. Numbers 14:20’s salach returns at v6, applied for the first time on these pages to an individual rather than a nation. The tenu’ah dictionary entry’s own forward promise (a father ‘overriding’ a daughter’s vow) lands exactly at v6, one clause from that same salach, precisely as promised.

Patterns worth carrying forward. The two-verb asymmetry between the father’s compact law (vv3-6, one override, no annulment verb at all) and the husband’s much longer one (vv7-16, override AND six uses of annul) is worth watching for a comparable weighting elsewhere in the Torah’s family law. And the liability-transfer reading at v16 — an unresolved vow’s guilt moving from the woman to the man who waited too long — is the kind of finding that came from reading the chapter’s three ‘Jehovah will forgive her’ formulas against its one exception, not from any single verse alone.

Echoes planted. Heni’a, ‘overrides’, is flagged here for its return at Numbers 32:7, where the same verb will be used of an entire nation’s resolve rather than one woman’s vow.
