“Here am I; send me”: in the year King Uzziah died, Isaiah sees the Lord on a throne with his skirts filling the temple, and seraphim with six wings who cry “Holy, holy, holy”. He is undone, a man of unclean lips among a people of unclean lips; a seraph touches his mouth with a glowing stone from the altar and says that his iniquity has departed and his sin is atoned for. He answers the question “Whom shall I send?” with “send me”, and is sent to a people whose heart is to be made fat, whose ears are to be made heavy and whose eyes are to be shut, until cities lie waste; a tenth that returns is burned again, and a holy seed is its stump.
Uzziah reigned about fifty-two years in the eighth century BC, and Isaiah 1:1 dates the book in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah; the chapter dates itself to the year of Uzziah’s death and the library gives no more exact year. Isaiah 7:3 shows Isaiah and his son Shear-jashub meeting Ahaz.

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verse 1 · Isaiah 6:1 “In the year that King Uzziah died, I saw the Lord” — a dated vision, a throne “high and lifted up”, and a hem that fills the temple

⭐ “In the year that King Uzziah died”. The book of Isaiah has already opened with “the vision of Isaiah son of Amoz”, “in the days of Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah” (Isaiah 1:1, now on these pages), so this is not where Isaiah begins to speak. It is the scene the book goes back to, five chapters in, to say when and how he was sent. Uzziah (encyclopedia) had reigned for about fifty-two years and ended his life shut away with a skin disease, and the sentence sets his death and the seated Lord side by side without comment; the page leaves them there. KJV has “In the year that king Uzziah died I saw also the Lord”, the ASV “In the year that king Uzziah died I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne”, the NWT 1984 “In the year that King Uz·ziʹah died I, however, got to see Jehovah”, and the Douay “In the year that king Ozias died”, with the king’s name in its Greek and Latin form.

⚠ “The Lord”, and what is seen. The name in this verse is Adonai (dictionary), “the Lord”, and this translation keeps it as “the Lord” here and at verses 8 and 11, and prints “Jehovah” only where the Hebrew has the name, at verses 3 and 5 and 12. The NWT 1984 prints “Jehovah” in all of them. “I saw” is the verb that Exodus 33:20 refuses: “You cannot see my face, for man shall not see me and live” (Exodus 33:20, already on these pages), and verse 5 of this chapter is the prophet’s reaction to having done it. What he reports is very little: a throne, one seated on it, and a hem. Ezekiel, who sees a throne too, goes on to describe “a likeness like the appearance of a human” upon it (Ezekiel 1:26, now on these pages); Isaiah says nothing above the hem.

⭐ “High and lifted up”. Ram ve-nissa are two adjectives, and the Hebrew does not say whether they belong to the throne or to the one on it, because both words are masculine. The pair stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and Isaiah 57:15, where “high and lifted up” is the speaker’s own title. The ASV has “upon a throne, high and lifted up”, which puts the words on the throne, and the NIV “the Lord, high and exalted, seated on a throne”, which puts them on him. The NWT 1984 has “on a throne lofty and lifted up”. The translation keeps the Hebrew’s order and so keeps the ambiguity.

⭐ “His skirts”. Shulav (dictionary) is the hem or the lower edge of a robe, and the word with this ending stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, and the hem of the priest’s robe, where Moses is told to put pomegranates and golden bells “so that he does not die” when its sound is heard as Aaron goes into the holy place (Exodus 28:33–35, now on these pages). The KJV and the ASV have “his train”, the NIV “the train of his robe”, the NWT 1984 “his skirts”, and the Douay “his train”. The Geneva has “the lower parts thereof”, which attaches the lower edge to the throne, and the TLB “the Temple was filled with his glory”. “The temple” is ha-hekhal (dictionary), the great hall of the house, and the same building is called ha-bayit, “the house”, in verse 4. The scene has company, as Micaiah’s does: he saw “Jehovah sitting on his throne, and all the host of heaven standing by him” (1 Kings 22:19, now on these pages), and the sons of God present themselves before Jehovah in Job (Job 1:6, now on these pages).

Verse 2 · Isaiah 6:2 “Seraphim were standing above him” — six wings, and what each pair does

⭐ “Seraphim”. Serafim is the plural of saraf, “a burning one” (dictionary), and the dictionary entry counts the plural, in the sense of a creature, in exactly three verses: the burning serpents that Jehovah sends among the people (Numbers 21:6, already on these pages) and these two verses, 2 and 6. Most of the versions print a snake in Numbers and a transliteration here, so an English reader cannot see that it is one word. The KJV has “seraphims”, the ASV “the seraphim”, the NWT 1984 “Seraphs”, and the TLB “mighty, six-winged angels of fire”. The verse does not describe a body. It gives them a face, feet and wings, and in verse 6 a hand, and that is all.

⭐ “Above him”. Mi-ma’al lo puts them over the one seated, as the ASV has it, “Above him stood the seraphim”, and the NIV “Above him were seraphim”. The Geneva has “The Seraphims stood upon it”, and the Douay, from the Latin, “Upon it stood the seraphims”, which put them on the throne.

⭐ Six wings, in three pairs. “Six wings, six wings to each” is the Hebrew, shesh kenafayim shesh kenafayim le-echad (kanaf), and the verse spends two pairs on covering and only one on flight. The four living creatures of Revelation, who each have “six wings” and say “Holy, holy, holy”, are drawn from this scene joined to Ezekiel’s (Revelation 4:8, already on these pages), though there they are living creatures and not seraphim. The face is covered, as Moses “hid his face, for he was afraid to look at God” (Exodus 3:6, now on these pages) and Elijah “wrapped his face in his mantle” at the cave (1 Kings 19:13, now on these pages). In this room it is the seraphim, the ones who stand closest, who cover their faces.

⚠ “Feet”. In two other verses “to cover the feet” is the courteous way of saying that a man is relieving himself: Saul “went in to cover his feet” in the cave (1 Samuel 24:3, now on these pages) and Eglon’s servants say he must be “covering his feet in the closet of the cool room” (Judges 3:24, now on these pages). The verb in those two verses is sakhakh, a different verb from the kasah of this verse, so the phrases are not the same idiom, and some readers still take the “feet” here as a delicate word, as they take “the hair of the feet” at Isaiah 7:20 (now on these pages). The translation keeps “feet” and does not vote.

Verses 3–4 · Isaiah 6:3–4 “Holy, holy, holy is Jehovah of armies” — the fullness of the earth, a shaking threshold, and a house filling with smoke

⭐ “Holy, holy, holy”. One calls “to another”, zeh el-zeh, literally “this one to that one”, and what they say is qadosh qadosh qadosh (dictionary), “set apart”, three times. The threefold word stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible; its Greek echo is the cry of the four creatures, “Holy, holy, holy, is Jehovah God, the Almighty” (Revelation 4:8, already on these pages). The name that follows is Jehovah tseva’ot, “Jehovah of armies” (dictionary), and the versions differ on that title: the ASV has “Jehovah of hosts”, the NWT 1984 “Jehovah of armies”, the NIV “the Lord Almighty”, the Douay “the Lord God of hosts”, and the TLB “the Lord Almighty” here and “the Lord of heaven’s armies” in verse 5. The book never lets go of what Isaiah hears here. “The Holy One of Israel” stands in thirty verses of the Hebrew Bible, and twenty-four of them are in this book, the first at Isaiah 1:4 (“spurned the Holy One of Israel”, now on these pages); of the other six, one is 2 Kings 19:22, the same oracle that Isaiah 37:23 carries, and the rest are in Psalms 71, 78 and 89 and Jeremiah 50 and 51. The psalmist has the same word for the same person: “But you are holy, you who dwell in the praises of Israel” (Psalm 22:4, now on these pages).

⚠ “The fullness of all the earth is his glory”. The Hebrew is four words: melo kol ha-arets kevodo, a string that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Melo is a noun, “fullness”, and kevodo is “his glory” (dictionary), so the sentence can be read either “the whole earth is full of his glory” or “the fullness of all the earth is his glory”. The KJV, the ASV and the NIV read “the whole earth is full of his glory”, the TLB “the whole earth is filled with his glory”, the Douay “all the earth is full of his glory”, and the Geneva “the whole world is full of his glory”; the NWT 1984 reads “The fullness of all the earth is his glory”, and this translation follows it. The other places where the earth and the glory meet are in verbs: “as the glory of Jehovah fills all the earth” (Numbers 14:21, now on these pages), and “the earth will be filled with the knowledge of the glory of Jehovah, as the waters cover the sea” (Habakkuk 2:14, now on these pages).

⭐ The posts, and the smoke. “The posts of the thresholds” is amot ha-sippim (dictionary), a phrase that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible. Amah is elsewhere the cubit, the length of a forearm, and here it must be whatever holds a door: the KJV has “the posts of the door”, the ASV “the foundations of the thresholds”, the NIV “the doorposts and thresholds”, the NWT 1984 “the pivots of the thresholds”, the Geneva “the lintels of the door cheeks”, the Douay “the lintels of the doors”, and the TLB “the Temple to its foundations”. The cry shakes them. The house then fills with ashan (dictionary), smoke, where the cloud (anan) fills the tent of meeting and the house of Solomon: “the cloud covered the tent of meeting, and the glory of Jehovah filled the tabernacle” (Exodus 40:34, 35) and “the cloud filled the house of Jehovah” (1 Kings 8:10–11, both now on these pages). Isaiah’s word is the one for Sinai: “Mount Sinai was all in smoke, because Jehovah had come down on it in fire” (Exodus 19:18, now on these pages). John of Patmos sees the temple “filled with smoke” in turn (Revelation 15:8, now on these pages). Amos dates his book “two years before the earthquake” in the days of the same king (Amos 1:1, now on these pages), and the page does not join the two.

Verse 5 · Isaiah 6:5 “Woe to me, for I am undone” — a man of unclean lips among a people of unclean lips, who has seen the King

⭐ “Woe to me”. Oi li is the cry of a man for himself, and the two words stand in four verses of the Hebrew Bible: here, Isaiah 24:16 and Jeremiah 10:19 and 15:10 (none of the others translated here yet). It is not the hoy that the book has been calling down on other people (dictionary). Isaiah 5 has six of them in a row, on those who join house to house, who rise early for strong drink, who call evil good (Isaiah 5:8, 11, 18, 20, 21, 22), and now the prophet who spoke them says the other word of himself.

⚠ “I am undone”. Nidmeiti is a form that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and it comes from a verb that can mean to be cut off or to be silenced. The versions split on exactly that: the KJV and the ASV have “I am undone”, the NIV “I am ruined”, the NWT 1984 “I am as good as brought to silence”, the TLB “My doom is sealed”, and the Douay, from the Latin, “because I have held my peace”. The same translation prints “has been silenced” of Moab’s cities at Isaiah 15:1. A man who has just heard a cry shake the doorposts is cut off, or has nothing to say, or both, and the translation keeps “undone”, which does not choose between them.

⭐ “Unclean lips”. The phrase “unclean of lips” stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one, twice: the man and the people. Tame (dictionary) is a ritual status, not a charge of bad character, and the one place where the Law puts lips and “unclean” together is the person with the scaly disease, who “shall cover his upper lip, and he shall call out, ‘Unclean! Unclean!’” (Leviticus 13:45, already on these pages). Uzziah lived out his last years apart with a skin disease, as the encyclopedia entry has it (2 Chronicles 26), and that is where the page stops; it does not say that Isaiah meant the connection. The Geneva has “polluted lips”, the TLB “a foul-mouthed sinner”, and the KJV, the ASV and the NIV “unclean lips”. He includes himself in the people: “and in the midst of a people of unclean lips I live”.

⭐ Three men and a mouth. Isaiah, Moses and Jeremiah each answer a call by naming their mouth. Moses says “I am of uncircumcised lips” and “heavy of mouth and heavy of tongue” (Exodus 6:12, 4:10, now on these pages), Jeremiah says “I do not know how to speak” (Jeremiah 1:6, now on these pages), and Isaiah says his lips and his people’s are unclean. Each is answered at the mouth: “I will be with your mouth and teach you what you shall speak” (Exodus 4:12), “Jehovah stretched out his hand and touched my mouth” (Jeremiah 1:9), and, in the next verses here, the touch from the altar. “The King” is the last word that matters in the verse: et ha-melekh (dictionary), the title the dead Uzziah held in the first verse, now given to the one whose hem filled the temple.

Verses 6–7 · Isaiah 6:6–7 “A glowing stone” from the altar — the tongs, the touch, and “your sin is atoned for”

⭐ “A glowing stone”, and “tongs”. The word is ritspah (dictionary), which is not the common word for a coal (gachelet) but a stone laid in the fire. The same root gives the hot stones on which the cake was baked for Elijah (1 Kings 19:6, now on these pages) and, in the same consonants, a paved floor (Ezekiel 40:17, and 2 Chronicles 7:3, now on these pages). The versions say “coal”: the KJV and the NIV have “a live coal”, the NWT 1984 “a glowing coal”, the Geneva “an hot coal”, and the TLB “a burning coal”. Melqachayim (dictionary), “tongs”, stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible: the golden tongs of the temple furniture (1 Kings 7:49, now on these pages, and 2 Chronicles 4:21, not yet) and this verse, where the same tool carries a stone from the altar. The text says “the altar” (dictionary) and does not say which.

⭐ “He touched my mouth”. The seraph touches the mouth and says, “this has touched your lips”. The words va-yagga al-pi, “and he touched my mouth”, stand in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: this one, and the verse in which Jehovah sends out his hand to Jeremiah (Jeremiah 1:9, now on these pages). The KJV says “he laid it upon my mouth”, which loses that verb; the ASV has “he touched my mouth with it”, the NWT 1984 “he proceeded to touch my mouth”, and the TLB “He touched my lips with it”.

⚠ “Your iniquity has departed, and your sin is atoned for”. The two nouns are avon (dictionary), the crookedness a man carries, and chattat (dictionary), sin. The first goes away on its own account: sar, “has turned aside, has departed”. The second is tekhuppar, a passive form of kipper (dictionary): it “is atoned for”, and the verb does not say who does the atoning. Compare the house of Eli, to whom it was sworn “that the iniquity of Eli’s house shall not be atoned for by sacrifice or by offering, forever” (1 Samuel 3:14, now on these pages); here nothing is offered, and the stone that touches the lips comes off the altar and is carried by a seraph. The KJV has “thy sin purged”, the ASV “thy sin forgiven”, the NIV “your guilt is taken away and your sin atoned for”, the NWT 1984 “your sin itself is atoned for”, the TLB “Your sins are all forgiven”, and the Douay “thy sin shall be cleansed”. The order of the chapter is the point: the cleansing comes before the question of verse 8 is asked.

Verse 8 · Isaiah 6:8 “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” — the plural, and “Here I am; send me”

⚠ “For us”. The question is singular and then plural: “Whom shall I send, and who will go for us?” (et mi eshlach u-mi yelekh lanu). Readers differ on who the “us” is, and this page does not vote. Some take a plural of majesty, some the heavenly company of verses 1 to 4, some the persons of the Trinity, as John has it when he says that Isaiah “saw his glory” (John 12:41, already on these pages). The other plurals of the same kind are “Let us make humankind in our image” (Genesis 1:26) and “Come, let us go down” (Genesis 11:7, both already on these pages). The nearest scene is the one Micaiah describes, where Jehovah asks his host, “Who will entice Ahab?”, and a spirit comes out and says, “I will entice him” (1 Kings 22:20–22, now on these pages): a question put to a room, and a volunteer. The KJV and the ASV have “who will go for us?”, the NIV “who will go for us?”, the Douay “who shall go for us?”, and the TLB “Who will go?”, which drops the plural.

⭐ “Here I am; send me”. Hineni shelacheni, a pair that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, is two words. Hineni (dictionary) is the answer of Abraham to God and to the angel (Genesis 22:1, 11), of Moses at the bush (Exodus 3:4) and of Samuel in the night (1 Samuel 3:4, all now on these pages), each time to a voice that has called a name. Here his name has not been called; the voice has asked a question, and he adds a verb of his own, shelacheni, “send me”, the imperative of shalach (dictionary). Compare Moses, to whom God says “Come now, and I will send you” and who then spends the rest of that chapter and most of the next asking to be excused (Exodus 3:10). The KJV has “Here am I; send me”, the NIV “Here am I. Send me!”, the NWT 1984 “Here I am! Send me.”, and the TLB “Lord, I’ll go! Send me.”

Verses 9–10 · Isaiah 6:9–10 “Hear indeed, but do not understand” — the commission, the heavy ears, and the verses quoted five times in the New Testament

⚠ An order to the prophet. What he is sent to say is a string of commands and refusals: shimu shamo’a ve-al taviynu, “Hear indeed, but do not understand”, and ur’u ra’o ve-al tede’u, “see indeed, but do not know”, each an imperative followed by the same verb again as an infinitive (shamo’a, ra’o), which is the Hebrew way of saying “indeed”. Then he is told what to do to them: hashmen (dictionary), “make fat”, the heart; hakhbed, “make heavy”, the ears; hasha’, “shut” or “smear over”, the eyes. The KJV has “Make the heart of this people fat, and make their ears heavy, and shut their eyes”, the Geneva “Make the heart of this people fat”, the NIV “Make the heart of this people calloused; make their ears dull and close their eyes”, the TLB “Dull their understanding, close their ears, and shut their eyes”, the NWT 1984 “Make the heart of this people unreceptive” and “paste their very eyes together”, and the Douay “Blind the heart of this people”. This translation does not soften it into a description. It is what the Hebrew says, and the notes on Matthew 13 and Acts 28 compare it with the Greek, which turns the order into a description of the people.

⭐ Three organs, three echoes. The verb kaved (dictionary) is the one Exodus uses of Pharaoh, “he made his heart heavy” (Exodus 8:11, 9:34, now on these pages); here it is the people’s ears, and it is the prophet who is told to make them heavy. Shamen, “to grow fat”, is the verb of “Jeshurun grew fat and kicked” (Deuteronomy 32:15, now on these pages), and the three organs, the heart, the eyes and the ears, are the three of Moses’s charge to the people: “Jehovah has not given you a heart to know, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, until this day” (Deuteronomy 29:3, now on these pages). The book’s first words to the people were the same sentence from the other side: “Israel does not know, my people do not understand” (Isaiah 1:3). The word “lest”, pen, opens the last clause of verse 10: “lest it see with its eyes, and hear with its ears, and its heart understand, and it turn and be healed”. The verb for “turn” is shuv (dictionary), and the healing, ve-rafa lo, is “and healing for it”, in the singular and in the third person.

⭐ Five quotations. The passage is quoted at Matthew 13:14–15, Mark 4:12, Luke 8:10, John 12:40 and Acts 28:26–27, all already on these pages. John’s “He has blinded their eyes and hardened their heart” sets the verse beside Isaiah 53:1 (“Who has believed what we heard?”, Isaiah 53:1) in one citation, and says that Isaiah said these things “because he saw his glory”.

Verses 11–12 · Isaiah 6:11–12 “How long, Lord?” — cities laid waste, the ground a desolation, and humankind sent far away

⭐ “How long, Lord?” Ad matai Adonai is the question of the laments, and the two words stand in twenty-eight verses of the Hebrew Bible, among them Psalms 6:4, 74:10, 80:5, 90:13 and 94:3. It is the prophet who asks it, not the people, and it is asked of the answer he has just been given. The answer does not say how long in years: it says until a condition is met. Ad asher im is “until”, and what must happen is that cities lie waste, mei-ein yoshev, “without an inhabitant”, and houses are empty, mei-ein adam, “without a man”, and ha-adamah (dictionary), the ground, is left shemamah, a desolation. The KJV has “Until the cities be wasted without inhabitant”, the NWT 1984 “Until the cities actually crash in ruins, to be without an inhabitant”, and the ASV “Lord, how long?”.

⚠ “Humankind”, and “the abandonment”. Verse 12 says et ha-adam, “the man”, the whole human race of the land, and Jehovah “removes” it; rachaq is to put far away, and the NIV has “until the Lord has sent everyone far away”. The land is left with ha-azuvah, “the abandoned thing”, a form with the article that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, and it “is great”, rabbah. The KJV reads “a great forsaking in the midst of the land”, the ASV “the forsaken places be many in the midst of the land”, and the TLB “they are all taken away as slaves to other countries far away”, which adds a deportation the Hebrew does not mention. The Douay, from the Latin, reads the same two words the other way round: “and she shall be multiplied that was left in the midst of the earth”, so that the forsaken one multiplies. The verses name no empire and no date. Readers have heard in them Assyria’s invasion in Hezekiah’s day, when “your land is a desolation, your cities burned with fire” (Isaiah 1:7, now on these pages) and the daughter of Zion “is left like a booth in a vineyard” (1:8), and the Babylonian exile after it, and the translation does not choose.

Verse 13 · Isaiah 6:13 “A holy seed is its stump” — a tenth that returns and is burned, the terebinth and the oak, and what stands in the felling

⭐ “A tenth”. Asiriyah (dictionary) is a form that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible, this one: the tenth part, the one in ten that is left. The Douay reads “a tithing”, from the Latin, which puts it in the language of the tithe (ma’aser), and the TLB reads “a tenth—a remnant—will survive”. The Hebrew says only a tenth. The book has already used the other word for what is left: “Had Jehovah of armies not left us a few survivors, we would have been like Sodom” (Isaiah 1:9), and Isaiah’s son bears the name Shear-jashub, “A-Remnant-Will-Return” (Isaiah 7:3, both now on these pages; she’erit), with the same verb shuv that is in the next clause here.

⚠ “It will return and be for burning”. Ve-shavah ve-hayeta le-va’er is two verbs, “and it turns back” or “and it again”, and “and it is for burning” (dictionary). If shavah means “it returns”, the remnant comes back and is burned once more; if it means “again”, a second fire passes over what the first left, and the translation keeps “return” because the Hebrew word is the verb of Isaiah’s son’s name. The KJV has “it shall return, and shall be eaten”, the ASV “it also shall in turn be eaten up”, the NIV “it will again be laid waste”, and the Douay “she shall turn, and shall be made a shew”.

⭐ The terebinth, the oak, and the stump. Elah (dictionary) and allon are two large trees, and the ASV and the NIV read “terebinth” and “oak” as this translation does, the Geneva “an elm or an oak”, the Douay “a turpentine tree” and “an oak”, and the NWT 1984 “a big tree” and “a massive tree”. Be-shalleket (dictionary) is the first of two uncommon words, “in the felling” or “in the casting down”: the KJV has “when they cast their leaves”, and the NIV “when they are cut down”. The second is the stump, matsevet. It is spelled and pointed exactly as the pillar that Jacob sets up of stone after he has spoken with God, matsevet even (Genesis 35:14, 35:20, now on these pages; dictionary) and as the pillar Absalom sets up for himself because he has no son (2 Samuel 18:18, now on these pages). A felled tree leaves a standing stone, and what stands is zera qodesh, “a holy seed”, a phrase without the article that stands in one verse of the Hebrew Bible; with the article, “the holy seed” is Ezra 9:2. The book called the same people “a seed of evildoers” in its first chapter (Isaiah 1:4), and the last clause of the chapter names what is left of them with the other adjective (dictionary). The KJV has “so the holy seed shall be the substance thereof”, the ASV “so the holy seed is the stock thereof”, the NIV “so the holy seed will be the stump in the land”, the NWT 1984 “a holy seed will be the stump of it”, and the Douay “that which shall stand therein, shall be a holy seed”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “the Lord” for Adonai and “Jehovah” for the name; “Jehovah of armies” for Jehovah tseva’ot, as at Isaiah 1:9; “holy” for qadosh; “glory” for kavod; “unclean” for tame; “iniquity” for avon, “sin” for chattat and “atoned for” for kipper; “Here I am” for hineni, as at Genesis 22:1; “make heavy” for hakhbed, as at Exodus 8:11; “humankind” for ha-adam, as at Genesis 1:26; and “seraphim” transliterated here and “burning serpents” in Numbers 21:6, with the note that it is one word. The Hebrew has no written and read pairs in this chapter.

Echoes paid. Uzziah and the days of the book (Isaiah 1:1); the throne, the host and the court (1 Kings 22:19, Job 1:6); the seeing that Exodus 33:20 forbids; the hem of the priest’s robe (Exodus 28:33–35); the burning serpents of Numbers 21:6; the faces covered by Moses and Elijah (Exodus 3:6, 1 Kings 19:13); the glory that fills the earth (Numbers 14:21, Habakkuk 2:14); the cloud and the smoke of Exodus 40, 1 Kings 8 and Sinai (Exodus 19:18); the leper’s covered lip (Leviticus 13:45); the mouths of Moses and Jeremiah (Exodus 4:10–12, 6:12, Jeremiah 1:6, 1:9); the iniquity that cannot be atoned for in 1 Samuel 3:14; the voices that answer “Here I am” (Genesis 22, Exodus 3:4, 1 Samuel 3); the heavy heart of Pharaoh and the fat heart of Jeshurun (Exodus 8:11, 9:34, Deuteronomy 32:15); the heart, eyes and ears of Deuteronomy 29:3; the five New Testament quotations; the survivors and Shear-jashub (Isaiah 1:9, 7:3); and the pillars of Jacob and Absalom (Genesis 35:14, 2 Samuel 18:18).

Echoes planted. The six woes of Isaiah 5 and the other cries of “woe to me” (Isaiah 24:16, Jeremiah 10:19, 15:10); “high and lifted up” at Isaiah 57:15; the Holy One of Israel in Psalms 71, 78 and 89, Jeremiah 50 and 51 and 2 Kings 19:22; the tongs of 2 Chronicles 4:21; the laments’ question in Psalms 6, 74, 80, 90 and 94; the holy seed of Ezra 9:2; and Uzziah’s own end, in 2 Chronicles 26. None of these is translated here yet.

⚠ A note on order: Isaiah stands on these pages at 1, 6, 7, 9, 11, 26, 40, 41, 43, 53, 54, 55, 58 and 61, chosen by how often they are read rather than in sequence. The sequential thread is unchanged: next in sequence is Isaiah 2.
