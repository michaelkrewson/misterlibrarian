The close of the letter to Titus: submission to rulers, 'the kindness and love for humankind of God our Saviour appeared,' 'the washing of rebirth,' the last faithful saying, a factious person refused after two warnings, and the travellers: Artemas, Tychicus, Zenas and Apollos, and a winter at Nicopolis.
Undated by the text; on the traditional reading, between Paul's imprisonments, mid-60s AD; many scholars date the Pastoral letters later.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · Rulers and authorities — and “obey,” the verb of Acts 5:29

“Remind them to be subject to rulers, to authorities, to obey, to be ready for every good work.” “Rulers” and “authorities” are the pair of Luke 12:11, “the rulers, and the authorities,” and “be subject” is the verb of Romans 13:1, “Let every soul be subject to the governing authorities.” Peitharcheō, “to obey one in authority,” stands in four verses: Peter’s “One must obey God rather than people” at Acts 5:29, 5:32, Paul’s “you ought to have obeyed me” at Acts 27:21, and here (dictionary). The verb that tells Titus’s people to obey rulers is the one Peter uses to refuse them, and the letter does not discuss where the line falls. “Every good work” is the phrase of 2 Timothy 2:21 and 2 Timothy 3:17. The Byzantine text reads “rulers and authorities.” The KJV reads “Put them in mind to be subject to principalities and powers, to obey magistrates”; the ASV reads “to be in subjection to rulers, to authorities, to be obedient”; the NWT 1984 reads “to be in subjection and be obedient to governments and authorities as rulers.”

“Not to be quarrelsome, to be reasonable, showing all gentleness toward all people.” “Slander” is blasphēmeō, the verb of 2:5, “so that the word of God may not be blasphemed.” Amachos, “not quarrelsome,” stands in two verses: the overseer at 1 Timothy 3:3, and here (dictionary). “Reasonable” is epieikēs, as at the same verse and at Philippians 4:5, “Let your reasonableness be known to all people” (dictionary). “Gentleness” is prautēs, as at Galatians 5:23 and 2 Timothy 2:25. What is asked of the overseer in 1 Timothy 3 is asked here of everyone, “toward all people.” The ASV reads “not to be contentious, to be gentle, showing all meekness toward all men”; the NWT 1984 reads “not to be belligerent, to be reasonable”; the Douay reads “not to be litigious, but gentle.”

Verses 3–7 · “We too were once” — “the washing of rebirth,” and heirs by grace

“For we too were once senseless, disobedient, led astray, slaves to various desires and pleasures.” Anoētos, “senseless,” stands in six verses, among them “O senseless Galatians” at Galatians 3:1 (dictionary). “Disobedient” is the word of Titus 1:16; “led astray” is planaō, as at 2 Timothy 3:13. Hēdonē, “pleasure,” stands in five verses, among them the “pleasures of life” that choke the seed at Luke 8:14 (dictionary). Stugētos, “hateful,” stands in one verse (dictionary). “We too” puts the writer in the list, after the Cretan line of Titus 1:12. The KJV reads “For we ourselves also were sometimes foolish, disobedient, deceived”; the NWT 1984 reads “For even we were once senseless, disobedient, being misled”; the ASV reads “hateful, hating one another.”

“But when the kindness and the love for humankind of God our Saviour appeared.” “Kindness” is chrēstotēs, as at Romans 2:4 and Romans 11:22 (dictionary). Philanthrōpia and its adverb stand in three verses: Julius treating Paul “with kindness” at Acts 27:3, the islanders’ “extraordinary kindness” at Acts 28:2, and this one; it is the word that gives English “philanthropy” (dictionary). “Appeared” is the verb of Titus 2:11: there grace appeared, here kindness. The KJV reads “the kindness and love of God our Saviour toward man appeared”; the ASV reads “the kindness of God our Saviour, and his love toward man, appeared”; the NWT 1984 reads “the kindness and the love for man on the part of our Savior, God.”

⚠ “Not from works done in righteousness that we ourselves had done, but according to his mercy he saved us.” The line is close to 2 Timothy 1:9, “not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace,” and to Ephesians 2:8 and 2:9, “by grace you have been saved … not of works.” “Mercy” is eleos (dictionary). The KJV reads “Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us”; the NIV reads “not because of righteous things we had done, but because of his mercy”; the NWT 1984 reads “owing to no works in righteousness that we had performed.”

⚠ “Through the washing of rebirth and renewal of the Holy Spirit.” Loutron, “washing, bath,” stands in two verses: “the washing of water with the word” at Ephesians 5:26, and here (dictionary). Palingenesia, “rebirth,” stands in two verses: the world’s “renewal of all things” at Matthew 19:28, and here, of a person (dictionary). Anakainōsis, “renewal,” stands in two verses: “the renewing of your mind” at Romans 12:2, and here (dictionary). Readers take the washing two ways: as baptism, through which the rebirth is given, read beside “be baptized and wash away your sins” at Acts 22:16; or as the inner cleansing the Spirit gives, of which baptism is the sign, read beside “you were washed” at 1 Corinthians 6:11. The syntax is read two ways as well: one washing that is both rebirth and renewal, or a washing of rebirth and, beside it, a renewal by the Spirit. Churches have taught each reading, and this page does not choose. The KJV reads “by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost”; the Douay reads “by the laver of regeneration”; the NWT 1984 reads “through the bath that brought us to life and through the making of us new by holy spirit,” with “holy spirit” lower-cased, in keeping with its teaching that the spirit is God’s active force.

“Whom he poured out on us richly through Jesus Christ our Saviour.” The relative pronoun is neuter in the Greek, agreeing with the neuter noun pneuma, and English must choose: this translation prints “whom,” as at Ephesians 4:30, “by whom you were sealed.” “Poured out” is ekcheō, as at Acts 2:33, “he has poured out this which you both see and hear.” “Richly” is the adverb of 1 Timothy 6:17, “God, who richly provides us.” “God our Saviour” in verse 4 and “Jesus Christ our Saviour” here give the one title to both, as at Titus 1:3 and 1:4. The KJV reads “Which he shed on us abundantly”; the NIV reads “whom he poured out on us generously”; the NWT 1984 reads “he poured out richly upon us through Jesus Christ our Savior.”

“So that, having been justified by his grace, we might become heirs according to the hope of eternal life.” “Justified by his grace” is the language of Romans 3:24, “being justified freely by his grace” (dictionary). “His” is ekeinou, “that one’s,” and readers refer it to God, the subject of the sentence, or to Jesus Christ, just named. “Heirs” is the word of Romans 8:17; “the hope of eternal life” is the phrase of Titus 1:2. The KJV reads “That being justified by his grace, we should be made heirs”; the NWT 1984 reads “after being declared righteous by virtue of the undeserved kindness of that one”; the ASV reads “we might be made heirs according to the hope of eternal life.”

Verses 8–9 · The fifth faithful saying — “profitable” and “unprofitable”

“Faithful is the saying, and about these things I want you to affirm confidently.” Pistos ho logos, “Faithful is the saying,” stands in five verses, all in the Pastoral letters: 1 Timothy 1:15, 1 Timothy 3:1, 1 Timothy 4:9, 2 Timothy 2:11 and this one, the last. Readers take “the saying” to be verses 4–7, or the line that follows. Diabebaioomai, “to affirm confidently,” stands in two verses: the false teachers’ “confident assertions” at 1 Timothy 1:7, and here, where Titus is to make them about the right things (dictionary). The KJV reads “This is a faithful saying, and these things I will that thou affirm constantly”; the ASV reads “Faithful is the saying, and concerning these things I desire that thou affirm confidently”; the NIV reads “This is a trustworthy saying.”

“So that those who have believed God may take care to lead in good works.” Phrontizō, “to take care, to be intent on,” stands in one verse (dictionary). “Lead” is proistēmi, the verb of “the one who leads” at Romans 12:8 and of elders “who lead well” at 1 Timothy 5:17 (dictionary); with “good works” it is read as giving oneself to them or as taking the lead in them. “Believed God” is the construction of Romans 4:3, “Abraham believed God.” The KJV reads “might be careful to maintain good works”; the NWT 1984 reads “may keep their minds on maintaining fine works”; the Douay reads “may be careful to excel in good works.”

“Good and profitable … unprofitable and futile.” “Profitable” is ōphelimos, the third of its three uses, after 1 Timothy 4:8 and 2 Timothy 3:16 (dictionary); anōphelēs, “unprofitable,” stands in two verses: here and Hebrews 7:18 (dictionary). “Stand clear of” is the verb of 2 Timothy 2:16; the controversies are those of 2 Timothy 2:23, the genealogies those of 1 Timothy 1:4. “About the law” is nomikos, the word that is Zenas’s title in verse 13, “the lawyer” (dictionary). “Futile” is mataios (dictionary). Westcott and Hort print “strife” in the singular. The KJV reads “But avoid foolish questions, and genealogies, and contentions, and strivings about the law; for they are unprofitable and vain”; the NWT 1984 reads “fights over the Law, for they are unprofitable and futile”; the ASV reads “but shun foolish questionings.”

Verses 10–11 · ⚠ “A factious person” — hairetikos, and a verse once used to justify putting heretics to death

⚠ The use this verse was put to. Thomas Aquinas, in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, question 11, article 3, “Whether heretics ought to be tolerated”), quotes this verse and argues that the Church, after “the first and second admonition,” “delivers him to the secular tribunal to be exterminated thereby from the world by death” (read in the English of the Fathers of the English Dominican Province, at newadvent.org). That is a misreading of what the verse says. What it commands is “refuse”: after two warnings, have nothing more to do with the person. It names no penalty and no court, and the next verse leaves the judgment with the person himself, “self-condemned.” The same letters tell the Lord’s slave to instruct opponents “with gentleness” (2 Timothy 2:25).

“A factious person, after a first and a second admonition, refuse.” Hairetikos stands in one verse of the New Testament (dictionary). It is built on hairesis, “party, faction”: the “factions” of Galatians 5:20 and 1 Corinthians 11:19, and “the party of the Nazarenes” at Acts 24:5 (dictionary). Later Christian usage made it “heretic,” one who holds a doctrine the church condemns; here readers take it as the one who causes division over the controversies of verse 9, or as one who holds and spreads a chosen error. Nouthesia, “admonition,” stands in three verses: 1 Corinthians 10:11, Ephesians 6:4 and this one. “Refuse” is the verb of 1 Timothy 4:7 and 2 Timothy 2:23. The KJV reads “A man that is an heretick after the first and second admonition reject”; the ASV reads “A factious man after a first and second admonition refuse”; the NWT 1984 reads “As for a man that promotes a sect.”

“Such a person is perverted and sins, being self-condemned.” Ekstrephō, “to turn inside out, to pervert,” stands in one verse (dictionary), and so does autokatakritos, “self-condemned” (dictionary). The KJV reads “Knowing that he that is such is subverted, and sinneth, being condemned of himself”; the ASV reads “is perverted, and sinneth, being self-condemned”; the Douay reads “being condemned by his own judgment.”

Verses 12–15 · Artemas, Tychicus and a winter at Nicopolis — Zenas the lawyer and Apollos

“When I send Artemas or Tychicus to you, do your best to come to me at Nicopolis.” Artemas stands in one verse of the New Testament (encyclopedia). Tychicus is the companion of Acts 20:4, Ephesians 6:21 and 2 Timothy 4:12 (encyclopedia). “Do your best” is the verb of 2 Timothy 4:9. Nikopolis, “city of victory,” was the name of more than one city; the one on the coast of Epirus is the usual identification, and the closing note of the Textus Receptus says “Nicopolis of Macedonia” (both reported here, not checked; encyclopedia). Paracheimazō, “to spend the winter,” stands in four verses: Acts 27:12, Acts 28:11, 1 Corinthians 16:6 and this one. The KJV reads “be diligent to come unto me to Nicopolis: for I have determined there to winter”; the NIV reads “because I have decided to winter there.”

“Diligently send Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way, so that they lack nothing.” Zēnas stands in one verse (encyclopedia). “Lawyer” is nomikos, Luke’s word for the experts in the Law, as at Luke 7:30; whether Zenas knew the law of Moses or Roman law the text does not say. Apollos is the Alexandrian “powerful in the Scriptures” of Acts 18:24 and the one who “watered” at 1 Corinthians 3:6 (encyclopedia). “Send on their way” is propempō, the verb of Romans 15:24 and 1 Corinthians 16:6, a congregation supplying travellers (dictionary). The KJV reads “Bring Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their journey diligently”; the Geneva reads “Bring Zenas the expounder of the Law”; the NIV reads “Do everything you can to help Zenas the lawyer and Apollos on their way.”

“Let our people also learn to lead in good works for pressing needs, so that they may not be unfruitful.” “Lead in good works” repeats verse 8, and “good works” closes the refrain of the letter: Titus 1:16, Titus 2:7, Titus 2:14, 3:1, 3:8 and here. Akarpos, “unfruitful,” is the word of the seed among thorns at Matthew 13:22 and of “the fruitless works of darkness” at Ephesians 5:11 (dictionary). The KJV reads “And let ours also learn to maintain good works for necessary uses, that they be not unfruitful”; the NWT 1984 reads “so as to meet their pressing needs”; the ASV reads “And let our people also learn to maintain good works.”

“Greet those who love us in faith. Grace be with you all.” “In faith” has no article in the Greek, and is read “in the faith” or “faithfully.” “You all” is plural, as at the end of the other two letters, 1 Timothy 6:21 and 2 Timothy 4:22. The Textus Receptus closes with a note that is not part of the letter, and the Geneva prints it: “To Titus, elect the first Bishop of the Church of the Cretans, written from Nicopolis in Macedonia.” The ASV reads “Salute them that love us in faith. Grace be with you all”; the KJV reads “Greet them that love us in the faith.”

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “obey,” the verb of Acts 5:29; “not quarrelsome” and “reasonable,” as at 1 Timothy 3:3; “gentleness,” as at 2 Timothy 2:25; “senseless,” as at Galatians 3:1; “appeared,” the verb of 2:11; “love for humankind” for philanthrōpia; “washing,” as at Ephesians 5:26; “rebirth” and “renewal” for two words; “poured out,” as at Acts 2:33; “whom,” as at Ephesians 4:30, with the neuter in the note; “justified by his grace,” as at Romans 3:24; “Faithful is the saying,” as in the other four; “lead,” as at 1 Timothy 5:17; “profitable” and “unprofitable”; “factious,” for the word later read as “heretic”; “refuse,” as at 2 Timothy 2:23; “lawyer,” as in Luke; and “good works.”

Echoes paid. Every good work from 2 Timothy 3:17; obey from Acts 5:29; reasonable from 1 Timothy 3:3; senseless from Galatians 3:1; the appearing of Titus 2:11; the love for humankind of Acts 28:2; the washing of Ephesians 5:26; the rebirth of Matthew 19:28; the renewing of Romans 12:2; the Spirit poured out at Acts 2:33; justified by his grace from Romans 3:24; the faithful saying of 1 Timothy 1:15; the confident assertions of 1 Timothy 1:7; profitable from 2 Timothy 3:16; the factions of Galatians 5:20; Tychicus from 2 Timothy 4:12; Apollos from Acts 18:24; and the seed made unfruitful at Matthew 13:22.

Echoes planted. “Unprofitable” again at Hebrews 7:18, and “pleasures” at James 4:1.

This is the last chapter of Titus, and of the three Pastoral letters. Next in the New Testament: Hebrews 2, already on these pages.
