The same letter, still at Ephesus. Paul returns to his own arrival in Corinth: he came without the surplus of speech or of wisdom, afraid and shaking, deciding to know nothing among them but Jesus Christ crucified. The chapter is his account of how the message got through anyway, by the Spirit and not by the performance.
c. AD 54–55, the same date as chapter 1, written from Ephesus. Acts 18:9–10 places the night vision that tells Paul not to be afraid in Corinth, some years before this letter; the fear he confesses here is the one the vision answered, remembered from inside.

Verses 1–5 · weakness, fear, and no surplus of speech

“Not with a superiority of speech or of wisdom.” Hyperochē, “superiority,” is a word for something standing over and above, a surplus. Paul is answering a charge the Corinthians were already making, and chapter 1 has made it before him: “not in wisdom of speech” (1:17). He does not say he was a poor speaker in general. He says he did not come with the surplus.

⚠ “The testimony of God.” The printed Greek reads martyrion, “testimony,” with the support of Treg and the Received Text. Westcott-Hort and NA28 read mystērion, “mystery.” This translation prints “testimony,” the reading of the printed text. The other reading would make verse 1 the first line about the mystery that verse 7 names, and the chapter would then open on its own theme. It is a real choice between two editions, and the translation does not pretend otherwise.

“In weakness, and in fear, and in much trembling.” Acts gives the city’s side of this. A night vision in Corinth tells Paul, “Do not be afraid; but speak, and do not keep silent” (Acts 18:9), and he stays about eighteen months. Paul’s own letter gives the inside: he came frightened and shaking. The two accounts are not in conflict, and the second one is the more interesting, because it says the preaching worked while the preacher was afraid.

“Persuasive words of wisdom.” Peithos is persuasion, the force that makes someone believe. The printed text has peithoi sophias with no further noun. Westcott-Hort, Treg and NA28 add logois, “words,” to give “persuasive words of wisdom,” which is what this translation prints. The variant changes the noun, not the sense: Paul rejects the art of the Greek speaker in both readings.

“A demonstration of the Spirit and of power.” Apodeixis is the word for proof shown in public: the demonstration in logic, the evidence set before a court. Paul says his proof came as a Spirit-and-power event. The same pairing of “power of God” is the theme of Romans 1:16, and verse 5 states the purpose outright: that the Corinthians’ faith would stand on God’s power, not on the wisdom of people.

Verses 6–9 · the mature, the rulers who are being brought to nothing, and a hidden wisdom

“We speak wisdom among the mature.” Teleioi, “mature” or “complete,” is the word chapter 13 uses for the complete thing that makes the partial vanish (13:10). Paul says he does have teaching for the complete. Chapter 3 will say that he could not speak to them this way at all, but only as to infants (3:1, already on these pages). The wisdom he speaks is not a level of initiation; it is the content of the message, offered to those who can take it.

“The rulers of this age, who are being brought to nothing.” Katargoumenōn, the verb katargeō, “to put out of service,” in the present participle. It is the verb chapter 13 runs four times, with the prophecies and the knowledge among the things that will be put out of commission. Here it describes the powers of this age as already in that process.

⚠ “A mystery, the hidden one, which God predestined before the ages.” Mystērion is a secret that has been kept and is now told; it is not a secret kept from outsiders by the initiated. The verb for “predestined” is proōrisen, the word of Romans 8:29, where God’s foreknowing and predestining are the start of the whole chain of calling and glory. The same two pieces, the hidden plan and the purpose for our glory, meet there.

⚠ “They would not have crucified the Lord of glory.” Peter makes the same point about the Jerusalem authorities: “I know that you acted in ignorance, as did your rulers” (Acts 3:17). Paul does not say which rulers he means. Archontes has been read as Jerusalem’s leaders, as Roman officials, and as the powers that stand behind the earthly ones; the Greek does not choose, and this translation does not choose for it.

⚠ “What no eye saw, and no ear heard…” Paul introduces the sentence as scripture, but its wording does not match any Greek text of Isaiah that this library could search. The nearest candidates are Isaiah 64:4 (not yet on these pages) and the new-creation passage at 65:17 (not yet on these pages), and commentators have proposed each. The honest reading is that Paul is quoting or paraphrasing from memory, and the translation prints the sentence as he gives it.

Verses 10–13 · the Spirit searches, and the words are taught by the Spirit

“God revealed these to us through the Spirit.” The printed text has apekalypsen ho theos, “revealed, God,” with the same word order in WH, Treg and NA28; the Received Text reverses it and adds autou, “his,” to the Spirit. The verb is the family of apokalypsis, the uncovering of what was hidden, the same movement as verse 7.

“The Spirit searches all things, even the depths of God.” Ereunaō is the verb of careful searching, and John uses it for the scriptures that testify about Jesus (John 5:39). Bathē, depths, is the word Paul uses in praise at Romans 11:33, “O the depth of riches,” so the two letters point the same way: God is deep, and the Spirit is the one who goes down.

⚠ “Who knows a person’s things except the person’s own spirit?” The argument is from self-knowledge: no one knows your inside but you. Verse 11 has a small apparatus question at the verb. The printed text has egnōken, “has come to know,” with WH, Treg and NA28. The Received Text has oiden, “knows.” The perfect puts the knowing in a state that holds, and the translation keeps “has known.”

“The spirit of the world… the Spirit from God.” Romans 8:15 says the Spirit given to believers is not a spirit of slavery; here the Spirit given is for knowing what God has freely given. Charisthenta is the verb of grace, and “freely given” keeps the gift in it.

⚠ “Matching spiritual things with spiritual words.” Synkrinontes has two live meanings, to compare or match things side by side, and to interpret or explain. The shelf splits. The KJV and the Geneva read “comparing”, the ASV and the NWT 1984 “combining”, the NWT 2013 and the TNM 2019 “explaining”, the NIV “explaining spiritual realities”, the RV60 «acomodando» (fitting), and the NVI «explicando». This translation keeps “matching,” because the Greek keeps both: the spiritual teaching is fitted to spiritual realities, and in the fitting it is explained.

Verses 14–16 · the natural person, the spiritual person, and the mind of Christ

⚠ “A natural person does not receive the things of God’s Spirit.” Psychikos comes from psychē, soul or life-principle: the person run by ordinary human life, without the Spirit. Jude uses the same adjective for people who divide the congregation and “do not have the Spirit” (Jude 19). The shelf splits. The NWT says “physical,” the Living Bible “the man who isn’t a Christian,” the NIV “the person without the Spirit.” This translation keeps “natural,” because the English word, like the Greek, is about what a person is made of before the Spirit arrives.

“They are examined spiritually.” Anakrinetai is the verb of a court’s examination, and it is the same verb as anakrinei, “examines,” in verse 15. The natural person cannot examine what is examined spiritually; the spiritual person examines everything and is examined by no one. “Judges” would be the familiar English, but the verb carries the courtroom, and the chapter after this one makes the courtroom explicit (4:3–4).

⚠ “Who has known the mind of the Lord, that he could instruct him?” Paul quotes Isaiah 40:13 from the Greek Old Testament, the question about who has given God counsel. The same line appears in Romans 11:34 (already on these pages), where it sits inside the doxology at the end of the argument. Here it is turned round: nobody has taught the mind of the Lord; the Corinthians, through Paul, have the mind of Christ.

“We have the mind of Christ.” Nous, mind, the thinking faculty, the word of the dictionary entry. The claim is not that believers have Christ’s intelligence in a general sense. The pronoun is plural, “we,” and the mind is one thing shared, not an individual gift. That is why the chapter can end on a community that judges no one by the standards of the room.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact words: “testimony” for martyrion, as the printed text reads it, with the mystery variant in the note; “persuasive words” for peithos; “mature” for teleioi, in the sense chapter 13 gives the word; “matching” for synkrinō, which keeps both of its live meanings; “natural” for psychikos; and “examine” for anakrinō wherever it appears.

Echoes paid. Acts 18’s night vision (“do not be afraid”) against the fear Paul confesses here; 1:17’s “not in wisdom of speech” now given its positive side; Acts 3:17’s “your rulers” behind the cross; Romans 8:15 and 8:29 behind the Spirit and the predestined glory; 13:10’s “complete” behind “the mature”; Jude 19 behind the natural person; and Romans 11:34, which quotes Isaiah 40:13 in the same words.

Echoes planted. Wisdom, redefined here as the content God revealed, returns with real force in chapter 3 (3:18–20, already on these pages). The rulers of this age, who are being brought to nothing, come back in every chapter that talks about the powers. Examination, the verb of verse 15, becomes the court in chapter 4 (4:3–4). The mind of Christ is the last line of the chapter and the first thing the next chapter has to deal with.

Next in this book: 1 Corinthians 3, already on these pages. Paul cannot speak to the Corinthians as to spiritual people but only as to infants (3:1), the planting and watering of Apollos and God’s giving the growth, and a building that will be tested by fire.
