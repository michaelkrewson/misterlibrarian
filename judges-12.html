Ephraim is called out, crosses to Zaphon (or northward) and threatens to burn Jephthah's house over him for not calling them to the war on Ammon. Jephthah answers that he cried to them and they did not save him; the men of Gilead strike Ephraim, hold the fords of the Jordan, and kill every man who says Sibboleth for Shibboleth, forty-two thousand of Ephraim. Jephthah judges six years and is buried in the cities of Gilead; Ibzan of Bethlehem judges seven, Elon the Zebulunite ten, and Abdon son of Hillel the Pirathonite eight.
Within the period of the judges, with no date the chapter gives for itself. Its own years run Jephthah 6, Ibzan 7, Elon 10 and Abdon 8, thirty-one in all, after Jephthah's war with Ammon (11:33) and before the Philistine forty years of Judges 13:1, now on these pages. The early/late question applies (c. 1200 BC on the early reckoning this project foregrounds, c. 1100 BC on the late).

Translator's Notes — verse by verse

Verses 1–3 · Zaphon or “northward”, Ephraim’s complaint, and Jephthah’s “my life in my palm”

Zaphon, or “northward”? Tsafonah is the word for “north” with the ending that means “toward”, and it is also a town: Zaphon stands in Gad’s share of the Jordan Valley in Joshua 13:27 (now on these pages). Ephraim lives west of the Jordan and Jephthah east of it, so the men must cross the river to reach him; a town in the valley fits the crossing, and a march to the north does not explain it. This translation reads the town. The shelf splits down the middle: the KJV “went northward”, the ASV “passed northward”, the NWT 1984 “crossed over northward”, the Douay “passing towards the north”; against them the NIV “crossed over to Zaphon” and the TLB “at Zaphon”. The Greek translation, which survives for Judges in two forms, splits the same way: the B text has eis borran, “to the north”, and the A text eis Sephina, a place.

“Were called out” and “I cried to you”. The verb of verse 1, vayitsa’eq, is the one that summoned Ephraim to Gideon’s pursuit: “all the men of Ephraim were called out” (Judges 7:24, now on these pages). Jephthah answers with its twin, za’aq: “I cried to you, and you did not save me from their hand.” It is the book’s word for Israel crying to Jehovah, and Jehovah’s answer at Judges 10:12 (now on these pages) was “I saved you out of their hand”. Ephraim was cried to as God is cried to, and did not come. The complaint itself is an old one: Ephraim told Gideon he had treated them badly, “not calling us when you went to fight” (Judges 8:1, now on these pages). Gideon soothed them. Jephthah argues.

A dispute, a deliverer, and a life in the palm. Ish riv hayiti is literally “a man of dispute I was”: riv is a quarrel argued like a case at law, the root of Jephthah’s own question to Ammon, “Did he ever contend with Israel, or fight against them?” (Judges 11:25, now on these pages). “You were no deliverer” is singular in the Hebrew, eynkha moshia, and moshia is the title the book gave Othniel (Judges 3:9, now on these pages). “I put my life in my palm” keeps kaf, the open palm, apart from yad, the hand Jehovah gives Ammon into in the same verse. The KJV has “I put my life in my hands”, the NWT 1984 “put my soul in my own palm”, and the NIV “I took my life in my hands”. Jonathan will say the same of David before Goliath (1 Samuel 19:5).

“Your house we will burn down over you with fire.” The Hebrew puts “your house” first, the thing threatened before the threat. The NIV has “burn down your house over your head”, and the Douay keeps only “we will burn thy house”. The same words, “we will burn … with fire”, come once more in this book, when the Philistines threaten Samson’s wife and her father’s house (Judges 14:15).

Verses 4–6 · Shibboleth — an ear of grain or a stream, the fords of the Jordan, and forty-two thousand

“Fugitives of Ephraim”, twice. The taunt of verse 4 is hard Hebrew: “You are fugitives of Ephraim, Gilead, in the midst of Ephraim, in the midst of Manasseh.” It seems to sneer at the Gileadites as runaways from the tribes west of the river. The shelf smooths it in different directions: the NIV “renegades from Ephraim and Manasseh”, the TLB “mere outcasts and the scum of the earth”, the KJV “fugitives of Ephraim among the Ephraimites”. The phrase pelitei Efrayim, “fugitives of Ephraim”, stands in two verses of the Hebrew Bible: the taunt of verse 4, and verse 5, where the fugitives of Ephraim are Ephraim’s own men running for the river. The insult comes back on the men who made it, and the translation keeps the same words in both verses so that it can.

The fords of the Jordan. Gilead takes the crossings “against Ephraim”, as Ehud did against Moab: the phrase “the fords of the Jordan” stands in three verses of the Hebrew Bible, Judges 3:28 (now on these pages) and verses 5 and 6 here. Ma’barot, “fords”, comes from avar, “cross”, and the root runs through the chapter: Ephraim crossed over (v. 1), Jephthah crossed over (vv. 1 and 3), and the man at the river says “Let me cross” (v. 5). The KJV calls them “the passages of Jordan”.

“Are you an Ephraimite?” The word is ha-Efrati, and Hebrew uses it for two peoples. It stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: here; for Elkanah of the hill country of Ephraim (1 Samuel 1:1, now on these pages, “an Ephraimite”); for Jeroboam from Zeredah (1 Kings 11:26); and for people of Bethlehem in Judah, Elimelech’s household (Ruth 1:2, now on these pages, “Ephrathites”) and Jesse (1 Samuel 17:12). The B Greek text asks mē Ephrathitēs ei, “Are you an Ephrathite?” The man at the ford says no, and the Gileadites do not take his word for it.

Shibboleth. Shibbolet has two meanings, an ear of grain and a stream, and the word stands in five verses of the Hebrew Bible: here; at Job 24:24, an ear of grain; at Psalm 69:3 and Psalm 69:16, a flood; and at Isaiah 27:12, “the shibbolet of the River”, usually read as its stream (all yet to come in this translation; the two Psalm verses are 69:2 and 69:15 in English Bibles). At a river crossing the second sense fits the place, but the chapter does not say which the Gileadites meant, and the test is the sound, not the sense. The man says sibbolet, spelled with samekh, an s, where the password has shin, sh. “And did not manage to say it so”: lo yakhin is “did not get it ready, did not get it right”. Readings differ on what the Ephraimite sound was; the text gives only the two spellings.

Ear of grain, or password. The two Greek texts translate the word differently. The B text gives its meaning, “Say ‘ear of grain’” (stachys); the A text gives its use, “Say ‘password’” (synthēma). The Douay, from the Latin, does both: “Say then, Scibboleth, which is interpreted, An ear of corn”. The TLB explains the slip in English letters, “couldn’t pronounce the H”, and the KJV keeps “could not frame to pronounce it right”. English took its word shibboleth, a test that tells one group from another, from this verse.

“Slaughtered him”, and forty-two thousand. The verb of verse 6 is shachat, the word for slaughtering an animal for the altar (dictionary); the B Greek text goes further with ethysan, “they sacrificed”. “Slaughtered” keeps the Hebrew’s weight. The number is arba’im u-shnayim elef, and elef is both “thousand” and “clan” or “unit” (dictionary), the word of Gideon’s “my clan” (Judges 6:15, now on these pages). Read as a numeral, forty-two thousand men die at the river; read as units, forty-two companies. The text gives a number, and this translation prints it as one.

Verse 7 · Jephthah’s six years, and a burial “in the cities of Gilead”

Buried “in the cities of Gilead”. Jephthah “judged Israel six years”, in the words given to Tola and Jair (Judges 10:2–3, now on these pages). Then the Hebrew says he was buried be’arei Gil’ad, “in the cities of Gilead”, plural, and the shelf supplies a word to make one town of it: the KJV and ASV “in one of the cities of Gilead”, the NIV “in a town in Gilead”. The NWT 1984 “in his city in Gilead” and the Douay “in his city of Galaad” agree with the reading both Greek texts have, “in his city”, a difference of a letter or two in the Hebrew. This translation prints the Masoretic plural and leaves it strange.

Two old readings of the plural. Josephus, in the Greek of the Antiquities (5.270, numbered 5.7.12 in Whiston), names one place: Jephthah was buried “in his own homeland, Sebee”, in Gilead. The midrash Genesis Rabbah (60:3) reads the plural as it stands: Jephthah, it says, died limb by limb, each limb buried where it fell, “for it does not say ‘in the city of Gilead’ but ‘in the cities of Gilead’”, and it calls this a punishment for the daughter’s blood (the vow is at Judges 11:30–31, now on these pages). The chapter says nothing of the kind; it gives the plural and moves on.

Verses 8–10 · Ibzan of Bethlehem — which Bethlehem, and thirty daughters “sent outside”

Which Bethlehem? The chapter does not say. There is Bethlehem in Judah, the town of Ruth (Ruth 1:1, now on these pages), and a Bethlehem in Zebulun’s town list (Joshua 19:15, now on these pages), the tribe of the next judge. Josephus (Antiquities 5.271, numbered 5.7.13 in Whiston) puts Ibzan in “the tribe of Judah”, and the Talmud (Bava Batra 91a) says, in the name of Rav, that Ibzan is Boaz, Ruth’s husband. Neither claim comes from the chapter, which gives a town and nothing more.

“Sent outside … brought in from outside.” The Hebrew gives the verbs and no more: thirty daughters “he sent outside” (shillach ha-chutsah), thirty daughters “he brought in from outside for his sons”. Marriage is what it means, and much of the shelf says so: the NIV “gave his daughters away in marriage to those outside his clan”, the TLB “married his daughters to men outside his clan”, the Douay “sent abroad, and gave to husbands”. The KJV and ASV keep the verbs with “sent abroad”, and the NWT 1984 has “He sent outside and brought in”. This translation keeps the verbs, so the reader sees the marriages through them; “outside” is the Hebrew’s word, where “abroad” suggests another country.

A judge remembered for his household. Like Jair with his thirty sons (Judges 10:4, now on these pages) and Abdon after him, Ibzan is known by his children and his years. Josephus says it outright: Ibzan did nothing in his seven years “worth telling or remembering” (Antiquities 5.271).

Verses 11–12 · Elon the Zebulunite, buried in Aijalon — one name in the letters twice

Elon in Aijalon. In the Hebrew letters the man and the place are one word, אילון; only the vowels the Masoretes added make him Elon (Eilon) and the town Aijalon (Ayyalon). The B Greek text reads them alike: Ailōm the Zebulunite was buried in Ailōm. The Douay runs them together the other way, calling the judge “Ahialon a Zabulonite”, and says only that he “was buried in Zabulon”. Elon is also a name in Zebulun’s family: one of Zebulun’s three sons (Genesis 46:14, now on these pages), whose descendants are “the clan of the Elonites” (Numbers 26:26, now on these pages). The judge carries an old clan name of his tribe, and is buried in a town spelled the same.

Which Aijalon? The Aijalon everyone knows is in the south, the valley where the moon stood still (Joshua 10:12, now on these pages), listed among Dan’s towns (Joshua 19:42, now on these pages). Zebulun’s own town list in the same chapter names no Aijalon, and an Aijalon “in the land of Zebulun” stands nowhere else. Where it was is not known.

Verses 13–15 · Abdon son of Hillel — seventy riders on seventy young donkeys, and Amalek in the hills of Ephraim

Forty and thirty make seventy. Abdon’s forty sons and thirty grandsons are seventy riders on “seventy young donkeys”. Seventy is the book’s number for a great man’s household: Gideon “had seventy sons” (Judges 8:30, now on these pages), and Abimelech killed “the seventy sons of Jerubbaal” on one stone (Judges 9:5, now on these pages). The donkeys are ’ayarim, the word of Jair’s sons who “rode on thirty young donkeys” (Judges 10:4, now on these pages), and the translation keeps “young donkeys” there and here. The KJV and ASV have “ass colts”, the NWT 1984 “full-grown asses”, and the NIV “seventy donkeys”. Josephus makes horsemen of them: all seventy were excellent at riding, hippazein (Antiquities 5.274, numbered 5.7.15 in Whiston, where they are “very skillful in riding horses”).

Son of Hillel, the Pirathonite. The Greek of Josephus calls Abdon “son of Elon” (Ēlōnos pais, Antiquities 5.273), the judge before him, though Whiston’s English prints “the son of Hilel”; the chapter gives “son of Hillel”. His town gives the Bible one more Pirathonite, Benaiah, “of the sons of Ephraim”, who commands the eleventh month’s division of David’s army (1 Chronicles 27:14).

“The hill country of the Amalekites”, in Ephraim. Amalek is the desert enemy that fought Israel at Rephidim (Exodus 17:8, now on these pages), yet Abdon is buried in the land of Ephraim, “in the hill country of the Amalekite”, singular in the Hebrew. The Song of Deborah already joins the two names: “From Ephraim, whose root is in Amalek” (Judges 5:14, now on these pages). The chapter does not explain how the name came to Ephraim’s hills. The A Greek text has “in the mountain of Lanak” where the B text has Amalek. The KJV has “in the mount of the Amalekites” and the NWT 1984 “in the mountain of the Amalekite”.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the words exact and distinct: “Zaphon” for tsafonah, read as the valley town; “were called out” for vayitsa’eq; “cried to” for za’aq, Israel’s word to Jehovah; “deliverer” for moshia; “palm” for kaf beside “hand” for yad; “fugitives of Ephraim” the same in verses 4 and 5; “cross” and “fords” for avar and ma’barot; “slaughtered” for shachat; “in the cities of Gilead”, the Masoretic plural, unsmoothed; “outside” for ha-chuts; and “young donkeys” for ’ayarim, as at Judges 10:4.

Echoes paid. Ephraim’s complaint to Gideon at Judges 8:1 is made again, and this time answered with a war. The fords Ehud seized against Moab at Judges 3:28 are seized against Ephraim. “I saved you out of their hand” at Judges 10:12 becomes “you did not save me from their hand”. The minor judges’ formula of Tola and Jair at Judges 10:2–5 runs four more times, for Jephthah, Ibzan, Elon and Abdon. Gideon’s seventy sons at Judges 8:30 and Jair’s young donkeys at Judges 10:4 return in Abdon’s household. Ephraim’s “root in Amalek” at Judges 5:14 gets a burial place. The clan name Elon of Genesis 46:14 comes back as a judge’s name.

Echoes planted. The threat to burn a house with fire comes again against Samson’s wife at Judges 14:15. “My life in my palm” returns for David at 1 Samuel 19:5 and for the woman at Endor at 1 Samuel 28:21. Ha-Efrati comes back for Jesse of Bethlehem at 1 Samuel 17:12 and for Jeroboam at 1 Kings 11:26. Pirathon gives David a commander at 1 Chronicles 27:14, and shibbolet is a flood at Psalm 69:3. None of these is translated here yet.

Next installment in this book: Judges 13 — Israel does evil again and is given into the hand of the Philistines for forty years; the angel of Jehovah comes to the barren wife of Manoah of Zorah, promises a son who will be a Nazirite from the womb, and goes up in the flame of the altar; and Samson is born.
