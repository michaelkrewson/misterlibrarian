An unknown writer of the second Christian generation sends a 'word of exhortation' to Jewish believers tempted to fall back from Christ — and opens with the most polished Greek in the New Testament, arguing that the Son is higher than the prophets and the angels: the radiance of God's glory, addressed as God and as the Lord who laid the earth's foundations, seated at God's right hand.
Before AD 70, most likely c. AD 60–69. ⚠ The dating clue is internal and strong: the letter speaks of the temple sacrifices in the PRESENT tense, as still going on (8:4; 9:6-9; 10:1-2), and its whole case that they are obsolete would have been sealed by the temple's destruction in AD 70 — which it never mentions. It is quoted by 1 Clement around AD 96, fixing an upper bound. ⚠ Its authorship is the New Testament's most famous unsolved question — Paul on the traditional ascription, but rejected by nearly all today (the Greek and the second-generation standpoint of 2:3 are unlike him); Origen's 'who wrote it, God knows' still stands. Reported, not resolved.

Translator’s Notes

Verses 1–2 · the most polished sentence in the New Testament, and the God who finally speaks

⚠ Hebrews opens without a greeting — no “Paul,” no addressee, no “grace to you” — and launches straight into a period of Greek so finished it is often called the finest in the New Testament. The first line rings with alliteration the translation cannot fully keep: polymerōs kai polytropōs palai — five p-sounds in four words — “in many parts and in many ways, long ago.” The contrast is the book’s whole thesis in miniature: God’s old speech was fragmentary and various (a piece to this prophet, a piece to that, in dreams, laws, visions, oracles), and his new speech is single, final and personal — not in a prophet but “in a Son.”

“In a Son” — and the Greek has no article. En huiō, not “in the Son”: the stress falls on the kind of messenger, son-wise rather than servant-wise. Then two clauses that already claim everything: “whom he appointed heir of all things” (the end of the story) “through whom also he made the ages” (its beginning). ⚠ “The ages” is tous aiōnas — the same word can mean the worlds (all that exists in space) or the ages (all that unfolds in time), and here it likely means both at once: the Son is the one through whom reality and history alike were made. KJV “the worlds”; NIV “the universe”; ASV “the worlds.” The Son as the agent of creation is the claim of John 1:3 and Colossians 1:16, stated here a third way.

Verses 3–4 · three words that the fourth century went to war over

⚠ “The RADIANCE of his glory.” Apaugasma — a rare word, and precise: the shining-out of a light, the beam thrown from its source. It can be read actively (the Son shines with the Father’s own light) or passively (a reflection), and the difference matters, because the point is that the Son is not a lamp lit from another lamp but the very radiance of the one light — you cannot have the sun without its shining. KJV “the brightness of his glory”; NIV “the radiance of God’s glory”; Douay “the brightness of his glory.”

⚠ “The exact IMPRINT of his substance.” Two loaded words. Charaktēr is the die that stamps a coin, and the stamp it leaves — the impression that carries every feature of the original exactly; it is the source of English “character.” And hypostasis — here “substance,” the underlying reality of a thing — is the very word the fourth-century church fought over for a century: how many hypostaseis in God, and how does the Son’s relate to the Father’s? The writer of Hebrews is not doing that later metaphysics; he is reaching for the strongest available way to say the Son shows God exactly. ⚠ KJV “the express image of his person”; ASV “the very image of his substance”; NIV “the exact representation of his being.”

“Upholding all things by the word of his power.” Pherōn is not passive support, a pillar under a weight, but active carrying — bearing all things along, toward their end. The one through whom the ages were made also keeps them from moment to moment, and does it “by the word of his power” (dynamis), the same effortless speech that made them — the echo of Colossians 1:17, “in him all things hold together.”

“Having made purification for sins… sat down.” The sentence has run from before creation to the cross in a single breath, and now it rests — on a seat. ⚠ “Sat down at the right hand of the Majesty” quotes the enthronement of Psalm 110:1 (it will return at verse 13), and the posture is the argument: Israel’s priests stood, because their work was never finished (10:11); this priest sat, because his was. Everything Hebrews will say about the sacrifice is folded into that one seated figure.

Verses 5–6 · the catena begins: a Son, and the angels told to worship him

What follows is a chain of seven Old Testament quotations — a catena, a string of proof-texts — all making one case: the Son is higher than the angels, who in this letter’s world were the great mediators, the givers of the law (2:2). The first two go together: “You are my Son; today I have begotten you” (Psalm 2:7, a coronation psalm) and “I will be to him a father” (2 Samuel 7:14, God’s promise to David’s line). ⚠ “Today” is not read as the eternal begetting but as a day in history — the enthronement, which Hebrews ties to the resurrection: the day the Son was declared what he always was. No such word was ever spoken to an angel.

⚠ “Let all God’s angels BOW DOWN before him.” When God brings “the firstborn” into the inhabited world, the angels are commanded to worship him — a line from the Greek of Deuteronomy 32:43 (and Psalm 97:7). Proskyneō is the prostration owed to God; to command it toward the Son, and from the angels, is to put him on the wrong side of the line that divides Creator from creature — deliberately. We render it “bow down before” to keep the bodily gesture the Greek names. KJV “let all the angels of God worship him.”

Verses 7–9 · servants made of wind and fire — and the Son called God

Angels are drafts of air and tongues of flame. Psalm 104:4 is read to make the angels changeable, elemental servants — “winds… a flame of fire” — over against the Son on a throne. Leitourgous, “ministers,” is the word for public servants, even liturgical ones; the angels do service, the Son is served.

⚠ “Your throne, O GOD, is forever.” Psalm 45:6-7 — a wedding song for a king — is now addressed to the Son, and it contains the word ho theos, “God.” ⚠ There is a real translation choice: ho theos can be a vocative, “Your throne, O God” (the Son directly addressed as God), or a nominative, “God is your throne” or “Your throne is God.” The vocative is the older and majority reading, and the flow of the argument — God speaking to the Son (verse 8, “but of the Son”) — presses hard toward it; a minority prefer the nominative precisely to avoid calling the Son God so baldly. KJV, ASV and NIV read the vocative, “Thy throne, O God”; the NWT reads “God is your throne,” the nominative — and here, as with every rendering that carries doctrinal freight, the library prints the reading with the stronger grammar and names the alternative rather than burying it. This is one of the plainest places in the New Testament where the Son is addressed as God.

“God, your God, has anointed you.” And in the very next breath the Son has a God — “therefore God, your God, has anointed you” — so the psalm holds both together without strain: the one addressed as God is also the anointed one who worships God. Hebrews will spend the rest of the letter inside that double truth, and never tries to flatten it.

Verses 10–12 · the psalm to the Creator, read to the Son

⚠ “You, Lord, in the beginning laid the foundation of the earth.” This is the boldest stroke in the chapter — a psalm to the CREATOR read straight to the Son. Psalm 102 is a prayer to Yahweh the everlasting Creator — and the writer of Hebrews simply hands it to the Son, address and all, “kyrie” included. ⚠ The move only works if the one who laid the earth’s foundations and the Son are the same — which is exactly the claim of verse 2, “through whom he made the ages.” The library met this Creator at the other end of the canon, at Genesis 1:1, “in the beginning God created”; Hebrews reads the Son back into that beginning.

“They will perish, but you REMAIN… you are the same.” The contrast is between a creation that ages like clothing — wearing out, rolled up, changed, folded away like a worn cloak — and a Son who does not change. “You are the same” (su… ho autos ei) is nearly a name; it returns at the letter’s end as the famous line, “Jesus Christ, the same yesterday and today and forever” (13:8). The heavens are his wardrobe; he outlasts them.

Verses 13–14 · the enthroned Son, and the angels who run errands for us

⚠ “Sit at my right hand.” The catena closes where the prologue pointed (verse 3), on Psalm 110:1 — the single most-quoted Old Testament verse in the New Testament, cited or echoed some three dozen times. To no angel was it ever said; the invitation to share God’s throne belongs to the Son alone, and the enemies-made-a-footstool is the one thing still outstanding, the “until” the rest of history is spent on.

“Ministering spirits, sent out for service.” The chapter ends by settling what angels are for: not objects of worship but leitourgika pneumata, servants on errands, “sent out” (the verb is the root of “apostle”) for the sake of “those who are to inherit salvation” — that is, ordinary believers. ⚠ It is a quiet reversal: the mighty angels of verse 5 turn out to be in service to the heirs of verse 2, running errands for the people the Son died to save. The whole hierarchy has been re-drawn around him.

Patterns worth carrying forward

Where this translation keeps the exact / distinct words: “in many parts and in many ways” for the alliterative polymerōs kai polytropōs, the fragmentary old speech against the single new one; “the ages” for aiōnas, worlds and time at once; “radiance” for apaugasma, the shining-out; “the exact imprint of his substance” for charaktēr and hypostasis, the coin-die and the underlying reality; “upholding” for pherōn, carrying-along and not merely propping; “bow down before” for proskyneō; and “Your throne, O God” at verse 8, the vocative kept with the nominative named.

Echoes paid. The Son as agent of creation, “through whom he made the ages,” as at John 1:3 and Colossians 1:16; “upholding all things” beside Colossians 1:17, “in him all things hold together”; the “exact imprint” beside the “image of the invisible God” of Colossians 1:15; and the Son who “laid the foundation of the earth,” read back into the “in the beginning” of Genesis 1:1 and the Word of John 1:1.

Echoes planted. Psalm 110:1, the seated Son at God’s right hand (v3, v13), which the whole letter’s priesthood argument will rest on — the priest who sits because the work is done (10:12), and the “priest forever after the order of Melchizedek” of Psalm 110:4, the chapter 7 theme; “you are the same,” which returns as 13:8; and the superiority that structures the book — better than angels here, and then better than Moses, Aaron, and the whole first covenant.

Hebrews is new to the library — the most polished Greek in the New Testament, an anonymous “word of exhortation” (13:22) whose author, in Origen’s famous shrug, “God only knows.” Next in this book: chapter 2 — “how shall we escape if we neglect so great a salvation?”, the Son made “lower than the angels for a little while” so that by God’s grace he might “taste death for everyone,” and the one who is not ashamed to call us brothers.
